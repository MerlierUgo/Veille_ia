## Rubric-Based AES with LLM Ensembles: Scoring of Student Responses in Critical Online Reasoning Using Ensembles of LLMs

Grading student responses is costly and time-consuming. Moreover, human annotators are prone to bias, fatigue, and rater drift, which further limits grading reliability. Various methods have been employed to develop automated essay scoring (AES) systems to reduce these costs and address bias-related issues. These methods range from early text-feature-based systems to current machine-learning and transformer-based models. Specifically, large language models (LLMs) offer the potential to streamline the development of AES systems. However, they are subject to well-known limitations, such as hallucinations and prompt sensitivity. This creates a need to explore the potential of a mixed-methods approach that leverages the synergies among individual LLMs through ensemble formation.

We evaluate the potential of ensembles of LLMs to match human-rater agreement when grading student responses in economics and medical education across 103 criteria. We use 631 German-language responses and corresponding human ratings by 2 to 5 annotators from a previously conducted assessment. We experiment with 523 rubric-based prompt configurations across 15 open-weight LLMs and assess their consistency with human raters using Krippendorff’s alpha for inter-annotator agreement (IAA) and the Alternative Annotator Test (alt-test).

Our results show that replacing one human rater with the best-performing LLM–prompt configuration maintains or improves IAA for 91 of the 103 criteria and that LLMs pass the alt-test for 90 criteria. Our findings suggest that current state-of-the-art LLMs can support AES of German responses in economics and medical education, reducing human workload and enabling faster processing, while validation against human ratings remains necessary.


### 1 Introduction

The grading or scoring of human-written texts, such as responses to tasks in higher education, is a central component of educational research, but it is also a lengthy and costly process [ 115 , 92 , 103 , 47 ] . Human raters exhibit biases such as halo effects, fatigue, and inconsistency, especially when evaluation rubrics become more fine-grained and large-scale assessment is required [ 44 , 82 , 97 , 70 ] . Consequently, the need for automated essay scoring (AES) systems in education has long been recognised [ 109 , 59 , 90 ] . This is supported by the fact that automation and machine learning (ML) have been used in education for a variety of tasks [ 76 ] . Such methods not only lower costs but also enable applications such as real-time feedback during the writing process [ 32 , 91 ] . Moreover, the success of large language models (LLMs), driven by their strong performance and ease of use through natural language, has led to extensive research and practical adoption of machine-learning methods [ 108 , 159 ] .

This raises the question of whether LLM-based approaches can perform scoring tasks: while they may reduce human biases, they introduce biases of their own stemming from the LLM and underlying training data, thereby potentially compromising scoring results [ 5 , 142 ] . Research, particularly beyond the field of education, highlights these limitations: Zhou et al. [161] , Wang et al. [135] evaluate LLMs for paper reviewing and participant simulation, reporting problems with long texts and harmful misrepresentation when humans are replaced by LLMs. In this vein, Ma [96] shows that “assigning a human-like identity to LLMs does not produce human-like behaviors” , while Piot et al. [112] suggest that, although LLMs are no substitute for humans, they can produce similar results in terms of relative rankings. Nevertheless, LLMs are widely used for annotation and assessment tasks, that is, as ‘LLMs as judges’ ; see, for example, LLM-Eval by Lin and Chen [89] , MT-Bench, Chatbot Arena [ 160 ] , and the surveys by Gu et al. [54] , Li et al. [85] , which provide overviews of relevant fields and applications. Research more often highlights the opportunities and strong performance of LLMs. They span a wide range of domains, both in education [ 71 , 39 ] and beyond, for example in medicine and healthcare [ 9 , 95 , 86 ] , law [ 22 ] , and finance [ 118 ] .

Consequently, we face a dilemma: although LLMs continue to evolve and are increasingly studied for their potential to complement or replace human linguistic abilities, their limitations are also becoming clearer, calling their broad applicability into question. Despite their widespread use and considerable potential, further research is needed to disentangle their strengths and limitations, especially in the assessment of human-produced work. This is exactly the task of the present article, with a focus on AES.

More specifically, we examine LLMs as means for rubric-based scoring of responses in the context of critical online reasoning (COR). In COR research, students are assessed on their ability to gather online information to complete a task by writing a response to a question, with COR examined across three phases or facets: (1) how they select sources and by which methods (OIA, online information acquisition), (2) how well they assess source reliability (CIE, critical information evaluation), and (3) their reasoning skills, as reflected in their response (REAS, reasoning with evidence, argumentation, and synthesis) [ 100 ] . With a focus on the third facet, REAS, we study the potential of using LLMs for the automated scoring of responses using previously collected responses and ratings from assessments in German economics and medical education. We provide empirical evidence on whether LLMs can replace individual human annotators while matching their level of agreement, i.e., without lowering the agreement of the rater group. Thus, our construct is COR performance, while we examine student responses as items and the features attributed to them by LLMs as observable indicators.

In this study, we address the following research questions:

- RQ1 To what extent can LLM-based AES systems replace individual human raters in grading student responses while maintaining the level of agreement among the raters?

- RQ2 How robust are LLM-based AES systems across eight tasks spanning the domains of economics and medicine?

- RQ3 Which prompt- and model-based features have the greatest influence on the performance of LLM-based AES systems?

Since our investigation focuses on LLMs as a potential replacement for human annotators of responses to COR tasks in the context of AES, section 2 is divided into four parts covering background on AES, related work on LLM-based annotation, LLM-based AES, and rater evaluation.


### 2 Related Work


#### 2.1 Background on AES

Automated essay scoring (AES) is the task of automatically grading written texts according to one or more assessment criteria. See Shermis and Burstein [121] , Lim et al. [88] for overviews of AES systems and methods. A closely related task is automatic short-answer grading (ASAG); see Burrows et al. [17] , Haller et al. [60] for overviews. Based on the criteria for short answers [ 17 ] , the statements we analyse in this study share characteristics of short answers, as they require external knowledge, are written in natural language, and are assessed mostly on content, but they are less restricted in length and openness. We therefore consider relevant research from both areas. Starting with early AES systems such as Project Essay Grade (PEG) [ 110 ] , researchers first studied feature-engineering-based approaches [ 18 , 116 , 153 , 27 , 33 ] before increasingly relying on neural networks that perform feature engineering as part of the training process [ 127 , 3 , 42 , 137 , 147 ] . Rather than relying solely on the latest developments in LLMs, several approaches use a wider range of machine-learning methods to perform AES. Wilson et al. [144] recently studied the automatic scoring of students’ scientific argumentation skills without the use of LLMs, relying on an ensemble of machine-learning algorithms. While they do not compare their results with LLM-based approaches, their approach achieves strong performance while avoiding LLM-specific limitations such as hallucinations. However, it still exhibits bias, for example, by assigning lower ratings to English learners, and requires more effort and expert knowledge to prepare.

With the use of LLMs, the preparation and development of such tools can potentially be shifted to prompt engineering, while natural language as an interface makes these systems more accessible to non-experts. The ability to instruct LLMs in natural language and obtain explanations is also identified as an advantage by Gao et al. [49] . They caution against focusing on English, especially in light of the limited amount of training data available for low-resource languages in LLMs. Emirtekin [46] presents a review of LLM use for automated assessment, giving an overview of domains, datasets and languages. At the same time, newer reasoning models can reduce the need for specialised fine-tuning [ 15 , 141 , 75 , 58 ] . Current advances in multimodal [ 126 ] , agentic [ 87 ] , and workflow-based AI [ 146 , 158 ] make LLMs increasingly capable of scoring not only text data, but also image data (see vision language models, and, more generally, multimodal LLMs [ 19 , 156 ] ) and process data such as visited websites and keystrokes [ 62 ] . Although these developments are outside the scope of the present study, they indicate how LLM-based assessment may develop beyond written statements alone.

In light of these developments, the prospect of AES increasingly based on LLMs arises. However, fundamental problems such as hallucinations remain unresolved, especially for LLMs. This raises the question of whether combining LLMs with prompting strategies can produce scoring results suitable for supporting or even replacing human scorers. In other words, can LLMs serve as scorers or annotators alongside humans? This question is the central focus of this article.


#### 2.2 LLM-based Annotation

The potential of using LLMs such as ChatGPT for annotation tasks has been demonstrated across different domains and tasks, although performance relative to human annotators varies: Gilardi et al. [52] show that ChatGPT-3.5 outperforms crowdworkers, but not trained annotators, whereas Törnberg [130] reports ChatGPT-4 outperforming both. However, Tavakoli and Zamani [129] identify calibration issues and prompt sensitivity as persistent problems even in recent models such as ChatGPT-4o and Claude 3. They therefore propose a human-in-the-loop workflow to improve the reliability of LLM-based annotation. Similarly, Wang et al. [136] use human-rated gold data to verify LLM-generated annotations and report improved accuracy after re-annotating failed annotations by human raters.

Borse et al. [14] investigate the replacement of a human rater. They compare ChatGPT-4o and ChatGPT-4.5 with the combined ratings of two human raters and report reliability in terms of Cohen’s kappa. They conclude that LLMs can replace human raters in the qualitative analysis of transcripts, but “have limitations in coding themes that are not domain-specific” . Other studies caution against replacing humans with LLMs, including Clarke and Dietz [30] and Shen et al. [120] . The latter conclude that, while models like ChatGPT and Llama can outperform other automated systems, they cannot fully replace humans, and report problems with LLMs’ ability to distinguish between results with similar performance, model bias, and decreasing correlation with human judgments for higher-quality summaries.

Model bias in automated annotation is not unique to LLM-based methods. Amorim et al. [5] show that feature-based machine-learning models trained on data from which human-rater bias has been removed perform better. For LLM-based annotation, Weidmann et al. [143] find that Llama 3.1 and ChatGPT-4o are able to perform annotation and approximate human judgments, but that the models’ bias is clearly noticeable and affects the annotation. They show that using an ensemble method is not sufficient on its own, but that combining two LLMs with opposing biases improves the results. The responsible use of LLMs, and AI more generally, is studied by Johnson and Zhang [72] , who observe that the average score assigned by the LLM is lower than that assigned by the human annotators. The authors therefore conclude that evaluation should include fairness and explainability. Similarly, Chen et al. [26] show that both humans and LLMs produce significant biases, although these biases differ and vary in degree.

Taken together, these findings strongly suggest the need for human-based validation of LLM-based annotation, even though human judgement is itself not free from biases and should be taken into account when evaluating LLM-based approaches. The findings motivate the approach in our study to compare multiple LLMs and prompts with individual human ratings. We discuss the potential and use of LLM-based tools and methods for AES specifically in the next section.


#### 2.3 LLM-based AES

Research on the assessment of written responses in education in both AES and ASAG also shows ambivalent results. In 2024, Chamieh et al. [24] analyse the use of LLMs to automatically score short-answer responses. They use existing datasets such as ASAP in their evaluation and test GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and Llama. The authors report low performance, which they were able to improve by fine-tuning the LLMs. They conclude that there “currently seems to be no basis for applying LLMs for short-answer scoring” , especially when tasks require domain knowledge or complex reasoning. Using Llama 2 and ChatGPT-3.5, Mansour et al. [98] evaluate LLM performance in automated essay scoring on the ASAP dataset and compare it with conventional non-LLM systems. They show the importance of prompt engineering for each task and LLM separately and observe differences in prompt performance between ChatGPT and Llama. Additionally, Llama’s performance fluctuates much more than ChatGPT’s, further highlighting the differences between more open and more closed LLMs. Compared with non-LLM systems, they report much lower performance for both LLMs, highlighting the limitations of models from just a few years ago. Conversely, in a different assessment setting, Latif and Zhai [81] report an average performance increase of 9.1% when comparing a fine-tuned GPT-3.5 model with a BERT-based approach in an assessment task in science education.

Since these studies, multiple LLM generations have significantly improved models’ reasoning abilities. Reasoning, as manifested, for example, in chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting [ 140 , 75 ] , concerns the models’ ability to break a task down into subtasks and elaborate on them in order to arrive at an overall solution, thereby giving the models greater control over response generation. Regardless of whether reasoning is explicitly elicited by prompting, newer models are trained to generate reasoning traces or argumentation chains to produce better answers; the approaches further discussed in this section focus on such models.

Yancey et al. [149] compare ChatGPT-3.5 and ChatGPT-4 with human annotators using essays written by English learners and report that ChatGPT-4 performs on a par with conventional models. Very recently, Cong et al. [31] also report performance matching or exceeding that of BERT-based models.

Lee et al. [83] compare GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 in scoring science assessments completed by U.S. middle school students and graded by human raters. They observe that there was no single best-performing prompt across all tasks and that LLMs are capable of generating explainable and interpretable ratings, while the use of CoT prompting and few-shot examples improves accuracy. Similarly, using student-writing assessment data from young students in the U.S., Huang and Wilson [65] evaluate GPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Flash, and Claude Sonnet 4 and compare them with ratings produced by trained professionals. Their findings confirm that structured prompts and few-shot examples improve Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK) alignment [ 41 , 35 ] and can achieve performance comparable to feature-based systems, which they report remain “the most reliable” .

The effects of rubric design are demonstrated by Frohn et al. [48] , who compare human ratings with ratings generated by 11 LLMs, including Claude, Gemini, ChatGPT, and Llama variants, on short-answer questions in mathematics and English. They score answers as correct or incorrect and evaluate three prompts that provide (1) only the question, (2) the question plus rating criteria, and (3) examples. Although the authors investigate ASAG, their comparison of multiple LLMs and different rubric information is directly relevant to our multi-model rubric-based experiment setting. They observe that the group of LLMs showed higher agreement reliability among themselves than the human raters. Results are reported using Fleiss’ kappa and show that LLM performance depends on rubric design: the more information provided in the prompt, the higher the reliability .

This phenomenon is also investigated in the AES field by Yoshida [157] , who use the TOEFL11 dataset, Claude 3.5 Haiku, ChatGPT-4o-mini, Gemini 1.5 Flash, and Llama 3, along with three rubric variants. Except for Gemini, they report higher QWK scores with more informative prompts; however, the differences are small and not significant. They conclude that Gemini’s lower performance may result from the longer prompt. While prompt differences were small, absolute scores reached only around 0.6, leading them to conclude that AES using LLMs “is not yet ready for practical use” .

Kortemeyer [77] further compares the general-purpose LLM ChatGPT-4 with specialised or fine-tuned models for automated short-answer grading. They show that ChatGPT does not match the performance of specialised models; however, they note the advantage of not having to train the LLM. Additionally, they test two variants, one of which provides the LLM with a reference answer and yields comparable performance.

Shibata and Miyamura [122] study automated essay scoring by asking LLMs to rank essay pairs rather than assign scores directly. The final score is then computed by training RankNet [ 16 ] on multiple comparison pairs and linearly mapping its output to the rubric value range. The method is evaluated on two datasets using five LLMs: ChatGPT-4o and 4o-mini, Llama 3.1 and 3.2, and Mistral. It is found to outperform other prompting-based methods in most cases.

Working with student data requires great care with regard to data protection and privacy. Stasuik [125] compares locally hosted LLMs (Llama 3.1 and 3.2) with closed-weight commercial models (ChatGPT-4o-mini, o1, and o3-mini) for scoring essays from English courses. Using mean absolute grade difference, Stasuik shows that none of the tested models can replace human assessment and that LLMs often assign lower scores than human raters. In that study, newer LLMs with larger context windows performed better. Locally hosted models performed much worse; however, the newer Llama version showed substantial improvement, suggesting that future models may become more capable.

Emirtekin and Özarslan [45] study agreement between human annotators and four closed- and open-weight LLMs (ChatGPT-4o, Gemini 2 Flash, DeepSeek V3, and Llama 3.3) using university students’ short-answer texts. They report moderate agreement in terms of the QWK metric, with much higher agreement among the LLMs themselves. Further, they show that LLMs can efficiently grade lower-order cognitive skills, with criteria targeting factual and definitional aspects showing higher agreement than those requiring inference or contextual awareness. However, Emirtekin and Özarslan conclude that fully automated assessment is limited when higher-order skills are considered and that LLMs cannot replace human raters in this case. In this way, Tate et al. [128] report positive results for holistic AES using ChatGPT-3.5 and 4 and find no statistically significant difference between humans and ChatGPT.

In medical education, Quah et al. [113] highlight the importance of rubric design and note that ChatGPT was unable to identify incorrect statements or irrelevant content. They compare human and ChatGPT-4 performance using IAA and correlation measures. Their results vary by assessed question but still show potential for LLM-based automated scoring. Similarly, Deng et al. [38] show that alignment with human ratings decreases as criteria become more complex, from binary to more fine-grained criteria.

This research often demonstrates a limited assessment potential of LLMs. That potential depends on various factors, including model maturity and size. However, the use of large commercial models raises significant data-protection concerns. Overall, LLMs show greater potential for assessing lower-order than higher-order cognitive skills. Based on the current state of research, LLM-based AES systems cannot be recommended without reservation for the latter.

This interim finding raises several questions that we address in this article: since LLM-based scoring cannot currently replace human scoring and remains limited in the range of cognitive skills it can assess, the question arises whether LLMs are better suited to supplement human raters. This would require adapting the models to specific scoring criteria, either through fine-tuning or specialised prompting techniques, provided that these criteria do not merely target low-level skills. However, for data-protection reasons, only non-commercial models that are available locally and do not transmit any data to external providers can be considered for this purpose. Consequently, the next section reviews related work on rater evaluation: given a low IAA among raters, supplementing human raters becomes a challenging task.


#### 2.4 Rater Evaluation

A general overview of rater evaluation through the measurement of agreement among annotators is presented by Artstein and Poesio [8] , covering, e.g., Krippendorff’s alpha or Cohen’s kappa. A more recent line of research examines how annotator agreement and reliability can be measured and interpreted, especially when using inexperienced raters or crowdworkers. In such cases, reliance on absolute inter-rater metrics or thresholds, such as Krippendorff’s alpha, is considered problematic by Wong et al. [145] . They present a novel measure based on Cohen’s kappa, named cross-replication reliability (xRR). In the same way, Andersen et al. [6] compare a set of measures in the context of evaluating AES systems and propose an evaluation method that considers acceptable ranges when multiple annotations are available. These findings show that measures and thresholds should be contextualised, e.g., through replication, rater experience, and the purpose of comparison. In our study, we use rater agreement on human- and LLM- assigned ratings. For this reason, we rely on the Alternative Annotator Test , which is presented below, as a complementary measure.

When no human-generated gold data are available, for example because of cost, another method is to use an LLM to judge the outputs of multiple LLMs and generate a final result [ 55 ] . These judges, however, still have to be evaluated against human annotations to determine their performance. Guerdan et al. [55] study such judge systems under the assumption that there is not always a single correct gold annotation, or that one annotator may regard multiple ratings as correct. They find that such systems differ both from one another and from the human annotators.

Wang et al. [138] present an evaluation framework based on Generalizability Theory (G-theory) and Many-Faceted Rasch Measurement (MFRM). Notably, the authors report results comparing Claude (3.5 Haiku and Sonnet) with the widely used ChatGPT models (4o and 4o-mini). Claude outperforms the ChatGPT models and is reported to perform on a par with fine-tuned ChatGPT variants, further highlighting the rapid pace of model development. Using the ASAP datasets, the authors show that LLMs perform well in relative ranking, but are limited in absolute scoring. Compared with humans, who perform strongly on holistic scoring, LLMs significantly outperform them on analytic scoring; however, both are affected by biases such as halo effects.

The Alternative Annotator Test (alt-test), which we use for comparison in our evaluation, is presented by Calderon et al. [20] . It is a statistical approach for evaluating whether human annotators can be replaced by LLMs and whether an LLM “offers a better alternative to recruiting human annotators” . Calderon et al. [20] position the alt-test as a more rigorous metric for comparing an LLM with a group of human annotators than measures such as IAA, which assess the group as a whole. Like IAA, the alt-test assesses alignment with the human group rather than correctness. Calderon et al. [20] apply the alt-test to six LLMs (ChatGPT-4o and 4o-mini, Gemini 1.5 Flash and Pro, Llama 3.1, and Mistral) and human annotators with different skill levels. Based on the test, they show that at least one LLM can replace human annotators in nine of the ten datasets, and that closed-weight models outperform locally hosted ones. This insight is particularly relevant in our context, as it highlights the challenges associated with our focus on local models, which are largely indispensable for data-protection reasons. In other words, achieving satisfactory alt-test results with local open-weight models would demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach in this setting. Because the alt-test is a recent method with limited independent validation, we use it as a complementary evaluation measure in combination with IAA for evaluating replaceability.


#### 2.5 Summary

Overall, the research shows the potential of LLMs for AES and highlights the need for extensive model testing and prompt engineering. It also cautions against overreliance on LLM capabilities, suggesting that any fully automated approach must be validated by human raters in their intended assessment setting for each combination of model, task, rubric or criterion, and prompt.

In this article, we study how a range of open, locally hosted LLMs from different families rate student answers and examine, among other things, whether they can supplement or even replace human annotators using inter-annotator agreements and the alt-test. Compared with other studies, we evaluate previously collected, real-world responses in German, a less-studied language, and economics, a less-studied domain. That is, we explicitly investigate the possibility of replacing human raters with local models in this specific application area. We show that, under these conditions, individual human raters can indeed be replaced without lowering the agreement among the raters, even though previous research has generally reported negative results for local models. This provides valuable insight into the potential of rapidly advancing research on automating human capabilities in areas heavily affected by data-privacy concerns.

#### 3.1 Data and Rubrics

We consider real-world statements as written responses to questions posed to economics and social-science students ( “economics” group), as well as medicine and physics ( “medicine” group) students, during an online assessment of online reasoning in higher education in the Critical Online Reasoning in Higher Education (CORE) project [ 102 , 61 ] . The assessment was conducted independently of this study. As part of the original assessment, students were asked to search online for sources to support a written response, which was then graded by a subset of two to five trained raters. Searching and evaluating online sources is part of the assessed construct [ 100 , 101 ] ; the students were not supplied with any curated reading materials beyond the task and scenario. The questions are divided into two domains, economics and medicine, each containing four tasks. We study the responses in the practical context (also referred to as clinical reasoning in medicine) in the CORE assessment. The four economics tasks require students to answer questions that ask for arguments both for and against a concept or topic introduced in a scenario text:

- • Nudging ( NU ) asks for arguments regarding the use of personalised nudging to save energy.

- • Pilot strike ( PS ) asks for arguments regarding the issue of pay gaps and different pay scales for pilots.

- • Startup ( SU ) asks for arguments regarding the key economic criteria for selecting a location when relocating a startup.

- • Wind park ( WP ) asks for arguments regarding the economic considerations in choosing between a municipal wind park business model or no wind park development.

The medical tasks ask for diagnoses, symptoms, treatments and therapies in the following scenarios:

- • Breathing ( BR ) addresses breathing problems during the ascent of Mount Everest.

- • Eye ( EY ) addresses declining vision in the context of the mother having glaucoma.

- • Circulation ( CI ) addresses circulatory problems resulting from a spider bite.

- • Middle ear ( ME ) addresses an ear infection in a toddler.

In total, we consider 631 student-written statements, with an average of 79 responses per task (see Tables 1 and 2 for statistics on responses length and number sentences, and Figure 2 for the underlying distribution). Task design, time limits, and length requirements were defined by the domain experts of the original assessment. The students were given a limited amount of time to complete the task and were asked to ensure that their responses were between 150 and 250 words for economics and no more than 300 words for medicine. We notice a few outliers in the medical responses, but the distribution is generally similar across both domains. These responses were rated by two to four human annotators in economics and three to five human annotators in medicine. The ratings are based on 115 criteria, comprising two general criteria shared across all eight tasks and multiple task-specific criteria. The rubric and criteria were developed as part of the original assessment to assess the REAS facet of COR and were based on prior performance-assessment research [ 100 , 101 ] , with task development being based on an Evidence-Centered Design approach [ 99 ] . The process for domain-general COR is described by Hartig et al. [61] and the domain-specific assessments in medicine and economics were developed within the same methodological framework. The students did not have access to the scoring rubric, they were only presented with the task. We use the original rubric without redesigning its criteria or constructs for the present LLM-based study. However, the wording and presentation of individual criteria were varied across prompt configurations. We excluded two criteria relating to consulted sources with and without traceable web references, as assessing them would have required giving the LLMs access to the web. Again, this would have raised data-protection concerns by making the students’ web searches traceable. Additionally, the question about the initial working diagnosis is not considered in the medicine tasks, leaving a total of 95 criteria for the LLM-based system.

The grading rubric comprises the following two task-independent criteria:

- 1. The FILTER criterion assesses whether the student engaged seriously with the task and did not entirely miss the subject matter. This Boolean criterion is formulated as a “ yes / no ” question, with ‘no’ encoded as 0.

- 2. The COHERENCE criterion relates to the assessment of the response’s coherence, asking whether the student’s argumentation is coherent, consistent, and free of contradictions. It is based on a discrete four-point scale from 0 to 3, where 0 is the lowest rating, that is, the response is completely incoherent.

We further consider the following five economics criteria in our analysis:

- 1. The Boolean REFERENCE criterion asks whether the student’s written statement refers to the problem situation.

- 2. The Boolean CONCLUSION criterion asks whether the student’s written statement reaches a clear conclusion.

- 3. The Boolean ARGUMENTATION criterion asks whether the student’s written statement contains coherent economic argumentation. Note that this criteria was not rated for the Pilot strike task. This task therefore only considers four specific criteria.

- 4. The ARGUMENTS_COUNT criterion asks whether the student’s written statement contains comprehensible arguments. It counts up to four arguments and is therefore based on a discrete five-point scale from 0 to 4, where 0 is the lowest rating, indicating that no argument is provided.

- 5. The Boolean SPECIFIC_ARGUMENTS criterion asks whether the student’s statement mentions both arguments for and against personalised nudging.

The rubric used to assess the economics tasks contains criteria that can be grouped into the following three categories: The first category is concerned with formal criteria, for which the rater needs to check for the existence of specific elements. These criteria essentially act as filters at different levels: (1) Has the student made an actual attempt to work on this task? ( FILTER criterion); (2) if the student has worked earnestly on the task, does their response consider the stated problem? ( REFERENCE ); and (3) does the student formulate a conclusion, or does the response remain unresolved? ( CONCLUSION ). The second category is concerned with the structure of the response. This maps to the two non-Boolean criteria in the economics tasks: the rater needs to consider the text in full and construct some form of argumentative structure rather than only checking for the presence of specific elements. They have to quantify the coherence ( COHERENCE criterion) on a predefined scale and count the number of arguments made ( ARGUMENTS_COUNT ). Finally, the third category assesses domain knowledge . Unlike the other criteria, which can mostly be graded without specific domain knowledge, raters need to apply their subject knowledge to judge whether students have correctly done so in case of the ARGUMENTATION and SPECIFIC_ARGUMENTS criteria.

Depending on the particular task, 12 to 20 task-specific criteria are rated for the medicine tasks, including the two shared criteria. All of these criteria are Boolean criteria formulated as “ yes / no ” questions.

- 1. The A1 criterion asks whether the student has identified the most likely differential diagnosis.

- 2. The B* criteria ask for additional mentioned differential diagnoses. For the Circulation task, there are up to seven B* criteria.

- 3. The C* criteria differ by task: in task Breathing , they ask whether measures indicated for acute care are listed; in Eye , whether tests to rule out any incorrect differential diagnoses are mentioned; in Circulation , whether an explanation of affected organs and systems is given; and in Middle ear , whether at least one necessary test to confirm the suspected diagnosis is provided in the response.

- 4. The D* criteria ask for tests to rule out or confirm differential diagnoses ( Eye and Middle ear ), treatment options ( Breathing ), and organ-specific symptoms ( Circulation ).

- 5. The E* criteria ask whether therapies or measures are mentioned for all tasks except Eye .

- 6. The following criteria only apply to the Middle ear task: The F* criteria ask for a recommendation for action; the G* criteria ask for naming complications; and the H* criteria ask whether at least one plausible sign of complications is mentioned.

Note that the criteria used in the medical assessment, with the exception of FILTER and COHERENCE , are not as directly comparable as the economics criteria. Because of the naming scheme, we still present them grouped by name, similar to the economics criteria, and provide a thematic grouping. In contrast to the economics tasks, all require domain knowledge. The first category contains criteria that ask for diagnoses and diagnostic testing. It asks what the condition is and how it is confirmed or excluded. This group includes the criteria A , B , C in tasks Eye and Middle ear and D in the same two tasks. The second category includes criteria that are concerned with symptoms , complications or clinical features: C and D for Circulation , and G and H . The final group asks for treatments , or management more generally. It comprises the remaining criteria: C ( Breathing ), D ( Breathing ), E and F .

Five of the seven criteria for the economics tasks are Boolean ( yes / no ), while the other two use discrete scales with four ( COHERENCE ) and five ( ARGUMENTS_COUNT ) values. Similarly, except for the task-independent COHERENCE criterion, all criteria are Boolean for the medicine tasks. In all cases, the lowest, or “default” , criterion value is 0.

A SUM_SCORE is provided for each participant based on all criteria except FILTER . The SUM_SCORE is calculated as the average of the remaining criterion values, normalised to a range from 0 to 1. This composite score can be interpreted as an overall measure of student performance. This makes the student performance comparable across different tasks and domains, even though the criteria themselves cannot be compared directly in all cases. Although we do not predict the SUM_SCORE using the LLM-based AES, we include it in the evaluation, bringing the total number of criteria examined to 103. The FILTER criterion is treated as a filter in the assessment and grading of the student response: If a rater sets this criterion to 0, that is, if the student did not engage seriously with the question, all other criteria are assigned the lowest value.

We employ an LLM-based AES system to rate each student response on the basis of the respective criteria per task. The LLM receives the student text, the rating criteria, and optional additional information, and is then prompted to assign a value to each criterion. In total, we experiment with 15 LLMs of various sizes, using Docker Unified UIMA Interface (DUUI) [ 84 ] as the NLP framework and LangChain [ 25 ] and Ollama [ 104 ] for LLM processing. All LLMs were queried with the same random seed and a temperature of 0.8. We fixed the temperature to the default value of LangChain and Ollama across all models to provide a common configuration for evaluation and did not perform model-, prompt-, or task-specific optimization regarding these parameters. Except for the GPT-OSS models, all models were instructed to return JSON via the format parameter. Depending on the prompt, we increase the context size ( num_ctx ) and leave the number of generated tokens unrestricted ( num_predict = -2) in all cases. The list of nine studied LLMs in the main study is given in Table 5 , and Table 6 shows the twelve models of the Nudging-only study, six of which also appear in the main study. Together, they include multiple versions and configurations of DeepSeek, GPT-OSS, Llama, and Qwen, as well as MiniCPM-V, Mixtral and Nemotron. Additionally, we include the MedGemma and Plutus LLMs, which are trained on medical and economic data, respectively.

In the main study, we cover all eight tasks using 49 LLM and prompt configurations based on nine LLMs ( Table 5 ) for WP , EY , CI , and ME ; 50 for BR ; and 51 for PS , SU , and NU , respectively. The aim of this first study is to test whether LLM-based ratings can be generalised across the two domains and their eight tasks. We study 79 additional prompt and LLM configurations based on twelve LLMs ( Table 6 ) to perform the LLM-based rating of student responses to the economics Nudging task only. The prompts included in the Nudging-only study examine two approaches: first, prompting for all criteria; and second, using prompts dedicated only to the two non-Boolean criteria. The two studies use partly different sets of LLMs (see Tables 5 and 6 ). While the main study covers all eight tasks with a fixed set of models, including the domain-specific MedGemma and Plutus, the Nudging-only study analyses a single task in more depth and covers a wider range of model sizes, versions, and families. Six models are included in both studies. To assess the consistency of the ratings under the non-deterministic temperature setting, we check whether the selected model configuration results in unstable ratings. We evaluate this on a subset of LLM–prompt combinations from the Nudging-only study, which we query to rate each response five times using different random seeds, keeping the other parameters fixed.

The prompts are mostly written in German, the language of the statements. An example template for each prompt variant of the main study is shown in F . All prompt templates of both studies are provided as supplementary material, with placeholders for the task texts, scenarios, rubrics, and student data. In the following, we summarise the prompt variants. We consider the following prompt variants in the main study.

- • SIMPLE SCENARIO The prompts in this category ask the LLM to produce ratings for all criteria in a single request as a structured, predefined JSON object. We provide the same information that was available to human annotators in our annotation tool when they assessed the scoring criteria and their value ranges. This includes the relevant scenario text for the task question. Additionally, the LLM is informed that the response comes from a student answering a question within a specific domain. The prompt assigns the LLM the role of an annotation expert. This variant serves as the baseline for the other variants, each of which extends or modifies it.

- • CHAIN NOANSWER SCENARIO These prompts make multiple requests to the model, rating each criterion separately. Criterion definitions are not provided in advance, but only when each criterion is requested. However, the model has access to the full message history. This decomposition relates to the chain-of-thought prompting [ 141 ] , which has shown to improve the accuracy of LLM-based scoring [ 83 ] .

- • FEATURES SCENARIO This group of prompts builds on SIMPLE SCENARIO with respect to the definition of criteria and JSON output. Additionally, we provide the LLM with a range of features derived from the students’ answer texts. Such text features are the basis of feature-based AES [ 18 , 116 , 153 , 27 , 33 ] , which remains competitive with LLM-based scoring approaches [ 65 ] . These features include basic statistics such as token, sentence, unique-token, and lemma counts, as well as hate-speech and toxicity detection, emotion recognition, sarcasm detection, topic classification, sentiment analysis, and readability measures. The following natural language processing (NLP) systems are used to calculate these feature groups: Statistics are derived from preprocessing the responses with spaCy [ 64 ] (efficient model). Additional features are generated using implementations in the DUUI NLP framework: The readability features comprise nine metrics, including the Flesch-Kincaid grade level and the Automated Readability Index, and are calculated using the implementation by DiMascio [40] . We use text2ddc [ 132 , 133 ] for topic classification using the Dewey Decimal Classification scheme, as well as a transformer-based model using the Media Topic schema of the IPTC [ 80 , 79 ] . Sentiment analysis is performed using the lexicon- and rule-based tool VADER [ 66 , 131 ] and two additional transformer models [ 56 , 10 ] . All further features are based on transformer models for detecting hate speech [ 4 , 37 ] , toxicity [ 124 , 1 ] , emotions [ 13 , 12 ] , and sarcasm [ 63 ] . All of these text features are provided to the LLM as raw JSON containing the feature group, model name, and values.

- • OPT SCENARIO We study a prompt that was automatically optimized using Claude Opus 4.7 [ 7 ] . Since LLM-based scoring is sensitive to the prompt and no prompt performs best across tasks [ 98 , 83 ] , we let the LLM optimize the SIMPLE SCENARIO prompt. In a second variant, we instruct the LLM to generate a prompt based on all prompt templates studied in this paper. Claude was selected as a state-of-the-art model during prompt development and limited to generating the updated prompt. It was used as a complementary proprietary LLM to the locally-hostable models used in our study. Following the data protection rules, the proprietary Claude model received only the prompt templates, without any inserted content such as the training transcripts or student data. The final prompt was fixed before evaluation and applied to all evaluated models in the study without change.

- • TRAINING SCENARIO This prompt category extends SIMPLE SCENARIO with additional context from a transcript of a rater meeting comprising a general introduction to the assessment that covers the FILTER and COHERENCE criteria, the annotation tool, and the rating of student statements. The transcript is included in full in the prompt. Since prior work reports higher agreement when more detailed rubrics are provided [ 48 ] , this variant gives the LLM some of the information that the human raters received before rating.

In the Nudging-only study, we perform a focused follow-up on the Nudging task. We use the same prompt classes as described above and test additional prompts and models. One important distinction is the exclusion of the scenario description. The question for the economics task Nudging is phrased very directly as key arguments for and against personalized nudging to promote energy conservation , whereas the other tasks require knowledge from the scenario text. For this reason, we omit the scenario from the prompts in this experiment.

- • SIMPLE and BASIC : These prompts mirror the SIMPLE SCENARIO prompt from the main study but exclude the scenario text. We add the BASIC prompt, an English prompt with a question–answer structure, that mainly differs in the question posed to the students: The SIMPLE and all other prompts focus on the written statement of arguments for and against, whereas BASIC considers the scenario and the online research needed to produce the answer.

- • CHAIN : This prompt uses the CHAIN NOANSWER SCENARIO prompt as a basis, again without the scenario text. In addition to the criterion definition, each message also contains the full student response, so that the answer is always kept in the most recent message. We prompt the LLM for explicit JSON output and provide the criterion definitions accordingly; in an alternative version, we do not specify an output format and instead provide the definitions in plain text.

- • FEATURES : Building on the FEATURES SCENARIO prompt group from the main study, we add three sentence-relation features to the prompt. Entailment, justification, and elaboration scores are generated by prompting an LLM (DeepSeek-R1:70b) to assign a confidence score to each sentence pair, for example, by asking whether one sentence entails the other. We then use the confidence scores for the first–last and last–first sentence pairs as two features in the criteria-rating prompt. The features are provided to the LLM not only as raw JSON but also as summarised text in this experiment. To this end, we use different LLMs to generate texts that summarise the most important characteristics of the student response and its text features. This summary is then used in the rating prompt instead of the raw feature data.

- • OPT : We optimise the SIMPLE prompt using the PE2 approach of Ye et al. [155] instead of only relying on the zero-shot capabilities of an LLM for optimisation as in OPT SCENARIO . The PE2 algorithm uses one LLM to generate prompt variants, which are then automatically validated by another LLM on a few examples. We run the optimisation for five iterations, generating five new prompts each time ( T =5, n =4, m =5, k =5) and rank them by the proportion of correctly predicted criteria. In this way, the OPT prompt uses an optimised system prompt based on SIMPLE .

- • TRAINING : We extend the TRAINING SCENARIO prompt by adding a transcript of a task-specific presentation of the economics task. In this experiment, we experiment with providing only the general discussion or both parts combined, using either the full transcript or an LLM-generated summary.

As in the main study, the prompts considered in the Nudging-only study cover all criteria. Additionally, we analyse the two non-Boolean criteria, COHERENCE and ARGUMENTS_COUNT , using dedicated prompts (37 prompts and 8 prompts, respectively) that consider no other criteria. These are classified as follows:

- • COHERENCE VERY SIMPLE : We employ different prompts for the coherence criterion, all using structured input, that is, a tag-like format enclosing the LLM inputs and contexts. This prompt is deliberately simple and asks only for a coherence rating, without further definition or explanation.

- • COHERENCE STRUCT : Three prompts test minor formulation changes, such as capitalisation and the placement of task context for the response being rated.

- • COHERENCE STRUCT FEATURES : As with the all-criteria prompts, we incorporate several text features and instruct the LLM to use them to assess text coherence.

- • COHERENCE STRUCT TRAINING : As in the previous prompts, we experiment with including different variants of the rater-meeting transcript.

- • COHERENCE STRUCT CHAIN FEWSHOT : Finally, we prompt the LLMs for a coherence rating using a few examples in two ways: (1) without specifying an output format, followed by a chained prompt requesting the value in JSON, and (2) by directly requesting structured output for comparison. In one variant, we begin with a prompt asking the LLM to analyse the examples and formulate rules for the given ratings.

- • ARGUMENTS COUNT CGPT : Based on SIMPLE , these prompts were optimised by asking ChatGPT 4.5 [ 106 ] to refine them, yielding two variants: one provides information about the question answered by the student, and the other instructs the LLM more generally to extract arguments from the student response. The output is expected to follow a predefined JSON schema consisting of a list of arguments the model can extract from the student’s answer. This includes the argument itself, a description of it, and a justification for its inclusion. ChatGPT received only the prompt templates, without any context such as student data.

- • ARGUMENTS COUNT STRUCT : Prompts in this class build on the ARGUMENTS COUNT CGPT prompt and use a structured format. In one variant, we ask the LLM to merge multiple occurrences of the same argument into a single output instance.

For this evaluation, we ignore the FILTER criterion and ask the model for a rating regardless of the quality threshold imposed by the filter.

In total, counted per task, this amounts to 523 model–prompt configurations: 399 in the main study (49–51 per task), 79 in the Nudging-only study, and 45 criterion-specific prompts for COHERENCE (37) and ARGUMENTS_COUNT (8).

As a result of experimenting with different LLMs and the prompting strategies described above, we consider a total of 346,386 ratings produced by the LLMs for the main study and 65,947 for the Nudging-only study (including the criterion-specific prompts). Figures 18 and 22 in the appendix show the distribution of ratings across all considered LLM results for the economics and medicine tasks in comparison to the human ratings. Similar distributions can be observed for the FILTER and REFERENCE economics criteria and, with some exceptions, for SPECIFIC_ARGUMENTS and CONCLUSION when compared with the human raters’ distributions. For the medicine tasks, the criteria distributions show less similarity.

In case of the economics Nudging task, the FILTER criterion was rated as false in about 4.2% of the ratings, i.e., the students were assessed by the LLMs as having performed the task inadequately. In comparison, the other three economics tasks, as well as all four medicine tasks, produced only positive FILTER ratings.

To account for unbalanced data, that is, not all responses were rated by all human raters and not all models produced results for all responses, we use the following strategies to calculate agreement. As a baseline, we treat missing LLM ratings as if the corresponding criteria had received the lowest value. This allows us to handle technical problems that may arise during model querying or output parsing in a predictable manner for this analysis. This leads to variants for handling the LLM ratings based on the filter rating: (1) strict , which sets all other criteria to the lowest, or default, value when the FILTER is false, as described above; and (2) lenient , which retains the criterion values produced by the LLM. Lenient allows the model to recover from an incorrectly rated FILTER criterion and can simplify and speed up prompting, as a single request covers all criteria, reducing costs and improving performance, for example, in real-time feedback. In the remainder of this analysis, we only consider the strict FILTER handling, since the ratings are fully one-sided, i.e., positive for all tasks except economics Nudging . There, we do not see any differences as the FILTER criterion is still extremely skewed.

We further evaluate whether a human rater can be replaced by an automatically generated LLM rating by examining inter-annotator agreement using Krippendorff’s alpha [ 78 ] , which measures how consistently annotators agree beyond chance. In this way, we assess how strongly a model affects IAA and whether replacing a human rater with an LLM results in an increase or decrease of the agreement in relation to the observed agreement between the human annotators. A non-negative change in IAA indicates that an LLM can be substituted for a human while preserving the overall group agreement. However, it does not make any argument regarding the validity of the rating itself, because no consolidated gold rating is available. We are analysing whether LLMs have the potential to reach a level of agreement comparable to the human annotators under the same rubric-based assessment setting. Each human rater is replaced in the analysis. Our objective is not to identify or approximate a specific individual rater using an LLM-generated rating, but to evaluate whether LLMs can substitute raters in the group while preserving overall group agreement. We select IAA for measuring the results as we do not compare the LLM-generated ratings to a single consolidated gold score, we treat the individual human ratings as equally plausible and evaluate agreement over the group of humans and LLM-based raters. Krippendorff’s alpha is used as the main method because it measures agreement beyond chance, supports more than two raters and handles missing ratings [ 8 ] . These features are relevant to our study because the student statements were rated by different numbers and combinations of human annotators. In comparison, QWK, a weighted form of Cohen’s kappa that is widely used for evaluating AES, supports primarily two raters, and Fleiss’s kappa requires equal amounts of ratings per item and assumes randomly sampled annotators; Krippendorff’s alpha has therefore been recommended as a general alternative in multi-rater evaluations [ 41 , 73 ] . However, Krippendorff’s alpha can be sensitive to imbalanced data and more difficult to interpret. We therefore report unanimous agreement, Cronbach’s alpha and ICC(3,k) as complementary measures of rater consistency [ 2 ] . Additionally, we test whether the LLMs can replace at least 50% of the human raters at a statistically significant level by employing the Alternative Annotator Test . It can be run on a small sample of 50–100 cases with at least three human annotators, without requiring consolidated gold annotations, and can incorporate annotator costs through a cost-benefit parameter. The test compares each human annotator’s alignment with the rest of the group to the LLM’s alignment with that same group, tests the difference for each annotator, and computes the LLM’s win rate from the rejected hypotheses. Following the authors of the alt-test, we use a cost-benefit parameter of 0.15, which they present for skilled workers. We consider this skill level to be the closest to the human annotators who rated the original student statements and additionally assess the dependence of the parameter choice in a sensitivity analysis. Finally, we take a further step towards full automation by examining changes in agreement when two human raters are replaced, leaving at least one human rater as the necessary source of verification. For this analysis, we replace all combinations of two human raters with all unique model–prompt configurations. In addition to the top-performing configurations, we examine the top 10 configurations to evaluate reliable models and prompts overall. That is, we select the top 10 configurations based on their best performance per task and criterion, measured by the highest IAA when replacing any of the human raters, including cases in which the change is negative. We then rank them by how often they appear in the top 10, grouped by domain and criterion and evaluate their Jaccard similarity. In this way, we address research questions RQ1 and RQ2 .


#### 3.3 SHAP Analysis

To study the influence of model and prompt choice, we perform a SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) [ 93 , 94 ] analysis on a proxy model trained to predict agreement. For this purpose, we train proxy models that predict the observed IAA based on the experiment, prompt, and model characteristics as input features. The proxy models are only used as explanatory models for the SHAP analysis in this study and are not systems designed for predicting IAA in practical use outside of this study. We experiment with both regression and classification models, first attempting to predict the actual IAA value and, second, whether a non-negative change can be achieved. The 33 features include the task and task domain, the LLM and prompt, the LLM family and size, and the prompt length, style, and output formats. They are grouped as follows:

- 1. The model group contains the three model-related features: model size, family, and full name.

- 2. The prompt group covers 17 features, including the prompt name, its class as a categorical feature, and multiple binary features. These encode whether the prompt is a chain prompt, contains training material or few-shot examples, is extended with textual features, contains the scenario text, is optimized, uses a structured method to provide the input, and specifies the expected output format.

- 3. The domain and task names are used in the task feature group, as well as the criterion itself.

- 4. The rater group consists of only one feature related to the human raters and contains the removed rater as a categorical feature.

- 5. The decoding group includes the LLM parameters temperature, format, and context size. Note that temperature was the same in all experiments.

- 6. The resource feature group summarises LLM application values, i.e., the mean, SD, min, and max statistics of the input and output tokens processed. These values are calculated as averages per task.

We use Histogram-based Gradient Boosting (HGBT) models for both regression and classification. The targets are the IAA alpha value and Boolean non-negative change, respectively. We include baseline models using the mean strategy for regression and most frequent strategy for classification. For numerical features, we use a median imputer and standard scaler ; for categorical features, we use a most frequent imputer and one-hot encoder . The models are trained using the scikit-learn [ 111 ] implementation with default parameters and all results are obtained using 5-fold cross-validation, stratified for the classification model. We then perform a SHAP analysis using its TreeExplainer on the proxy models to determine which experimental characteristics contribute to the LLM performance. Additionally, we examine the correlation between IAA and the model size as well as the number of processed tokens using Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients calculated over the mean sizes per LLM–prompt configuration. This analysis provides insights into research question RQ3 .

#### 4.1 Human Rater Agreement

We present the inter-annotator agreements of the human raters based on Krippendorff’s alpha [ 78 , 23 ] using the interval difference function. The results of the four economics tasks presented in Table 7 show, in some cases, considerable differences in the agreement for the same criteria across different tasks. Good agreement, with alpha values over 0.8 [ 21 , 8 ] is only achieved for the FILTER criterion in case of the two economics tasks Wind park and Startup . For task Nudging , the agreement is only tentatively acceptable, with a value of 0.68. Similarly, the criterion SUM_SCORE in tasks Wind park and Pilot strike are placed within the same agreement range. The inter-annotator agreement for the other criteria can be considered unreliable, with values well below 0.5 in multiple cases. This is particularly evident for CONCLUSION in task Nudging , for which the alpha value is close to 0 and thus only slightly above the level of chance.

We report unanimous rating agreement as an additional metric. In the event of highly skewed rating distributions, a low IAA may result from disagreement over a small number of instances of under-represented values, which can have a strong impact on the agreement score. This is visible for the FILTER criterion in Pilot strike : here we see an unanimous rating in 93% of the responses, but an IAA alpha of only 0.15.

The intraclass correlation coefficients ICC(3,k) range from 0.586 to 0.957, indicating mostly good to excellent inter-rater reliability [ 123 , 29 , 134 ] . The ICC results generally align with the Krippendorff’s alpha agreements. Similarly, Cronbach’s alpha (calculated using the listwise deletion method) estimates internal item consistency ranging from poor to excellent, with values from 0.35 to 0.953 [ 34 , 51 , 134 ] .

These measurements emphasise the challenges involved in rating the economics tasks based on the presented criteria. In our analysis, we acknowledge that these human ratings differ and that no consolidated gold-standard annotation was provided as part of the original assessment data. We therefore evaluate the LLM-based ratings in this multi-rater setting without assuming or constructing a single gold-standard annotation. Instead, each human rating is treated as an equally plausible reference annotation, and the LLM is evaluated by the agreement between the individual raters in the rater group rather than against a single gold label.

Looking at the three groups of criteria, in the first group of formal criteria, we notice extremely low human rater agreement on the CONCLUSION criterion in the Nudging task. In contrast, the other two criteria display the highest agreement values. This suggests a difficulty or misunderstanding regarding what qualifies as a conclusion in the context of the task. This appears to be a consequence of the ratings performed by rater B. However, this seems to be specific to this task, as the other three tasks show agreement values comparable with the other criteria. This task-difference is also evident for the REFERENCE criterion, for which Nudging behaves differently: agreement is relatively high with 0.648, while the other three tasks display alpha values around 0.24. In the second group, which contains the structure criteria, no clear distinction in agreement is evident when comparing the different value ranges. Despite their larger value range, the two non-Boolean criteria exhibit IAA scores in the mid-range. The IAA among the human raters varies in third category comprising domain knowledge , with the ARGUMENTATION criterion being lower except for the Nudging task. This could suggest a problem with rater calibration. Since this criterion accepts only “yes” or “no” as a rating, the raters might use different thresholds when assessing economic argumentation. Similar to the CONCLUSION criterion, rater B displays different behaviour in task Nudging ; however, this time, the difference appears only in comparison with rater A and not with rater C.

In the pairwise Krippendorff’s alpha inter-annotator agreements between the human raters we observe that the SUM_SCORE results are stable despite the varying outcomes of the other criteria. For the other criteria, we notice low agreements e.g., with rater B in task Nudging or rater A in Startup . This suggests that there are systematic differences in evaluation rather than random variation. This finding shows that, although the raters disagree on multiple criteria, the composite score can compensate for differences in how the human raters understand, evaluate and apply the criteria.

Human raters tend to disagree on most of the economics criteria, which may suggest that they have different expectations regarding the students’ statements. The SUM_SCORE , which is composed of the other criteria and can therefore partially compensate for differences and disagreements, shows comparatively high agreement across all four economics tasks. While still below 0.80 in all cases, the agreement is often higher than that of the other individual criteria. This implies that the human raters do not fully agree on how to assess students’ responses on the criteria level, but they do agree more on a holistic assessment and can produce reliable overall performance ratings. Additionally, as indicated by Cronbach’s alpha, they mostly rank the criteria similarly. The SUM_SCORE may therefore be more suitable for assigning a holistic performance level to student responses. Future work should study the direct generation of such a score by LLMs in comparison with a calculated SUM_SCORE .

Compared with these results, the inter-annotator agreements for the medical tasks criteria show similar tendencies (see Table 8 ). We observe comparable SUM_SCORE agreement values, which demonstrates the practicality of this holistic criterion across domains. Similarly to the IAA patterns across the economics criteria, we do not see any systematic behaviour in agreement across the three groups of medical criteria. The agreement for comparable criteria varies per task; overall, this pattern is similar across the two domains.

Together, the inter-annotator agreements across the eight tasks highlight the challenge of manual annotation when assessing student writing: in both domains, ratings range from low to good, depending on the task and criterion, with a larger share of criteria reaching at least tentatively acceptable agreement in medicine (32 of 72) than in economics (5 of 31). We use these results to set the baseline for model-based annotation, analysing to what extent LLMs can replace individual human raters while maintaining the level of agreement among the raters (RQ1). In other words, given these divergent inter-annotator agreements, how effectively can LLMs replace one or two human raters in this rating task? Maintaining the agreement does not necessarily mean that the assessment is correct: if the LLM provided a more accurate rating than human raters, agreement would decrease. However, our aim is to verify whether an LLM can replace a rater in this context based on these human results and similar instructions and information.

### 5 Discussion

Unlike other studies that conclude that LLMs are currently unable to replace human annotators (e.g., [ 120 , 24 , 157 ] ), our results suggest a more promising outlook in line with studies reporting LLM performance matching conventional models (e.g., [ 149 , 31 ] ). We show that, for most criteria, at least one LLM–prompt configuration can replace an individual human annotator in essay-grading tasks in educational economics and medicine without lowering the agreement among the raters ( RQ1 ). We demonstrate this for eight tasks across these two domains and per-task rubrics comprising 103 criteria in total ( RQ2 ). We confirm our finding using two metrics: (1) inter-annotator agreement based on Krippendorff’s alpha, which shows replaceability based on agreement patterns; and (2) the Alternative Annotator Test , which provides statistical significance and additionally accounts for the cost of the human annotation. Our evaluation shows that the agreement is preserved or increased for most criteria when replacing one or two human raters with LLMs, and that multiple LLMs pass the alt-test, meaning they can replace at least 50% of the human raters.

These are best-case results: the best configuration differs by task and criterion, and most tested configurations lower the agreement. Since we compare the LLM ratings with those of the human raters and do not evaluate their absolute quality, a change in agreement does not show whether the LLM-based ratings are better or worse. An increase may also result from replacing the only rater who rated a response correctly, while a decrease may result from LLM ratings that are more accurate than those of the human raters. The same applies to the alt-test, which also uses the human raters as the reference. Instead, we address the question that arises in practice, where gold-standard ratings are rarely available and human raters partly disagree: whether an LLM can take the place of one of these human raters without lowering their agreement.

We rely on real-world data from the original assessment, manually annotated by multiple trained human raters. Their inter-annotator agreement shows that constructing a single gold label can be challenging (as shown in Table 7 ). We therefore treat each rater’s score as an equally plausible reference and compare our LLM ratings with those of all human raters: separately in the case of annotator agreement and combined using the alt-test. In future work, this rating indeterminacy could additionally be evaluated more explicitly, e.g., following the framework presented by Guerdan et al. [55] . Although our aim is not to imitate individual raters, the replaced rater matters: replacing a rater whose ratings deviate from the others can increase agreement ( Figure 4 ), and the SHAP analysis identifies the replaced rater as relevant for whether a non-negative change is achieved ( Figure 13 ).

While the alt-test shows that LLM–prompt configurations can replace a human rater for all criteria in Nudging and nearly all criteria overall, including SUM_SCORE , the IAA results show that the best agreement is achieved by different LLM–prompt combinations. Using an ensemble of LLMs therefore maximises their effectiveness. However, our experiments with criterion-focused prompts for task Nudging , in which the LLM is queried for only one criterion rather than all criteria, show no clear improvement. This suggests that the LLMs benefit from the explicit inclusion of multiple criteria in their rating process and the interdependencies among criteria in the overall student rating. Querying one criterion after another while keeping the message history, rather than asking for ratings of all criteria at once, has been shown to be effective for the medical tasks, where the number of criteria is much higher than in economics. This directly relates to the finding that model size and the number of tokens influence the outcome, as shown in the SHAP analysis RQ3 . Using a larger model appears to be more important than prompt design. However, our results also show that, for the majority of tested configurations, the agreement change when replacing a human rater is negative. Figure 15 shows the prompt size, measured as the number of input and output tokens, compared with the change in inter-annotator agreement. Note that the number of tokens can be considered only an estimate, as it comprises the prompt query, the student response text, additional features, and the LLM outputs. Although there is a general trend indicating that agreement alphas increase with a larger number of tokens, the results also show that, for all tasks, the best IAAs are not achieved with the largest prompt. Using an LLM to grade student answers reduces costs and enables applications such as real-time feedback, where prediction speed matters. This depends directly on the number and size of the prompts.

The ability to use local LLMs is relevant because of AI regulations, data-protection rules, and the need for reproducibility. Additionally, the use of proprietary LLMs can be complex or forbidden (cf. [ 67 , 28 ] ) and expensive, especially for larger reasoning models (cf. [ 74 , 43 ] ). Therefore, given the data-protection reasons regarding the student statements, our evaluation is restricted to locally hosted, open-weight LLMs. This conclusion is supported by our results on model parameter size and by the best-performing models based on the alt-test. These models include a mixture of sizes and families, albeit with a focus on the Llama 3.3 70b LLM, which was also used in the most experiments, suggesting that smaller models and shorter prompts can still deliver sufficient performance. However, larger models tend to perform better and show greater influence than prompt design. Our results demonstrate the potential of LLM-based AES, particularly for open-weight LLMs, which have been shown to perform worse than closed LLMs in previous studies (e.g., [ 98 , 125 ] ). We show that open state-of-the-art models can reach agreement comparable to that among our human annotators. To this end, while proprietary LLMs might still outperform open models, the quality of the latter has increased in the recent years and now enables their use in this scenario. The same applies to the need for fine-tuning LLMs and to the development and training of classical approaches. Although the research by Chamieh et al. [24] , Kortemeyer [77] , Latif and Zhai [81] mentions improvements achieved through fine-tuning, our study suggests that the general knowledge of current LLMs already leads to agreement comparable to those of human annotators. Not having to fine-tune or further train a LLM makes it more feasible for non-experts to use and develop such a rubric-based LLM-AES system. This improves the already high level of usability enabled by the ability to control the LLM using natural language. The results still show that, in the best case, a human rater can be replaced without lowering the agreement for about 88% of the criteria. This finding further supports the use of self-hosted open-weight models, reducing resource utilisation, reliance on proprietary LLM providers, and issues related to privacy concerns.

Our results confirm that, while fine-tuning is not necessarily required, the agreement is sensitive to the prompt, as also reported by, e.g., Mansour et al. [98] , Lee et al. [83] . However, we also observe that the SIMPLE prompts perform on a par with the more advanced ones when using the Qwen 2.5 and Nemotron LLMs. The rubric definition in these prompts is kept very concise. Research shows that LLMs perform worse when irrelevant information is included in the prompt [ 152 ] , and OpenAI’s recommendation for prompting state-of-the-art reasoning models is to use “straightforward prompts” [ 107 ] . By comparison, the better-performing prompts for the Llama model are larger prompts, containing e.g., extracted textual features or training transcripts. Considering the distribution of the top-performing models and the results of the SHAP analysis, our results indicate advantages of more complex prompts regarding most of the criteria covered in our study, similar to the findings of Huang and Wilson [65] .

We use a direct approach in LLM-based AES, where we ask the LLM for a concrete value and interpret its answer as a rating without further processing, except simple parsing or transformation. Using the output of an LLM as input for another system – e.g., as feature generator for a classifier or another LLM – rather than relying exclusively on the LLM’s output for the rating, may improve performance. This also removes the need for model fine-tuning but instead requires the construction of subsequent tools. Additionally, combining the outputs of multiple LLMs can balance different model biases and compensate for errors. See, e.g., Shibata and Miyamura [122] , Sahoo et al. [117] , Baumartz et al. [11] , who evaluate such a classifier-based method in the domain of medical education for assigning holistic ratings. This should also be evaluated in the field of economics and for more fine-grained rubrics in future work.

To assess the reproducibility of LLM-based ratings, we examined how consistent the LLMs are in their ratings ( subsection 4.7 ). Since a temperature of 0.8 permits stochastic generation, this also indicates whether the selected model configuration results in unstable ratings. The high average consistency suggests stability in the LLMs’ application of the rubric, which can facilitate the evaluation of known biases in LLMs and fairness in AI use (cf. [ 72 , 26 , 143 ] ).

For holistic essay scoring, earlier work by Tate et al. [128] found no statistically difference between human and ChatGPT scores. More recent research by Emirtekin and Özarslan [45] , hovever, shows greater agreement for LLM-generated ratings on criteria that are based more on factual and definitional aspects. They mention, that a holistic score, such as the SUM_SCORE in our study, is harder for an LLM to rate. By contrast, for a human rater, this is an easier task than assessing analytical criteria. The results of our study indicate that this is the case. We report non-negative IAA changes and mostly high agreement values across all eight tasks for the best human-replacing LLM. However, the SUM_SCORE is not predicted directly by the LLM using a prompting approach, but is calculated from the values of the other criteria. This is consistent with how the SUM_SCORE is generated from the human ratings – human raters did not assign a holistic score directly. This means that the quality of all other criteria factors into the outcome of the composite SUM_SCORE . This shows that the performance differences can be smoothed out by the SUM_SCORE for both human and LLM-generated ratings. Direct prediction should be further explored in future work, and, ideally, also compared with direct assignment by human raters.

In total, we see that the ratings generated by the LLMs do not lower the group agreement when one human rater is replaced by one LLM for 91 of the 103 criteria. This trend continues when we replace two humans with LLMs: we observe a tendency toward higher agreement among LLMs than among human raters, a finding that was also reported by Frohn et al. [48] .

Unlike the results presented by Deng et al. [38] , we do not observe a decrease in agreement for the non-Boolean criteria that we studied. Across all categories, automatic LLM-based grading is able to produce ratings that achieve a comparable level of agreement with the human annotators for all criterion categories, i.e., regarding formal , structural and domain-specific criteria in economics and medical education.

Our results also show that human review is still required before employing LLM-based AES. While simple prompts can perform adequately, the variation in IAA scores and the influence of the tasks itself suggests a validation is necessary, especially when introducing new tasks, questions, or domains into the assessments. In practice, LLM-based scoring should therefore be validated against human ratings per task and criterion before use, since the best configuration varies. As starting point, our results suggest larger models and prompts that include training material for economics and criterion-wise chaining for medicine.


### 6 Limitations

This study evaluates whether LLM-based systems can replace human raters in a group without reducing agreement. We do not assess or analyse whether the LLMs produce objectively correct ratings. We provide empirical evidence that current state-of-the-art open-weight LLMs can replace individual human annotators in assessing student responses in economics and medical education without lowering the agreement: our results suggest preserved or increasing agreement among the top performers when replacing one or two human raters with LLM-based systems. However, the majority of tested LLM–prompt configurations lead to decreasing agreement. This indicates that a full replacement of all human raters is not possible and that human validation is still required.

The central limitation concerns the quality of the human ratings we use for reference in our evaluation. This is reflected by the level of disagreement measured by Krippendorff’s alpha, especially in economics, where only 5 of 31 criteria reach at least tentatively acceptable agreement, and could indicate an issue with the rubric we study. The student statements were evaluated by two independent rater groups, which received task-specific instructions and training, including trial ratings. However, no consolidated gold rating is available for use in our study. Our results must therefore be interpreted as empirical evidence in the context of the observed human ratings and cannot make any assumption on the objective correctness of the LLM-generated ratings. In particular, for criteria with low human agreement, not lowering the agreement is a weaker requirement than for criteria on which the human raters agree, and a change in agreement does not indicate whether the LLMs are better or worse. We provide complementary measures using Cronbach’s alpha and ICC(3,k) to offer another perspective on the human rating agreement. Additionally, the Alternative Annotator Test further provides insights into the replaceability in relation to the human rater group as a whole.

Further limitations concern the number of student responses we analyse, as well as our restriction to a subset of tasks within each of the two disciplines. In addition, the tasks, their length requirements, and the rubrics were designed separately for each domain as part of the original assessment, differences between the domains, e.g., in human agreement, may partly reflect these design differences and should be interpreted with caution.

We use only open-weight and locally hostable LLMs for data-protection reasons. Consequently, we do not compare our results with proprietary models. While this may seem limiting at first, the literature reviewed in section 2 suggests that proprietary models could achieve even better scores because of their larger training corpora and parameter spaces [ 125 ] . Our results show a small to moderate correlation between model parameter size and observed IAA-based performance.

Finally, we only consider criteria that do not require the LLM to use other content than the students’ responses. Using agentic or tool-based LLM workflows for on-demand access to additional data, such as online resources, or using a RAG-based approach, should be considered in future work.


### 7 Conclusion

We have shown the feasibility of using open-weight LLM-based AES for student assessments in the fields of educational economics and medicine. Our results, which are based on real-world data from four tasks per domain, show that, for most criteria, at least one LLM–prompt configuration can replace an individual human rater without the need for fine-tuning while maintaining comparable inter-annotator agreements, as measured by Krippendorff’s alpha. For 88%, i.e., 91 of the 103 criteria in our rubric, the agreement is not lowered when replacing one human rater, with similar results when replacing two. At least one configuration also passes the Alternative Annotator Test with statistical significance, confirming the potential to replace at least 50% of human raters across 87% of the criteria. These are best-case results: most tested configurations lower the agreement. We show that the best-performing prompts and LLMs vary by criterion, however, there is no clear indication that criteria-specific prompts lead to improvements. As no gold standard for the ratings is available, our results show agreement with the observed human raters rather than the absolute correctness of the LLM ratings. Performance is associated more with model and prompt size than with prompt design. There is no visible difference in LLM performance across the three criteria categories; the models are able to assign ratings similar to those of the human raters for formal , structural , and domain-knowledge criteria across both domains. While LLMs avoid the individual effects of the human raters they replace, they introduce their own biases into the ratings. However, using automated methods can lead to more stable assessments and enable faster processes, including real-time feedback to students while they are writing. Human validation is still needed to ground LLM-based AES systems during development. Together, these findings suggest the use of LLM ensembles to benefit from higher top performances per criterion and to mitigate model and prompt biases.
