## Latent Lattice Language Models

Language models typically increase per-token computation through deeper or looped layers and latent reasoning steps, while richer residual connections improve information flow across layers. We instead increase computational capacity : the number of active computational states a model maintains. We introduce the Lattice of Latents , a set of per-layer horizontal latent streams that run recurrently along time, alongside the vertical residual stream that flows through depth. Like hyper-connections, mHC, and attention or value residuals, the Lattice enhances the residual stream, but rather than adding or rerouting residual pathways through depth, it provides companion computational states that persist across tokens. Unlike hybrid Transformer–SSM architectures, whose recurrent states primarily serve as internal memory for sequence mixing, these latents directly condition the vertical stream’s attention, KV-cache writes, MLP computation, and decoding. On Transformers trained on 20B FineWeb-Edu tokens, a 406M-parameter Lattice model lowers validation perplexity from $15.41$ to $14.40$ over a parameter-matched Transformer. These gains extend across architectural variants. On Gated DeltaNet, Mamba-2, Triadic GDN, and GDN–Mamba-2 hybrids, it improves perplexity at every tested native state size, with Lattice-GDN and Lattice-Triadic-GDN variants outperforming pure baselines with $4\times$ larger native states. ### 1 Introduction

Language models accept raw text as input, transform it through internal representations, and predict subsequent text. Like most learned systems, this process relies on some form of latent computation. But what, precisely, is the latent computation in a large language model?

In a standard autoregressive LLM, each token is processed by a stack of layers along a single residual stream, and this one latent does several jobs at once. At every layer, it reads from memory through attention queries, writes to memory through keys and values, updates itself through the MLP, and, at the top, is decoded into the next token. The memory it writes to, the KV cache, is an append-only record that stays fixed once written; the residual stream carries all of the active computation. Recent work increases the computation performed on this stream: deeper or looped layers apply more updates per token Dehghani et al. (2019) ; Geiping et al. (2025) ; Zhu et al. (2026) , continuous latent reasoning continues latent processing between tokens Hao et al. (2024) ; Wu et al. (2026) ; Zeng et al. (2026) ; Shen et al. (2025) , and richer residual connections, such as hyper-connections, mHC, and attention or value residuals, improve how the stream carries information across depth Zhu et al. (2025) ; Xie et al. (2026) ; Team et al. (2026) ; Zhou et al. (2025) .

These approaches give the residual stream more to compute. We focus instead on increasing computational capacity : the number of active computational states a model maintains. We propose a lattice of computational latents : alongside the vertical latent $h_{t}^{\ell}$ that flows through depth (blue arrows in Fig. 1 ), each Lattice layer maintains its own horizontal latent $r_{t}^{\ell}$ (orange arrows), updated recurrently across timesteps. We call this architecture the Lattice of Latents , and a language model built on it the Latent Lattice Language Model (LLLM) . Like residual-stream enhancements ( Zhu et al., 2025 ; Xie et al., 2026 ; Team et al., 2026 ) , the Lattice augments the vertical stream, but rather than widening or rerouting it through depth, it adds companion computational states that persist across tokens. The horizontal latents add fixed-size recurrent state without additional token positions or KV-cache entries. So, how does this horizontal flow of information support the vertical latent?

At each step of computation – layer $\ell$ , timestep $t$ – two streams of active information are available: the vertical latent $h_{t}^{\ell-1}$ from the layer below, and the horizontal latent $r_{t}^{\ell}$ , formed by updating the same layer’s previous state $r_{t-1}^{\ell}$ . In our Transformer instantiation, both latents jointly drive the layer’s attention and MLP computations. The horizontal latent conditions attention queries and KV-cache writes, modulates the MLP, and, at the topmost Lattice layer, also contributes to output decoding alongside the final vertical latent. We design the horizontal update to be (a) expressive enough to serve as a useful companion to the residual stream, and (b) compatible with token-parallel, teacher-forced training.

Towards these goals, we start with an affine Mamba-style ( Gu and Dao, 2024 ) update in which $r_{t}^{\ell}$ is updated from $r_{t-1}^{\ell}$ using the incoming vertical latent $h_{t}^{\ell-1}$ . Conditional on the layer-input sequence, this affine recurrence admits an exact associative scan across token positions. Although the update resembles those in Mamba and hybrid Transformer–SSM models, its role differs . There, the recurrent state acts as a sequence mixer: an internal memory that replaces attention or operates alongside it. Here, attention (or the backbone’s native memory) remains the sequence mixer, and the horizontal latent acts as a companion to the residual stream , conditioning how the vertical stream reads and writes that memory and how its MLP processes it.

In this base update, $r_{t-1}^{\ell}\to r_{t}^{\ell}$ , the horizontal latent receives new information only through the incoming vertical latent (the residual stream) $h_{t}^{\ell-1}$ . We therefore add three enhancements that enrich the horizontal pathway: input injection feeds the token embedding directly into every layer’s update, so each horizontal latent sees the current token without relying on the vertical stream to preserve it; a nonlinear depth-wise readout stream lets each layer’s readout of its horizontal latent depend on those of the layers below, adding nonlinear cross-layer computation on top of the per-layer affine recurrences; and top-to-bottom feedback feeds the top layer’s horizontal latent into the bottom layer’s update at the next timestep, so the most processed state of the previous token informs the earliest computation on the current one. The first two preserve the parallel scan, keeping our main models single-pass at approximately matched compute. The top-to-bottom feedback does not preserve token parallelism, so we approximate it with a few token-parallel Jacobi passes ( Wu et al., 2026 ; Zeng et al., 2026 ) , at about $2.6\times$ the FLOPs for three passes during training; at inference, the same model can instead be rolled out sequentially at the base FLOPs.

Across pretraining budgets from 10B to 30B FineWeb-Edu tokens, the Lattice of Latents improves language-modeling quality at approximately matched parameters and compute (Tab. 1 , Tab. 3 , Fig. 7 ). At 20B tokens, a 406M-parameter LLLM with input injection and the depth-wise readout stream lowers FineWeb-Edu perplexity from $15.41$ to $14.40$ , matching a $1.5\times$ larger, 40-layer Transformer. A separately trained LLLM with the nonlinear top-to-bottom refiner, run with three Jacobi sweeps (at higher FLOPs), lowers perplexity further to $14.03$ (Tab. 1 ). These gains extend to out-of-distribution perplexity, downstream tasks, and finetuned mathematical reasoning (GSM8K). As a memory-conditioning residual-stream enhancement, the Lattice alone lowers perplexity more than hyper-connections, mHC, or value residuals, and it composes with them (Sec. 4.2 ): combined with hyper-connections and input residuals, it reaches $13.61$ , versus $14.22$ without the Lattice (Fig. 3 ).

The Lattice is not specific to softmax attention. We also instantiate it on Gated DeltaNet, Mamba-2, Triadic GDN, and GDN–Mamba-2 hybrids, where the horizontal latents condition the backbones’ native memory-read, write, and update operations. After 10B-token pretraining, at approximately matched parameter counts, the Lattice improves perplexity at every native state size we test; Lattice GDN and Lattice Triadic GDN each outperform pure baselines holding $4\times$ as many native-state entries. Downstream gains also hold across GDN, Mamba-2, and GDN–Mamba-2 hybrids: adding the Lattice improves the nine-task average in nearly every comparison, spanning five native-state budgets and both zero- and five-shot settings. Probing GDN’s recurrent memory reveals a structural correlate of these gains: at matched native-state sizes, Lattice models exhibit more concentrated state spectra and greater query alignment with the leading memory direction. These observations are consistent with more effective use of native memory.

Overall, comparisons at similar parameter and compute budgets show that allocating capacity to both vertical and horizontal latents outperforms spending it on residual-stream width or network depth alone in the tested configurations. Together, these results identify persistent per-layer state as a complementary axis for scaling language-model computation.


### 2 Related Work

Recent work increases how much computation a language model applies to each token. Along depth , models add layers or repeatedly apply a shared stack ( Dehghani et al., 2019 ; Geiping et al., 2025 ; Zhu et al., 2026 ) ; along time , continuous latent reasoning feeds the final hidden state back as the next input, running additional latent steps before emitting a token ( Hao et al., 2024 ; Shen et al., 2025 ; Zeng et al., 2026 ) , with Jacobi-style refinement recovering token-parallel training ( Zeng et al., 2026 ; Wu et al., 2026 ) . Both directions apply more updates to one active stream, and often grow persistent memory with it: extra layers and loop-specific caches, or latent thoughts held as extra positions, add KV-cache entries. The Lattice instead increases computational capacity by maintaining one active latent per Lattice layer that persists across tokens, without adding per-token KV-cache entries.

A complementary line of work changes how the residual stream carries information through depth. Hyper-connections expand it into parallel streams with learned mixing ( Zhu et al., 2025 ) , which mHC constrains to be doubly stochastic ( Xie et al., 2026 ) ; attention residuals replace the fixed residual sum with attention over earlier layers’ outputs ( Team et al., 2026 ) ; value residuals reuse first-layer values in later layers ( Zhou et al., 2025 ) ; and input residuals re-inject the token embedding before every layer. These methods widen or reroute per-token computation through depth, but none gives a layer its own state that persists from one token to the next. The Lattice adds such states and composes with these enhancements: in our experiments, adding it lowers perplexity on top of standard, input-residual, and hyper-connected residual streams (Sec. 3.3 ).

Our affine horizontal update shares the scan-parallel form of linear recurrent models such as Mamba ( Gu and Dao, 2024 ; Dao and Gu, 2024 ) , Gated DeltaNet ( Yang et al., 2025 ) , and DeltaProduct ( Siems et al., 2025 ) , whose transitions remain linear or affine in the previous state. In these models, and in hybrids that replace attention with SSM layers at selected depths ( Lieber et al., 2024 ) or run them alongside attention heads ( Dong et al., 2024 ) , the recurrent state is a sequence mixer: it substitutes for or supplements attention as the means of reading the past. In the Lattice, the horizontal latent is instead a companion to the residual stream: in Transformers, softmax attention is retained, and the latent conditions attention, its KV-cache writes, and the MLP. Because this role is distinct from sequence mixing, the Lattice also complements recurrent backbones: on GDN, Mamba-2, Triadic GDN, and GDN–Mamba-2 hybrids, it lowers perplexity at approximately matched parameter counts (Sec. 3.4 ).

Other work brings recurrence into Transformers without replacing attention. Mozer et al. (2026a) argue that feedforward Transformers push evolving state into ever-deeper layers, exhausting depth, and call for recurrence. NextLat ( Teoh et al., 2025 ) keeps the architecture unchanged and adds an auxiliary objective that trains a latent dynamics model to predict the next hidden state, encouraging recurrent-like belief states; the Lattice instead adds explicit recurrent states to the architecture. Several concurrent works add recurrent or multi-stream pathways, typically feeding a deeper representation from the previous token back into the current token’s computation ( Wang et al., 2026 ; Huang et al., 2026 ; Cai et al., 2026 ; Mozer et al., 2026b ) , or separating state preparation from next-token prediction into distinct streams ( Monea et al., 2026 ) . Among these, T 2 MLR ( Cai et al., 2026 ) is closest to our Jacobi refiner, though its recurrent object is a cached vertical representation rather than a lattice of layer-wise horizontal states. These works largely add a single feedback pathway or separate two roles, whereas the Lattice maintains a persistent latent at every Lattice layer; Appendix A.1 gives detailed description of concurrent works.

We propose the Lattice of Latents , an augmentation that distributes computation across a lattice of latents rather than relying only on the usual single residual-stream latent per token. The latent computation spans two axes. Along the vertical axis (network depth), the per-token latent is initialized from the current token embedding and flows bottom-to-top through the backbone’s layers, where it is updated by attention or recurrent state computations followed by a position-wise MLP, before being decoded into the next-token distribution. Along the horizontal axis (time), the Lattice of Latents adds one persistent latent vector at each layer of an intermediate Lattice block, evolving across timesteps rather than through depth. Each horizontal latent remains at its own layer and abstraction level, assisting the vertical stream by conditioning that layer’s memory reads and updates. In our Transformer instantiation, we additionally study MLP modulation. The Lattice thereby increases computational capacity –– the number of active computational states a model maintains by adding companion states to the residual stream, which condition the computation of the residual stream, rather than simply widening the residual stream or adding residual pathways through depth.

We integrate the Lattice of Latents into standard Transformers, Transformers with residual-stream enhancements (hyper-connections, mHC, value residuals, and input residuals), and recurrent backbones: Gated DeltaNet (GDN), Mamba-2, Triadic GDN, and GDN–Mamba-2 hybrids. We begin with background on the per-token computational graph of the vertical latent (Sec. 3.1 ), then formulate the Lattice of Latents (Sec. 3.2 ), its composition with residual-stream enhancements (Sec. 3.3 ), and its extension to recurrent backbones (Sec. 3.4 ).

#### 3.1 Background: Computation with a Single Active Latent

Consider an autoregressive Transformer of depth $N$ and width $d_{\mathrm{model}}$ , processing a token stream $x_{1},x_{2},\dots$ . At timestep $t$ , the model maps the input token $x_{t}$ to a distribution over the next token $x_{t+1}$ through a single residual-stream latent $h_{t}^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{model}}}$ , initialized from the token embedding $h_{t}^{0}$ and refined across the $N$ layers. Layer $\ell\in\{1,\dots,N\}$ updates this latent with a pre-norm attention block followed by an MLP block:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle q_{t}^{\ell},\,k_{t}^{\ell},\,v_{t}^{\ell}$ $\displaystyle=W_{q}^{\ell}\hat{h},\;W_{k}^{\ell}\hat{h},\;W_{v}^{\ell}\hat{h}$ $\displaystyle\bar{h}_{t}^{\ell}$ $\displaystyle=h_{t}^{\ell-1}+W_{o}^{\ell}\,\mathrm{Attn}\!\big(q_{t}^{\ell},\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\ell}\big)$ $\displaystyle h_{t}^{\ell}$ $\displaystyle=\bar{h}_{t}^{\ell}+\mathrm{MLP}^{\ell}\!\big(\mathrm{Norm}(\bar{h}_{t}^{\ell})\big),$

where $\hat{h}=\mathrm{Norm}(h_{t}^{\ell-1})$ , $\mathrm{Norm}$ is RMSNorm and $\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\ell}=\{(k_{s}^{\ell},v_{s}^{\ell})\}_{s\leq t}$ is the layer- $\ell$ KV cache to which each token appends its keys and values. The final latent is decoded as $p(x_{t+1}\mid x_{\leq t})=\mathrm{softmax}\big(W_{U}\,\mathrm{Norm}(h_{t}^{N})\big)$ . Thus one latent per token does all the active computation (reading memory, writing to it, and producing the output), while the KV cache passively stores its past projections.

Continuous latent reasoning ( Hao et al., 2024 ; Shen et al., 2025 ; Zeng et al., 2026 ) extends this pathway across timesteps by feeding the previous step’s final-layer latent back as the next input, $h_{t}^{0}=h_{t-1}^{N}$ , bypassing decoding and re-embedding. The feedback can thus carry more than the $\log_{2}V$ bits of a discrete token, and the vertical latent becomes a running state across time. A standard Transformer instead re-initializes its vertical latent from the token embedding at every timestep.


#### 3.2 The Lattice of Latents

How can we increase the computational capacity of this single active latent? One could widen it (larger $d_{\mathrm{model}}$ ), replicate it into parallel depth-wise streams as in hyper-connections, or add latents along a different axis. We take the third route: each Lattice layer $\ell$ maintains its own horizontal latent $r_{t}^{\ell}$ that runs across timesteps, initialized from a learned state and updated from its previous value $r_{t-1}^{\ell}$ and the incoming vertical latent $h_{t}^{\ell-1}$ . The vertical latent advances through depth at each timestep and the horizontal latents advance through time at each layer; together they form a lattice. We compare this against widening and show that it composes with residual-stream enhancements. Below we describe how the horizontal latents act (Sec. 3.2.1 ) and how they are updated (Sec. 3.2.2 ).


##### 3.2.1 How the horizontal latents act

Let $r_{t}^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{r}}$ be the horizontal latent at layer $\ell$ and timestep $t$ , with width $d_{r}$ chosen independently of $d_{\mathrm{model}}$ . A standard Transformer layer computes from the vertical latent $h_{t}^{\ell-1}$ and its KV cache. In the Lattice, layer $\ell$ has two latent inputs: the vertical latent $h_{t}^{\ell-1}$ from the layer below, and the horizontal latent $r_{t}^{\ell}$ , updated from the same layer’s previous state $r_{t-1}^{\ell}$ . Together they condition the layer’s attention and MLP, and thus both the residual update that produces $h_{t}^{\ell}$ and the keys and values written to memory. Optionally, the topmost Lattice layer’s horizontal latent also contributes to decoding. We detail each contribution below (Fig. 4 ); all our Lattice Transformers modulate both attention and the MLP. All contributions read the normalized latent $\hat{r}_{t}^{\ell}=\mathrm{Norm}(r_{t}^{\ell})$ .

Conditioning Attention via FiLM. The horizontal latent $\hat{r}_{t}^{\ell}$ modulates the query, key, and value projections of $\hat{h}=\mathrm{Norm}(h_{t}^{\ell-1})$ via Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) ( Perez et al., 2017 ) :

The multiplicative gain $1+\gamma\in(0,2)$ is bounded, while $\beta$ is an additive shift; each of $q,k,v$ has its own layer-specific, zero-initialized $W^{\gamma}$ and $W^{\beta}$ , so the modulation starts as the identity. ##### 3.2.2 How the horizontal latents are updated

We divide the backbone’s layers into an early block (E), an intermediate Lattice block (L), and a late block (D); horizontal latents exist only in L. For $\ell\in L$ , the update takes the form:

[Tableau] $$r_{t}^{\ell}=U\big(r_{t-1}^{\ell};\;h_{t}^{\ell-1},\;h_{t}^{0},\;r_{t-1}^{\mathrm{top}}\big)$$

The base update uses the layer’s own history $r_{t-1}^{\ell}$ and the incoming vertical latent $h_{t}^{\ell-1}$ . Input injection adds the token embedding $h_{t}^{0}$ , and the optional Jacobi refiner adds feedback $r_{t-1}^{\mathrm{top}}$ from the top Lattice layer one timestep back, injected only at the bottom Lattice layer and taken from the previous sweep. The depth-wise readout stream ( vstream or vs in model labels), which passes information upward from lower horizontal latents within a timestep, enriches each layer’s readout rather than entering $U$ . We describe each mechanism below (Fig. 5 ).

> Figure 5: Horizontal latent update. On top of the Mamba-style affine update, we further introduce input injection, vstream (not shown) and an optional cross-timestep nonlinear flow. See webpage for animations.

Affine update from $r_{t-1}^{\ell}$ to $r_{t}^{\ell}$ . To keep training token-parallel and teacher-forced, we need a recurrence that admits an exact associative scan across timesteps rather than a token-by-token loop. An affine transition with input-dependent coefficients has this property. Following linear attention and Mamba-style state-space models, we use one whose coefficients depend only on the current layer input, never directly on the previous horizontal state. The affine update reads a normalized copy of the vertical latent $n_{t}^{\ell}=\mathrm{Norm}_{\mathrm{in}}(h_{t}^{\ell-1})$ , and is defined as:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\Delta_{t}^{\ell}$ $\displaystyle=\mathrm{softplus}\big(W_{\Delta}^{\ell}n_{t}^{\ell}+b_{\Delta}^{\ell}\big)$ $\displaystyle g_{t}^{\ell}$ $\displaystyle=\exp\big(-\exp(a^{\ell})\odot\Delta_{t}^{\ell}\big)$ $\displaystyle c_{t}^{\ell}$ $\displaystyle=\mathrm{SiLU}\big(W_{r}^{\ell}n_{t}^{\ell}+b_{r}^{\ell}\big)$ $\displaystyle b_{t}^{\ell}$ $\displaystyle=(1-g_{t}^{\ell})\odot c_{t}^{\ell}$ $\displaystyle r_{t}^{\ell}$ $\displaystyle=g_{t}^{\ell}\odot r_{t-1}^{\ell}+b_{t}^{\ell}.$

Here $r_{0}^{\ell}$ is a learned initial state before the first token $x_{1}$ , and $a^{\ell}$ and $b_{\Delta}^{\ell}$ are learned per-channel log-decay rates and time-step biases. Because $g_{t}^{\ell}$ and $b_{t}^{\ell}$ depend on $n_{t}^{\ell}$ alone, the transition is exactly affine given the layer-input sequence, so the horizontal states at all token positions are computed with a parallel scan during training. Layers are still evaluated in depth order, each scanning the vertical inputs produced by the layer below. The readout used downstream is $\hat{r}_{t}^{\ell}=\mathrm{Norm}(r_{t}^{\ell})$ . Although the update follows a Mamba-style gated affine recurrence, the horizontal latent plays a different role, a companion to the residual stream rather than a sequence mixer, which we revisit for backbones that already maintain recurrent memory (Sec. 3.4 ).

We call this configuration the base update and label the corresponding models $\mathbf{LLLM}(d_{\mathrm{model}},d_{r})$ , with vertical width $d_{\mathrm{model}}$ and horizontal width $d_{r}$ ; additional mechanisms are marked separately in model labels. The base update can integrate history across time, but on its own it applies no learned nonlinear transformation to its previous state. We therefore study three complementary mechanisms: input injection ( inj ) and the depth-wise readout stream ( vs ) add expressivity within a timestep while preserving the parallel scan, whereas the Jacobi refiner adds a nonlinear top-to-bottom feedback pathway across timesteps at the cost of the single-pass guarantee.

Adding the input embedding to the horizontal latent. The base update sees the current input only through $h_{t}^{\ell-1}$ , which by layer $\ell$ may have discarded information about the raw token identity (see the narrow-vertical-latent ablation, Fig. 6 ). Input injection ( inj ) also feeds the normalized token embedding $h_{t}^{0}$ into $\Delta_{t}^{\ell}$ and $c_{t}^{\ell}$ through zero-initialized projections. These coefficients still depend only on current-timestep inputs, so the transition remains exactly affine and the same parallel scan applies (more details in Appendix A.2.2 ).

Enhancing layer-wise nonlinearity of the horizontal latent. At layer $\ell$ , the readout $\hat{r}_{t}^{\ell}$ from the base update and input injection receives information from lower Lattice layers’ horizontal latents only indirectly, through whatever survives in the vertical latent. The depth-wise readout stream ( vstream or vs ) adds a direct path: a purely depth-wise state $s_{t}^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{r}}$ that runs bottom to top across the Lattice, seeded with $s_{t}^{\ell_{0}-1}:=0$ at the first Lattice layer $\ell_{0}$ :

where $[\cdot,\cdot]$ denotes concatenation. The stream carries same-timestep communication across depth, separately from the recurrent update $U$ , which acts across time. Because $s_{t}^{\ell}$ depends only on same-timestep quantities, it adds a nonlinear dependency across depth but none across time, leaving the base recurrence and its parallel scan untouched. replaces $\hat{r}_{t}^{\ell}$ wherever it conditions attention or the MLP (Sec. 3.2.1 ). The state carried across timesteps remains the raw $r_{t}^{\ell}$ , not $\tilde{r}_{t}^{\ell}$ ; the enriched readout influences higher layers’ updates only indirectly, through the vertical stream.

Cross-timestep nonlinear update. Input injection and the depth-wise readout stream use only current-timestep information, so neither feeds a higher layer’s more deeply processed latent back to a lower layer at the next timestep. In the Transformer instantiation, we add this top-to-bottom feedback: the top Lattice layer’s latent at $t-1$ , passed through a zero-initialized SwiGLU, is added to the bottom Lattice layer’s update at $t$ , making the recurrence nonlinear across timesteps. This feedback is straightforward in a token-by-token rollout but breaks single-pass teacher-forced training, so we approximate it with a Jacobi refiner ( Zeng et al., 2026 ; Wu et al., 2026 ) : after an initial pass over the Lattice block, each refinement sweep reruns all Lattice layers in parallel across tokens, taking the feedback from the previous sweep. Unlike Zeng et al. (2026) ; Wu et al. (2026) , which apply Jacobi iteration to the vertical latent ( $h_{t}^{0}=h_{t-1}^{N}$ ), we apply it to the horizontal latents. J0 denotes the single-pass model without this cross-timestep feedback and J3 the initial pass followed by two refinement sweeps ( $\approx 2.6\times$ the FLOPs). At inference, the feedback can instead be computed exactly by sequential rollout; we evaluate both (details in Appendix A.2.3 ).


#### 3.3 Composing with Residual-Stream Enhancements

Because the Lattice conditions each block’s computation rather than the residual pathway itself, it combines with residual-stream enhancements without changing the horizontal update. An input residual re-injects the normalized token embedding before every layer, $h_{t}^{\ell-1}\leftarrow\lambda_{r}^{\ell}h_{t}^{\ell-1}+\lambda_{0}^{\ell}\,\mathrm{Norm}(h_{t}^{0})$ , with learned per-layer scalars; the horizontal update then reads this re-injected layer input as $h_{t}^{\ell-1}$ . This differs from input injection ( inj ), which feeds the embedding into the horizontal update rather than into the residual stream. Hyper-connections ( Zhu et al., 2025 ) expand the residual stream into $n$ parallel streams that a learned router mixes into each block’s input and remixes after its output (mHC ( Xie et al., 2026 ) constrains this mixing to be doubly stochastic). With hyper-connections, each Lattice layer’s horizontal update reads the mixed block input in place of $h_{t}^{\ell-1}$ , FiLM conditions that block’s QKV and MLP computations as in Sec. 3.2.1 , and the router writes the block outputs back to the streams. A single horizontal latent per Lattice layer is shared across the $n$ streams. A value residual ( Zhou et al., 2025 ) mixes each layer’s attention values with those of the first layer; in Lattice layers, the mix is applied after FiLM conditioning and before attention. In our experiments, we compare the Lattice against these residual-stream enhancements and evaluate their combinations.


#### 3.4 Lattice of Latents on Recurrent Backbones

Although the horizontal update uses a Mamba-style recurrence, the Lattice also complements backbones that already maintain recurrent memory. We instantiate it on GDN, Mamba-2, Triadic GDN, and GDN--Mamba-2 hybrids. Unlike a Transformer’s growing KV cache, these backbones keep native memories of fixed size: matrix-valued states per head in GDN and Mamba-2, and a three-dimensional state per head in Triadic GDN, which adds a routing axis. The Lattice leaves these memories intact and adds a separate horizontal latent that conditions how they are used. 1 1 We use “state” and “memory” interchangeably for the backbones’ native recurrent memories, which play a role analogous to the KV cache. The horizontal latent is also recurrent state, but its role is to condition the computations driven by the vertical residual stream.

We keep the base update, with input injection and the depth-wise readout stream as options, and apply FiLM to each backbone’s memory read, write, and update quantities: in GDN, Q, K, V, the retention ( $\alpha$ ) and update-strength ( $\beta$ ) pre-activations, and the output gate; in Mamba-2, the read (C) and write (B) projections, the input stream, the time-step pre-activation, and the output gate. Triadic GDN additionally conditions its routing factors, and GDN–Mamba-2 hybrids follow the settings of each layer’s backbone. All modulation weights are zero-initialized, and modulation is applied before each quantity’s constraining transformation. These comparisons use unmodulated MLPs, the ordinary decoding head, and no Jacobi refiner; further details are in the Appendix.

To separate this from simply enlarging native memory, we compare models with and without the Lattice across native-state budgets at approximately matched parameters. In GDN, for example, a 256-dimensional horizontal latent combined with 65,536 native state entries per layer achieves lower perplexity than a pure GDN with 262,144 entries. We also analyze GDN’s state geometry (spectra, effective and stable rank, and query alignment with the leading memory direction) with and without the Lattice. The distinction is functional: the horizontal latent is itself persistent state, but rather than expanding the backbone’s associative memory, it provides a learned, history-dependent pathway that conditions the vertical stream’s computation. We hypothesize that this added computational capacity helps the model use its native memory more effectively, complementing rather than replicating increases in memory capacity; our experiments are consistent with this hypothesis.

#### 4.2 Composing with Residual-Stream Enhancements

The Lattice adds a horizontal pathway that carries latents across time, whereas recent residual-stream enhancements reshape how information flows vertically, across depth. We test whether the two are complementary by pretraining 400M models on 20B FineWeb-Edu tokens with hyper-connections (HC) ( Zhu et al., 2025 ) , manifold-constrained hyper-connections (mHC) ( Xie et al., 2026 ) , value residuals ( Zhou et al., 2025 ) , and nanochat-style input residuals (x0) ( Karpathy, 2025 ) , alone and in combination, with and without the Lattice (Fig. 3 ); the Lattice models narrow the vertical width from $1024$ to $932$ to remain iso-parameter (details in Appendix Sec. A.4.2 ). On its own, the Lattice lowers FineWeb-Edu perplexity from $15.41$ to $14.47$ , more than HC ( $15.28$ ), mHC ( $14.93$ ), or value residuals ( $14.69$ ), and is second only to x0 ( $14.34$ ). More importantly, its gains add to theirs: the Lattice lowers HC from $15.28$ to $14.21$ ( $-7.0\%$ ) and HC + x0 from $14.22$ to $13.61$ ( $-4.3\%$ ). Lattice + HC + x0 is the best model overall, $11.7\%$ below the plain Transformer and $3.9\%$ below the strongest Transformer composition (x0 + value, $14.16$ ). Thus, horizontal latents are not a substitute for a better residual stream; they are an orthogonal axis that compounds with it.


#### 4.3 Recurrent Backbones

We pretrain GDN, Mamba2, Triadic GDN, and GDN–Mamba2 hybrids without sliding-window attention (no-SWA) on 10B FineWeb-Edu tokens, pairing each pure backbone with a Lattice counterpart of the same native-state geometry and approximately matched parameter count ( $\sim$ 405M). We sweep 16K, 32K, 64K, 128K, and 256K native-state entries per mixer (1K $=1024$ scalar entries), excluding the added horizontal latents and auxiliary convolution states. For GDN, the state size is $Hd_{k}d_{v}$ for $H$ heads with per-head key and value dimensions $d_{k},d_{v}$ ; we use $(H,d_{k},d_{v})=(16,16,64)$ , $(16,32,64)$ , $(16,64,64)$ , $(8,128,128)$ , and $(4,256,256)$ , and vary the other backbones similarly (Appendix). The horizontal latent modulates only the mixer’s memory read, write, and update quantities, not the MLP or decoding. Perplexity results use input-injection + vstream , with the Lattice model’s vertical residual stream ( $d_{\mathrm{model}}$ ) narrowed to offset the added parameters, keeping every comparison iso-parameter. For downstream results, at each state budget and shot setting we select, by nine-task average, the best of three couplings— input-injection + vstream and two vstream -only variants that restore the parameter budget by reducing either $d_{\mathrm{model}}$ or the FFN width (more details in Appendix Sec. A.3 ).

On FineWeb-Edu (Fig. 2 ), the Lattice lowers perplexity at every native-state size: by $1.2$ – $2.6\%$ for GDN, $0.6$ – $2.2\%$ for Mamba2, $1.1$ – $2.1\%$ for Triadic GDN, and $0.3$ – $1.0\%$ for the GDN–Mamba2 hybrid ( $15.85$ vs. $15.92$ at 256K). Lattice models can also outperform pure models with four times as many native-state entries per mixer: 64K Lattice GDN beats 256K pure GDN ( $16.05$ vs. $16.11$ ), and 64K Lattice Triadic GDN beats 256K pure Triadic GDN ( $15.98$ vs. $16.09$ ). These results are consistent with the hypothesis that horizontal latent helps vertical residual stream control memory writes and reads, enabling more effective use of native memory (Sec. 4.4 ).

On the same nine-task average (Figs. 12 and 13 ), the selected Lattice model outperforms its pure counterpart in almost all paired comparisons, spanning three backbones (GDN, Mamba2, and the no-SWA hybrid), five state budgets, and two shot settings. Averaged over state budgets, the gains range from $+0.53$ to $+0.67$ points zero-shot and $+0.76$ to $+0.95$ points five-shot across backbones, peaking at $+1.50$ (Mamba2, 16K, five-shot), and they persist at the largest budget: at 256K, the Lattice adds $+0.86$ and $+1.06$ points to GDN. Lattice models can again outperform pure models with larger native states. With $8\times$ fewer native entries per mixer, 32K Lattice GDN surpasses 256K pure GDN in both settings ( $44.88$ vs. $44.45$ zero-shot), as does the 32K hybrid over the 256K hybrid, and 16K Lattice Mamba2 over 128K pure Mamba2; with $16\times$ fewer, 16K Lattice GDN still exceeds 256K pure GDN zero-shot ( $44.57$ vs. $44.45$ ).

Finally, we ask how the Lattice changes the way GDN uses its native memory. Each GDN head maintains a memory matrix $S\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}\times d_{v}}$ , written with keys and read with queries, where $d_{k}$ and $d_{v}$ are the per-head key and value dimensions. Writing $S=U\Sigma V^{\top}$ with singular values $\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\cdots$ and leading left singular vector $u_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}$ , we measure how concentrated the memory is, using effective and stable ranks. The entropy effective rank $r_{\mathrm{ent}}=\exp\!\big(-\sum_{i}p_{i}\log p_{i}\big)$ , with $p_{i}=\sigma_{i}/\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}$ , is the effective number of directions the spectrum spreads over; the stable rank $r_{\mathrm{stable}}=\|S\|_{F}^{2}/\sigma_{1}^{2}=\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}/\sigma_{1}^{2}$ measures how strongly the leading direction dominates. Both lie in $[1,\min(d_{k},d_{v})]$ ; lower values mean a more concentrated spectrum, but do not by themselves imply less stored information. We also measure the query alignment gain $G_{1}=d_{k}\,(u_{1}^{\top}q)^{2}/\|q\|_{2}^{2}$ : the squared cosine between the normalized query $q\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}$ used for reading and $u_{1}$ , scaled by $d_{k}$ so that an isotropic random query gives $\mathbb{E}[G_{1}]=1$ .

Table 2 shows a consistent pattern at every state budget: the Lattice lowers $r_{\mathrm{ent}}$ by $9.6$ – $18.3\%$ and $r_{\mathrm{stable}}$ by $10.9$ – $15.0\%$ , while raising $G_{1}$ by $33.5$ – $67.4\%$ . The alignment gap widens with state size, from $+1.89$ at 16K to $+5.36$ at 256K, where Lattice queries align with the leading memory direction $13.3\times$ more than chance, versus $8.0\times$ for the pure model. Thus, with Lattice, GDN writes a more concentrated memory and reads more strongly along its dominant direction , consistent with the hypothesis that the horizontal latent helps the vertical stream control memory writes and reads.


### 5 Conclusion

We introduced the Lattice of Latents , which distributes computational capacity across the vertical residual stream and persistent, layer-wise horizontal latents. Rather than replacing sequence memory, these latents act as companion computational states that steer the backbone’s computation. We use an affine horizontal update and introduce enhancements that increase latent expressivity while preserving token-parallel teacher-forced computation within each pass. LLLMs improve language modeling and downstream task averages across Transformer and recurrent backbones. The Lattice also composes with residual-stream enhancements: combined with hyper-connections and input residuals, it outperforms every Transformer variant we test. On recurrent backbones, gains arise at matched native-state sizes and approximately matched parameter counts, with Lattice GDN and Lattice Triadic GDN outperforming pure baselines that use $4\times$ as many native-state entries. Overall, our results suggest that distributing active computational capacity across interacting latents is a promising complement to scaling width, depth, memory, or the residual stream alone.

### Appendix A Appendix


#### A.1 Extended Related Work

Extra computation along depth or time often comes with extra persistent memory. In depth-based methods, each added layer brings its own KV cache, and looped models may keep iteration-specific caches, although recent work shares or compresses state across loops ( Vendrell et al., 2026 ; Neill and Reid, 2026 ) . Among continuous latent reasoning methods, Coconut feeds the final hidden state back as the next input, CODI distills such reasoning from an explicit chain-of-thought teacher, and PonderLM-2 brings it into pretraining ( Hao et al., 2024 ; Shen et al., 2025 ; Zeng et al., 2026 ) ; when latent thoughts are held as extra positions, they also add KV-cache entries. Because consecutive thoughts depend on each other, single-pass teacher forcing no longer applies; PonderLM-2 and PCCoT recover token parallelism with Jacobi-style refinement ( Zeng et al., 2026 ; Wu et al., 2026 ) , which inspires our optional Jacobi refiner (App. A.2.3 ).

We detail three residual-stream enhancements: input residuals, value residuals, and hyper-connections. An input residual re-injects the normalized token embedding before every layer, $h_{t}^{\ell-1}\leftarrow\lambda_{r}^{\ell}h_{t}^{\ell-1}+\lambda_{0}^{\ell}\,\mathrm{Norm}(h_{t}^{0})$ , with learned per-layer scalars $\lambda_{r}^{\ell},\lambda_{0}^{\ell}$ . Value residual learning ( Zhou et al., 2025 ) adds a similar shortcut inside attention: each layer mixes its attention values with those of the first layer, $v_{t}^{\ell}\leftarrow\lambda_{1}^{\ell}v_{t}^{1}+\lambda_{2}^{\ell}v_{t}^{\ell}$ , with learned per-layer scalars, so later layers retain direct access to early token information. Hyper-connections ( Zhu et al., 2025 ) expand the residual stream into $n$ parallel streams: each block reads a learned, input-dependent mixture of the streams, and its output is written back as the streams are remixed; mHC ( Xie et al., 2026 ) constrains this mixing to be doubly stochastic. All three act within a timestep, so they compose directly with the Lattice (Sec. 3.3 ); the horizontal update reads whatever input the residual design produces. With hyper-connections one horizontal latent per layer is shared across the $n$ streams.

Beyond Mamba-2’s gated recurrence, Gated DeltaNet adds delta-rule state updates, and DeltaProduct composes several Householder-style corrections into a more expressive but still linear transition ( Yang et al., 2025 ; Siems et al., 2025 ) . Work on nonlinear recurrences is complementary: ParaRNN parallelizes general nonlinear recurrences with Newton iterations, and M 2 RNNs combine nonlinear updates with matrix-valued states ( Danieli et al., 2025 ; Mishra et al., 2026 ) . We keep the temporal update affine, add expressivity through its inputs and readouts, and obtain nonlinear feedback across time only through optional Jacobi refinement. Advances in parallelizable nonlinear recurrences could further strengthen the horizontal latent as a temporal companion to the residual stream.

Full-Bandwidth Transformer ( Wang et al., 2026 ) feeds the previous token’s top-layer state back alongside the next embedding; Latent Recurrent Transformer ( Huang et al., 2026 ) reuses a selected high-level representation as recurrent memory; and T 2 MLR ( Cai et al., 2026 ) routes a deeper representation from the previous token into an earlier layer through a gated pathway. T 2 MLR is the closest to our Jacobi refiner, but its recurrent object is a cached vertical representation, whereas our Jacobi refiner links the top and bottom of a lattice of horizontal states that each follow their own temporal recurrence. Recirculation ( Mozer et al., 2026b ) also sends deep activations to shallower layers across steps, but is a training-free, inference-time intervention with sequential prefill, whereas the Lattice is trained end to end in a token-parallel fashion. The State-Prediction Separation Transformer ( Monea et al., 2026 ) shares our view that a single representation can be burdened by multiple roles, but separates them within each token’s computation rather than keeping a persistent latent at each layer.

#### A.2 Additional Method Details


##### A.2.1 Decoding Modulation (How the horizontal latents act)

When this optional head is enabled, the topmost Lattice layer’s horizontal latent, with its own normalization, joins the final vertical latent in a shared readout space of dimension $d_{s}$ , and the next token is decoded from their sum:

[Tableau] $$p(x_{t+1}\mid x_{\leq t})=\mathrm{softmax}\Big(W_{U}\big(P_{v}\mathrm{Norm}(h_{t}^{N})+P_{r}\mathrm{Norm}_{\mathrm{head}}(r_{t}^{\mathrm{top}})\big)\Big),$$

where $P_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}\times d_{\mathrm{model}}}$ and $P_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}\times d_{r}}$ are learned projections and $W_{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times d_{s}}$ maps to vocabulary logits. Like every other injection in Sec. 3.2.1 , $P_{r}$ is zero-initialized. Hence at initialization the horizontal latent contributes nothing: attention and the MLP reduce to the standard Transformer layer, and the output is decoded from the vertical latent alone. The horizontal latent gains influence only through training. We use decoding modulation only in experiments with a highly compressed vertical latent ( $d_{\mathrm{model}}{=}256$ ). When enabled, the horizontal decoding head reads the separately normalized raw top-layer state $r_{t}^{\mathrm{top}}$ , not the vstream -enriched readout.


##### A.2.2 Input Injection (How the horizontal latents are updated)

The base update receives new information only through $h_{t}^{\ell-1}$ , so its access to the current input is limited by what the vertical latent preserves, which we probe by ablating a narrower vertical latent (Fig. 6 ). By layer $\ell$ , the vertical latent may also have discarded information about the raw token identity. Input injection ( inj ) therefore feeds the token embedding, with its own normalized read $\hat{h}_{t}^{0}=\mathrm{Norm}_{e}(h_{t}^{0})$ , directly into both pre-activations of the update:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\Delta_{t}^{\ell}$ $\displaystyle=\mathrm{softplus}\big(W_{\Delta}^{\ell}n_{t}^{\ell}+W_{e,\Delta}^{\ell}\hat{h}_{t}^{0}+b_{\Delta}^{\ell}\big)$ $\displaystyle c_{t}^{\ell}$ $\displaystyle=\mathrm{SiLU}\big(W_{r}^{\ell}n_{t}^{\ell}+W_{e,c}^{\ell}\hat{h}_{t}^{0}+b_{r}^{\ell}\big)$

with $W_{e,\Delta}^{\ell}$ and $W_{e,c}^{\ell}$ zero-initialized. The recurrence $r_{t}^{\ell}=g_{t}^{\ell}\odot r_{t-1}^{\ell}+b_{t}^{\ell}$ is otherwise unchanged: $\Delta_{t}^{\ell}$ and $c_{t}^{\ell}$ still depend only on current-timestep inputs ( $n_{t}^{\ell}$ and $\hat{h}_{t}^{0}$ ), never on $r_{t-1}^{\ell}$ , so the transition remains exactly affine and the same parallel scan applies.


##### A.2.3 Jacobi Refiner (How the horizontal latents are updated)

> Figure 9: Ablating top-to-bottom residual-stream flow. Feeding each token’s top-layer state back into the bottom of the network at the next token adds a nonlinear path through the residual stream. Jacobi on vertical applies this feedback to the vertical residual stream, analogous to the concurrent Full-Bandwidth Transformer ( Wang et al., 2026 ) ; Jacobi on Lattice applies it to the horizontal latents; the last bar applies both. We call it “Jacobi” because the feedback is trained and evaluated with three parallel, teacher-forced Jacobi sweeps (J3; App. A.2.3 ), at the cost of extra forward passes. Both forms of feedback help, and they compound: $16.94$ (Transformer) and $15.52$ (single-pass Lattice) fall to $14.92$ with feedback on both. FineWeb-Edu val perplexity, trained on 10B tokens.

The input-injection and vstream pathways use only information available at the current timestep, so the base update keeps an exact parallel scan across time. Neither feeds a higher layer’s horizontal latent back to a lower layer at the next timestep. Such top-to-bottom feedback is straightforward in a token-by-token rollout, but breaks the single bottom-to-top sweep of teacher-forced training, because the top layer’s latent is not yet available when the bottom layer updates. Following Zeng et al. (2026) ; Wu et al. (2026) , we approximate it during training with Jacobi iteration. Unlike these works, which iterate over a recurrence that feeds the final vertical latent back as the next input, we apply it to the horizontal latents.

Let $J\geq 1$ be the total number of sweeps: J0 denotes the base single pass and J3 a base pass followed by two refinement sweeps. Sweep $j=0$ runs the Lattice block bottom to top exactly as in the base update, producing $r_{t}^{\ell,(0)}$ for every layer and timestep. Each subsequent sweep $j=1,\dots,J-1$ takes the top Lattice layer’s latent from the previous sweep, shifted one timestep back, and maps it through a SwiGLU:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle N_{t}^{(j)}$ $\displaystyle=W_{J,\mathrm{down}}\big(\mathrm{SiLU}(W_{J,\mathrm{gate}}\hat{z}_{t}^{(j-1)})\odot(W_{J,\mathrm{up}}\hat{z}_{t}^{(j-1)})\big),\qquad\hat{z}_{t}^{(j-1)}=\mathrm{RMSNorm}\big(r_{t-1}^{\mathrm{top},(j-1)}\big),$

with hidden width $2d_{r}$ , $\hat{z}$ set to zero at the first timestep, and $N_{t}^{(0)}=0$ . Only the bottom Lattice layer $\ell_{0}$ receives the correction, which is added to its candidate content $c_{t}^{\ell_{0},(j)}=\mathrm{SiLU}(W_{r}^{\ell_{0}}n_{t}^{\ell_{0},(j)}+b_{r}^{\ell_{0}})$ , computed from the normalized vertical input $n_{t}^{\ell_{0},(j)}$ (plus the input-injection term when used; full update in Sec. 3.2.2 ):

[Tableau] $\displaystyle b_{t}^{\ell_{0},(j)}=(1-g_{t}^{\ell_{0},(j)})\odot\big(c_{t}^{\ell_{0},(j)}+N_{t}^{(j)}\big),$

which enters the same affine recurrence $r_{t}^{\ell}=g_{t}^{\ell}\odot r_{t-1}^{\ell}+b_{t}^{\ell}$ . Every sweep reruns the whole Lattice block from the same early-block outputs and the same learned initial states, with parameters shared across sweeps and all layer inputs and coefficients recomputed; the final sweep supplies the block output. Because $N_{t}^{(j)}$ depends only on the fully resolved previous sweep, each sweep’s scan remains exactly affine and token-parallel; the cost is $J$ serial sweeps instead of one.

$W_{J,\mathrm{gate}}$ and $W_{J,\mathrm{up}}$ are initialized from $\mathcal{N}(0,0.02^{2})$ and $W_{J,\mathrm{down}}$ is zero-initialized, so a J3 model equals its J0 counterpart at initialization. We train with a fixed number of sweeps ( $J=3$ ) and backpropagate through all of them. At inference, the top-layer latent of the preceding token is already available, so exact recurrence ( $N_{t}=F(r_{t-1}^{\mathrm{top}})$ ) can be evaluated in a single token-by-token pass without refinement sweeps (“sequential rollout”); finite number of sweeps need not reproduce this rollout. We report both three-sweep & sequential-rollout eval (Sec. 4.1 ).

The base-update model performs best on MultiArith and GSM-Hard, while input injection and vstream produce the best ASDiv accuracy; these differences among Lattice variants are small (at most $3.2$ points). The trend persists at the 600M scale: LLLM $(1024,1024)$ outperforms the 609M Base-40L Transformer on every task and raises mean accuracy from $40.24$ to $47.74$ ( $+7.50$ points), despite using slightly fewer parameters. These results show that gains from horizontal latent capacity transfer beyond pretraining perplexity to math reasoning. Finetuned accuracy is not determined by pretraining quality alone: Base-40L and LLLM $(908,256)$ with input-injection + vstream have the same FineWeb-Edu perplexity ( $14.40$ ) and nearly the same zero-shot average ( $47.36$ vs. $47.38$ ), yet differ by $10.1$ points on GSM8K ( $33.5$ vs. $43.6$ ). Since each model is finetuned once, we do not interpret the small differences among Lattice variants; the gap to the Transformers is larger and consistent, with every Lattice model ahead of every Transformer baseline by at least $10.1$ GSM8K points at both scales.

#### A.4 Training Details

Unless otherwise noted, all models use the same training recipe; only model width and depth, token budget, and Lattice-specific settings vary across runs.


##### A.4.1 Transformer Backbones

We build on a LLaMA-style decoder with the GPT-NeoX tokenizer (vocabulary size $V=50{,}304$ ). Layers use pre-RMSNorm ( $\epsilon=10^{-5}$ ), rotary position embeddings (RoPE) with a context length of $2048$ , SwiGLU MLPs with intermediate size $2752$ , and an untied output head. Attention uses $16$ query heads and $16$ key/value heads, each of dimension $64$ , so the attention width ( $16\times 64=1024$ ) is fixed and decoupled from the residual-stream (vertical-latent) width $d_{\mathrm{model}}$ . We vary $d_{\mathrm{model}}$ $\in\{256,512,908,932,945,1024,\dots\}$ and the depth $N\in\{22,24,40\}$ to sweep model size. Input embeddings are scaled by $\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{model}}}$ . Backbone attention and MLP projections have no biases, and all weights not listed below use the standard $\mathcal{N}(0,0.02^{2})$ initialization.

We use AdamW with $\beta_{1}{=}0.9$ , $\beta_{2}{=}0.95$ , weight decay $0.1$ , and gradient-norm clipping at $1.0$ . Weight decay is not applied to biases, to the gains of the backbone RMSNorm layers, or, following Mamba-2 practice, to the horizontal-latent decay parameters $\mathrm{A\_log}$ and $\mathrm{dt\_bias}$ ; all other parameters, including the learned initial state $r_{0}$ and the normalization gains inside the Lattice update, are decayed. All Lattice injection maps—the FiLM $\gamma/\beta$ projections, input-injection and vstream maps, Jacobi refiner’s output projection, and output-head carry projection—are zero-initialized. Consequently, the Lattice model is functionally identical to a plain Transformer of the same width and depth at initialization, and the horizontal latent acquires influence only through training.

The peak learning rate is $1\times 10^{-3}$ and is decayed with a cosine schedule to a floor of $10\%$ of the peak ( cosine_with_min_lr , $\texttt{min\_lr\_rate}{=}0.1$ ), following a linear warmup over the first $2\%$ of training steps. We tuned the peak learning rate by grid search for both the Transformer and Lattice backbones.

We use a global batch size of $512$ sequences of length $2048$ ( $\approx 1.05$ M tokens per step), realized with a per-device batch size of $8$ and gradient accumulation of $8$ across $8$ GPUs. Token budgets correspond to $9{,}537$ steps for 10B tokens, $19{,}074$ for 20B, and $28{,}611$ for 30B.

Training uses bfloat16 mixed precision, FlashAttention-2, and gradient checkpointing; attention inside Lattice layers uses PyTorch’s fused scaled-dot-product attention. For numerical stability, the diagonal gated-linear carry scan and its FiLM application are computed in fp32 .

##### A.4.2 Residual-Stream Enhancements

Fig. 3 (Sec. 4.2 ) compares ten 400M models trained on 20B FineWeb-Edu tokens ( $19{,}074$ steps) with the Transformer recipe above, changing only the residual stream. The seven Transformer variants (plain, +HC, +mHC, +value, +x0, +HC + x0, +x0 + value) use the 24-layer, $d_{\mathrm{model}}{=}1024$ model. The three Lattice variants (+Lattice, +Lattice + HC, +Lattice + HC + x0) use the vstream -only configuration (E1/L20/D1, $d_{\mathrm{model}}{=}932$ , $d_{r}{=}256$ , no input injection), narrowed to stay iso-parameter with the Transformer. Fig. 14 (App. A.3.3 ) adds four runs with learned value residuals, two Transformers and their Lattice counterparts.

Following nanochat ( Karpathy, 2025 ) , we compute $h^{0}=\mathrm{RMSNorm}(\text{scaled token embedding})$ once, without a learned gain, and before every layer $\ell$ (early, Lattice, and late) set $h\leftarrow\lambda_{r}^{\ell}h+\lambda_{0}^{\ell}h^{0}$ . The $2N$ scalars are initialized linearly in depth, $\lambda_{0}^{\ell}=0.20-0.15\,\ell/(N{-}1)$ and $\lambda_{r}^{\ell}=1.15-0.10\,\ell/(N{-}1)$ , and trained in separate AdamW groups: $\lambda_{0}$ at $25\times$ the base learning rate ( $2.5\times 10^{-2}$ ) without weight decay, and $\lambda_{r}$ at $0.25\times$ ( $2.5\times 10^{-4}$ ) with weight decay $0.05$ ; both follow the same warmup and cosine schedule.

In Fig. 3 , we use the fixed (constant) ResFormer variant ( Zhou et al., 2025 ) : every layer after the first replaces its values with $\tfrac{1}{2}v^{1}+\tfrac{1}{2}v^{\ell}$ , with no added parameters. The learned variant (all results in Fig. 14 ) instead uses $\alpha^{\ell}v^{1}+\beta^{\ell}v^{\ell}$ , with two unconstrained scalars per layer initialized to $0.5$ , adding $2(N{-}1)$ parameters ( $46$ for the Transformer, $42$ for the Lattice). Like the x0 scalars, $\alpha^{\ell}$ is trained at $25\times$ the base learning rate without weight decay, and $\beta^{\ell}$ at $0.25\times$ with decay $0.05$ .

HC ( Zhu et al., 2025 ) and mHC ( Xie et al., 2026 ) use $n{=}4$ residual streams, each initialized with a copy of the token embedding and summed before the final RMSNorm. Each attention and MLP sub-layer has its own router with three sets of weights: read weights that mix the $n$ streams into the sub-layer input, write weights that distribute the sub-layer output back to the streams, and an $n\times n$ mixing matrix that recombines the streams. In HC, following the dynamic formulation of Zhu et al. (2025) (App. J), each weight is a static term plus a token-dependent term computed from the LayerNorm-normalized streams. The static terms are initialized so that each sub-layer reads from one stream and the mixing matrix is the identity; the token-dependent terms are zero-initialized and scaled by learnable gates initialized to $0.01$ . The attention and MLP output projections are scaled by $1/\sqrt{n}=0.5$ at initialization. In mHC, following Xie et al. (2026) (Eqs. 7–9), a single linear map of the RMS-normalized, flattened streams produces all three: read weights $\sigma(\cdot)$ , write weights $2\sigma(\cdot)$ , and mixing logits, which $20$ Sinkhorn–Knopp iterations project toward a doubly stochastic matrix. The static biases are zero for the read and write weights and the identity for the mixing logits. In both, the static router weights are excluded from weight decay.

We combine the Lattice with HC: each Lattice layer keeps a single horizontal latent, shared by all four streams, which reads the attention router’s mixed input and steers the attention and MLP exactly as in the single-stream model. The horizontal latent does not modulate the HC read, write, or mixing weights; we leave this to future work. ##### A.4.3 Recurrent Backbones

All recurrent models (Sec. 4.3 ) are attention-free stacks of $22$ pre-RMSNorm layers, each a token mixer followed by a SwiGLU MLP (intermediate size $2752$ ), with the GPT-NeoX vocabulary ( $V=50{,}304$ ), untied embeddings, and no positional encoding. All mixers run on the Triton kernels of the flash-linear-attention library ( fla , v0.4.1) 2 2 github.com/fla-org/flash-linear-attention ; we add only a small PyTorch-2.4 compatibility shim and modify no kernel or layer code. . Baseline and Lattice models share the same mixer code; the Lattice versions only add the steering described below. Native-state geometries and widths are listed in Tab. 7 .

We use fla ’s GatedDeltaNet layer ( Yang et al., 2025 ) unchanged: short causal convolutions on $q,k,v$ , in-kernel $L_{2}$ normalization of $q$ and $k$ , per-head decay $\alpha$ and write strength $\beta$ , a gated output RMSNorm, and the chunked training kernel. The state per head is $d_{k}\times d_{v}$ , varied through the number of heads $H$ and the per-head key and value dimensions.

We implement the Mamba-2 SSD layer ( Dao and Gu, 2024 ) on fla ’s chunk_simple_gla kernel rather than the mamba_ssm package: a joint input projection to $[z,\,xBC,\,\Delta]$ , a size-4 depthwise causal convolution with SiLU on $xBC$ , a scalar per-head decay $\Delta A$ with $A=-\exp(A_{\log})$ and $A_{\log}$ initialized to $\log(1,\dots,H)$ , values $x\Delta$ , a $D$ skip connection, a $z$ -gated RMSNorm, and the standard log-uniform $\Delta$ -bias initialization on $[10^{-3},10^{-1}]$ . The inner width is $H\cdot d_{\mathrm{head}}=1024$ with one $B/C$ group, and the state per head is $d_{\mathrm{state}}\times d_{\mathrm{head}}$ .

The official triadic linear attention kernels support only a per-head key dimension of $128$ on Hopper/Blackwell GPUs and provide no fallback, so we reimplement the layer inside our GDN stack. Each head writes with the joint key $\kappa=k_{2}\otimes k$ and reads with the joint query $\pi=q_{2}\otimes q$ , where $k,q\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}$ are $L_{2}$ -normalized GDN-style factors and $k_{2},q_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{E}$ ( $E{=}2$ slices) are $L_{2}$ -normalized softplus projections of the input. The recurrence is the standard gated delta rule on the joint key dimension $Ed_{k}$ , computed with fla ’s chunk_gated_delta_rule . Our one simplification relative to the original is a scalar per-head decay shared across slices.

We follow the Gated DeltaNet-H2 layout ( Yang et al., 2025 ) with only GDN and Mamba layers: $11$ blocks of [Mamba2, MLP, GDN, MLP], i.e., $22$ mixers. Its Mamba2 and GDN layers are the same as in the pure backbones above.

Each Lattice layer adds the same horizontal-latent update as in the Transformer, with $d_{r}=256$ , input injection, and vstream . The pure GDN, Mamba2, and Triadic GDN backbones use E1/L20/D1, so $20$ of the $22$ mixer layers carry a latent. The hybrid applies the same split at the level of its $11$ blocks (E1/L9/D1): each of the $9$ Lattice blocks gives its Mamba2 mixer and its GDN mixer their own latent, for $18$ latents in total. Instead of attention, the latent steers the mixer by FiLM, $x\leftarrow x\odot(1+\tanh\gamma)+\beta$ , applied to every quantity that controls native-memory writes and reads: for GDN, $q,k,v$ (after the short convolution and before $L_{2}$ normalization), the decay and write-strength logits (before softplus and sigmoid), and the output-gate input; for Mamba2, $C$ , $B$ , $x$ (the analogues of $q,k,v$ ), the per-head $\Delta$ logit (before softplus), and $z$ ; for Triadic GDN, additionally the $k_{2},q_{2}$ logits before softplus. The MLPs, the output head, and the native-state geometry are unchanged, and all FiLM maps are zero-initialized, so each Lattice model equals its pure counterpart of the same width at initialization. To keep each pair iso-parameter, we reduce $d_{\mathrm{model}}$ of the Lattice model (Tab. 7 ). The two vstream -only variants used for downstream selection have no input injection and instead either reduce $d_{\mathrm{model}}$ at FFN width $2752$ or keep $d_{\mathrm{model}}{=}1024$ and reduce the FFN width.

All recurrent runs use a standalone PyTorch DDP trainer on $8$ GPUs with the Transformer recipe above (AdamW, peak learning rate $10^{-3}$ , cosine decay to $10\%$ , weight decay $0.1$ , clipping $1.0$ , global batch $512\times 2048$ , $9{,}537$ steps $\approx$ 10B FineWeb-Edu tokens, bfloat16 , gradient checkpointing, seed $42$ ), with two differences: linear warmup over the first $10\%$ of steps, and weight decay applied only to matrices other than the token embedding (no decay on embeddings, normalization gains, biases, $r_{0}$ , $A_{\log}$ , $\Delta$ /dt biases, or $D$ ). Pure and Lattice models within each pair share this recipe exactly.
