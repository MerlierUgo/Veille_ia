## Résumé

Offline treatment learning must balance imitation, which stays close to logged evidence but ignores outcomes, against value maximisation, which uses outcomes but drifts toward treatments the records cannot verify. Numerical reinforcement learning strikes this balance during training. For large language models (LLMs), directly using sparse retrospective outcomes for fine-tuning raises challenges of delayed credit assignment and limited evidence for alternative treatments. We present R-LLMC, which strikes the balance at inference for sepsis resuscitation. A behaviour-supervised LLM, decoded into an exact distribution over 25 vasopressor-fluid actions, proposes clinician-anchored candidates; an offline critic selects among them by estimated long-term value. The policy has a closed form for off-policy evaluation, and its rationale is written after selection, so explanation cannot steer treatment. In experiments on held-out MIMIC-III patients, R-LLMC exceeded the clinician reference in doubly robust value by +0.214 (95\% CI 0.091-0.300), with fitted Q evaluation agreeing.

### 1 Introduction

Sepsis resuscitation is a sequential treatment problem ( Hotchkiss et al., 2016 ; Singer et al., 2016 ; Evans et al., 2021 ) . Fluids may restore perfusion while increasing fluid burden, vasopressors support arterial pressure with competing risks, and each intervention changes the context of later decisions whose consequences become apparent only as the patient’s course unfolds. Whether a treatment strategy benefits patients is therefore a property of the policy over the whole course, and retrospective records reveal outcomes only for treatments that were actually given. Learning treatment policies from such records faces a well-known tension ( Fujimoto et al., 2019 ; Kumar et al., 2020 ) . Imitating clinicians keeps recommendations close to logged evidence but ignores outcomes, so it reproduces practice rather than improving on it. Maximising estimated outcome value uses that evidence, but estimates for treatments rarely given in comparable states are extrapolations, and maximisation is drawn toward precisely the treatments the records cannot verify. A useful aid must balance the two. It must also be assessable from retrospective records before prospective use, and it should expose the clinical context behind each recommendation for review.

Existing data-driven approaches address different parts of this problem. Risk models built on longitudinal EHR trajectories support recognition and stratification ( Shashikumar et al., 2021 ; Zhu et al., 2025 ; Cissoko et al., 2025 ; Li and Law, 2026 ) , and workflow-integrated systems bring such estimates closer to care delivery ( Goh et al., 2021 ; Adams et al., 2022 ) , but neither specifies the next treatment. Sequential decision models trained on ICU records do specify one, using reinforcement learning (RL) to connect a present action to a delayed outcome ( Komorowski et al., 2018 ; Liu et al., 2024 ) . Value-maximising policies such as the AI Clinician sit at the second end of the tension, ranking candidate actions across the full action space. Offline RL balances the two ends inside the training objective: conservative penalties lower the values of rarely logged actions ( Kumar et al., 2020 ) , behaviour-constrained methods restrict the learned policy toward logged actions ( Fujimoto et al., 2019 ) , and in-sample methods avoid querying unlogged actions in the value backup ( Kostrikov et al., 2021 ) . Because these policies define probabilities over a fixed treatment space, their value can also be estimated retrospectively by off-policy evaluation (OPE) ( Thomas and Brunskill, 2016 ; Jiang and Li, 2016 ; Gottesman et al., 2019 ) . What they lack is a clinical interface. The patient state is a standardised numerical vector and the recommendation is a dose index, so the clinical context supporting a recommendation is not itself exposed for review ( Sokol et al., 2025 ) .

Large language models (LLMs) offer that interface. They answer clinical questions ( Singhal et al., 2023 ; Singhal et al., 2025 ) , read serialised records with named clinical fields ( Zhou et al., 2025 ; Wu et al., 2024 ) , and are being connected to structured treatment recommendations ( De Vito et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2025 ) , so their inputs and outputs are expressed in clinical terms. Direct outcome-driven fine-tuning of LLMs is challenging in this retrospective setting. The records contain outcomes only for logged treatment trajectories, leaving the outcomes of alternative recommendations unobserved. Direct policy-gradient fine-tuning against a terminal outcome faces delayed credit assignment across clinical decisions and action tokens. A trajectory-importance-weighted implementation can additionally suffer from high variance under limited policy–behaviour overlap. These challenges motivate our use of offline value information for inference-time selection rather than direct outcome-driven fine-tuning of the proposal model. For LLM recommenders trained by likelihood or imitation, proposal likelihood alone does not measure long-horizon treatment value ( Thirunavukarasu et al., 2023 ; Gu et al., 2024 ) . Their fluency compounds the problem. A justification generated together with a recommendation can be mistaken for evidence of benefit ( Farquhar et al., 2024 ; Asgari et al., 2025 ; Turpin et al., 2023 ) , and a free-text recommendation is a sample of text rather than an explicit distribution that OPE could evaluate. Work that combines the two families has typically kept the policy numerical; MORE-CLEAR, for example, uses a frozen LLM to encode clinical notes as state features for offline treatment learning ( Lim et al., 2026 ) . What is missing is a way to strike the balance for a policy defined by the LLM itself, without outcome gradients, while keeping that policy explicit enough to evaluate.

We present R-LLMC, which moves the balance from training to inference and realises it through structure rather than through a regularised objective (Fig. 1 ). The two ends of the tension are assigned to separate components, connected by a shared 25-action vasopressor–fluid vocabulary. A behaviour-supervised LLM reads named clinical fields and emits a structured action, and constrained factorised decoding over its valid tokens yields the exact proposal probability of every action; imitation thus determines which candidates are considered. A frozen numerical critic, trained by expectile temporal-difference learning on the aligned numerical view of the same state, then ranks $N$ candidates sampled from this proposal, with a preference for low twin-head disagreement; value thus decides among them. Outcome information enters through selection rather than through gradients to the LLM. Because candidates are drawn from the proposal, selection reorders clinician-like actions rather than searching the full action space, and the Best-of- $N$ construction ( Beirami et al., 2024 ) bounds how far the policy can move from the proposal, making the number of candidates a single control of the trade-off. This anchoring does not by itself guarantee logged-data coverage for every selected action. We express the induced policy, including the disagreement preference, fallback and tie-breaking, in closed form, which supplies exact target probabilities for OPE. Finally, a separately trained LLM generates a rationale only after the action is fixed, so that, unlike a jointly generated justification, the text exposes the displayed evidence for review without altering the action.

Our contributions are threefold. (1) We formulate an LLM treatment recommender as an explicit, exactly computable distribution over a clinical action vocabulary through constrained factorised decoding. This places language policies within the same offline value-learning and off-policy evaluation framework as numerical treatment policies. (2) We realise the imitation–value trade-off for such a policy at inference rather than in training. A frozen value-guided selection operator admits delayed-outcome information without outcome-derived gradients to the proposal model, redistributes probability only within the proposal support, and bounds the policy’s divergence from the proposal. Its induced policy, including the disagreement preference, fallback and tie-breaking, has a closed form that supplies exact target probabilities for evaluation. (3) We evaluate the framework on held-out MIMIC-III patients, where value-guided selection improved estimated outcome value over both the supervised proposal and an estimated clinician reference under doubly robust and fitted Q evaluation. Numerical-proposal and direct-critic controls attribute this gain to the selection operator, and show that proposal-constrained selection requires fewer clipped importance ratios than direct critic maximisation under the reported OPE protocol. A frozen language proposal was also reused at an independent site with locally fitted numerical components.

### 2 Related work

Sequential treatment learning from retrospective EHRs. The AI Clinician learns treatment values over clustered physiological states and a discrete vasopressor–fluid grid ( Komorowski et al., 2018 ) . Subsequent work studies deep value functions, continuous state representations, and other ICU interventions ( Liu et al., 2024 ; Sokol et al., 2025 ) . Retrospective policy evaluation depends on the cohort, state and action definitions, and assumptions linking logged trajectories to target-policy value ( Thomas and Brunskill, 2016 ; Jiang and Li, 2016 ; Gottesman et al., 2019 ) . R-LLMC retains a long-horizon treatment objective while constructing the action policy through language proposals and numerical candidate selection.

Clinical language models and treatment interfaces. Medical language models support question answering and note-centred tasks ( Singhal et al., 2023 ; Singhal et al., 2025 ; Thirunavukarasu et al., 2023 ) , and can process serialised or longitudinal clinical records ( Zhou et al., 2025 ; Wu et al., 2024 ) . Their use in decision making requires attention to temporal grounding, calibration, and reliable structured output ( Gu et al., 2024 ; Farquhar et al., 2024 ; Asgari et al., 2025 ) . MORE-CLEAR uses a frozen LLM to represent clinical notes and combines that representation with structured variables for offline treatment learning ( Lim et al., 2026 ) . Here the language model occupies a different position: it defines the treatment proposal distribution itself, which is then refined by a numerical selector.

Offline value learning and sequence-policy refinement. Conservative objectives limit optimistic value extrapolation ( Kumar et al., 2020 ) , behaviour-constrained methods restrict policy construction ( Fujimoto et al., 2019 ) , and implicit Q-learning uses an upper-expectile state value fitted at logged actions ( Kostrikov et al., 2021 ) . Value-predictor disagreement is also used as a heuristic for extrapolation sensitivity ( An et al., 2021 ) . Numerical value learning can guide sequence policies at different stages: QDT uses dynamic programming to relabel return-to-go targets before sequence policy training ( Yamagata et al., 2023 ) ; QT combines Q-value regularisation during training with value-based selection among return-conditioned candidates at inference ( Hu et al., 2024 ) . Best-of- $N$ provides a sampling-and-selection mechanism whose induced policy has been studied theoretically ( Gao et al., 2023 ; Beirami et al., 2024 ) . R-LLMC combines an expectile-trained numerical critic with a supervised language proposal through a shared treatment vocabulary. Its explicit total action order accounts for disagreement, value, and fallback in the finite-action Best-of- $N$ distribution. Unlike QDT and QT, R-LLMC leaves the training objective of the language proposal unchanged and introduces value information only through a frozen selection operator whose induced policy is available in closed form.


### 3 Methodology

R-LLMC separates action proposal from outcome-based ranking, and gives each role one of two aligned representations of the same longitudinal EHR (Fig. 1 ). The language policy reads a clinical textual view and learns a behaviour-anchored proposal distribution from logged clinician actions. The numerical value model reads the corresponding standardised trajectory and estimates long-horizon outcome utility from retrospective transitions. The two communicate only through a shared code for the discrete vasopressor–fluid actions.

At each decision point the proposal supplies sampled candidates, the critic scores each of them on the numerical view with a conservative value and a twin-head disagreement signal, and a deterministic rule prioritises threshold-passing candidates and returns the highest-ranked one. The components do not vote: the proposal fixes what is considered, the critic fixes the order. The explanation is generated only once the action is fixed and cannot affect selection. Each component serves one of the requirements set out in Section 1. The proposal turns the LLM’s recommendation into an explicit distribution learned from logged clinician actions. Behaviour cloning shapes proposal probabilities without imposing an empirical support mask. The critic contributes delayed-outcome information within the proposal support. The selection rule yields a closed-form policy that can be evaluated retrospectively. The rationale exposes the displayed evidence for review without entering selection.


#### 3.1 Problem formulation

We formulate longitudinal sepsis resuscitation as a finite-horizon Markov decision process $\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,R,\gamma)$ on a 4-hour clinical timescale, using recorded clinical summaries as an approximation of the patient state. A trajectory is $\zeta=(s_{0},a_{0},r_{0},\ldots,s_{T-1},a_{T-1},r_{T-1},s_{T})$ , where $s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}$ is the recorded clinical summary at decision time $t$ . The action $a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}$ is a haemodynamic treatment. The unknown patient dynamics $P$ are represented only through retrospective transitions. The objective of a treatment policy $\pi$ is

[Tableau] $$J(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim(P,\pi)}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^{t}r_{t}\right].$$ (1)

Thus, the policy objective concerns expected long-horizon patient outcome rather than the local plausibility of an individual action proposal.

All learning takes place on logged data. We write the available transitions as $\mathcal{D}=\{(s_{t},a_{t},r_{t},s_{t+1},d_{t})\}$ , where $d_{t}$ marks a transition that reaches a terminal state. Two properties of $\mathcal{D}$ shape every design choice below: the reward is observed only at the end of a stay, and it is observed only for the treatment that was actually given. The outcomes of alternative actions at that decision point are therefore not observed.

The agent operates at two coupled timescales. At the decision level , one action is chosen every 4 hours and its consequences can unfold over the rest of the ICU trajectory. At the generation level , an action is expressed by an autoregressive token sequence. Optimising the language model directly with the terminal outcome would nest the second horizon inside the first. If $w_{t,1:L_{t}}$ denotes the tokens used to express decision $t$ , a sequence-level policy-gradient estimator would contain a term of the form

[Tableau] $$\nabla_{\theta}J=\mathbb{E}\!\left[(G-b)\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L_{t}}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}\big(w_{t,\ell}\mid w_{t,<\ell},o_{t}\big)\right],$$ (2)

in which a single delayed and sparse return $G$ must be credited across every clinical decision of the stay and across every token used to express each of them. A compact action string shortens the inner sum but leaves the decision-level credit assignment unchanged. A trajectory-importance-weighted estimate of the expectation in Eq. 2 would involve products of importance ratios over the stay, which can become concentrated on a few trajectories (Appendix C ). Such an estimator can have high variance under limited policy–behaviour overlap, motivating our choice to introduce outcome information through a separate numerical critic rather than directly fine-tuning the language proposal. If one model produced both the action and its explanation, such fine-tuning would also move the two distributions together.

R-LLMC instead performs a single step of critic-guided policy improvement, in the spirit of generalised policy iteration and of approaches that connect sequence policies to numerical value learning ( Hu et al., 2024 ; Yamagata et al., 2023 ) . A supervised language proposal policy $\pi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{prop}}$ is fitted by behaviour cloning on clinician actions and defines the behaviour-anchored candidate distribution. An independent numerical selection critic $\mathcal{E}_{\omega}$ estimates the downstream utility of sampled candidates from logged trajectories. A non-parametric operator $\mathcal{I}_{N,\xi}$ then uses these distinct outputs to improve the proposal at inference:

[Tableau] $$\pi^{\text{R-LLMC}}_{N,\xi}=\mathcal{I}_{N,\xi}\big[\pi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{prop}},\mathcal{E}_{\omega}\big].$$ (3)

No outcome-derived gradient reaches $\theta$ , so the sum over tokens in Eq. 2 never has to be formed. Outcome information enters only through the critic and the selection operator, at the timescale on which the outcome is defined.


#### 3.2 Dual-view clinical action interface

The proposal and critic require different representations, but their outputs must refer to the same treatment decision. This section defines the shared interface that makes this possible.

A dual-view clinical state. The same recorded summary $s_{t}$ is exposed to the two components through different but deterministically aligned representations,

[Tableau] $$x_{t}=g_{\mathrm{num}}(s_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad o_{t}=g_{\mathrm{text}}(s_{t})\in\mathcal{O}.$$ (4)

The numerical state $x_{t}$ contains the standardised values of 20 variables, including vital signs, laboratory measurements, SOFA, GCS, and treatment context accumulated through the current row (maximum vasopressor exposure and cumulative intravenous fluid). It is the sole input to the selection critic. The textual observation $o_{t}$ serialises the corresponding unstandardised measurements into semantically labelled fields, for example <HeartRate:74, normal> | <MeanBP:76, normal> | <Lactate:1.1, normal> | … . Proposals are formed from the current observation only ( $H_{\mathrm{prop}}=1$ ). Accumulated treatment context is already part of that observation, which keeps the language input compact. The rationale may additionally draw on up to $H_{\mathrm{rat}}\leq 4$ immediately preceding observations of the same trajectory, so that a stated trend can be checked against the values the clinician is shown. Both views originate from the same patient and time row and share an identical action code; they are aligned representations of the same longitudinal EHR, not independent raw clinical modalities. Because the two views carry the same measurements, a difference between proposals built on them reflects the model class rather than the information available; the numerical-proposal control of Appendix D relies on this. The textual view keeps the language policy’s input and output in clinical terms, which is what makes its recommendations reviewable. The standardised numerical view gives the critic a stable, low-dimensional input for temporal-difference learning and prevents it from scoring unconstrained free text.

Treatment action and language outputs. The environment action is

[Tableau] $$a_{t}=(v_{t},f_{t})\in\mathcal{A}=\{0,\ldots,4\}\times\{0,\ldots,4\},\qquad\iota(a_{t})=5f_{t}+v_{t}\in\{0,\ldots,24\},$$ (5)

where $v_{t}$ and $f_{t}$ are the vasopressor and intravenous-fluid intensity bins for the next 4-hour interval. For each treatment dimension, bin 0 denotes zero exposure. Non-zero doses are partitioned into bins 1–4 at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles of the non-zero empirical dose distribution among training decision-target rows. The compact $5\times 5$ grid balances dosing resolution with the number of logged observations per action bin. Discretisation does not ensure adequate state-specific coverage for every action. The fixed index $\iota$ is the shared action interface between textual proposals and numerical critic outputs. Internal selection and evaluation use this 25-action joint space. At the independent site, the proposal is marginalised over fluid before selection and evaluation over five vasopressor levels.

Structured proposal and post-selection rationale. The language proposal policy must return a valid, inspectable action distribution rather than an unconstrained free-text recommendation. It is supervised to emit the compact target V:<0-4> F:<0-4> . At inference, the policy does not rely on unconstrained free-text sampling or on rejecting malformed completions. The next-token distributions are instead restricted to the five valid dose bins and all five fluid conditionals are evaluated, yielding the exact normalised proposal

[Tableau] $$p_{\theta,T_{\mathrm{s}}}(v,f\mid o_{t})=p_{\theta,T_{\mathrm{s}}}(v\mid o_{t})\,p_{\theta,T_{\mathrm{s}}}(f\mid o_{t},v),\qquad(v,f)\in\mathcal{A}.$$ (6)

Each factor is normalised over $\{0,\ldots,4\}$ , so Eq. 6 defines a proper distribution over all 25 actions; $T_{\mathrm{s}}$ is the sampling temperature applied to the restricted logits. The proposal probability of every action, including actions the language policy would rarely emit, is therefore available in closed form, and the policy does not depend on whether a generated string can be parsed. The factorisation is exact by the chain rule and requires only a fixed, small number of next-token evaluations per decision. Because the same decoding applies to any backbone, including one without fine-tuning, every language policy in Section 4 is evaluated as an explicit distribution. The temperature controls how concentrated the proposal is and hence how many distinct candidates Best-of- $N$ sampling considers.

Selection and explanation use separate language outputs. After the selector fixes $a_{t}^{\star}$ , the rationale model produces an evidence-linked justification from the past-only clinical history $h_{t}=(o_{\max(0,t-H_{\mathrm{rat}}+1)},\ldots,o_{t})$ , $1\leq H_{\mathrm{rat}}\leq 4$ :

[Tableau] $$\rho_{t}^{\star}\sim\pi_{\varphi}^{\mathrm{rat}}\bigl(\cdot\mid h_{t},a_{t}^{\star}\bigr).$$ (7)

Conditioning on the already selected action, rather than generating the action and its justification together, keeps the explanation outside the selection pathway. No property of the generated text can alter the fixed recommendation, and the rationale is not selection evidence. The complete clinician-facing response $z_{t}=(a_{t},\rho_{t})$ therefore factorises as

[Tableau] $$p(z_{t}\mid x_{t},o_{t},h_{t})=\pi_{N,\xi}^{\mathrm{R\mbox{-}LLMC}}(a_{t}\mid x_{t},o_{t})\,\pi_{\varphi}^{\mathrm{rat}}(\rho_{t}\mid h_{t},a_{t}),$$ (8)

where only $a_{t}$ enters the clinical transition and the outcome objective. The rationale is a reviewable, post-selection justification. We do not treat it as an account of the computation that produced the action, as evidence used to select the action, or as a guarantee of clinical correctness.

Supervised language interface. The proposal and rationale outputs require separate supervision because only the proposal distribution enters action selection and evaluation. Let $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{prop}}=\{(u^{\mathrm{prop}}(o_{t}),y_{t}^{\mathrm{act}})\}$ contain textual observations paired with logged structured actions, and let $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{rat}}=\{(u^{\mathrm{rat}}(h_{t},a_{t}),\rho_{t})\}$ contain past-only histories and the corresponding logged clinician actions paired with structured rationale targets. The proposal and rationale modules are fitted on the two supervision sets with completion-only objectives,

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{m}(\vartheta_{m})=-\,\mathbb{E}_{(u,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{m}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{|y|}\log\pi_{\vartheta_{m}}\!\big(y_{\ell}\mid y_{<\ell},u\big),\qquad m\in\{\mathrm{prop},\mathrm{rat}\}.$$ (9)

The proposal parameters $\vartheta_{\mathrm{prop}}=\theta$ and the rationale parameters $\vartheta_{\mathrm{rat}}=\varphi$ are fitted separately, so that supervision on explanatory style cannot perturb the proposal distribution used by the selector. Neither objective includes an outcome-based loss. Rationale supervision was conditioned on past-only histories and the corresponding logged clinician actions; no quantity computed by the selection critic entered rationale training, whereas at inference the model receives the action already fixed by the selector. Because the selector chooses only among actions sampled from a proposal fitted to clinician behaviour, the actions that condition the rationale at inference resemble the logged actions it was trained on.

Terminal outcome reward. The value model needs a target defined on the patient’s later trajectory rather than on the likelihood of a proposal. Let the last transition of a trajectory arrive at the recorded hospital outcome. The raw and optimisation rewards are

[Tableau] $\displaystyle r^{\mathrm{raw}}_{t}$ $\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&s_{t+1}\ \text{is non-terminal},\\ +100,&s_{t+1}\ \text{is terminal and the patient survives},\\ -100,&s_{t+1}\ \text{is terminal and the patient dies},\end{cases}$ (10) $\displaystyle r_{t}$ $\displaystyle=0.01\,r^{\mathrm{raw}}_{t},$ $\displaystyle G_{t}$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{k=t}^{T-1}\gamma^{\,k-t}r_{k}.$

The corresponding action value,

[Tableau] $$Q^{\pi}(x_{t},a_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi,P}\!\left[G_{t}\mid x_{t},a_{t}\right],\qquad V^{\pi}(x_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x_{t})}[Q^{\pi}(x_{t},a)],$$ (11)

is the numerical signal the agent uses to rank candidate treatments. Using only the recorded hospital outcome avoids fixing trade-offs between physiological targets in advance, at the cost of a signal that is sparse and arrives late; Section 3.3 propagates it backwards along the numerical trajectory. With $\gamma=0.99$ and terminal label $Y\in\{-1,+1\}$ , the initial return is $G_{0}=\gamma^{T-1}Y\in[-1,1]$ , so all reported values are discounted outcome utilities on this scale.


#### 3.3 Outcome-aligned ensemble critic

The selector needs a numerical ranking signal for each proposed candidate, but the record contains an outcome only for the action that was given. Estimating the others offline invites two failure modes: a backup that maximises over all treatments assigns unchecked optimistic values to treatments seldom given in comparable states, and a single fitted network carries seed-specific error that is invisible at selection time. The selection critic $\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(x,a)$ of Eq. 3 addresses both by keeping the backup within logged actions and returning a twin-head disagreement heuristic alongside each value.

We train the selection critic on the numerical view of the logged transitions, $\{(x_{t},a_{t},r_{t},x_{t+1},d_{t})\}$ , using $M=3$ independently initialised members. Each member has a numerical-state encoder, twin action-value heads $Q_{m,1}$ and $Q_{m,2}$ over the 25 actions, and a scalar state-value head $V_{m}$ .

In-sample state-value evaluation. Offline maximisation over all actions can assign spuriously high values to actions poorly represented in the data. We instead regress the state value to an upper expectile of target-network action values at logged actions. For critic member $m$ , let superscript ${-}$ denote its slowly updated target network and define $L_{\tau}(z)=|\tau-\mathbf{1}[z<0]|z^{2}$ for $\tau\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1)$ . The state-value loss is

[Tableau] $$\delta_{t,m}=\min_{j\in\{1,2\}}Q^{-}_{m,j}(x_{t},a_{t})-V_{m}(x_{t}),\qquad\mathcal{L}^{(m)}_{V}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\left[L_{\tau}(\delta_{t,m})\right].$$ (12)

The upper expectile summarises the upper range of target values associated with logged actions. This avoids maximisation over unobserved actions in the state-value backup, while retaining a continuation signal for subsequent action-value learning. As $\tau\to 1$ , $V_{m}$ approaches the maximum of the action value over actions with support in the data at that state ( Kostrikov et al., 2021 ) ; an intermediate $\tau$ trades this optimism against sensitivity to noisy targets. Taking the smaller head inside $\delta_{t,m}$ further limits overestimation of the target.

Outcome propagation to action values. The sparse terminal outcome must be propagated to earlier treatment decisions before candidates can be ranked. The action-value heads use a one-step target bootstrapped through the target state value,

[Tableau] $$\hat{y}_{t,m}=r_{t}+(1-d_{t})\gamma V^{-}_{m}(x_{t+1}),\qquad\mathcal{L}^{(m)}_{Q}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\left[\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\ell_{\beta}\!\left(Q_{m,j}(x_{t},a_{t})-\hat{y}_{t,m}\right)\right],$$ (13)

where $\ell_{\beta}$ is the Huber loss. Applied repeatedly along a trajectory, this backup propagates the single outcome recorded at discharge into an estimated long-horizon score for each treatment decision. Training remains restricted to actions in the empirical distribution. Each member independently minimises $\mathcal{L}^{(m)}_{V}+\mathcal{L}^{(m)}_{Q}$ . Although each head outputs values for all 25 actions, only logged actions receive regression targets, so values for treatments rarely given in comparable states are extrapolations of the shared encoder. For this reason the critic ranks candidates drawn from a clinician-anchored proposal rather than maximising over the full action space (Section 3.4 ; Appendix D ).

Disagreement-aware evaluation. Candidate ranking also requires a conservative summary of the fitted ensemble and an explicit signal for head disagreement. The two heads are first averaged across ensemble members,

[Tableau] $$Q^{\mathrm{ens}}_{j}(x,a)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}Q_{m,j}(x,a),\qquad j\in\{1,2\}.$$ (14)

Only then does the frozen selection critic form the conservative value and disagreement returned to the selector:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(x,a)$ $\displaystyle=\bigl(\bar{Q}_{\omega}(x,a),\Delta_{\omega}(x,a)\bigr),$ (15) $\displaystyle\bar{Q}_{\omega}(x,a)$ $\displaystyle=\min_{j\in\{1,2\}}Q^{\mathrm{ens}}_{j}(x,a),$ $\displaystyle\Delta_{\omega}(x,a)$ $\displaystyle=\left|Q^{\mathrm{ens}}_{1}(x,a)-Q^{\mathrm{ens}}_{2}(x,a)\right|.$

Here $\omega$ denotes the complete ensemble. Averaging across members before taking the minimum first reduces seed-specific error and then applies pessimism, so that $\bar{Q}_{\omega}$ and $\Delta_{\omega}$ reflect divergence between heads rather than between individual members. Taking the smaller of the two heads is intended to limit overestimation. Because the twin heads share targets but differ in initialisation, their gap tends to widen where the data constrain them weakly, a property used as a heuristic for extrapolation sensitivity ( An et al., 2021 ) . We therefore use $\Delta_{\omega}$ only to order candidates, and we fix its cutoff without any access to the test cohort:

[Tableau] $$\xi=\operatorname{Quantile}_{\tau_{u}}\left\{\Delta_{\omega}(x,a):(x,a)\ \text{is a logged validation pair}\right\}.$$ (16)

The gap is a ranking signal, not ensemble variance, calibrated epistemic uncertainty, or a clinical-safety probability. Because the expectile backup estimates a high-value continuation within the logged action support, this critic is also not an estimate of $Q^{\pi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{prop}}}$ ; its role is to score candidates for inference-time improvement, and it is kept separate from every outcome model used later for evaluation.


#### 3.4 Disagreement-aware Best-of- $N$ selection

What remains is an explicit rule that turns proposal sampling and numerical ranking into a single recommendation, while keeping the induced target policy in a form that can be written down and evaluated. Let $p_{t}(a)=p_{\theta,T_{\mathrm{s}}}(a\mid o_{t})$ from Eq. 6 and draw $A_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,A_{t}^{(N)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}p_{t}$ . Define the low-disagreement action set

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{F}_{t}=\{a\in\mathcal{A}:\Delta_{\omega}(x_{t},a)\leq\xi\}.$$ (17)

If at least one sampled action lies in $\mathcal{F}_{t}$ , only sampled threshold-passing actions remain; otherwise the selector falls back to all sampled actions. The resulting candidate pool is

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{C}_{t}=\begin{cases}\{A_{t}^{(k)}:A_{t}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}\},&\text{if this set is non-empty},\\ \{A_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,A_{t}^{(N)}\},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}$$ (18)

and the highest conservative value in this pool is returned as the agent’s treatment recommendation:

[Tableau] $$a_{t}^{\star}=\underset{a\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}{\arg\max}\;\bar{Q}_{\omega}(x_{t},a).$$ (19)

Ties are broken by the smaller fixed action index $\iota(a)$ . The order of the two steps carries the design intent. Filtering before maximising prioritises sampled candidates below the validation-defined disagreement threshold before their conservative values are compared. This reduces the influence of a high value paired with greater head disagreement. The fallback keeps the policy defined at every state, at the cost of making the threshold a preference over candidates rather than a hard support or safety constraint. The value $\bar{Q}_{\omega}$ is queried only on $(x_{t},a)$ pairs in the numerical action space, never on generated text. The rationale of Eq. 7 is produced only after the recommended action is fixed and is never used as selection evidence.

Exact policy induced by filtered Best-of- $N$ . Sampling makes the recommendation stochastic. If the agent’s action distribution were available only through simulation, evaluation against logged data would require a sampling approximation. The procedure above avoids this by inducing a distribution over the 25 actions that can be written in closed form. To expose it, define the disagreement–value rank

[Tableau] $$c_{t}(a)=\left(\mathbf{1}\!\left[\Delta_{\omega}(x_{t},a)\leq\xi\right],\bar{Q}_{\omega}(x_{t},a),-\iota(a)\right),$$ (20)

ordered lexicographically. Maximising $c_{t}$ over the $N$ samples is exactly equivalent to filtering first, falling back if necessary, and then maximising value with deterministic tie-breaking. Let the lower cumulative proposal masses under this total order be

[Tableau] $$F_{t}(a)=\sum_{b:c_{t}(b)\leq c_{t}(a)}p_{t}(b),\qquad F_{t}^{-}(a)=\sum_{b:c_{t}(b)<c_{t}(a)}p_{t}(b).$$ (21)

The probability that $a$ is the best-ranked member of $N$ independent proposals is therefore

[Tableau] $$\boxed{\pi_{N,\xi}^{\text{R-LLMC}}(a\mid x_{t},o_{t})=F_{t}(a)^{N}-\big(F_{t}^{-}(a)\big)^{N}.}$$ (22)

This finite-difference expression gives the complete 25-action target policy used for evaluation without a Monte Carlo approximation. The distribution normalises to one, reduces to $p_{t}$ at $N=1$ , and redistributes mass only within the proposal support at that decision point, rather than prohibiting a treatment across states. An action with $p_{t}(a)=0$ has $F_{t}(a)=F_{t}^{-}(a)$ and therefore receives no probability, and as $N$ grows the policy concentrates on the highest-ranked action among those the proposal supports. The operator depends on the proposal only through $p_{t}$ , so any proposal that defines explicit action probabilities can be substituted without changing the selector or the evaluation; Appendix D uses this to replace the language proposal with a numerical one. The same probabilities serve as OPE target probabilities and allow treatment allocation to be read directly across clinical states (Fig. 4 ).

Departure from the proposal. Because Eq. 20 defines a total order, Eq. 22 is a Best-of- $N$ policy over that order, and its divergence from the clinician-anchored proposal is bounded by

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi_{N,\xi}^{\text{R-LLMC}}(\cdot\mid x_{t},o_{t})\,\middle\|\,p_{t}\right)\leq\log N-\frac{N-1}{N}$$ (23)

( Beirami et al., 2024 ) , about $1.2$ nats at $N=8$ , independently of the critic. $N$ is therefore a single, interpretable control of how far outcome information may move the policy away from the proposal distribution. This bound is relative to $p_{t}$ , not to the fitted behaviour reference $\mu$ , and does not guarantee empirical action coverage. By contrast, maximising $\bar{Q}_{\omega}$ over all actions is not constrained by the proposal at all.

Because the frozen selection critic is an offline approximation rather than the exact $Q$ -function of the proposal, we describe this operation as critic-guided policy improvement and assess its policy value separately using DR and FQE. Algorithm 1 summarises the complete decision-support agent.

### 4 Experiments

The experiments address four questions that follow from the design in Section 3 . (Q1) Does value-guided selection over an explicit LLM proposal yield higher estimated outcome value than the clinician reference on held-out patients? (Q2) Which elements produce the estimated gain: behavioural supervision, value-guided selection, the disagreement preference, the proposal model class, or the backbone? (Q3) Can a frozen language proposal be reused at an independent site when only the numerical components are refitted? (Q4) What does the explicit action distribution make inspectable, and how does the post-selection rationale relate to the displayed physiology and the selected action?


#### 4.1 Experimental setup

Data sources and cohorts. We conducted a retrospective study using preprocessed trajectories derived from MIMIC-III ( Johnson et al., 2016 ) , with the primary analysis restricted to adult ICU stays meeting the Sepsis-3 definition ( Singer et al., 2016 ) . The existing patient-level development/test split contained 4,493/500 trajectories. We reserved 450 development trajectories for validation by stratified sampling on hospital mortality, leaving 4,043 for training. Preprocessing statistics, model fitting, early stopping, and hyperparameter selection used only the training and validation partitions; the 500-patient test set was reserved for final evaluation.

The independent-site analysis used a separate cohort from the ICU of Peking University Third Hospital. Sepsis status was determined from the site-provided sepsis indicator, and trajectory-level eligibility and temporal-coverage criteria yielded 571 patients. Table 1 summarises the analysed cohorts.

Preprocessing. Internal trajectories used a 4-hour grid and the 20 physiological and treatment-context variables listed in Appendix A . Missing measurements were imputed by past-only within-trajectory forward filling, followed by training-partition medians for unresolved entries, and training-set standardisation was frozen for validation and test. Vasopressor and fluid exposures defined the $5\times 5$ action grid of Eq. 5 , with zero exposure as level 0 and training-set quartiles of positive exposures as levels 1–4. At row $j$ , state variables stop at $j$ and the action describes treatment in the next interval. The terminal outcome was scaled to $\{-1,+1\}$ and assigned on arrival at the terminal state.

Compared policies. In the internal experiments, every compared policy is expressed as a distribution over the same 25-action grid for selection and OPE. Random assigns uniform probability to the 25 actions. Clinician behaviour is an estimated policy $\mu(a\mid x)$ fitted to logged actions by behaviour cloning and serves as the paired-comparison reference. Neural SARSA-Q follows the AI-Clinician paradigm ( Komorowski et al., 2018 ) with a continuous-state $Q$ -network and SARSA updates bootstrapping on the next logged action, rather than clustered states; its Boltzmann temperature is selected on validation data to match the clinician model’s mean action entropy. Base LLM uses the backbone without fine-tuning and the same constrained decoding interface ( $N=1$ ). SFT proposal samples the behaviour-supervised language proposal directly ( $N=1$ ), isolating behavioural supervision from selection. R-LLMC applies Best-of-8 selection with the disagreement preference; unfiltered and $N=4$ variants appear in the component analyses. Appendix D also evaluates the same selector with a numerical behaviour-cloning proposal and the selection critic used directly as a greedy or Boltzmann policy.

Evaluation. Policy values are estimated from retrospective trajectories by off-policy evaluation (OPE) ( Jiang and Li, 2016 ) . The primary estimator is a self-normalised, stepwise weighted doubly robust (DR) estimator ( Thomas and Brunskill, 2016 ) , which combines outcome regression with an importance-weighted correction. For each target policy, three independently seeded fitted Q evaluation (FQE) models were trained on the training transitions, selected by validation TD loss, and frozen before test prediction ( Le et al., 2019 ; Hao et al., 2021 ) . Their averaged action values enter both DR and the complementary FQE-direct estimate. The evaluation models are fitted separately from the selection critic of Section 3.3 . DR uses a per-step log-ratio cap of $\log 20$ in magnitude. Percentile 95% intervals come from $B=500$ outcome-stratified patient-bootstrap resamples conditional on the fitted models, and differences from the clinician reference use matched resamples. Because DR and FQE-direct fail in different ways (importance-weight variance versus regression extrapolation), we treat agreement between them as more informative than either estimate alone. Appendix C gives the estimator, resampling definitions, and weight diagnostics.

Implementation. The primary experiments use a BF16 Qwen3-4B backbone, with proposal and rationale parameters fitted separately ( Yang et al., 2025 ) . The backbone-scale analysis repeats the action-policy pipeline with a separately fitted Qwen3-14B proposal while sharing all backbone-independent components. The selection critic is a three-member ensemble of compact multilayer networks, each with twin action-value heads and a state-value head. Unless stated otherwise, the proposal uses the current observation and the rationale model at most four consecutive observations. Outcome propagation uses $\gamma=0.99$ and expectile $\tau=0.8$ . The selector draws $N=8$ candidates at sampling temperature $T_{\mathrm{s}}=0.7$ , and the disagreement cutoff $\xi$ of Eq. 16 is the $0.75$ quantile of disagreement on logged validation pairs. Remaining settings are given in Appendix E .

Independent-site adaptation protocol. To test whether a language proposal can be reused, the MIMIC-III proposal remained frozen and retained source-domain input processing. Numerical preprocessing, selection critics, behaviour models, action thresholds, overlap calibration, and evaluation models were fitted locally. The joint proposal was first marginalised over fluid, $p_{t}^{\mathrm{site}}(v)=\sum_{f=0}^{4}p_{t}(v,f)$ , and selection and OPE then operated over five vasopressor levels. All site policies were evaluated in this five-level action space. Patient-level, outcome-stratified three-fold cross-fitting fitted local components on two folds and applied them to the held-out fold. A common overlap constraint, calibrated within each outer-training partition, was applied to all non-clinician target policies, and both DR importance ratios and FQE policy expectations used the constrained probabilities. Pooled out-of-fold predictions were evaluated with $B=500$ patient bootstraps stratified jointly by outer fold and terminal outcome, holding fitted models fixed. Appendix C.2 defines the probability transformation and fold-local parameter-selection rule.

#### 4.3 Sources of the estimated gain

Behavioural supervision and value-guided selection. Starting from the base LLM, we cumulatively added behavioural supervision, unfiltered Best-of-8 selection, and the disagreement preference (Table 3 ). Supervision raised FQE-direct to approximately the clinician level, whereas DR became unstable. Value-guided selection produced the largest increase under both estimators. The two learning signals play the separate roles intended by the design. Supervision encourages behavioural alignment : it favours clinician-like proposals but does not guarantee empirical action coverage or add outcome information. The disagreement preference prioritises sampled candidates below the validation-defined gap threshold; if none passes, selection falls back to all sampled candidates. ### 5 Discussion

R-LLMC addresses three requirements that existing approaches meet only in part: a recommendation should specify the next treatment and relate it to delayed outcome, it should be informed by retrospective treatment data and be evaluable from those records, and the clinical context behind it should be open to review. Numerical RL policies meet the first and are evaluable, but expose only a state vector and a dose index. LLM recommenders expose clinical context, but define neither an explicit treatment distribution nor a link to outcome. The framework meets all three by assigning each requirement to a separate component connected through a shared action vocabulary. The language proposal defines the treatment distribution and its support. The numerical critic introduces outcome information through selection within that support. The closed-form induced policy makes the result evaluable, and the post-selection rationale exposes the displayed evidence without influencing the action.

The experiments indicate how these components divide the work. Behavioural supervision encourages behavioural alignment: it favours clinician-like proposals but does not guarantee adequate logged-data coverage or add outcome information. Estimated value is added at selection, the only point at which outcome information enters the policy. The same pattern held when the language proposal was replaced by a numerical behaviour-cloning proposal, and at both backbone sizes. The estimated gain is therefore a property of the selection operator applied to a behaviour-supervised proposal rather than of the language model class or its scale. Two further results show why selection is constrained to the proposal. Gains saturated with the number of candidates, while selection remained within the proposal support. Direct maximisation of the critic over all actions reached a similar FQE-direct estimate but required more clipped importance ratios under the reported OPE protocol. The disagreement preference acted as a conservative mechanism that stabilised evaluation rather than as an independent source of value.

These results define the role of the language model in the framework. The evidence does not support a claim that a language proposal yields higher estimated value than a numerical one. Its contribution is structural: the interface turns an LLM recommender into an explicit policy that offline value information can improve and OPE can evaluate on the same scale as numerical policies, while its inputs and outputs remain in clinical terms. The base LLM illustrates this. Used directly, it was a poorly performing policy, and the interface made that deficit measurable rather than hidden behind fluent output. Because the selector and evaluation depend only on proposal probabilities, the proposal can be replaced without changing either of them. This modularity also allowed a frozen language proposal to be reused at an independent site while the numerical components were refitted locally. The site estimates were smaller and their clinician comparison included zero, and a frozen numerical proposal could be reused in the same way. The analysis therefore establishes that such reuse is feasible, not that it confers a language-specific transfer advantage.

The rationale model is deliberately separated from the decision. Generating text only after the action is fixed means that an explanation, however fluent, cannot alter the treatment. Accordingly, its theme declarations tracked the displayed physiology, while treatment allocation responded to the same inputs through the proposal and the critic. The rationale should therefore be read as an action-conditioned description of the visible evidence, not as a reconstruction of the numerical computation that selected the action, a distinction emphasised in work on rationale faithfulness and explanation standards ( Turpin et al., 2023 ; Arrieta et al., 2020 ; Longo et al., 2024 ) . The explicit policy distribution complements this case-level description at the population level: treatment allocation can be read directly from action probabilities across observed clinical states, as in the resuscitation landscape, rather than inferred from sampled outputs.

Limitations. All value estimates are retrospective. Exact target probabilities make OPE applicable, but they do not make it precise. Trajectory-weight ESS was low for every policy that departed from the estimated behaviour model, including an imitation policy, and a large fraction of importance ratios was clipped. The bootstrap intervals are conditional on fitted models and do not include retraining variability. Unmeasured clinical factors may also affect the estimates. Reported values are therefore estimates of discounted outcome utility under the specified protocol, not identified treatment effects or changes in mortality. The action space is a discretised $5\times 5$ vasopressor–fluid grid, reduced to five vasopressor levels at the independent site, and the terminal reward does not encode intermediate physiological targets. The language proposal uses only the current structured observation. It therefore does not yet exploit clinical notes or longer histories, the settings in which a language interface may contribute most beyond a numerical one. Results are reported for one model family and two cohorts. Rationale targets were derived from model-generated explanations of logged actions rather than authored by clinicians, and the illustrative review sequence demonstrates the interface without evaluating clinician use. Prospective evaluation would be required before any clinical application.


### 6 Conclusion

We presented R-LLMC, a framework that makes an LLM treatment recommender an explicit and evaluable policy for sepsis resuscitation. Constrained decoding turns the language proposal into an exact distribution over a clinical action vocabulary. A frozen numerical critic introduces delayed-outcome information by selecting among proposal samples, without outcome-derived gradients to the proposal model and without leaving the proposal’s support. The induced policy has a closed form that supplies the target probabilities required for off-policy evaluation, and a separate model explains the selected action without influencing it. On held-out MIMIC-III patients, value-guided selection improved estimated outcome value over both the supervised proposal and an estimated clinician reference. Controls attributed this gain to the selection operator rather than to the proposal model class, and a frozen language proposal could be reused at an independent site with locally fitted numerical components. The framework provides a basis for developing language-based treatment policies that can be improved with outcome data and evaluated on the same terms as conventional treatment policies, with prospective evaluation as the necessary next step.

Each MIMIC-III trajectory is represented on a 4-hour decision grid using the following 20 physiological and treatment-context variables: HeartRate , SysBP , DiasBP , MeanBP , RespRate , SpO2 , SOFA_24hours , GCS_min , ANIONGAP , BICARBONATE , CREATININE , CHLORIDE , LACTATE , PLATELET , INR , PT , BUN , GLUCOSE , max_dose_vaso , and volum_total .

Past-only forward filling imputes missing measurements, followed by training-partition medians for unresolved entries. Training-partition feature means and standard deviations are held fixed thereafter.

Actions follow Eq. 5 , using exposure in the next 4-hour interval as the target for state row $j$ ; state variables stop at $j$ , and the final state has no outgoing action. Cut points fitted to positive training exposures were fixed for validation and test. ### Appendix B Language-interface implementation

The proposal is supervised to emit action-only completions of the form V:<v> F:<f> . Each conditional distribution is obtained by restricting logits to valid digit tokens $0,\ldots,4$ , dividing by $T_{\mathrm{s}}=0.7$ , and applying softmax. Vasopressor probabilities and the five vasopressor-conditioned fluid distributions are combined as in Eq. 6 . Candidates are sampled independently with replacement from this joint distribution. In the internal analysis, these materialised proposal probabilities define the selector and its induced policy used for OPE. The proposal uses the current observation only ( $H_{\mathrm{prop}}=1$ ).

The proposal and rationale modules are fitted separately with completion-only supervision. Neither objective includes an outcome-based loss. Proposal supervision contributes to the action policy evaluated by OPE; the rationale module describes the fixed action in relation to displayed observations. The teacher-generated source explanations were produced with access to full retrospective trajectories and outcomes. Rationale targets were reconstructed from the archived observed-action explanations after alignment with logged actions; outcome and counterfactual fields were excluded from student supervision. Following lexical screening, feature statements were checked against past-only histories, with source themes constraining selection, and matched evidence was rendered anew into the fixed themes , evidence , and rationale schema. Evidence entries identify the feature, relative observation time, value, and qualitative direction. The corpus comprised 2,464 training and 279 validation examples. The rationale module conditions on up to four past-only observations and a fixed action—logged during training and selected at inference—without selection-critic values, disagreement scores, or terminal outcomes. Its parameters and outputs do not participate in action selection or OPE. Removing explicit outcome fields and reconstructing targets from past-only evidence cannot rule out residual hindsight bias inherited from the teacher-generated explanations. This limitation concerns rationale supervision and its descriptive evaluation; the rationale model does not participate in action selection or OPE.

For the deterministic rationale analysis, each decision’s conditioning action is held fixed while theme declarations are compared with the visible past-only observations. This analysis concerns action-conditioned text; OPE evaluates the stochastic Best-of- $N$ action distribution separately.


### Appendix C Off-policy evaluation implementation


#### C.1 Policy and estimator definitions

The following estimator definitions describe the internal analysis. Let $\pi$ denote the target action distribution: R-LLMC uses Eq. 22 , and the other policies use their corresponding 25-action distributions. At the independent site, joint proposals are first marginalised over fluid to five vasopressor levels before selection and OPE. Each held-out fold uses a locally fitted five-action behaviour policy and overlap-constrained target probabilities; these same probabilities enter both DR importance ratios and FQE policy expectations. The behaviour policy $\mu(a\mid x)$ is fitted from logged clinician actions on the training partition and selected using the validation partition. Small probabilities are floored at $10^{-4}$ and the distribution is renormalised. No test outcomes are used in fitting the behaviour policy.

A separate fitted Q evaluation (FQE) model is trained for each target policy. These models are fitted separately from the selection critic used by R-LLMC. Three independently initialised FQE models are trained for each policy. Their predictions are averaged:

[Tableau] $$\widehat{Q}^{\pi}(x,a)=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{m=1}^{3}\widehat{Q}^{\pi}_{m}(x,a),$$ (24)

and

[Tableau] $$\widehat{V}^{\pi}(x,o)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a\mid x,o)\widehat{Q}^{\pi}(x,a).$$ (25)

DR and FQE-direct use the same averaged FQE predictions. The complementary FQE-direct estimate is

[Tableau] $$\widehat{V}^{\pi}_{\mathrm{FQE}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{V}^{\pi}(x_{i,0},o_{i,0}).$$ (26)

For patient trajectory $i$ and decision time $t$ , the clipped log importance ratio is

[Tableau] $$\ell_{i,t}=\operatorname{clip}\left(\log\frac{\pi(a_{i,t}\mid x_{i,t},o_{i,t})}{\mu(a_{i,t}\mid x_{i,t})},-\log 20,+\log 20\right).$$ (27)

The cumulative and self-normalised weights are

[Tableau] $$W_{i,t}=\exp\left(\sum_{k=0}^{t}\ell_{i,k}\right),\qquad\widetilde{W}_{i,t}=\frac{W_{i,t}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}W_{j,t}}.$$ (28)

The primary estimator is

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\widehat{V}_{\mathrm{DR}}^{\pi}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{V}^{\pi}(x_{i,0},o_{i,0})+\sum_{t=0}^{T_{\max}-1}\gamma^{t}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{W}_{i,t}\Big[r_{i,t}+\gamma(1-d_{i,t})\widehat{V}^{\pi}(x_{i,t+1},o_{i,t+1})-\widehat{Q}^{\pi}(x_{i,t},a_{i,t})\Big].$ (29)

Following the final observed transition, absorbing-state padding sets subsequent temporal-difference residuals to zero and carries the last cumulative weight forward, equivalent to unit importance ratios during padding. All trajectories remain in each time-step normalisation denominator.

The unclipped sensitivity analysis uses the same estimator but replaces $\ell_{i,t}$ by the untruncated log importance ratio.


#### C.2 Independent-site overlap constraint

At the independent site, joint language and numerical proposals are first marginalised over fluid, and any Best-of- $N$ selection then operates over five vasopressor levels. For outer fold $k$ , let $q_{k}(a\mid x)$ denote the resulting raw target policy, or the corresponding five-action baseline, and let $\mu_{k}(a\mid x)$ denote the local behaviour model. The latter uses softmax temperature $1$ , followed by the $10^{-4}$ probability floor and renormalisation described above. The evaluated target is a convex mixture with this fitted behaviour reference:

[Tableau] $$\pi_{k}^{\mathrm{site}}(a\mid x)=\mu_{k}(a\mid x)+\lambda_{k}^{\mathrm{eff}}(x)\bigl[q_{k}(a\mid x)-\mu_{k}(a\mid x)\bigr].$$ (30)

The fold-level coefficient $\lambda_{k}\in[0,1]$ is reduced at each state when needed to bound the action-probability ratios. Define

[Tableau] $$r_{k}(a,x)=\frac{q_{k}(a\mid x)}{\mu_{k}(a\mid x)},\qquad r_{k}^{\max}(x)=\max_{a}r_{k}(a,x),\qquad r_{k}^{\min}(x)=\min_{a}r_{k}(a,x).$$ (31)

With $C=20$ and the numerical boundary $C^{\prime}=C(1-10^{-12})$ , set

[Tableau] $\displaystyle U_{k}(x)$ $\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\dfrac{C^{\prime}-1}{r_{k}^{\max}(x)-1},&r_{k}^{\max}(x)>1,\\ 1,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}$ $\displaystyle L_{k}(x)$ $\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1-1/C^{\prime}}{1-r_{k}^{\min}(x)},&r_{k}^{\min}(x)<1,\\ 1,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}$ (32) $\displaystyle\lambda_{k}^{\mathrm{eff}}(x)$ $\displaystyle=\min\{\lambda_{k},U_{k}(x),L_{k}(x)\}.$

Thus $1/C\leq\pi_{k}^{\mathrm{site}}(a\mid x)/\mu_{k}(a\mid x)\leq C$ for every action. The statewise reduction uses only $q_{k}$ and $\mu_{k}$ , not the logged action, reward or outcome. Raw target probabilities receive no additional floor; the final mixture is checked for non-negativity and normalisation. This constraint bounds ratios relative to a fitted behaviour model, rather than guaranteeing adequate empirical action coverage. The mixture can assign mass outside the raw target support; proposal-support preservation applies to the Best-of- $N$ operator before this site-specific transformation.

Within each outer-training partition, $15\%$ of patients form an outcome-stratified inner-validation split. Local behaviour models are fitted on the inner-training split and selected by inner-validation action cross-entropy. Each of the five vasopressor levels must have at least five logged actions in the inner-training split before overlap calibration. The ratio cap, probability floor, following candidate grid and diagnostic thresholds are fixed; only $\lambda_{k}$ is selected within the fold:

[Tableau] $$\Lambda=\{0,0.025,0.05,0.10,0.20,0.40,0.60,0.80,1\}.$$ (33)

For each candidate, cumulative importance weights are computed on inner-validation trajectories using the transformed target probabilities. After termination, the last cumulative weight is carried forward. If $u_{i,t}$ denotes the normalised weight at time $t$ , then $\mathrm{ESS}_{t}=1/\sum_{i}u_{i,t}^{2}$ . For $n_{\mathrm{val}}$ validation patients, every time point must satisfy $\mathrm{ESS}_{t}\geq\min\{n_{\mathrm{val}},\max(10,0.10n_{\mathrm{val}})\}$ and $\max_{i}u_{i,t}\leq\max\{0.10,1/n_{\mathrm{val}}\}$ , with zero stepwise ratio clipping. The largest candidate meeting all conditions simultaneously for the Base LLM, SFT proposal, filtered Best-of-4, filtered Best-of-8 and unfiltered Best-of-8 policies is selected. The $\lambda_{k}=0$ candidate returns the fitted behaviour policy; calibration fails if no candidate passes. The same fold-level coefficient is then applied to every non-clinician target policy, including Random, Neural SARSA-Q and the numerical-proposal controls, which do not influence its selection. Effective statewise coefficients may differ between policies. The calibration criteria use states, logged actions and fitted probabilities, without reward, outcome, DR or FQE values as selection objectives. Both FQE policy expectations and DR importance ratios use $\pi_{k}^{\mathrm{site}}$ from Eq. 30 ; the fitted clinician reference remains $\mu_{k}$ .


#### C.3 Resampling and implementation

Each FQE ensemble is fitted once on the corresponding development partition. Percentile 95% patient-bootstrap intervals use $B=500$ trajectory resamples stratified by terminal outcome. Policy differences from the clinician reference use matched bootstrap indices. All fitted models are held fixed. For the independent-site analysis, pooled out-of-fold predictions are resampled within joint outer-fold and terminal-outcome strata; models are not refitted or cross-fitted again within bootstrap replicates.


#### C.4 Evaluation diagnostics

The $u_{i}$ sum to one, whereas the reported diagnostic weights $w_{i}$ have mean one. Effective sample size is $\mathrm{ESS}=1/\sum_{i}u_{i}^{2}=n^{2}/\sum_{i}w_{i}^{2}$ . When the target equals the estimated behaviour model, ratios are one, giving ESS $=500$ and maximum weight $=1$ . This is an implementation identity, rather than a validation of the fitted behaviour model.

#### C.5 Interpretation of retrospective estimates

Sequential clinical OPE depends on modelling and data coverage because logged trajectories contain outcomes for administered treatments. Policy differences can concentrate importance weights on a small portion of the cohort, limiting the information available to weighted estimators ( Thomas and Brunskill, 2016 ; Komorowski et al., 2018 ) . Here the trajectory-weight ESS for R-LLMC was $1.9/500$ , and $47.06\%$ of stepwise ratios were clipped. Low trajectory ESS is not specific to value-guided selection: a numerical proposal fitted to clinician actions had a trajectory ESS of $2.46/500$ (Appendix D ). Lee et al. ( Lee et al., 2024 ) reported a WIS ESS of $3.33/2531$ ICU admissions ( $0.13\%$ ) and related it to differences between learned and clinician policies. This is context for a shared evaluation difficulty, not a reliability threshold for the present estimator. Trajectory-weight ESS is distinct from the sample size used to fit FQE. FQE depends on function approximation and data coverage, and DR and FQE-direct share its predictions. The bootstrap intervals condition on fitted models and outcome-stratified resampling; they do not incorporate model-retraining uncertainty. These features, together with possible unmeasured clinical factors, bound the retrospective interpretation: reported values estimate discounted outcome utility under the specified protocol, rather than identified treatment effects or changes in mortality.

### Appendix E Implementation settings

The numerical architecture settings below describe the internal experiments with 25 joint actions. Independent-site numerical components use the five-level vasopressor action space of the adaptation protocol.

The primary language backbone is Qwen3-4B and the within-family scale analysis uses Qwen3-14B. Models are operated in BF16. The Qwen3-4B proposal is fitted with completion-only LoRA supervision using rank 16, AdamW, learning rate $10^{-4}$ , batch size 8, maximum sequence length 512, and 800 training iterations. The proposal uses a single current observation ( $H_{\mathrm{prop}}=1$ ).

The Qwen3-14B scale-analysis proposal uses the same structured action interface, LoRA rank, optimiser, learning rate, batch size, maximum sequence length, and $H_{\mathrm{prop}}=1$ setting, and is trained for 400 iterations. The Qwen3-4B rationale model is fitted separately from the proposal model. It uses LoRA rank 16, AdamW, learning rate $10^{-4}$ , batch size 2, maximum sequence length 1600, and 1300 training iterations. Its input contains at most four past-only observations. The proposal and rationale parameters are therefore separate. Only the proposal distribution enters action selection and off-policy evaluation.

The three-member selection critic uses a two-layer, width-256 LayerNorm encoder with twin 25-action $Q$ heads and a scalar state-value head. Training uses AdamW (learning rate $3\times 10^{-4}$ , weight decay $10^{-4}$ , batch size 512), at most 150 epochs with validation patience 25, Polyak rate $0.005$ , Huber $\beta=1.0$ , and gradient-norm cap 10. The remaining selection settings are given in Section 4.1 . In the internal experiments, the validation-pair disagreement quantile of 0.75 yielded $\xi\approx 0.03623329$ for the frozen ensemble.

The clinician-behaviour model is a 25-action LayerNorm MLP with two hidden layers of width 256. It is trained using AdamW with learning rate $3\times 10^{-4}$ , weight decay $10^{-4}$ , batch size 512, at most 80 epochs, and validation patience 12. Neural SARSA-Q uses the same numerical encoder and action space and is trained with logged next actions. Its Boltzmann temperature is selected on validation data by matching mean action entropy to the clinician-behaviour model rather than by optimising test-set policy value.

For each target policy, three independently initialised FQE networks are used. Each is a two-layer LayerNorm MLP with hidden width 256 and a 25-action output. FQE training uses learning rate $10^{-3}$ , weight decay $10^{-4}$ , batch size 512, at most 800 epochs, validation patience 60, Polyak rate $0.01$ , Huber parameter $\beta=1.0$ , and gradient-norm cap 10. Predictions from the three models are averaged before computing either FQE-direct or doubly robust estimates.
