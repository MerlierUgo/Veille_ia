### 1 Introduction

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in integrating visual and textual information for tasks ranging from visual question answering to complex multimodal reasoning [ 31 , 33 , 19 , 3 , 36 ] . A central premise behind these models is that information from different modalities can be effectively integrated to support more informed predictions. Yet, most multimodal evaluation implicitly assumes that the available modalities are mutually consistent or complementary [ 17 , 24 , 8 ] . In practice, this assumption does not always hold. The textual and visual inputs may provide contradictory evidence due to noisy annotations, outdated or erroneous descriptions, imperfect retrieval, or inconsistencies among heterogeneous information sources. Recent work has therefore begun to investigate modality conflict , where different modalities support incompatible answers to the same question [ 39 , 37 , 38 ] . Such conflicts expose a fundamental aspect of multimodal reasoning that remains largely hidden when modalities agree: which modality ultimately governs the model’s prediction?

Existing studies [ 39 , 37 , 38 , 25 , 4 , 27 ] reveal that MLLMs do not treat the modalities neutrally under conflicts. Instead, they exhibit systematic modality preferences, tending to favor one source of evidence over another when the two disagree. Controlled conflict settings [ 38 ] have shown that this behavior is widespread across MLLMs and can vary substantially across models and tasks. As a result, modality preference can directly undermine model reliability. Misleading visual evidence can override an answer that a model correctly derives from text alone [ 10 ] , while erroneous textual information can similarly dominate contradictory visual evidence [ 35 ] . Recent studies consequently identify modality conflict as an important source of multimodal hallucination and robustness failures [ 39 , 32 , 28 ] .

More importantly, modality conflict is not merely an artificial stress test but a realistic failure mode in practical multimodal applications, where the authoritative modality is often specified by the task but not effectively utilized by the models. For example, medical imaging may serve as the primary evidence for diagnosis while patient metadata provides auxiliary context that should not override visual findings [ 12 , 7 ] . Similarly, in autonomous driving, real-time visual observations should take precedence over risky navigation instructions when safety is at stake [ 26 ] . In these cases, the desired behavior is clear: predictions should follow the designated trusted modality when conflicts happen. However, an MLLM’s intrinsic modality preference may not align with this externally specified priority, potentially leading to consequential errors.

When the trusted modality is explicitly specified by the task or application, a seemingly straightforward solution is to instruct the model to prioritize the trusted one or simply remove the non-trusted modality. However, neither provides a satisfactory mechanism for controlling multimodal arbitration. Prompt-based instructions preserve both modalities but offer limited controllability. Removing a modality changes the multimodal input itself and bypasses rather than resolves cross-modal conflicts. It also discards potentially useful information in the more common case where the modalities are aligned and sacrifices flexibility when the designated trusted modality varies across tasks or contexts.

In this work, we investigate whether modality arbitration under modality conflict can be effectively controlled through inference-time intervention. We first identify a small set of attention heads, which we term arbitration heads , whose attention patterns are strongly associated with whether model predictions follow visual or textual evidence. We then selectively intervene on these heads at inference time, modulating their attention toward the designated trusted modality. This yields a lightweight, training-free mechanism for bidirectional modality control and we can steer predictions toward either visual or textual evidence according to the specified modality. Importantly, arbitration heads can be identified from only a small calibration set of 10 samples and transfer effectively across benchmarks, suggesting that they capture a reusable mechanism for modality arbitration rather than benchmark-specific correlations. To conclude, our contributions can be summarized as:

- • We identify a compact set of arbitration heads associated with whether MLLMs follow visual or textual evidence under modality conflict.

- • We propose a lightweight, training-free intervention on these heads that enables bidirectional control toward a designated trusted modality while preserving both modalities as input.

- • We show that arbitration heads identified from a small calibration set generalize across benchmarks, suggesting a generalized mechanism for modality arbitration.


### 2 Related Work


#### 2.1 Modality Conflict in MLLMs

Prior work has shown that multimodal models can exhibit imbalanced reliance across modalities. Such preferences may arise from unimodal shortcuts in the training data, enabling models to solve multimodal tasks by disproportionately relying on a single modality [ 9 , 5 , 14 ] . More recent studies have shifted attention to more concrete settings of modality conflicts in MLLMs to study the model behaviors. Zhang et al . [ 39 ] formalized conflicts in multimodal inputs and showed that such conflicts in questions and images can induce hallucinations. Cai et al . [ 4 ] demonstrated that irrelevant or misleading information from one modality can degrade reasoning that should rely on another, while CLASH [ 27 ] evaluates whether MLLMs can explicitly detect contradictions between visual and textual evidence. Beyond characterizing failures under conflicts, recent work also examines how models resolve conflicting evidence. Zhang et al . [ 37 ] systematically evaluate modality preference with controlled conflicts and show that preference directions are encoded in hidden states and can be steered. Another work [ 38 ] further decomposes the model behavior under modality conflicts into inherent modality preference and instance-specific relative reasoning uncertainty. Nguyen et al . [ 25 ] investigate the internal conflict-resolution process and find that conflict detection and resolution exhibit distinct representational and attention patterns across layers. Together, these studies reveal that MLLMs exhibit systematic yet steerable behaviors under conflict.

The most relevant work is [ 37 ] , which introduces MPS, a method that identifies a modality-preference direction in the model’s representation space using contrasting conflict examples and steers generation by adding the resulting vector to the model’s hidden states. In contrast, we identify and intervene on attention heads that causally influence conflict arbitration, enabling targeted modality control while providing mechanistic evidence for modality preference. Our intervention also yields stable generation without the additional tuning required by MPS.


#### 2.2 Inference-time Interventions

Inference-time interventions aim to improve model performance without retraining. A major line of work in MLLMs modifies decoding to improve visual grounding, including layer-contrastive decoding [ 6 ] , visual contrastive decoding [ 15 ] , over-trust penalty and retrospection-allocation [ 11 ] , etc. Other methods intervene on attention or representations, such as amplifying image-token attention [ 23 ] , reallocating attention from output tokens to visual tokens [ 29 ] , post-hoc hallucination correction [ 34 ] , and steering modality preference in latent space [ 37 ] . A line of work in text-only LLMs also intervenes on a sparse set of attention heads, such as shifting activations along probed directions [ 20 ] , pruning memory-preferring heads [ 13 ] , reweighting attention to specified spans [ 40 ] , or steering heads with superposed signals [ 18 ] . These methods collectively demonstrate that model behavior can be improved at inference time by modifying decoding, attention, or representations.


### 3 Method


#### 3.1 Preliminaries and Problem Formulation


##### Multimodal autoregressive generation.

We consider a multimodal large language model that takes an image $x^{v}$ , a textual context $x^{t}$ , and a question $q$ as input. The model generates an answer $y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{N})$ autoregressively:

[Tableau] $$p_{\theta}(y\mid x^{v},x^{t},q)=\prod_{n=1}^{N}p_{\theta}(y_{n}\mid x^{v},x^{t},q,y_{<n}),$$

where $\theta$ denotes the model parameters. In common MLLM architectures, the image is first encoded into visual features and projected into the language-model embedding space. These visual tokens are then concatenated with textual tokens from the context and question. We denote the resulting input token sequence as $z=[z^{v};z^{t};z^{q}]$ , where $z^{v}$ , $z^{t}$ , and $z^{q}$ correspond to visual tokens, context tokens, and question tokens, respectively.


##### Evidence aggregation through attention.

Since the input sequence contains heterogeneous information sources, the model needs to route information among visual, textual, and question tokens before decoding an answer. For a transformer layer $\ell$ and attention head $h$ , let $q_{i}^{(\ell,h)}$ , $k_{j}^{(\ell,h)}$ , and $v_{j}^{(\ell,h)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{h}}$ denote the query, key, and value vectors of tokens $i$ and $j$ , where $d_{h}$ is the head dimension. The unnormalized attention score, i.e. the logit assigned to token $j$ before normalization, is

[Tableau] $$a_{i,j}^{(\ell,h)}=\frac{\left\langle q_{i}^{(\ell,h)},\,k_{j}^{(\ell,h)}\right\rangle}{\sqrt{d_{h}}}+M_{i,j},$$

where $M$ is the causal attention mask that sets $M_{i,j}=-\infty$ for $j>i$ . Normalizing these scores over all admissible source tokens and using them to mix the values yields the head output at position $i$ ,

[Tableau] $$o_{i}^{(\ell,h)}=\sum_{j}\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(a_{i,j}^{(\ell,h)}\right)v_{j}^{(\ell,h)},$$

where the softmax is taken over the index $j$ . The resulting weight $\operatorname{softmax}(a_{i,j}^{(\ell,h)})$ measures how much token $i$ draws on token $j$ when forming $o_{i}^{(\ell,h)}$ , so the score $a_{i,j}^{(\ell,h)}$ can be read as the evidence that token $j$ contributes before it is put in competition with the other source tokens. Under this view, attention provides a natural interface for studying how question tokens gather evidence from the visual or textual components of the input.


##### Modality conflict.

Let the multimodal input be $I=\{x^{v},x^{t},q\}$ , where $x^{v}$ and $x^{t}$ denote the visual and textual inputs, respectively, and $q$ denotes the question. Given the question, let $p_{v}(y)$ and $p_{t}(y)$ denote the model’s modality-specific predictions based on either visual or textual evidence. We consider a modality conflict when the two modalities favor different answers:

[Tableau] $$\operatorname*{argmax}_{y}p_{v}(y)\neq\operatorname*{argmax}_{y}p_{t}(y).$$

More generally, the degree of agreement between $p_{v}(\cdot)$ and $p_{t}(\cdot)$ provides a continuous signal of modality conflict, which we later exploit for adaptive steering in Section 3.5 . In our setting, the modality to prioritize under conflict is externally specified by the task or application. Our goal is therefore not to determine which modality is more reliable, but to control model arbitration toward the designated modality while retaining both modalities as input.

#### 3.3 Arbitration Head Localization

> Figure 1 : Overview of our method. (a) Arbitration head localization: we causally identify attention heads that causally influence modality preference by patching head activations from image-only and text-only inputs into conflicting multimodal inputs and measuring their effects on the answer logit margin. (b) Inference-time intervention: we select the most influential arbitration heads and steer predictions toward a designated modality by adjusting their attention to visual and textual tokens.

We first characterize the modality preference under conflicting multimodal inputs, where the visual and textual evidence support different answers. For each conflicting input $I_{C}=\{x^{v},x^{t},q\}$ , we define the answer logit margin as

[Tableau] $$\Delta(I_{C})=\log p(y^{v}\mid I_{C})-\log p(y^{t}\mid I_{C}),$$ (1)

where $y^{v}$ and $y^{t}$ denote the candidate answers supported by the visual and textual modalities, respectively. A positive margin, $\Delta(I_{C})>0$ , indicates that the model assigns a higher probability to the vision-supported answer under conflict input $I_{C}$ , whereas a negative margin, $\Delta(I_{C})<0$ , indicates a preference for the text-supported answer.

We then perform activation patching to localize attention heads that causally influence modality preference. For each conflicting example, we construct three corresponding inputs: the original conflict input $I_{C}$ , a vision-only input $I_{V}$ , and a text-only input $I_{T}$ . For each head $h$ at layer $\ell$ , we record its output $o_{i}^{(\ell,h)}$ at the final prompt position, immediately before answer generation, at the input to the attention output projection during the vision-only and text-only forward passes, denoting the resulting activations by $o_{V}^{(\ell,h)}$ and $o_{T}^{(\ell,h)}$ . We subsequently patch the conflict input with the corresponding activation from either unimodal run.

Specifically, the causal effect of patching the vision-derived activation into the conflict run is defined as

[Tableau] $$C^{(\ell,h)}_{V}(I_{C})=\Delta\!\left(I_{C};\operatorname{patch}\!\left(o^{(\ell,h)}_{V}\right)\right)-\Delta(I_{C}),$$ (2)

while the effect of patching the text-derived activation is

[Tableau] $$C^{(\ell,h)}_{T}(I_{C})=\Delta(I_{C})-\Delta\!\left(I_{C};\operatorname{patch}\!\left(o^{(\ell,h)}_{T}\right)\right).$$ (3)

These quantities measure the change in the answer logit margin caused by replacing the conflict-run head output with an activation obtained from a modality-specific input. We average the effect over a calibration set $\mathcal{D}$ of conflicting examples, and assign each head an influence score by

[Tableau] $$C^{(\ell,h)}=\mathbb{E}_{I_{c}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\min_{m\in\{V,T\}}\operatorname{ReLU}\left(C^{(\ell,h)}_{m}\right)\right].$$ (4)

This score prioritizes heads exhibiting positive causal effects under both modality-specific patching conditions while suppressing heads whose effects are weak or inconsistent across conditions.

We visualize $C^{(\ell,h)}$ across layers and attention heads using a heatmap and select the highest-scoring heads as the arbitration heads for subsequent intervention experiments. An example of the visualization with Qwen3-VL-8B is provided in Figure 2 . We also examine the stability of head-wise effects with respect to the number of calibration samples. The high-effect heads consistently concentrate at similar layer–head locations as $n$ increases. Notably, this spatial pattern is already apparent with only a few samples and remains stable with larger calibration sets.

> Figure 2 : Stability of head-wise effects across different sample sizes. Normalized head-wise effects $C^{(\ell,h)}$ for Qwen3-VL-8B, computed using $n$ samples. Despite varying $n$ , high-effect heads consistently concentrate in similar layers and head positions, indicating that the identified pattern is stable even with a small number of samples.

#### 3.4 Inference-Time Intervention

Based on the heads identified through activation patching, we perform targeted inference-time interventions to test whether manipulating their attention patterns can systematically alter the model’s reliance on a particular modality. Specifically, the interventions are applied directly to the attention computation of the selected heads during inference.

We take the top- $k$ heads by influence score $C^{(\ell,h)}$ as the arbitration head set $\mathcal{H}$ , and weight each of them by its normalized influence score

[Tableau] $$\widehat{c}^{(\ell,h)}=\frac{C^{(\ell,h)}}{\max_{(\ell^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in\mathcal{H}}C^{(\ell^{\prime},h^{\prime})}+\epsilon},$$ (5)

where $\epsilon>0$ is a small constant, so that $\widehat{c}^{(\ell,h)}\in(0,1)$ . We then bias these heads toward a target modality by adding a positive offset to the pre-softmax attention scores of tokens from that modality and subtracting the same offset from tokens of the competing modality. Recall that $a_{i,j}^{(\ell,h)}$ denotes the attention score from query position $i$ to key position $j$ before normalization, and let $\mathcal{T}_{m}$ and $\mathcal{T}_{\bar{m}}$ denote the sets of token positions associated with the target modality $m$ and the competing modality $\bar{m}$ , respectively. For each head $(\ell,h)\in\mathcal{H}$ , the intervened scores are defined as

[Tableau] $$\widetilde{a}_{i,j}^{(\ell,h)}=a_{i,j}^{(\ell,h)}+\alpha\,\widehat{c}^{(\ell,h)}\left(\mathbb{I}\!\left[j\in\mathcal{T}_{m}\right]-\mathbb{I}\!\left[j\in\mathcal{T}_{\bar{m}}\right]\right),$$ (6)

where $\alpha>0$ controls the overall intervention strength, and scaling by $\widehat{c}^{(\ell,h)}$ concentrates the perturbation on the heads with the strongest effect. The $\mathbb{I}[\cdot]$ denotes the indicator function. The intervention is applied at every query position in the selected heads during both prompt prefill and autoregressive decoding, with generated positions appended as neutral keys during decoding. The head output is then recomputed as $\widetilde{o}_{i}^{(\ell,h)}=\sum_{j}\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\widetilde{a}_{i,j}^{(\ell,h)}\right)v_{j}^{(\ell,h)}$ . As the offsets act inside the softmax, this intervention increases the relative attention allocated to the target modality while preserving access to information from the competing modality. Section 3.5 evaluates whether adaptive steering can retain the benefits of multimodal inference when the inputs are aligned instead of being conflicted.

The intervention effect is empirically quantified using the change in performance by comparing the generated responses against the image- and text-supported answers. The setup connects the diagnostic and intervention stages: activation patching identifies attention heads associated with modality-dependent answer selection, while inference-time intervention evaluates whether selectively redirecting the attention of these heads is sufficient to steer the model’s behavior toward a desired modality.


#### 3.5 Extension: Adaptive Steering

To avoid unnecessary intervention when modalities are already consistent, we introduce adaptive steering, which dynamically adjusts the intervention strength according to the detected degree of modality conflict. We augment an existing modality-conflict benchmark, MC 2 , whose original data consists of images and corresponding conflicting text. We generate aligned text by rewriting the conflicting text with image-supported answers, so that each image yields both an aligned and a conflicting multimodal input.

To detect conflicts, we compare the model’s image-only and text-only logit distributions using the union of their top-50 token logits, $\ell_{2}$ -normalize the resulting vectors, and compute their cosine similarity $s$ . As shown in Figure 3 , conflicting and aligned samples exhibit clearly different similarity distributions, supporting the use of this signal for adaptive conflict detection. A threshold $\tau$ , calibrated on 200 samples, separates conflicting from aligned inputs. During inference, the steering strength for each sample is determined as

[Tableau] $$\alpha=\alpha_{\max}\,\sigma\!\left(\frac{\tau-s}{\lambda}\right),$$ (7)

where $\alpha_{\max}$ is the maximum allowed strength, $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the logistic function, and $\lambda$ controls how sharply the steering strength changes around the conflict threshold. This ensures that stronger intervention is applied when the two modalities disagree. The resulting strength is then substituted into the intervention above.


### 4 Experiments


#### 4.1 Setup


##### Benchmarks and Metrics

We evaluate our method on four modality-conflict benchmarks, including MMCD [ 25 ] , MC 2 [ 37 ] , CLASH [ 27 ] , and CorrelationQA [ 10 ] , and one visual hallucination benchmark PhD [ 22 ] . These benchmarks cover diverse scenarios in which different modalities may lead to competing predictions.

MMCD, MC 2 , and CLASH provide annotations for the answers supported by each modality independently, enabling us to explicitly evaluate the model’s preference between conflicting modalities. We consider two evaluation settings: Image-Primary and Text-Primary . For methods that support bidirectional modality steering, we steer generation toward the designated primary modality in each setting. We then report accuracy compared against image-/text-supported answers under each setting. Consequently, successful image-primary steering should increase image accuracy while reducing text accuracy, whereas successful text-primary steering should exhibit the opposite behavior. This bidirectional evaluation directly measures whether a method can controllably shift model predictions toward a designated modality rather than merely improving overall task accuracy. CorrelationQA provides ground-truth answers based on textual information only. We therefore supplement it with corresponding image-supported answers to enable the same bidirectional evaluation. Details of this annotation procedure are provided in Appendix B.4 .

We further evaluate on two subsets of PhD [ 22 ] , a benchmark designed to assess visual hallucination in the presence of textual context. Following prior work [ 37 ] , we sample 1000 samples from the subsets PhD-sec and PhD-icc, which contain irrelevant or conflicting textual information, respectively. PhD complements the modality-conflict benchmarks by evaluating whether modality steering can strengthen visual grounding to mitigate hallucinations in the presence of misleading textual context. For PhD, we evaluate both settings using image accuracy, measuring how effectively each method preserves or suppresses reliance on visual evidence.


##### Models and Baselines

We conduct experiments on four open-source MLLMs to examine whether our modality steering generalizes across different multimodal architectures. Specifically, we use Qwen2.5-VL-7B [ 2 ] , Qwen3-VL-8B [ 1 ] , LLaVA-OneVision-7B [ 16 ] and InternVL3-8B [ 41 ] . We compare our method against standard inference strategies and various approaches. In the subsequent sections, Base denotes standard model inference without additional intervention, while CoT [ 30 ] employs chain-of-thought prompting to encourage explicit reasoning before prediction. We additionally include VCD (Visual Contrastive Decoding) [ 15 ] , a decoding-based method for increasing vision grounding and mitigating hallucination. These methods do not explicitly support controlling the direction of modality preference and therefore produce a single set of predictions regardless of whether the desired target is the image or text modality.

We further compare against two baselines capable of bidirectional modality steering. CAP (Conflict-Aware Prompting) explicitly instructs the model to answer according to either the image or the textual context, providing a prompting-based baseline for modality control. MPS (Modality Preference Steering), introduced in MC 2 [ 37 ] , provides a baseline for steering predictions toward a specified modality by computing a steering vector from a set of samples. For each MLLM, our method uses 10 samples from MC 2 to localize top-30 arbitration heads among over 1000 heads, and uses $\alpha=30$ as intervention strength for the main experiments where conflicts are explicitly presented. We additionally use the augmented MC 2 to demonstrate the adaptive steering. For CAP, MPS, and our method, we evaluate the image- and text-directed variants under the image-primary and text-primary settings, respectively. More experiment details are provided in Appendix B .

We evaluate whether adaptive steering can preserve the benefits of multimodal inference while resolving conflicts when necessary. When modalities are aligned, information from both modalities can be complementary, allowing multimodal inference to outperform relying on one modality alone.

As shown in Figure 4 , when conflicts are sparse, Base and adaptive steering outperform the unimodal baseline, confirming that complementary cross-modal evidence is worth exploiting. The comparison isolates what adaptive steering provides over discarding a modality, that conflict robustness that does not require giving up the complementary information available when the modalities agree.

We conduct ablation studies to examine the effects of steering strength, the number of selected arbitration heads, head selection, and modality suppression. As shown in Figure 5 , increasing the steering strength $\alpha$ progressively shifts predictions toward the designated modality, with performance saturating at moderate values. We therefore use $\alpha=30$ by default. Similarly, Figure 6 shows that increasing the number of top-ranked arbitration heads in a moderate range consistently strengthens modality control, with performance becoming stable around $k=20$ – $30$ . Table 5 further demonstrates that which heads are selected is critical. We cover a few head selection criteria: Random chooses 30 heads from all heads, while layer-matched chooses each head from the same layers as the heads selected by ours. Reversed selects 30 heads by filtering the lowest influence scores and All Heads applies the intervention on all heads. Random, layer-matched random, and reverse-ranked selection all yield substantially weaker control than our identified arbitration heads. Notably, intervening on all heads (over 1000) does not further improve performance, despite using many more heads, confirming that effective steering relies on selectively targeting arbitration heads rather than broadly modifying attention. Finally, removing suppression causes a small performance drop, indicating that augmenting the trusted modality accounts for most of the steering effect, while suppressing the competing modality provides a modest additional improvement.


### 5 Conclusion

We study how multimodal arbitration in MLLMs can be controlled under modality conflicts and introduce a training-free approach for controlling which modality governs model predictions. By identifying a small set of arbitration heads associated with modality arbitration and intervening on their attention, our method can steer answer generation toward a trusted modality. Extensive experiments across multiple MLLMs and modality-conflict benchmarks demonstrate consistent and effective bidirectional modality control. The arbitration heads identified from only a small calibration set generalize effectively across benchmarks. Overall, our findings suggest that modality arbitration can be effectively controlled through targeted intervention on a compact set of identified attention heads.


##### Limitations and Future Work

Our evaluation uses a simplified modality-conflict setup in which the modality to prioritize is externally specified, making routing to unimodal inference a potentially strong baseline. In realistic settings, however, conflicts may be partial, with the correct answer requiring the integration of distinct evidence from multiple modalities. We therefore focus on controlling modality arbitration while retaining both input sources. Benchmarking when both modalities provide useful yet partially conflicting evidence, for which unimodal routing is insufficient, remains an important direction for future work. More discussion is detailed in Appendix E .

### Appendix A Benchmark Information and Examples

We include examples from the benchmarks to illustrate the conflict settings used in our evaluation. For each benchmark, we present one image and text context, along with the question and the corresponding gold answer in the benchmark. We include the following benchmarks: MMCD [ 25 ] , MC 2 [ 37 ] , CLASH [ 27 ] , CorrelationQA [ 10 ] , and PhD (sec and icc subsets) [ 22 ] .

MMCD is a synthetic modality-conflict benchmark built from simple visual objects, such as different shapes and colors. The benchmark contains controlled image–caption conflicts involving five shapes and eight colors, making it useful for conflict arbitration without complex scene understanding. In the example, the image and caption assign different colors to the same shape. There are 5600 examples in the dataset.

MC 2 is a controlled modality-conflict benchmark of 2000 samples designed to measure modality preference in MLLMs. The examples pair image and text evidence that can support different answers, along with a question, an image-supported answer, and text-supported answer. The images in MC 2 come from MS COCO 2014 [ 21 ] . We additionally augmented it with text that yields the image-supported answers, and the details are described in Section B.3.

CLASH is a multimodal conflict benchmark designed to evaluate model behavior when visual and textual evidence contradict each other. The benchmark provides modality-specific answers supported independently by the image and text. CLASH uses images from the MS COCO 2017 [ 21 ] training split and constructs contradictory textual descriptions based on the corresponding captions. Compared to MC 2 , CLASH has shorter text descriptions. We use the held-out test set containing 1289 samples.

CorrelationQA studies visual illusions caused by spurious images that are relevant to the question but inconsistent with the answer supported by the text. The benchmark contains 5540 text-image pairs across multiple categories and is designed to measure whether misleading visual context biases multimodal models away from the correct textual answer. CorrelationQA constructs its visual inputs using a combination of Stable-Diffusion-generated images, typography images, and realistic images retrieved from the Internet.

PhD is a visual hallucination benchmark that evaluates whether MLLMs remain grounded in visual evidence in the presence of potentially misleading textual context. Following prior work [ 37 ] , we evaluate 1000 samples from each of the PhD-sec and PhD-icc subsets, which contain irrelevant and conflicting textual information, respectively. Unlike the modality-conflict benchmarks, we evaluate PhD using image accuracy, measuring how strongly the model preserves or suppresses reliance on visual evidence.

##### Visual Contrastive Decoding (VCD)

VCD [ 15 ] is a training-free decoding method originally proposed for mitigating object hallucination. VCD contrasts the output logit distribution conditioned on the original image with the distribution conditioned on a distorted image, thereby down-weighting tokens that are more likely to arise from language priors or statistical biases rather than from the visual input. This baseline is relevant because many of our examples require the model to remain grounded in the reliable image input. However, we note that VCD is primarily designed to improve visual grounding without identifying which modality or input component is trustworthy under conflicts. It therefore provides a useful comparison for cases where visual grounding is beneficial, but it is not a general method to handle conflicts, especially when text should be trusted.

### Appendix C Computational Efficiency

Experiments were conducted on a machine with NVIDIA driver 580.173.02 and three RTX A5000 GPUs, each with 24 GB and each run used a single GPU. The main software dependencies included Python 3.10.20, PyTorch 2.12.0+cu130, Transformers 5.7.0, CUDA 13.0, and cuDNN 9.2.

We evaluate the computational efficiency of different inference methods using end-to-end latency (s/request), time to first token (TTFT; s), decode latency per output token (s/token), token throughput (tokens/s), peak GPU memory (GiB), and output token count (tokens). End-to-end latency measures the wall-clock time from the beginning of an inference request until the decoded response is returned, including input preprocessing and any method-specific computation. TTFT measures the time from the start of generation to the first generated token, while decode latency measures the subsequent generation time normalized by the number of decoded tokens. Token throughput is computed as the total number of generated tokens divided by the total generation time, including prefill. Peak GPU memory corresponds to the maximum CUDA memory allocated during inference, including both the resident model and temporary inference tensors. Output token count measures the number of tokens generated per request.

We report efficiency results on MC 2 and compute all metrics over unique inference batches to avoid double-counting the image- and text-reference evaluation rows corresponding to the same generation. End-to-end latency (s/request) and TTFT (s) are averaged across requests, decode latency (s/token) is pooled across generated tokens, token throughput (tokens/s) is computed from the total output tokens and generation time, and peak GPU memory (GiB) is taken as the maximum across inference batches. Output tokens are reported as the average number of generated tokens. For our method, we measure the main intervention’s computational efficiency for comparison with other methods, while we note that the adaptive method requires additional unimodal inference for conflict detection. ### Appendix E Detailed Discussion

Our work highlights several limitations and opportunities for future work. First, as we find that heads identified using only 10 samples generalize well across the evaluated benchmarks, it remains unclear whether the same patterns transfer to substantially different domains or real-world tasks. Second, our evaluation uses a simplified modality-conflict setup in which the modality to prioritize is externally specified. In realistic settings, both modalities may provide useful yet partially conflicting evidence, requiring the model to integrate complementary information rather than rely exclusively on one modality. Our current evaluation does not fully capture such cases, and benchmarking our intervention in settings where unimodal routing is insufficient remains an important direction for future work. Automatically assessing modality reliability and identifying which evidence to trust are also open challenges. Following this, the aligned MC 2 evaluation in our work measures whether adaptive steering retains the benefit of multimodal inference when both modalities support the same answer, but it does not test examples that require combining distinct evidence from both modalities. Preservation of useful competing-modality information under partial conflict therefore remains to be evaluated. Finally, we introduce an adaptive steering extension that detects conflicts by comparing image-only and text-only predictions. While this approach demonstrates practical applicability in settings where conflicts are sparse, it introduces additional inference cost for conflict detection. Developing more efficient conflict signals that can be extracted directly from a single multimodal forward pass would be a promising direction for future work.
