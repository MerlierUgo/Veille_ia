## Efficient Streaming Audio-Visual Target Speaker Extraction for Real-World Acoustic Scenes

Streaming audio-visual target speaker extraction (AVTSE) is essential for latency-sensitive applications. However, current causal systems face two coupled gaps. First, standard benchmarks fix the number of speakers at two and ignore the near-/far-field interference, reverberation, and sudden noise of real scenes. Second, high-performance causal architectures are too heavy for edge deployment. Lightweight alternatives try to reclaim capacity by repeatedly invoking a small separator, which offsets the savings. To address the first gap, we release RealSSA, a realistic AVTSE benchmark consisting of RealSSA-Sim and RealSSA-Real. RealSSA-Sim provides controllable simulated scenes with 1–5 dynamic near-field interfering speakers; scenes with far-field interference additionally include 10–15 far-field interfering speakers. RealSSA-Real provides a held-out real-recorded evaluation set. To address the second gap, we propose Falcon, a causal AVTSE method. Its Separator performs multi-scale time-frequency separation in a single encoder–decoder pass. We train Falcon with an encoder-space speaker contrastive loss. This loss suppresses near-field leakage at zero inference cost and transfers across backbones. Falcon achieves state-of-the-art extraction quality on RealSSA-Sim, LRS2-2Mix, LRS3-2Mix, and VoxCeleb2-2Mix. Compared with the causal AV-TFGridNet baseline, it cuts parameters by 91.9%, computation by 20.2 $\times$ , and GPU inference latency by 11.1 $\times$ . Code and demo are available at https://cslikai.cn/Falcon/ .


### 1 Introduction

Audio-visual target speaker extraction (AVTSE) leverages a speaker’s visual stream, typically lip movements, as enrollment cues to separate the target speaker’s voice from a multi-speaker mixture [ 1 , 2 ] . Although this setting has often been described in prior work as audio-visual speech separation [ 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 ] , these methods all condition on the visual stream of a single target speaker, which by definition is target speaker extraction; therefore, we use the term AVTSE throughout this paper. Many latency-sensitive applications now demand causal AVTSE, such as video conferencing [ 7 ] and hearing-assistive devices [ 8 ] . However, the current causal AVTSE methods fall short along two axes: the data used to train and evaluate causal models, and the architecture of the causal model itself.

Existing AVTSE benchmarks do not reflect the acoustic conditions of deployment. Some benchmarks built on LRS2 [ 9 ] , LRS3 [ 10 ] , and VoxCeleb2 [ 11 ] fix the number of speakers at two, draw mixtures at controlled SNRs, and hold room acoustics and background noise constant, leaving out the near-/far-field geometry, reverberation, and sudden noise events that characterize everyday recordings (Figure 1 ) [ 12 , 13 ] . None of them are paired with a real-recorded evaluation set, and reported gains offer little evidence that a causal model will hold up once deployed. Therefore, the AVTSE task requires a benchmark that can simulate simultaneous changes in the number of speakers, spatial geometry, and noise, while also providing real recordings to evaluate real-world deployment performance.

On the model side, current causal AVTSE faces an efficiency gap. Causal variants of strong non-causal architectures preserve the extraction quality. Some methods include AV-TFGridNet [ 14 ] , AV-Mossformer2 [ 15 ] , and RTFSNet [ 16 ] , obtained under the protocol of SwiftNet [ 17 ] . However, their parameter counts and per-frame FLOPs push inference latency beyond real-time budgets on edge hardware. Lightweight alternatives such as SwiftNet [ 17 ] recover capacity by repeatedly invoking a small separator after audio-visual fusion. Yet each additional cycle multiplies per-frame computation and erodes the latency savings of the smaller module. On the training side, SI-SNR penalizes deviation from the clean target [ 18 ] , but does not explicitly push the estimate away from near-field speakers. This leaves residual leakage from spatially close talkers, and the leakage grows with the number of simultaneously active near-field speakers in realistic scenes [ 1 , 19 ] .

To close this data gap, we release RealSSA , a realistic AVTSE benchmark consisting of RealSSA-Sim and RealSSA-Real. RealSSA-Sim provides controllable simulated scenes with 1–5 dynamic near-field interfering speakers across four scene types; scenes with far-field interference additionally include 10–15 far-field interfering speakers. RealSSA-Real provides a held-out real-recorded evaluation set. We jointly sample room reverberation, ambient noise, and sudden noise events, so that the number of interfering speakers, spatial geometry, and environmental acoustics vary together rather than one axis at a time. RealSSA-Real further allows causal models trained on simulation to be evaluated on deployment-side distributions without adaptation.

To close the model-side gap, we propose Falcon , a single-pass causal AVTSE method. At its core, the Separator applies an encoder–decoder trunk to the audio-visual fused feature. In a single decoder traversal, it performs frequency-axis, time-axis, and joint time-frequency modeling, assigned to different decoder levels from coarse to fine scales. We further introduce an encoder-space speaker contrastive loss , computed in the separator’s STFT encoder space against the clean target and each near-field interferer. This loss suppresses residual leakage at zero inference cost and transfers across AVTSE backbones. On RealSSA-Sim, LRS2-2Mix [ 9 ] , LRS3-2Mix [ 10 ] , and VoxCeleb2-2Mix [ 11 ] , Falcon matched or exceeded the strongest causal baselines [ 14 , 15 , 17 , 20 ] in separation quality (Table 3 ). On RealSSA-Real, it achieved competitive perceptual quality, WER, and target-speaker similarity. Meanwhile, it reduced parameters, computational cost, and GPU inference latency by 91.9%, 20.2 $\times$ , and 11.1 $\times$ relative to the causal AV-TFGridNet [ 14 ] .


### 2 Related Works


#### 2.1 AVTSE Benchmarks

AVTSE benchmarks can be organized along three axes: speaker configuration, acoustic conditions, and evaluation realism (Table 1 ). Along the first axis, LRS2-2Mix [ 9 ] , LRS3-2Mix [ 10 ] , and VoxCeleb2-2Mix [ 11 ] fix two near-field speakers per mixture, and therefore do not expose models to varying counts of near- and far-field interferers. These three corpora are used by most AVTSE models [ 4 , 6 , 5 , 16 , 20 ] and their causal variants [ 17 ] . Along the second axis, these corpora inherit acoustic conditions from the source videos, and offer no controlled reverberation or sudden noise events. The LRS3+WHAM! noisy protocol, reused by [ 21 , 22 , 23 ] , adds WHAM! [ 12 ] background noise at SNR roughly in $[-6,+3]$ dB, but still keeps two speakers, anechoic mixing, and no sudden noise. The NTCD-TIMIT-based 2-speaker+noise protocol [ 21 , 24 ] likewise fixes two speakers, and varies only the noise SNR in $[-5,20]$ dB. Along the third axis, evaluation relies on held-out splits of the same source videos rather than independent real recordings. The gap between simulated mixtures and true on-device capture therefore remains undiagnosed. As summarized in Table 1 , RealSSA is the only AVTSE benchmark that satisfies all three axes jointly.

#### 2.2 Causal Audio-Visual Target Speaker Extraction

Causal AVTSE methods mainly differ in two aspects: separator architecture and training objective. On the architecture side, causal evaluation largely follows the protocol of SwiftNet [ 17 ] , which provides a recipe for converting non-causal AVTSE backbones into their causal counterparts by restricting temporal operations to past and current frames; earlier time-domain separators [ 25 , 26 , 27 , 6 ] and recent time-frequency separators are causalized under this protocol. Among the time-frequency family, AV-TFGridNet [ 14 ] and AV-Mossformer2 [ 15 ] retain strong extraction quality at the cost of large parameter counts and heavy per-frame computation, while SwiftNet and the causalized RTFSNet [ 16 ] and AVLiT [ 21 ] variants reuse a single compact block across multiple iterative cycles, trading parameters for per-frame FLOPs. Falcon instead employs level-specific time-frequency blocks within a single encoder–decoder forward pass.

On the training side, AVTSE systems were typically optimized with waveform reconstruction losses such as SI-SNR [ 18 ] , and prior attempts to suppress residual interferers either adjust the training mixture distribution or add routing-level gates over the separator output [ 19 , 14 ] rather than explicitly separating the estimate from each interfering speaker. Falcon instead adds an encoder-space speaker contrastive loss, discarded at inference time.


### 3 The RealSSA Dataset

RealSSA consists of two parts: RealSSA-Sim, a controllable simulated-scene suite used for training, validation, and testing, and RealSSA-Real, a held-out real-recorded evaluation set used to assess generalization under non-simulated acoustic conditions. RealSSA-Sim provides systematic control over speaker configuration, source distance, reverberation, environmental noise, and sudden noise events, while RealSSA-Real reflects raw on-device recordings in everyday acoustic environments.

RealSSA-Sim: Simulated Scene Suite. RealSSA-Sim defines four acoustic scene types that span a wide range of room sizes, reverberation levels, and noise conditions (Table 2 ). Small meeting rooms have short RT60 and only near-field interferers, representing close-range conversations. Medium indoor spaces introduce far-field speakers and moderate reverberation. Large public spaces further increase RT60 and the number of interferers, reflecting scenarios such as lecture halls. Outdoor environments have minimal reverberation but high ambient noise levels. Across the four scenes, the number of near-field interfering speakers varies from 1 to 5; scenes with far-field interference additionally include 10–15 far-field interfering speakers. Each scene independently samples RT60, near-/far-field geometry, environmental noise, and sudden noise events. We classify an interferer as near-field when it lies within $3\,\mathrm{m}$ of the microphone and as far-field otherwise; this threshold approximates the critical distance in typical indoor rooms, beyond which reverberant energy dominates the direct path [ 28 ] . Per-scene spatial layouts are illustrated in Figure 5 (Appendix A.1 ).

Target audio-visual data are sampled from LRS3 [ 10 ] , while near-field and far-field interfering speakers come from LibriSpeech [ 29 ] . Non-speech noise is drawn from WHAM! [ 12 ] for continuous backgrounds and Hive [ 30 ] for sudden noise events. The generation pipeline proceeds in three stages. (1) Room simulation. For each sample, we randomly select a scene type and sample the room geometry and RT60 from the corresponding ranges in Table 2 . Room impulse responses are computed with gpuRIR [ 31 ] . (2) Source placement. The target speaker is placed at a random position within the near-field range of the selected scene. Interfering speakers are placed according to the scene-specific near-field and far-field distance ranges. (3) Mixing. The target speech is normalized to $-23$ LUFS following the EBU R128 broadcast loudness standard [ 32 ] . Interfering speakers and environmental noise are added at scene-specific SIR ranges, and the final mixture is peak-limited to $-1\,\mathrm{dBFS}$ . All audio is single-channel at $16\,\mathrm{kHz}$ . RealSSA-Sim contains $100{,}826$ training, $9{,}133$ validation, and $9{,}169$ test samples, totaling approximately $153$ hours. The four scene types are uniformly sampled, with an average of $2.5$ near-field interfering speakers per sample. Source corpora and scene-design rationale are detailed in Appendix A.1 , the full mixing pipeline in Appendix A.2 , and per-split distribution statistics in Appendix A.3 . We evaluated the generalization from RealSSA-Sim training to RealSSA-Real in Section 6.3 .

RealSSA-Real: Real-World Evaluation Set. In addition to the simulated suite, we collect a real-recorded evaluation set, denoted as RealSSA-Real, to assess generalization under non-simulated acoustic conditions. RealSSA-Real covers everyday indoor, public, and outdoor environments with natural background noise, room reverberation, and uncontrolled interfering speech. It is recorded with a single mobile device and preserves the raw acoustic conditions of real on-device capture. The recordings are post-processed only to match the input format used by the AVTSE pipeline, including audio resampling to 16 kHz, video conversion to 25 fps, and extraction of 96 $\times$ 96 grayscale mouth regions. RealSSA-Real is used exclusively for evaluation and is never used for training or validation. We assess perceptual quality with DNSMOS and NISQA, speech recognizability with WER, and target-speaker consistency with speaker similarity. Detailed recording protocol, environment composition, and privacy processing are provided in Appendix A.4 .


### 4 Methods


#### 4.1 Overall Pipeline

> Figure 2 : Overall pipeline of Falcon for audio-visual target speaker extraction.

Given a mixture waveform $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}$ of length $L$ and a mouth video $\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times T_{\text{v}}}$ of the target speaker with spatial resolution $H\times W$ and $T_{\text{v}}$ frames, Falcon aims to extract the target speech $\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}$ as captured at the microphone. The mixture is modeled as $\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{s}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{b}_{k}+\mathbf{f}+\mathbf{n},$ where $\mathbf{s},\mathbf{b}_{k},\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}$ denote the target speech, the $k$ -th near-field interfering speech signal, and the sum of far-field interfering speech signals, respectively, all captured at the microphone, including room reverberation. The term $\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}$ denotes additive environmental noise, and $\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{0}$ for scenes without far-field interferers.

As illustrated in Figure 2 , the FRCNN visual backbone used in CTCNet [ 6 ] , frozen during training, extracts mouth features from $\mathbf{m}$ and maps them through a visual bottleneck to obtain $\mathbf{V}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{v}}\times T_{\text{v}}}$ , where $C_{\text{v}}$ is the visual feature dimension. The audio encoder $E_{\text{a}}:\mathbb{R}^{L}\to\mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{e}}\times T\times N_{\text{f}}}$ applies STFT to its input, concatenates the real part, imaginary part, and magnitude spectrum as a three-channel tensor, and processes them through a 2D convolutional layer; we write $\mathbf{A}_{0}=E_{\text{a}}(\mathbf{x})$ , where $T$ and $N_{\text{f}}$ denote the number of STFT frames and frequency bins, and $C_{\text{e}}$ is the audio encoder output dimension. An audio bottleneck then projects $\mathbf{A}_{0}$ to $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times T\times N_{\text{f}}}$ .

We refine each modality before fusion. The audio feature $\mathbf{A}$ is processed by an AudioBlock $\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{a}}$ , whose structure is the same as the separator detailed in Section 4.2 , yielding $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}=\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{a}}(\mathbf{A})\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times T\times N_{\mathrm{f}}}$ . The visual feature $\mathbf{V}_{0}$ is processed by the VideoBlock $\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{v}}$ of SwiftNet [ 17 ] , which aggregates temporal context with a causal LSTM and upsamples to the audio frame rate, yielding $\tilde{\mathbf{V}}=\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{v}}(\mathbf{V}_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times T}$ . $\tilde{\mathbf{V}}$ is expanded along the frequency axis and added to $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$ , producing $\mathbf{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times T\times N_{\mathrm{f}}}$ . Then, the fused feature $\mathbf{F}$ is processed by the separator $\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{a}}$ to obtain the target-speaker feature $\mathbf{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times T\times N_{\mathrm{f}}}$ . Each application of $\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{a}}$ completes multi-scale time-frequency modeling in one encoder–decoder forward pass and is not unrolled along the time axis. Then, the mask generator predicts a time-frequency mask $\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{e}}\times T\times N_{\text{f}}}$ from $\mathbf{E}$ . We split the channel dimensions of $\mathbf{M}$ and $\mathbf{A}_{0}$ evenly into real and imaginary sub-bands, denoted $\mathbf{M}=[\mathbf{M}_{\text{r}};\mathbf{M}_{\text{i}}]$ and $\mathbf{A}_{0}=[\mathbf{A}_{0,\text{r}};\mathbf{A}_{0,\text{i}}]$ , and obtain the masked embedding by complex multiplication,

[Tableau] $$\hat{\mathbf{A}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{M}_{\text{r}}\odot\mathbf{A}_{0,\text{r}}-\mathbf{M}_{\text{i}}\odot\mathbf{A}_{0,\text{i}}\\ \mathbf{M}_{\text{r}}\odot\mathbf{A}_{0,\text{i}}+\mathbf{M}_{\text{i}}\odot\mathbf{A}_{0,\text{r}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{e}}\times T\times N_{\text{f}}},$$ (1)

where $\odot$ is element-wise multiplication. The audio decoder finally maps $\hat{\mathbf{A}}$ back to real and imaginary spectral components and reconstructs the target speech $\hat{\mathbf{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}$ via iSTFT.

Separator plays a central role in Falcon, since it is responsible for extracting the $\mathbf{F}$ into the target speaker features $\mathbf{E}$ . We next introduce our single-pass multi-scale design for Separator.


#### 4.2 Single-Pass Multi-Scale Separator

> Figure 3 : Single-Pass Multi-Scale Separator. $\phi$ denotes the upsampling operation.

The Separator is an asymmetric U-Net (Figure 3 ): time-frequency modeling is concentrated at the encoder bottleneck to form a single coarse scene context, while a level-specific TF block at every decoder resolution recovers fine spectral structure step by step. A single encoder–decoder pass therefore already yields four distinct TF scales, replacing the multi-iteration recurrence of SwiftNet [ 17 ] .

The input feature $\mathbf{F}$ first passes through a depthwise $1\times 1$ convolution with PReLU to form a residual branch $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times T\times N_{\text{f}}}$ , which serves both as the entry to the hidden path below and as a global skip to the separator output. A pointwise $1\times 1$ convolution with PReLU then projects $\mathbf{R}$ to the hidden channel dimension $C_{\text{h}}$ , and a streaming-causal layer normalization $\operatorname{LN}_{\text{c}}$ produces $\mathbf{X}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{h}}\times T\times N_{\text{f}}}$ . For any input $\mathbf{Z}$ , $\operatorname{LN}_{\text{c}}$ is defined per time-frequency position as

[Tableau] $$[\operatorname{LN}_{\text{c}}(\mathbf{Z})]_{c,t,f}=\gamma_{c}\cdot\frac{Z_{c,t,f}-\mu_{t,f}}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}_{t,f}+\epsilon}}+\beta_{c},\qquad\mu_{t,f}=\tfrac{1}{C_{\text{h}}}\!\sum_{c}Z_{c,t,f},$$ (2)

with $\sigma^{2}_{t,f}$ defined analogously, $\gamma,\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{h}}}$ learnable, and a small $\epsilon$ for stability. $\operatorname{LN}_{\text{c}}$ is frame-local and cache-free, and is reused throughout the blocks below.

> Figure 4 : Time-Frequency Refinement Block.

Encoder. The encoder has $L{=}4$ levels. $\mathcal{D}_{0}$ is a stride- $1$ depthwise $3{\times}3$ convolution; $\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathcal{D}_{2},\mathcal{D}_{3}$ are stride- $2$ depthwise $5{\times}5$ convolutions that halve both axes. All convolutions use left-aligned padding along time and symmetric padding along frequency, adding no temporal lookahead. Starting from $\mathbf{S}_{0}=\mathcal{D}_{0}(\mathbf{X}_{0})$ , subsequent levels are produced in order $l=1,2,3$ by $\mathbf{S}_{l}=\mathcal{D}_{l}(\mathbf{S}_{l-1})$ , giving

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{S}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{h}}\times\frac{T}{2^{l}}\times\frac{N_{\text{f}}}{2^{l}}},\quad l=0,\dots,3.$$ (3)

Each $\mathbf{S}_{l}$ is kept as a skip. Time-frequency modeling is applied only at the bottleneck,

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{G}=\mathcal{R}_{3}(\mathbf{S}_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{h}}\times\frac{T}{8}\times\frac{N_{\text{f}}}{8}},$$ (4)

where $\mathcal{R}_{l}(\cdot)$ is the Time-Frequency Refinement Block (TFRB) at level $l$ (Figure 4 ); we identify $\mathbf{S}_{3}\equiv\mathbf{G}$ in what follows.

Each TFRB is a cascade of three stages applied in order: first a bidirectional LSTM along frequency $\mathcal{F}_{l}^{\text{rnn}}$ , then a unidirectional LSTM along time $\mathcal{T}_{l}$ , and finally a 2D self-attention $\mathcal{A}_{l}^{\text{2D}}$ ,

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{R}_{l}(\mathbf{X})=\mathcal{A}_{l}^{\text{2D}}\left(\mathcal{T}_{l}\left(\mathcal{F}_{l}^{\text{rnn}}(\mathbf{X})\right)\right),$$ (5)

where $\mathcal{A}_{l}^{\text{2D}}$ operates on the flattened time-frequency plane with a block-causal mask: a query at $(t,f)$ attends to every key $(t^{\prime},f^{\prime})$ with $t^{\prime}\leq t$ . Each stage is wrapped in a pre-norm residual block with $\operatorname{LN}_{\text{c}}$ .

Global context injection (GCI) module. For $l=0,\dots,3$ , we inject $\mathbf{G}$ into each skip via

[Tableau] $$\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{l}=\mathcal{I}_{l}(\mathbf{S}_{l},\mathbf{G})=\phi_{\text{loc}}(\mathbf{S}_{l})\odot\sigma\!\left(\phi_{\text{gate}}(\mathbf{G})\right)+\phi_{\text{glb}}(\mathbf{G}),$$ (6)

where $\mathbf{G}$ is first upsampled to the resolution of $\mathbf{S}_{l}$ by nearest-neighbor interpolation when needed, and $\phi_{\text{loc}},\phi_{\text{glb}},\phi_{\text{gate}}$ are depthwise convolutions with $\operatorname{LN}_{\text{c}}$ . The gate $\sigma(\phi_{\text{gate}}(\mathbf{G}))$ lets the global context emphasize or suppress frequency bands of the local skip.

Decoder. The decoder restores resolution coarse-to-fine: for $l=2,1,0$ in that order,

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{Y}_{l}=\mathcal{R}_{l}\left(\mathcal{I}^{\text{d}}_{l}(\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{l},\mathbf{Y}_{l+1})+\mathbf{S}_{l}\right),$$ (7)

with $\mathbf{Y}_{3}\equiv\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{3}$ and $\mathcal{I}^{\text{d}}_{l}(\cdot)$ the decoder-side GCI module whose coarser argument is first upsampled. The $+\mathbf{S}_{l}$ term is an identity residual that preserves gradient flow and a raw spectral reference when the gate in $\mathcal{I}^{\text{d}}_{l}$ collapses $\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{l}$ toward zero. Since each $\mathcal{R}_{l}$ operates at a different resolution, the four TFRBs span four TF scales in one forward pass.

The decoder output $\mathbf{Y}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{h}}\times T\times N_{\text{f}}}$ is projected to $C$ channels and added to the residual branch, $\mathbf{E}=\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Y}_{0})+\mathbf{R},$ where $\mathcal{O}(\cdot)$ is a pointwise projection.


#### 4.3 Encoder-Space Speaker Contrastive Loss

Standard reconstruction losses such as SI-SNR minimize the distance between the estimate and the clean target but do not explicitly push the estimate away from near-field interferers. We add a contrastive loss that reuses the audio encoder $E_{\text{a}}$ for both the query and the references, so no separate encoder is introduced.

The query is the estimated target in encoder space, $\hat{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{e}}\times T\times N_{\text{f}}}$ , obtained from the complex masking of Section 4.1 . The positive and negative references are the clean target $\mathbf{s}$ and each near-field interferer $\mathbf{b}_{k}$ , both captured at the microphone and therefore carrying the same room response as the mixture, passed through $E_{\text{a}}$ with stop-gradient on the reference path:

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{A}^{+}=\operatorname{sg}(E_{\text{a}}(\mathbf{s})),\qquad\mathbf{A}^{-}_{k}=\operatorname{sg}(E_{\text{a}}(\mathbf{b}_{k})),\quad k=1,\dots,K.$$ (8)

Gradients flow into $E_{\text{a}}$ only through the query $\hat{\mathbf{A}}$ , not through the references. Far-field interferers are excluded from the negative set: both distance attenuation and, in reverberant scenes, late reverberation drive their residual below the SI-SNR error floor, so adding them as negatives yields no additional signal. A lightweight speaker pooling function $\mathcal{P}_{\text{spk}}(\cdot)$ then maps each encoder-space feature to a unit-norm speaker embedding by averaging over frequency, adaptively pooling over time, and projecting it to a fixed-dimensional vector:

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{q}=\mathcal{P}_{\text{spk}}(\hat{\mathbf{A}}),\qquad\mathbf{p}=\mathcal{P}_{\text{spk}}(\mathbf{A}^{+}),\qquad\mathbf{e}_{k}^{-}=\mathcal{P}_{\text{spk}}(\mathbf{A}^{-}_{k}).$$ (9)

Writing $s_{k}\!\triangleq\!\cos(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{e}_{k}^{-})$ , the contrastive loss combines a positive attraction term with a softmax-weighted, margin-based repulsion over negatives:

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{spk}}=\bigl(1-\cos(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})\bigr)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\exp(\tau\,s_{k})}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp(\tau\,s_{j})}\,\operatorname{ReLU}(s_{k}-m),$$ (10)

where $m{=}0.5$ (a $60^{\circ}$ angular margin) deactivates the repulsion once $\mathbf{q}$ is sufficiently far from $\mathbf{e}_{k}^{-}$ , leaving SI-SNR to handle the remaining residual. The softmax weight acts via an inverse temperature $\tau$ : $\tau{=}0$ gives uniform weighting across negatives, and larger $\tau$ sharpens the distribution toward the hardest negative. We linearly anneal $\tau$ from $0$ to $1$ during training.

The final training objective is $\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}+\lambda_{\text{spk}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{spk}}$ with $\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}=-\operatorname{SI\text{-}SNR}(\hat{\mathbf{s}},\mathbf{s})$ [ 18 ] and $\lambda_{\text{spk}}=1.0$ . Since query and reference signals share the same audio encoder, this loss can be readily attached to any audio encoder AVTSE backbone with no additional architectural changes required.


### 5 Experiment Configurations


#### 5.1 Dataset Setup

We evaluated on three public AVTSE benchmarks (LRS2 [ 9 ] , LRS3 [ 10 ] , VoxCeleb2 [ 11 ] ), RealSSA-Sim (Section 3 ) and RealSSA-Real (Appendix A.4 ). Mixtures on the three public benchmarks followed the protocol of prior works [ 6 , 17 ] . All clips were $16\,\mathrm{kHz}$ audio paired with $96\times 96$ grayscale mouth crops at $25$ fps, and dataset splits followed the official partitions of each corpus. RealSSA-Real was used exclusively for evaluation. Further preprocessing details are given in Appendix B .


#### 5.2 Model Configurations and Evaluation Metrics

The separator hidden dimension $C_{\text{h}}$ was set to $64$ , and the full width assignment across $C_{\text{e}}$ , $C$ , $C_{\text{v}}$ , and $\dim(\mathbf{q})$ is tabulated in Appendix C . The audio encoder $E_{\text{a}}$ used an STFT with window size $256$ and hop $128$ ( $8$ ms hop at $16\,\mathrm{kHz}$ ), and fed the real, imaginary, and magnitude components as a three-channel input, following Section 4.1 . For all models trained in this work, we used AdamW [ 33 ] (learning rate $1\times 10^{-3}$ , weight decay $0.01$ ), with the learning rate halved whenever the validation loss plateaued for $15$ consecutive epochs and early stopping applied after a patience of $30$ epochs. Full training hyperparameters are provided in Appendix D . We evaluated separation quality with SI-SNRi and SDRi [ 34 ] on the simulated benchmarks, and non-intrusive perceptual quality on RealSSA-Real with DNSMOS [ 35 ] and NISQA [ 36 ] . On RealSSA-Sim and RealSSA-Real, we additionally measured word error rate (WER) with Mega-ASR [ 37 ] and target-speaker similarity with a pretrained ResNet34 speaker encoder from WeSpeaker [ 38 ] . We also reported the number of parameters, MACs, CPU and GPU inference latency, and memory usage to assess computational efficiency, all measured on a $2$ s, $16\,\mathrm{kHz}$ input clip paired with $50$ mouth-crop frames at $25$ fps. Metric definitions and the inference-latency measurement protocol are detailed in Appendix E .

### 7 Conclusions

RealSSA combines RealSSA-Sim and RealSSA-Real to benchmark AVTSE under variable speaker counts, near-/far-field geometry, reverberation, and sudden noise, with held-out real recordings for evaluating generalization. Falcon performs multi-scale time-frequency separation in a single encoder–decoder pass and uses an encoder-space speaker contrastive loss that suppresses near-field leakage, transfers across backbones, and adds no inference cost. Across RealSSA-Sim, RealSSA-Real, LRS2-2Mix, LRS3-2Mix, and VoxCeleb2-2Mix, Falcon matches or surpasses strong causal baselines. Relative to causal AV-TFGridNet, it reduces parameters by 91.9%, computation by 20.2 $\times$ , and GPU inference latency by 11.1 $\times$ , supporting progress toward low-latency on-device AVTSE.

Limitations. Both the separator and the encoder-space speaker contrastive loss in Falcon operate on a single channel, and we leave the four-element array simulated in Appendix A.2 to future multi-channel extensions. RealSSA-Real covers approximately $84$ minutes over $10$ participants and eight environments, which is sufficient to reveal cross-architecture trends and can be scaled up in follow-up releases. The contrastive loss currently relies on clean per-source waveforms of near-field interferers available in simulation, and adapting it to unlabeled real recordings is a natural direction for future work.

RealSSA-Sim is built from four publicly available corpora that play complementary roles in the mixture (Table 10 ). ##### Scene design rationale.

The four scene types in Table 2 are not arbitrary. They are chosen to cover an acoustic gradient that separators must traverse in deployment, with three controlled axes: room volume, reverberation time, and the presence of far-field interferers. Small meeting rooms have the shortest RT60 (0.3–0.6 s), match the scale of two-person to small-group offices, and contain only near-field interferers because no point in the room is beyond the 3 m near-/far-field threshold; this makes them the “clean” end of the gradient. Medium indoor spaces (e.g., kitchens, living rooms) introduce moderate reverberation (0.5–0.9 s) together with the first far-field talkers, since the room becomes large enough for some speakers to fall outside the critical-distance neighborhood of the microphone [ 28 ] . Large public spaces such as cafeterias and lecture halls extend RT60 to 1.0–2.0 s, which is the regime in which reverberant energy dominates the direct path and in which far-field speech becomes the harder interferer to suppress. Outdoor environments invert the indoor pattern: very low RT60 (0.05–0.1 s) but high environmental SIR difficulty, so that the stress test moves from reverberation to wide-band ambient noise. Together the four scenes span the regime in which residential and on-the-go AVTSE systems are deployed, and the RT60 ranges fall within the values reported for occupied rooms of comparable size in standard architectural-acoustics references [ 28 ] .

#### A.2 Generation Pipeline


##### Spatial simulation.

Room impulse responses (RIRs) are computed with gpuRIR [ 31 ] using the image-source method. For every sample, we draw a room geometry and an RT60 target from the ranges in Table 11 , then convert the RT60 target into image-source attenuation parameters using Sabine’s estimator at the gpuRIR cut-offs of $15\,\mathrm{dB}$ and $60\,\mathrm{dB}$ , which controls when image sources stop contributing direct and reverberant energy. The microphone is modelled as a four-element linear array with $2.8\,\mathrm{cm}$ spacing whose array centre is placed at a height of $1.2$ – $1.8\,\mathrm{m}$ above the floor; this geometry mirrors the form factor of speakerphones and laptop microphones used in conferencing devices. Although the array is generated to enable future multi-channel extensions, all experiments in the main paper use the first-channel signal only, so RealSSA in its current form is a single-channel benchmark and the released waveforms are the channel-1 streams of the simulated array. Speaker heights are sampled from $1.0$ – $1.9\,\mathrm{m}$ to span the range of seated and standing posture.


##### Outdoor simulation.

Outdoor scenes are approximated by a $50\times 50\times 20\,\mathrm{m}$ enclosure with wall absorption coefficient $0.99$ , which yields RT60 in $0.05$ – $0.1\,\mathrm{s}$ and produces an essentially anechoic propagation profile in which the direct path dominates. We adopt this approximation because gpuRIR’s image-source model is constructed for closed rooms and does not natively model open-field propagation. The simplification preserves the inverse-distance amplitude law and the geometric arrival times of all sources, which are the dominant cues that an AVTSE model uses, and removes only late reverberation, which is in any case negligible in true outdoor scenes. We make this assumption explicit so that downstream users of RealSSA can interpret outdoor results accordingly.


##### Mixing pipeline.

Algorithm 1 formalizes the per-sample mixing procedure. The target speech is first convolved with the target RIR and loudness-normalized to $-23\,\mathrm{LUFS}$ following the EBU R128 broadcast loudness recommendation [ 32 ] , which is the standard reference loudness for speech-centric content and removes the residual loudness variability of LRS3. Near-field interferers are added at the per-scene Near SIR ranges in Table 11 . For medium-indoor, large-public, and outdoor scenes, far-field interferers are scaled to an applied SIR of $20$ – $35\,\mathrm{dB}$ , as listed in the Applied Far SIR column. Small-meeting scenes contain no far-field interferers. Continuous environmental noise is the sum of one WHAM! stream and one Hive ambient stream; when both are present we attenuate each by $3\,\mathrm{dB}$ before summation so that the total noise SIR matches the per-scene environmental-noise SIR specification rather than doubling. Sudden-noise events are inserted at uniformly-sampled non-overlapping positions according to the per-scene event count, with each event scaled to the same SIR distribution as the environmental noise. The final mixture is peak-limited at $-1\,\mathrm{dBFS}$ to prevent digital clipping, and per-source gains are clipped to the range $[-40,+40]\,\mathrm{dB}$ to suppress the rare numerical instability that can arise when an exceptionally quiet source is paired with a high target SIR.

##### Reproducibility.

All randomness is controlled by a fixed master seed of $42$ , with disjoint seed offsets for the training, validation, and test partitions so that re-running the pipeline regenerates the released splits bit-for-bit. The released metadata records the sampled room geometry, RT60, per-source positions, RIR hashes, SIR values, and Hive label identifiers for each sample. The pipeline depends on gpuRIR , pyloudnorm , torchaudio , and soundfile ; on a single NVIDIA RTX 3090 the full $100{,}826$ -sample training set takes approximately 48h, GPU-hours to generate, which is dominated by the gpuRIR convolution stage. We will release the generation scripts and the metadata together with the dataset.

#### A.4 RealSSA-Real: Real-World Evaluation Set


##### Recording protocol.

RealSSA-Real complements the simulated suite and is collected separately, so that no participant or location is reused between the two. Each session is captured by a single Apple iPhone 17 Pro, whose rear camera and built-in microphones acquire video and audio in one device pass at $60$ fps and $48\,\mathrm{kHz}$ , respectively. The audio is then downsampled to $16\,\mathrm{kHz}$ and the video to $25$ fps to match the AVTSE pipeline used during training. The iPhone is placed on a table at conversational height in indoor scenes and held at chest level in outdoor scenes, which keeps the device-to-source geometry within the range used in the simulated suite of Appendix A.2 . We deliberately do not denoise, dereverberate, or volume-normalize the recordings, so that the evaluation reflects the raw on-device signal that a deployed AVTSE system would receive.


##### Scenes and contents.

The set contains $54$ sessions totalling $5{,}035$ s (approximately $84$ minutes) of overlapping speech across eight environments that span quiet indoor rooms, crowded public venues, and open streets (Figure 7 ): a study room, a classroom, an internet cafe, a billiard hall, a shopping mall, a supermarket, and two street scenes captured in daytime and at night. Each environment carries its own non-stationary noise signature: mall and supermarket sessions combine crowd chatter with venue ambient noise, including noise from nearby diners in the mall and from passing shoppers in the supermarket; billiard-hall sessions add intermittent ball-collision impacts together with background music playing in the venue; internet-cafe sessions contain venue ambient noise layered with crowd chatter; the two street scenes contain vehicular traffic, pedestrian chatter, and occasional music from nearby storefronts; classroom sessions add desk-tapping and footstep events; study-room sessions add coughing and door-opening events. ### Appendix E Evaluation Metrics and Inference Latency Protocol


##### Target speech extraction quality.

On the simulated benchmarks (LRS2-2Mix, LRS3-2Mix, VoxCeleb2-2Mix, and RealSSA-Sim), clean references are available and we reported scale-invariant signal-to-noise ratio improvement (SI-SNRi) and signal-to-distortion ratio improvement (SDRi) [ 34 ] , both in dB, relative to the unprocessed mixture.


##### Perceptual quality on real recordings.

We assessed the perceptual quality of extracted speech on RealSSA-Real with DNSMOS [ 35 ] and NISQA [ 36 ] . These non-intrusive predictors estimate perceived speech quality directly from the extracted waveform, complementing the speech recognizability and target-speaker consistency measured by WER and speaker similarity, respectively.


##### Speech recognition.

We used Mega-ASR [ 37 ] to transcribe the extracted speech and compute word error rate (WER) against the target transcript. WER is the number of substituted, deleted, and inserted words divided by the number of reference words, reported as a percentage. Evaluation covered all $9{,}169$ RealSSA-Sim test samples and all $54$ RealSSA-Real recordings.


##### Target-speaker similarity.

We extracted speaker embeddings using the ResNet34-LM checkpoint released by WeSpeaker [ 38 ] 2 2 Official checkpoint: https://huggingface.co/Wespeaker/wespeaker-voxceleb-resnet34-LM . . This model was trained on VoxCeleb2 and further fine-tuned with a large-margin objective. For each test sample, we computed the cosine similarity between the extracted speech and an independent enrollment utterance from the same target speaker. The enrollment utterance differed from the test utterance and was used only for evaluation. We reported the mean cosine similarity over each test set, multiplied by $100$ . This evaluation encoder is separate from Falcon’s audio encoder.


##### Ground-truth reference scores.

Evaluating the ground-truth target speech on RealSSA-Sim yielded $11.83\%$ WER and $64.69$ speaker similarity under the same protocol. Since similarity was measured against an independent utterance from the same speaker, the ground-truth score provided an empirical reference for interpreting the absolute values. Falcon achieved $43.05$ , leaving a gap of $21.64$ points to this reference. RealSSA-Real has no isolated target waveforms, so corresponding ground-truth scores are unavailable.


##### Computational efficiency.

We reported trainable parameters, multiply-accumulate operations (MACs), CPU and GPU forward-pass times, and memory usage. Each measurement used a $2$ s, $16$ kHz audio clip paired with $50$ mouth-crop frames at $25$ fps and a batch size of one. The hardware was an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU and an Intel Xeon Gold 6226R CPU. CPU measurements used one execution thread. We discarded five warmup runs and reported the median of the following $50$ forward passes. GPU time was measured with CUDA events, excluding host-side overhead. Memory measurements reported system RAM during CPU inference and peak GPU memory during GPU inference under the same protocol. Falcon required $51.3$ ms on the GPU and $1361.2$ ms on the CPU for the $2$ s input.


##### Lookahead and streaming schedule.

At $25$ fps, the visual stream buffers three future frames, corresponding to $120$ ms of lookahead. The audio STFT uses a $256$ -sample window and a $128$ -sample hop, corresponding to $16$ ms and $8$ ms at $16$ kHz. The audio and visual timelines are aligned, the output advances at the $8$ ms hop, and the configuration uses no additional future-chunk buffer.

Dividing the measured forward-pass times by approximately $250$ hops per $2$ s input gives an amortized cost of $0.21$ ms per hop on the GPU and $5.44$ ms per hop on the CPU. These values describe average computation amortized over a complete input clip. Per-hop execution overhead and end-to-end latency require measurement with the streaming implementation, including input availability, window alignment, and output scheduling.


### Appendix F Causalization of Dolphin and AV-Mossformer2 Baselines

Dolphin [ 20 ] and AV-Mossformer2 [ 15 ] were originally released as non-causal AVTSE models. To compare them against Falcon under a common causal protocol, we re-implemented both baselines with the minimal modifications required to remove future-context access at every layer, while preserving their parameter counts, block topologies, and training objectives. Both baselines used the common optimizer and learning-rate schedule described in Section 5.2 . This appendix lists, for each model, the operations that needed rewriting; every module not listed is already causal in the released code and was kept unchanged.

Across both models we used four rewriting primitives [ 17 ] . Causal self-attention adds a lower-triangular mask before the softmax. Causal convolution replaces symmetric padding by left padding only, with the same kernel size and dilation. Causal pooling and interpolation replaces full-axis adaptive average pooling and nearest-neighbor interpolation along time by their one-sided (past-only) variants. Streaming-causal layer normalization replaces full-axis LayerNorm or GroupNorm along time by the frame-local $\operatorname{LN}_{\text{c}}$ of Section 4.2 . Frame-wise operators, including pointwise convolutions, element-wise gates, and LayerNorm on the feature axis, were kept as released.


#### F.1 Dolphin Causalization


##### Visual encoder and refinement.

The DP-LipCoder visual encoder [ 20 ] was frozen during AVTSE training, as in the original paper. Inside it we added causal masks to the temporal axis of every dual-path attention block, while the spatial axes remain bidirectional, and we rewrote every temporal downsample and upsample as a causal convolution. The post-encoder visual refinement block uses dilated depthwise convolutions with symmetric padding, which we rewrote as causal convolutions.


##### Audio-Visual Fusion.

The fusion module has three paths: a resize path that upsamples the visual feature to the audio frame rate by nearest-neighbor interpolation, a key-value path that multiplies a pointwise-projected audio feature by the upsampled visual feature, and an attention path that softmaxes a projected visual feature over its time axis and upsamples the result to the audio time axis. The two non-causal operations are the nearest-neighbor upsamplings, which we rewrote as one-sided, and the softmax over the visual time axis, which we rewrote as a causal softmax that masks visual frames later than the current audio frame. The element-wise sum of the three paths is unchanged.


##### U-shape Separator.

The Separator alternates Global Attention and Local Attention blocks across encoder and decoder stages, joined by injection-based skip fusion and a global Bottleneck. Global Attention compresses the input by adaptive average pooling along time, applies relative-position multi-head self-attention, and restores the resolution by nearest-neighbor upsampling; we rewrote the pooling and the upsampling as one-sided, added a causal mask to the inner attention, and tightened the relative-position clip to non-positive offsets. Local Attention contains Heat Diffusion Attention, which as released applies a discrete cosine transform (DCT) along the full time axis, a per-frequency exponential decay, and an inverse DCT. We replaced the full-sequence DCT pair with a causal windowed DCT on a past-only window of length $W$ , so that the heat-diffusion interpretation is restricted to a finite past and the transform pair becomes a causal convolution of effective kernel $W$ . The surrounding depthwise convolutions and GroupNorm layers in this block were rewritten as causal convolutions and as $\operatorname{LN}_{\text{c}}$ , respectively. The Bottleneck aggregates encoder-stage outputs by adaptive average pooling along time before applying multi-head self-attention and a feed-forward network; we rewrote the aggregation pool as one-sided, added a causal mask to the attention, and rewrote the feed-forward depthwise convolution as causal. The injection-based skip fusion modules align the global feature to each skip’s resolution by nearest-neighbor interpolation along time, which we rewrote as one-sided; the projection sub-modules inside the injection module reuse Heat Diffusion Attention and inherit its causal windowed DCT rewrite.


##### Audio encoder and decoder.

The strided 1D audio encoder and its transposed-convolution decoder were rewritten as causal convolutions along the time axis.


#### F.2 AV-Mossformer2 Causalization


##### Pre-separator audio-visual fusion.

The visual encoder was frozen as in the original paper [ 15 ] . The pre-separator fusion layer upsamples the visual feature to the audio frame rate by nearest-neighbor interpolation, which we rewrote as one-sided; the pointwise projection and the element-wise gated sum that follow are unchanged.


##### MossFormer block.

Each MossFormer block runs a quadratic intra-group attention with squared-ReLU scores in parallel with a linearized cross-group attention. The released code already exposes a causal switch: when enabled, it adds a strictly upper-triangular mask to the squared-ReLU score tensor inside each group, and replaces the bidirectional cross-group statistics by one-sided cumulative sums followed by a one-step shift that excludes the current group from its own summary. We enabled this switch on every MossFormer block, and no further modification was required to the joint attention path.


##### Gated FSMN.

The Gated FSMN module is already causal in the release: its memory taps span only past frames, the surrounding pointwise convolutions have time receptive field $1$ , and the cumulative LayerNorm computes statistics only over past frames. The block was therefore kept unchanged.

Three patterns emerge from the grid. The scene axis did not follow a simple monotone difficulty ordering with respect to reverberation time. Outdoor cells consistently yielded the highest SI-SNRi for every architecture because the near-anechoic propagation of Table 11 leaves the target direct path largely intact, so that the separator only needs to suppress wide-band ambient noise and a small number of geometrically well separated interferers. Large public cells were the next easiest on average, since the dominant interference is reverberant rather than spatially co-located with the target and the visual enrollment cue is sufficient to disambiguate far-field talkers. Medium indoor cells lay in the middle and combine moderate reverberation with the first far-field interferers. Small meeting cells were the hardest, because the near-field interferers lie inside the critical distance of the microphone and therefore inject energy whose spectral envelope, onset timing, and reverberation fingerprint closely match those of the target. The fact that a low-reverberation outdoor scene produces higher SI-SNRi than a short-RT60 small meeting confirms that crowding of near-field talkers, rather than reverberation alone, is the dominant difficulty axis on RealSSA-Sim.

The speaker-count axis followed a non-monotone trajectory that is characteristic of audio-visual target speaker extraction rather than of blind speech separation. In the outdoor scene every competitive model saw its SI-SNRi rise as $N_{\text{near}}$ grows from one to four, for instance Falcon improved from $9.0$ dB at $N_{\text{near}}=1$ to $9.4$ dB at $N_{\text{near}}=4$ , which is consistent with the input mixture becoming progressively more distorted and therefore admitting a larger improvement margin before the metric saturates. In the small meeting scene the improvement peaked around three or four near-field speakers and then declined, with Falcon rising from $6.7$ dB at $N_{\text{near}}=1$ to $7.1$ dB at $N_{\text{near}}=3$ and $N_{\text{near}}=4$ before dropping to $6.8$ dB at $N_{\text{near}}=5$ . This non-monotonicity arises because once a small room contains four or more nearby talkers with closely matched reverberation fingerprints, the visual enrollment cue remains able to resolve the target identity, but the total near-field interfering energy begins to exceed the budget that a single-channel mask can suppress while still preserving the target spectrum. The aggregate RealSSA-Sim column of Table 3 averages over this behavior and therefore underreports the cell-level gap that separates strong causal architectures from lightweight ones in the crowded-room regime.

The grid further shows that Falcon extended the quality of heavyweight causal baselines to conditions in which lightweight ones collapsed. Falcon obtained the best SI-SNRi on eleven of the sixteen cells and the second-best on five more. In the small-meeting and medium-indoor cells, its SI-SNRi margin over AV-TF-GridNet ranged from $-0.1$ to $+0.4$ dB despite using $91.9\%$ fewer parameters (Table 5 ); its margin over Swift-Net-12 was $0.3$ to $0.6$ dB at the same parameter count. The lightweight iterative baselines lost ground most sharply on this same column, with RTFSNet-12 falling below $4$ dB on every small meeting cell and AV-ConvTasNet dropping below $1$ dB, which indicates that repeated application of a small separator cannot by itself compensate for the spectrally similar near-field interference that small rooms produce. The single-pass multi-scale separator and the encoder-space speaker contrastive loss of Falcon therefore act where they were designed to, namely in the crowded near-field cells that the aggregate RealSSA-Sim score tends to wash out, while remaining within $0.2$ dB of the strongest heavyweight baseline in the reverberation-dominated large public and outdoor cells.

### Appendix J Visualization of Encoder-Space Speaker Embeddings

To further analyze the effect of the proposed encoder-space speaker contrastive loss, we visualized speaker embeddings extracted from models trained with and without this loss. We selected the top-15 target speakers with the most utterances from the RealSSA-Sim training set, resulting in 5–8 utterances per speaker. For each utterance, we extracted an encoder-space speaker embedding using the speaker pooling head and projected all embeddings into two dimensions using principal component analysis (PCA).

As shown in Figure 9 , without the contrastive loss, embeddings from different speakers were scattered and overlapped with each other, indicating that the encoder-space representations were weakly structured with respect to speaker identity. In contrast, with the proposed contrastive loss, same-speaker embeddings became more compact and different speakers were more clearly separated. This visualization supports that the proposed loss introduces a speaker-discriminative structure in the encoder space, which helps the separator distinguish the target speaker from near-field interferers.
