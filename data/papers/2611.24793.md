## The Elephant in the Question: Unified Models Picture the Concepts Behind Their Answers

Visualizing intermediate representations could help us inspect the reasoning steps underlying a model output. Unified multimodal models offer this opportunity by combining understanding and generation in one architecture. Their image generators can access representations formed while processing text. We introduce the V-lens , which uses this connection to visualize a chosen token’s hidden state without training a decoder. Across four diffusion-based and two purely autoregressive unified models, the resulting images reveal key intermediate concepts that connect a question to its answer even when the concepts are never named explicitly. Controlled tests show how this content changes with context, token position, and depth. We then introduce lens tuning, which supervises selected hidden states with pictures through the V-lens . Across BAGEL, SenseNova-U1, and HunyuanImage-3.0, the rate of correct visualizations of pronoun referents rises from 2–11% to 86–94%. Surprisingly, such visual supervision also improves text answers without supervising the answer tokens directly. Code is available at https://github.com/multimodal-ai-lab/V-lens .


### 1 Introduction

To answer On which continent does the biggest land animal live? , a model must connect the question to an elephant and the elephant to Africa. The models in correctly answer Africa without ever generating the word elephant . During the generation of their text answers, the models must therefore internally represent the concept of an elephant before shifting their representation toward the output token Africa. While prior work has developed methods to verbalize such intermediate reasoning concepts [ 13 , 18 ] , we aim for a complementary route. Motivated by human reasoning, which can draw on mental imagery [ 33 , 19 ] to solve problems, we aim to visualize them using the model’s own means of visualization. Specifically, we ask:

Can intermediate concepts be made visible within a model’s computation without training a separate decoder?

We find that the model’s own image generator can indeed visualize these concepts from text token representations in its residual stream. These visualizations offer a new way to inspect and supervise content that the text response omits.

Unified multimodal models make this possible through their architecture. They combine understanding and generation across text and images in one system, allowing the image generator to read representations computed while understanding text. Building on this connection, we introduce the V-lens . Transformer layers maintain contextualized token representations known as hidden states [ 42 ] . We center and rescale a selected hidden state to account for differences in the average and spread of states across layers. We then insert it into a minimal drawing prompt and let the model’s own generator draw an image. This requires no decoder training. Prior work makes a model’s intermediate states interpretable as text tokens [ 29 , 4 , 18 ] . Our approach visualizes them as images and traces how these visualizations evolve during text generation. As the V-lens uses the model’s own image generator, it is not restricted to one specific generation paradigm. We show that our approach works for models that process text and image within one shared transformer [ 45 , 39 ] , models that also use a separate set of weights during image generation [ 11 , 12 ] , and purely autoregressive generators that draw token by token [ 8 , 44 ] .

To assess the level of detail captured by the V-lens , we vary the context around specific text tokens, such as object references and words with two meanings. We find that the same token can yield different visualizations depending on its context, most consistently for words with two meanings. An analysis across layers reveals that unnamed objects can be visualized at intermediate depths and fade toward the final layers in most models. The link from a hidden state to its V-lens picture can also be trained. BAGEL [ 11 ] , SenseNova-U1 [ 12 ] , and HunyuanImage-3.0 [ 39 ] often answer reference questions correctly while the visualization of the referent shows the incorrect object. We add small adapters to the text layers and supervise the hidden state with self-generated images through the V-lens . On held-out texts, correct visualizations rise from 2% to 86% in BAGEL, 11% to 94% in SenseNova-U1, and 6% to 86% in HunyuanImage-3.0. We call this procedure lens tuning and also test it with questions in which an event changes an object’s state. Despite purely visual supervision, lens tuning improves text answers on supervised stories in Show-o2 [ 45 ] and HunyuanImage-3.0. Finally, transferring a hidden state from a lens-tuned model to the original model carries part of the correction, showing that the states we visualize are directly connected to the model’s text answers.

Our work makes the following three contributions:

- • The V-lens , which visualizes intermediate hidden states of text tokens without training a separate decoder.

- • Controlled tests in six unified models showing that visualizations follow a token’s context and, in five of them, reveal unnamed concepts at specific tokens and depths.

- • Lens tuning that uses only visual supervision through the V-lens to improve visualizations and text answers.


### 2 Related work


##### Reading hidden states.

Prior work makes neural computations more interpretable by visualizing learned features [ 30 ] and identifying computational subgraphs (circuits) that implement specific behaviors [ 31 ] . Sparse autoencoders reconstruct activations through combinations of learned features that are often easier to interpret than individual neurons [ 21 ] . Natural language autoencoders learn to reconstruct activations through text descriptions [ 13 ] , while activation oracles learn to answer questions about them [ 22 ] . Hidden-state lenses also aim to make intermediate computations interpretable for humans. The logit lens reads earlier states through the model’s final word prediction layer [ 29 ] , an approach also used to analyze visual tokens in vision-language models [ 28 ] . Because states at different layers can use different representations, the tuned lens first learns a linear map and offset for each layer to match the model’s final token predictions [ 4 ] . The Jacobian lens, or J-lens, uses averaged derivatives of the model’s computation to carry a state toward the final layer before reading it through the vocabulary [ 18 ] . Other methods use hidden states to estimate uncertainty [ 6 ] , assess truthfulness [ 2 ] , and detect harmful interactions [ 26 ] . This work’s V-lens translates hidden states into images as an additional pathway for analysis and model improvement.


##### Interpreting hidden states through new prompts.

Patchscopes and SelfIE use a model’s ability to generate text to explain its hidden states. They place a state into a new prompt to describe its content [ 15 , 7 ] , though decoding can favor the model’s own prior over the patched context [ 17 ] . The V-lens follows the Patchscopes construction with an image as the response, using the model’s own image generator. Activation patching also replaces states, but measures changes in the original task to test their causal role [ 27 ] . Lindsey injects concept representations into a model’s activations to test whether it notices and names them [ 24 ] . Work on multi-step reasoning asks whether a model identifies an unnamed concept in time to use it when answering [ 46 , 5 ] . Our questions use their two-step structure to visualize connecting objects from hidden states.


##### Reading text representations as pictures.

Diffusion Lens is the closest prior method [ 40 ] . It decodes intermediate text-encoder representations through a separate diffusion model to trace the image prompt across layers. Diffusion Steering Lens visualizes vision transformer submodule outputs through diffusion [ 37 ] . The V-lens extends this idea to unified models that generate images and text without depending on an external text-encoder. It enables the visualization of a selected hidden state within questions, stories, or their own replies rather than mere image generation prompts. We center and rescale each coordinate of a chosen state before inserting it into a drawing prompt for the same model. This reads text computations through denoising or autoregressive image generation, complementing work that uses visual grounding or imagined pictures to aid language understanding and reasoning [ 38 , 47 , 25 , 48 , 36 ] .


##### Unified models.

Cho et al. [9] were among the first to explore unifying multimodal understanding and generation within one transformer framework. Subsequent models differ in parameter sharing and image generation objectives. The fully unified models Show-o2 and HunyuanImage-3.0 process text and image tokens through a shared transformer backbone [ 45 , 39 ] , while BAGEL and SenseNova-U1 use separate transformer blocks connected through attention [ 11 , 12 ] . These models generate images through diffusion or flow matching. Autoregressive alternatives such as Liquid and Janus-Pro instead predict discrete image tokens sequentially [ 44 , 8 ] . We show that the V-lens works for these different architectures and generation paradigms.


### 3 The V-lens

In their responses, modern language models may use concepts that they do not mention explicitly. To surface such concepts visually, the V-lens centers and rescales a text token’s intermediate hidden state before inserting it in a dedicated drawing request. We call the layer where we take the state the extraction layer $\ell$ , and the layer where we place it the insertion layer $R$ . We fix $R$ per model and vary $\ell$ to read a token at different depths. Figure 2 shows how a state reaches the image generator in three kinds of unified models. We then describe lens tuning , which trains hidden states on images via the V-lens to improve their visualizations, and examine how this affects the model’s text answers.

##### The insertion layer.

We calibrate the insertion layer to find the latest point where the model can still draw recognizable objects from hidden states. We take 12 common, visually distinct nouns from the concept lists of Udandarao et al. [41] , which CLIP [ 34 ] recognizes reliably [ 32 , 41 ] . Each word’s state from an ordinary sentence replaces thing in a photo of a thing at the same layer. We count how often CLIP ranks the intended word first among 100 concept names and choose the latest tested layer with the highest count. This gives $R=8$ of 28 for BAGEL, $R=27$ of 28 for Show-o2, $R=24$ of 42 for SenseNova-U1, $R=9$ of 32 for HunyuanImage-3.0, $R=6$ of 28 for Liquid, and $R=8$ of 30 for Janus-Pro, each recovering 12/12 words. All calibration and sampling settings are in Supp. A and I .


##### Carrying a hidden state between layers.

The V-lens matches the state’s center and spread to the insertion layer so that the model can draw from states at different depths. Let $h_{\ell,p}$ be the hidden state at token position $p$ , $\mu_{\ell}$ the average state at layer $\ell$ , and $\sigma_{\ell,j}$ the spread of coordinate $j$ around that average. With $x=h_{\ell,p}-\mu_{\ell}$ , we obtain

[Tableau] $$v=\mu_{R}+s_{R}\frac{Dx}{\lVert Dx\rVert_{2}},\qquad D=\operatorname{diag}\!\left(\frac{\sigma_{R}}{\sigma_{\ell}}\right).$$ (1)

Here $s_{R}$ is the median distance of insertion-layer states from $\mu_{R}$ . We estimate each squared spread from the mean squared deviation and floor it at 1% of its layer’s median before taking the square root. This avoids amplifying nearly constant coordinates. All statistics come from plain-text passages unrelated to the test items, so the transport requires no training or fitting on pictures. In Supp. A , we provide the passage splits and estimators. Centering yields a token’s deviation from its layer’s average. The diagonal rescaling gives each coordinate its spread at the insertion layer, and normalization puts the result at the usual distance from $\mu_{R}$ . At $\ell=R$ , Equation ( 1 ) only rescales the centered state.


##### Visualization.

A word’s carried state replaces thing in a photo of a thing , or thing alone for SenseNova-U1, whose system prompt already asks for an image. For a word split across tokens, we extract its final token’s state. Each model then draws with its usual sampler and guidance. Supp. E extends this to two hidden states in one request.


##### Lens variants.

We chose the V-lens procedure among simpler and heavier ways of transporting a hidden state ( Tab. 1 ). The simplest variants copy the state unchanged or apply the model’s final normalization, including its gain, following Diffusion Lens [ 40 ] . The heavier variants fit an average Jacobian map from each layer to $R$ with gradients on 64 prompts, once with an added gain per layer. Writing the read word (e.g., animal ) into the request without its hidden state gives a context-free reference. Each lens variant is scored by how often it visualizes the concept that a question describes without explicitly naming it, with items and scoring detailed in Supp. A.3 . The coordinate scaling of Eq. 1 performs best on the validation set and remains best on the test set of fresh items, while requiring only simple layer statistics. Implementation details are in Supp. A .


##### Lens tuning.

The V-lens passes gradients, so a picture can supervise the hidden state it visualizes. For a target picture with representation $z$ , Gaussian noise $\epsilon$ , and $t$ uniform on $[0.4,1]$ , we mix them as $z_{t}=(1-t)z+t\epsilon$ , where larger $t$ means more noise. The frozen denoiser $f$ receives the drawing request $c(h_{\theta})$ , in which the chosen token’s state $h_{\theta}$ , computed with text adapters $\theta$ and carried by the V-lens , replaces thing . Lens tuning minimizes

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{picture}}=\mathbb{E}_{z,\epsilon,t}\bigl\|f(z_{t},t,c(h_{\theta}))-(\epsilon-z)\bigr\|_{2}^{2}.$$ (2)

We disable guidance and train only text adapters before layer $R$ , which act only on the source text.

### 4 Experiments

We first test whether a token’s visualization aligns with its context and how this alignment changes across layers. These tests use BAGEL [ 11 ] , Show-o2 [ 45 ] , SenseNova-U1 [ 12 ] , and HunyuanImage-3.0 [ 39 ] , with a fixed insertion layer per model, and the depth study also includes the autoregressive Liquid [ 44 ] . We then examine purely autoregressive models and apply lens tuning to improve hidden-state visualizations as well as answers about object states.


#### 4.1 Setup

We visualize each hidden state with three seeds. Three multimodal judges, Qwen3-VL [ 3 ] , Gemma-3 [ 14 ] , and LLaVA-OneVision-2 [ 1 ] , identify a picture’s main subject, such as the elephant from animal in . They are asked to ignore incidental background objects and written words. An image is successful when at least two judges identify its correct subject. A state has a correct visualization if at least two of three seeds generate a successful image. On a separate labeled calibration set, the model-based judgment is correct on 146/156 pictures, including 67/72 with background distractors. Three human raters each agreed with the judgment on at least 88% of 600 sampled pictures. Supp. B shows the questions, calibration, and rating breakdown.

Two tests analyze a fixed token’s hidden state as its context in the text prompt changes. The first uses 30 pairs of factual questions describing unnamed objects, such as an animal with a long trunk and a tall plant with a thick trunk . The V-lens reads the last word of each description, here trunk , and the judges check whether its picture shows the described object, an elephant or a tree. The second test uses 20 homonyms (words with at least two meanings), each appearing twice in a sentence after clues to its meaning, such as a whiskered seal and a red wax seal . Reversing the order of their occurrence yields 40 sentences and 80 states to visualize. Supp. G explains how the items were built.

For descriptions, a pair is considered a success only when both texts’ hidden states succeed at the same layer. For words with two meanings, both occurrences must succeed in the sentence, and the word’s score averages the two orders. Sharing the final word across descriptions and reversing the order of the two meanings test whether pictures follow context, while controlling for a word’s usual associations and its position in the text. For these tests, the extraction layer is the insertion layer $R$ . To estimate what pictures that ignore context would score, we randomly swap the intended and alternative labels and rescore the entire test. The average over 10,000 such relabelings estimates chance performance, and the one-sided $p$ -value is the share of relabelings that score at least as high as the true labels. Supp. C also reports results at other extraction layers.

#### 4.2 The same word in different contexts

Context can change the object visualized from the same word’s hidden state ( Tab. 2 ). Words with two meanings give the most consistent result, with 12.5/20 for BAGEL and 15/20 for each other model, all far above label-swap chance. These words already name the pictured objects, suggesting that choosing a meaning is easier to visualize than inferring an object. For unnamed objects, Show-o2 recovers both subjects in 8/30 description pairs and HunyuanImage-3.0 in 6/30. #### 4.3 Intermediate concepts across tokens and layers

Having established that context can change a token’s V-lens visualization, we ask at what point in a model’s computation an intermediate concept becomes visualizable. To answer this, we create sixteen two-hop reasoning questions that describe a concept (first hop) and then ask for a short answer that depends on the described concept (second hop), like the teaser question On which continent does the biggest land animal live? ( ). We retain questions that the model answered correctly without naming the intermediate concept and read the description’s last word ( animal in the teaser) and every later token, including the instruction and chat-format tokens. As a reference, we visualize the concept’s token in a plain sentence, such as Yesterday I saw an elephant on my way home. Figure 4 shows the shares of questions with successful visualizations at the description’s last word, their best later token, and in the plain sentence.

At the description’s last word, the V-lens recovers the concept in at least two thirds of questions at $R$ in all five models. Later recovery is much weaker in BAGEL and HunyuanImage-3.0, while Show-o2, SenseNova-U1, and Liquid often make the object visualizable at chat-format tokens. These tokens open the answer turn in Show-o2 and close the empty thinking block in SenseNova-U1, recovering the concept more often than any single question word, consistent with low-content positions serving as computation sites [ 10 ] . Even here, recovery depends on depth. Just before the answer, Show-o2 falls from 11/15 at layer 24 to 0/15 at layer 27, a prespecified difference of 0.73 with a 95% interval of $[0.53,0.93]$ . The concept’s token in a plain sentence remains readable in all 15 cases at both layers. Among the other models, only Liquid shows the concept immediately before the answer, in up to 4/13. Supp. D breaks these curves down by token. Text readouts of the same hidden states (logit lens, tuned lens, J-lens) rank the object among their ten most likely words at the description’s last word in at most 4 questions per model at $R$ (Supp. D.1 ).


#### 4.4 Picturing a thought while copying a sentence

Following the setup of earlier work [ 23 , 24 ] , we ask the two fully unified models Show-o2 and HunyuanImage-3.0 to copy a sentence verbatim while thinking about an unrelated object. We then read the V-lens only at the tokens of the copied sentence in the response. Responses only count when their case-insensitive Ratcliff–Obershelp similarity [ 35 ] to the original target sentence is at least 0.85. In Show-o2, the word the in the copy shows a bicycle at layer 25 in all three seeds when the model is asked to think about a bicycle, and in no seed when it is asked to think about a rose or receives no such instruction ( Fig. 5 shows one seed). In HunyuanImage-3.0, the final period token of the copy reveals a bread roll at layer 25. These two tokens carry little content of their own, like the chat-format tokens in Sec. 4.3 .

#### 4.6 Supervision through the lens

We test whether the V-lens can visualize the referent of the pronoun it in 20 pairs of texts that use a verb to specify which one of two named objects is referred to. For example, it is the sandwich in A candle and a sandwich were on the table. He ate it. and the candle when the text ends with He lit it. At the insertion layer, the untrained models reveal both referents in 0/20 pairs for BAGEL, 4/20 for SenseNova-U1, and 0/20 for HunyuanImage-3.0, while Show-o2 correctly visualizes 16/20 ( Tab. S7 ). We therefore apply lens tuning ( Sec. 3 ) to the first three models.

##### Training setup.

Each training text also names two things and uses a verb to identify one of them. We use 48 nouns for training and 18 each for development and testing. Each model is trained on the texts in which it correctly names the referent. We evaluate on three test sets of 80 texts each, all unseen during training. The judges pick one of four options for each picture, namely the specified referent, the other named object, both, or neither. A pair succeeds only when both texts show their specified referent. We train rank-8 low-rank adapters (LoRA) in the attention and feed-forward weights [ 20 ] , in layers 0 to 7 for BAGEL, 0 to 23 for SenseNova-U1, and 0 to 8 for HunyuanImage-3.0. As image targets, we use pictures the frozen model draws when prompted with the referent. Details are in Supp. F and G .

We compare lens tuning with text supervision of the referent’s name, word state supervision that aligns centered pronoun and referent hidden states by cosine similarity, and ordinary drawing supervision using the same pictures and loss through the usual drawing route with A photo of it. appended to the text. The comparisons share texts, adapter locations, and update budgets. In Tab. 3 , lens success requires both pronoun states of a pair to show their referents through the V-lens in two of three seeds. We evaluate lens success on unseen texts to test whether lens tuning generalizes and makes states more visually interpretable. The column Verbs uses unseen verbs, Nouns tests generalization on unseen nouns, and Phrases uses sentence forms that were not encountered during training, such as Our neighbour kept a goat and a purse. One day he fed it.

This motivates an investigation into whether visual supervision through the V-lens can also affect a model’s verbal answers.


#### 4.7 Training the pictured state changes the answer

> Figure 8 : Training the pictured state changes the answer in Show-o2 and HunyuanImage-3.0. The V-lens visualizes the subject of supervised stories before and after lens tuning. Below each picture is the model’s text answer with its corresponding probability.

We next ask whether lens tuning, which uses purely visual supervision, can also change a model’s textual response behavior. We test the fully unified Show-o2 and HunyuanImage-3.0 on 40 paired scenarios (80 stories). For example, A slice of white bread was put in the toaster for three minutes. is paired with A slice of white bread was left in its bag for three minutes. We ask Is the slice of bread now white or brown? We supervise the second bread , in the question, through the V-lens with the loss of Eq. 2 , again using the model’s own pictures of brown toast for the toaster story and white bread for the bag story. Because models favor one option position, the experiment also asks each question with the options reversed, as in Is the slice of bread now brown or white? A story counts as correct only when the one-word replies name the right option in both orders ( Tab. 4 ), and Fig. 8 reports a two-option probability from the margin averaged over both orders.

Training the model with pictures that show the correct state of the object raises the share of stories that Show-o2 answers correctly from 36.3% to 49.6%, while its corresponding V-lens visualizations show the right state in 73.3% instead of 52.5% ( Tab. 4 ). Supervision with pictures that show the incorrect state leaves the answers near the untrained level and reduces the correct-picture rate to 27.1%. In HunyuanImage-3.0, correct answers rise from 40.0% to 50.8%, but training on the incorrect state also raises them to 47.5%. Figure 8 shows the pictured state and the preferred answer changing together, as when a snowman in the spring sun disappears or white bread put in the toaster turns brown.

To further test the impact of lens tuning on the hidden states, we use a second set of 56 stories and perform isolated swaps of hidden states. Specifically, in each story we replace the subject token’s state at every layer of the original Show-o2 with the corresponding state from the picture-trained counterpart. We then read the same question and check whether the original model can now answer it correctly using the subject’s swapped hidden state. Indeed, correct answers rise from 41/56 to 47–49/56, transferring 21–39% of the margin gain. ### 5 Conclusion

The V-lens enables the visualization of intermediate hidden states in four unified models with diffusion-based image generators and two purely autoregressive models. Lens tuning strengthens state-referent associations, making pronoun referents visualizable in BAGEL, SenseNova-U1, and HunyuanImage-3.0, and correcting text answers on supervised stories in Show-o2 and HunyuanImage-3.0. Transferring the trained subject word’s states to the original Show-o2 carries part of the answer correction, which connects the states we visualize to the answers the model writes. What we can visualize depends on the token, layer, and drawing request. Importantly, a failure to visualize a concept does not establish that the hidden state lacks information about it. The answer corrections were measured on supervised stories, and the state transfers test whole hidden states without isolating the information visible in their pictures. The lens thus connects the inspection of intermediate hidden states with training through the model’s own generator.

#### A.2 Carrying a hidden state between layers

With $x=h_{\ell,p}-\mu_{\ell}$ , the transported hidden state is

[Tableau] $$v=\mu_{R}+s_{R}\frac{D_{\ell,R}x}{\|D_{\ell,R}x\|_{2}},\qquad D_{\ell,R}=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{R}/\sigma_{\ell}).$$ (S1)

All statistics come from plain-text passages unrelated to the test items. The means $\mu_{\ell}$ are estimated from 64 passages. The coordinate spreads $\sigma_{\ell}$ use those passages and 40 additional ones. For each coordinate we average $(h_{\ell,p,j}-\mu_{\ell,j})^{2}$ , floor this value at 1% of the layer’s median coordinate variance, and take the square root. The radius $s_{R}$ is the median distance from $\mu_{R}$ over 20 separate passages. We use positions from the fifth token to the token before the final marker. At $\ell=R$ the diagonal matrix is the identity, but the centered state is still placed at the usual radius. The statistics remain fixed for all image tasks and adapter training, so the lens is never fitted on pictures.

#### B.1 The main-subject question

The judges are Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B-Instruct, Gemma-3-27B-it, and LLaVA-OneVision-2-8B-Instruct. Here we describe the default main-subject question with four choices. The lens-variant comparison uses the six choices of Sec. A.3 , and the two-state and general capability studies use the questions of Secs. E.1 and F.3 . A judge sees only the picture and its answer choices, never the source sentence, layer, model, or training condition. The question is Which of these is depicted as a main subject of the picture? Ignore incidental background objects and written words. The choices are the intended object, the alternative object, both, as main subjects , and neither, as a main subject , with the first two shuffled by picture identifier, followed by Answer with the letter only. We select the letter with the highest model probability. A picture succeeds when at least two judges choose the intended object alone. Unless a study specifies otherwise, a hidden state succeeds when two of its three drawing seeds succeed. A pair succeeds only when both texts succeed at the same layer. For words with two meanings, both occurrences must succeed within one sentence. Averaging the two sentence orders gives a word score of zero, one half, or one.

#### B.2 Chance levels and intervals

Context scores are compared with 10,000 label swaps within pairs, or within words with both meanings exchanged together. Each swap recomputes the paired score, the chance estimate is the mean swapped score, and the one-sided $p$ -value is $(1+n_{\geq})/(1+10{,}000)$ . Context intervals use 2,000 bootstrap samples of pairs or words. The depth and two-state comparisons use paired bootstrap samples of questions or object pairs. Training intervals use 10,000 bootstrap samples of whole noun pairs, keeping both noun orders together and averaging the seeds within each text pair. The training seeds therefore do not count as independent test items in these intervals.

### Appendix E Reading two hidden states

The two-state experiments use unchanged copies to visualize an object’s color or pattern from two hidden states ( Fig. S2 ). Both states enter $R$ without the centering and rescaling of Eq. 1 . Eight development pairs and eight held-out pairs with new objects and sentence forms assign two colors or patterns to each object. After The car was painted bright red. Later I looked at it. , we copy the states of red and it unchanged into separate slots at the insertion layer. The modifier and noun states from an explicit drawing request check that both can be recovered together. We compare this with the pronoun alone, the pronoun in both slots, and states exchanged between texts with another color, pattern, or object.

All models recover the modifier and object from the explicit drawing requests, except for one HunyuanImage-3.0 development pair. With both required in the picture, the modifier and pronoun from contextual texts succeed in 5/8 held-out pairs for Show-o2, 3/7 for HunyuanImage-3.0, 1/7 for SenseNova-U1, and 0/7 for BAGEL. Neither the pronoun alone nor the duplicated pronoun succeeds, and the noun alone almost never shows a value. In HunyuanImage-3.0 the noun alone already shows the colored car and cup, one pair per split. In Show-o2 the gain over the pronoun alone is 0.63, with a 95% interval of $[0.25,0.88]$ . SenseNova-U1 often shows the intended color on a different object. Exchanging the modifier changes the color with the object present in 3/8 held-out Show-o2 pairs. Exchanging the pronoun changes the object in 13/16 held-out value cases, using the main-subject question with the two objects as choices. These comparisons show that the modifier and pronoun states make separate contributions to Show-o2’s pictures. Together, the two states can show a colored object even though neither expresses both properties.

### Appendix F Training through the lens


#### F.1 Data and training

We train adapters in BAGEL, SenseNova-U1, and HunyuanImage-3.0. During lens tuning, adapters act only on the source text, while the drawing request and generator remain frozen. The texts use 84 noun labels in six categories, each category with eight training, three development, and three held-out labels ( Tab. S23 ). Only categories whose selecting verbs cannot apply to the other object are paired. Each pair appears in both noun orders, with each noun selected in turn. For BAGEL and SenseNova-U1, the training pool has 60 pairs in four sentence forms, 960 texts in all. For each model, we retain the texts whose referent it names correctly, 696 for BAGEL and 798 for SenseNova-U1. Three test sets of 20 noun pairs, named as in Tab. 3 , use new pairs of training nouns with a held-out selecting verb per category ( Verbs ), held-out noun labels ( Nouns ), and new pairs of training nouns in two held-out sentence forms ( Phrases ). Each test set has 80 texts, forming 40 pairs with the same noun order and a different selected referent. Both texts must give the intended referent. For development, we use 20 additional pairs whose noun labels do not occur in training or testing.

For BAGEL and SenseNova-U1, LoRA adapters of rank 8 and scaling 16 modify the query, key, value, attention-output, gate, up, and down projections of the understanding weights in layers 0 to 7 and 0 to 23, respectively. These models use AdamW with learning rate $10^{-4}$ and no weight decay for 600 updates of 16 texts sampled with replacement. We evaluate the final checkpoint with no selection on test data. For each training noun, we keep up to four target pictures from the frozen model’s own drawings with seeds 0 to 5 when at least two judges recognize the noun. For each loss evaluation, we sample a target, a noise level uniformly on $[0.4,1]$ , and fresh Gaussian noise, and use the conditional denoising loss without guidance on VAE latents for BAGEL and pixels for SenseNova-U1. Gradients pass through the frozen generator to the text adapters. During lens tuning the adapters act only on the source text, and the drawing request uses unchanged weights.

For HunyuanImage-3.0, we place adapters in layers 0 to 8 before insertion layer 9 and train them for 200 updates of 16 texts. The four training arms use the same test items, measures, scorer, and three training seeds as the other models. Lens tuning succeeds on 0.71 of the pairs in Verbs , 0.94 in Nouns , and 0.92 in Phrases . Word state supervision gives 0.65, 0.97, and 0.99. Its overall gain over text supervision is 0.79 with a 95% interval of $[0.73,0.84]$ . Ordinary drawing supervision raises pair-level drawing success from 0.03 to 0.69. Answers on the untrained question forms average 0.71 before training, 0.79 after text supervision, 0.75 after lens tuning, and 0.79 after ordinary drawing.

#### F.4 State questions and answer changes

Lens tuning also changes answers to questions about an object’s state after an event. Each scenario has two stories, one in which the change happens and one in which it is prevented, followed by a question with two answer options asked in both orders. Both models use the same 40 scenarios, giving 80 stories. The object is named in the question, as in Is the slice of bread now white or brown? Twenty of the scenarios were added to an earlier set under the same writing rules and selected from screened candidates using a rule fixed before training. A second set of 28 scenarios (56 stories) uses questions about it for the interchange test of Sec. F.5 . We supervise the question’s subject at the insertion layer in the model’s answer format.

The HunyuanImage-3.0 checkpoint has 80B parameters, 13B active parameters, and 32 transformer layers shared by text generation and image denoising. We use insertion layer 9, selected by the concrete-word calibration of Appendix A . For text generation, we use the system instruction Answer every question with exactly one word. Never explain your answer. Rank-8 LoRA adapters with scaling 16 modify layers 0 to 26 in Show-o2 and layers 0 to 8 in HunyuanImage-3.0. They act on the attention and feed-forward projections in Show-o2 and on the attention and shared MLP projections in HunyuanImage-3.0. The adapters act only while reading the source text, and the drawing request and image generator use the frozen weights.

Each target is the frozen model’s own drawing of a photo of a { state } , with four target pictures per state in Show-o2 and two in HunyuanImage-3.0. The right-state objective uses the state implied by the story, and the wrong-state objective uses the alternative state of the same scenario. Both apply the denoising loss defined in the main paper through the subject’s hidden state carried by the V-lens . AdamW uses learning rate $10^{-4}$ , no weight decay, and batches of eight texts. We train both models for 600 updates with each target. Every setting uses training seeds 0, 1, and 2. Each loss evaluation samples a target picture, fresh noise, and a noise level uniformly on $[0.4,1]$ . HunyuanImage-3.0 uses noised image latents with timestep $1000t$ and target $\epsilon-z$ , and its target pictures are generated at $1024\times 1024$ pixels.

A story is answered correctly when the greedy one-word replies to the question in both option orders name the right option, and Tab. S20 also reports each order separately. We ask every question in both orders because the models prefer one option position. Untrained Show-o2 answers 48 of the 80 stories correctly as written and 53 with the options reversed, so a single order mixes the story’s effect with this preference. Requiring both orders, or averaging the answer margin over both orders as Fig. 8 does, removes it. In the state-question experiment, the pictures of the subject’s hidden state use drawing seed 0 at the insertion layer. A picture is correct when two of the three judges choose the right state in the main-subject question of Appendix B , whose options are the scenario’s two states, both, and neither. The figure’s two-option probability is $1/(1+\exp(-m))$ , where $m$ is the preferred option’s log-probability margin at the first reply token, averaged over both option orders.

#### F.5 Interchanging the subject’s hidden states

For the second set of 56 stories, we carry the trained subject word’s hidden states from each of the three Show-o2 donors lens-tuned on these stories into the untrained model while it answers the identical question. At each layer from 0 to 27, the subject’s hidden state is replaced by its raw state from the trained model’s reading of that text. All other token positions are recomputed by the untrained model while no adapters are active. We use both option orders and repeat the intervention for all three trained models. Self-swapping each state reproduces every answer margin exactly in both the original and trained models.

The trained subject’s hidden states raise the untrained model’s correct answers from 41 to 47, 47, and 49 of 56, carrying 38%, 39%, and 21% of the mean-margin gain ( Tab. S21 ). The transferred share is the interchange margin minus the original margin, divided by the trained margin minus the original margin. Replacing only the state entering layer 27 leaves the mean margin within 0.01 of the original value. Transferring the subject’s states across layers carries part of the answer change, though this swap does not isolate the information shown in their pictures.

### Appendix L Limitations

The V-lens depends on what the model’s generator can draw. We study objects, colors, and visible states, and do not establish reliable readings of numbers, relations, or abstract ideas. Each reading also requires generating a full image, taking 9–110 seconds in our setup. A missing picture does not establish that a hidden state lacks the concept, as the untrained pronoun results illustrate. Our scores depend on three multimodal judges choosing among a few options, and calibration and human checks cannot rule out acceptance of similar but incorrect objects. The item sets are small and mostly written by hand, with 16 two-hop questions, 30 description pairs, 20 two-meaning words, 20 pronoun pairs, 16 two-state pairs, 40 state scenarios, and 28 interchange scenarios, in addition to the lens-variant sets of Sec. A.3 . Recovery of unnamed objects is modest and is not significantly above chance in BAGEL or SenseNova-U1. The best later token in Fig. 4 selects among many positions for each question and layer, so its curve is a descriptive summary.

Lens tuning trains hidden states through the same lens used to evaluate them. Gains in ordinary drawing are small, and word state supervision without pictures exceeds lens tuning on unseen phrases in SenseNova-U1 and on unseen nouns and phrases in HunyuanImage-3.0. The gains in text answers correspond to about ten stories out of 80 and are measured on the supervised stories. The interchange test transfers whole hidden states and has no control donor trained on different picture targets, so it does not isolate the contribution of the pictured content. Finally, the general capability checks use one training seed, and their intervals still allow losses of several percentage points.
