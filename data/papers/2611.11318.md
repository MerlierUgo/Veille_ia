## PROLA: Principal-Orthogonal Low-rank Adaptation for Predictive Spatiotemporal Weather Downscaling

High-resolution weather prediction is important for resolving local atmospheric patterns missed by coarse global forecasts. Large pretrained weather backbones have improved global forecasting, but adapting them to produce future high-resolution fields from recent coarse states remains challenging and costly. We formulate this setting as Predictive Spatiotemporal Weather Downscaling ( PSWD ), a task that jointly refines spatial and temporal resolution while predicting future high-resolution trajectories. To this end, we propose a framework that decomposes prediction into spatial and temporal stages using a shared pretrained backbone, fixed numerical scaffolds, and lightweight residual heads. For backbone adaptation, we introduce PROLA (PRincipal-Orthogonal Low-rank Adaptation), which allocates a fixed low-rank budget between pretrained weights’ principal directions and their orthogonal complement. PROLA further rescales rank-1 optimizer updates using gradient signals, improving adaptation without increasing trainable rank. Experiments in the PSWD setting show that PROLA outperforms representative low-rank adaptation baselines. Further experiments across diverse datasets and tasks support the broader applicability of PROLA, showing that its benefits extend beyond the primary weather downscaling setting.


### 1 Introduction

Machine learning-based weather modeling has primarily developed along two paradigms: weather forecasting [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] , which predicts future states at fixed spatial resolution, and weather downscaling [ 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 ] , which refines either spatial resolution at a fixed time or temporal resolution at a fixed grid. These standard formulations typically emphasize a subset of three axes: future prediction, spatial refinement, and temporal refinement. However, real-world applications that forecast a high-resolution target from a different, lower-resolution modality naturally involve all three axes. For example, minute-scale wind-power forecasting for renewable integration [ 9 ] , sub-hourly precipitation nowcasting for flood-risk monitoring [ 10 ] , and fine-scale aviation turbulence guidance for flight safety [ 11 ] all require future predictions at fine spatial and temporal resolution, a regime that no prior work has explored. This raises a natural question: Can a single model jointly refine spatial and temporal resolution while extending predictions into the future?

To answer this, we introduce Predictive Spatiotemporal Weather Downscaling (PSWD), the task of mapping past low-resolution states to future high-resolution states, with resolution gaps in both space and time. PSWD formalizes joint spatiotemporal refinement and future prediction as a unified learning task. It extends task-specific adaptation benchmarks for weather foundation models (WFMs) [ 12 ] to a setting that requires all three capabilities jointly. PSWD requires a model to infer fine-scale spatial structure and its evolution from the same coarse history. We address this task with a scaffold-and-correct pipeline built around a shared pretrained weather backbone. The spatial stage predicts and refines a high-resolution state at the final lead time. The temporal stage then uses this predicted endpoint and the upsampled present state to construct the target trajectory. Fixed spatial upsampling and temporal interpolation provide the scaffolds, while lightweight correction heads learn the residual details.

In the scaffold-and-correct pipeline, the shared backbone provides the forecast that anchors both spatial and temporal refinement. To align this forecast with the final trajectory objective, we adapt the backbone jointly with the correction heads. Since the backbone already contains useful forecasting knowledge, this adaptation should build on its pretrained structure. At the same time, predicting high-resolution future trajectories may require corrections beyond the dominant pretrained directions. A single low-rank update provides a compact parameterization for parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) [ 13 ] , but it does not explicitly separate these two needs under a fixed rank budget. This motivates an adaptation design that initializes one update path from the pretrained singular structure [ 14 ] while reserving another path for corrections outside the selected principal directions [ 15 ] .

To this end, we propose PROLA ( PR incipal- O rthogonal L ow-rank A daptation), a dual-branch low-rank adaptation method for the shared backbone. PROLA divides the fixed rank budget between a principal branch initialized from dominant singular components and an orthogonal branch projected outside the selected principal subspaces. The principal factors remain freely trainable after initialization, while the orthogonal branch uses fixed projectors defined by the pretrained weight. Beyond this allocation, PROLA rescales rank-1 optimizer updates using gradient-based branch and component scores, without increasing trainable rank. Branch scores adjust the relative update strength between the two paths, while component scores modulate individual updates within each path.

Experiments on WeatherBench 2 [ 16 ] with the pretrained WFM backbone [ 4 ] show that PROLA improves over representative low-rank PEFT baselines under matched low-rank budgets for multi-variable, multi-lead global weather downscaling. Ablations and diagnostics further support the scaffold-and-correct pipeline, the dual-branch design of PROLA, and gradient-guided scaling of optimizer updates. Together, these results support PSWD as a challenging evaluation setting and PROLA as an effective adaptation method for pretrained weather backbones, with broader applicability across datasets and tasks.

Our contributions are fourfold:

- • We formulate Predictive Spatiotemporal Weather Downscaling (PSWD) to predict future high-resolution weather trajectories from recent coarse-resolution states, and introduce a scaffold-and-correct pipeline to address it.

- • We propose PROLA ( PR incipal- O rthogonal L ow-rank A daptation), a low-rank PEFT method that allocates a fixed rank budget between principal and orthogonal branches and rescales optimizer updates using gradient-based component scores.

- • We show that PROLA outperforms representative PEFT baselines under a matched low-rank budget and narrows the performance gap between low-rank PEFT and full fine-tuning in our PSWD setting.

- • We demonstrate PROLA’s effectiveness across datasets and the transferability of its adaptation design beyond weather downscaling.


### 2 Predictive Spatiotemporal Weather Downscaling

In Section 2.1 , we first formulate predictive spatiotemporal weather downscaling (PSWD) as a challenging task that jointly requires forecasting, spatial downscaling, and temporal downscaling. We then introduce a scaffold-and-correct pipeline designed to address this task in Section 2.2 .


#### 2.1 Task Formulation

Notation. Let $\mathbf{x}$ denote a context input and $\mathbf{y}$ a prediction target. For an atmospheric state with $V$ variable channels, the input on the low-resolution grid is represented by $\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{LR}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times H_{\mathrm{LR}}\times W_{\mathrm{LR}}}$ and the target on the high-resolution grid by $\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{HR}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times H_{\mathrm{HR}}\times W_{\mathrm{HR}}}$ . Let $\mathcal{H}_{c}$ denote the set of input lags at the coarse sampling interval $\delta_{c}$ , and $\mathcal{H}_{f}$ the set of target leads at the fine sampling interval $\delta_{f}$ . Relative to the anchor time $t$ , the input at lag $h\in\mathcal{H}_{c}$ is $\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{LR}}_{t-h}$ , and the target at lead $h\in\mathcal{H}_{f}$ is $\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{HR}}_{t+h}$ . With this indexing, $\mathcal{H}_{c}$ consists of the lags in $\{k\delta_{c}\mid k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\}$ within the context window, and $\mathcal{H}_{f}$ consists of the leads in $\{k\delta_{f}\mid k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\}$ within the forecast window.

> Figure 1: Overview of the proposed scaffold-and-correct pipeline for PSWD. Fixed numerical operators $U_{s}$ and $U_{t}$ and WFM backbone $\mathcal{B}$ build spatial and temporal scaffolds, while the residual heads $C_{s}$ and $C_{t}$ provide learned corrections.

Comparison with existing weather tasks. We compare PSWD with related weather problem settings along three axes: forecasting, spatial downscaling and temporal downscaling. Forecasting predicts future states from past observations, changing only the forecast horizon while typically keeping the spatial and temporal grids fixed. Spatial downscaling reconstructs an HR state from its LR counterpart at the same valid time, $\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{LR}}_{t}\mapsto\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{HR}}_{t}$ . It refines spatial resolution by recovering unresolved local structure, but does not change the temporal horizon. Temporal downscaling maps a coarsely sampled trajectory to a denser one on the same spatial grid over the same time interval. Existing settings usually address a single axis, whereas our target setting, Predictive Spatiotemporal Weather Downscaling (PSWD) , combines all three. At forecast initialization time $t$ , PSWD takes coarse-step past and present LR inputs and predicts fine-step future HR targets:

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{LR}}_{t}=(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{LR}}_{t-h})_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{c}},\qquad\mathcal{Y}^{\mathrm{HR}}_{t}=(\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{HR}}_{t+h})_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{f}},$$ (1)

where both sequences are ordered by increasing valid time and the targets are sampled more frequently than the inputs, i.e., $\delta_{f}<\delta_{c}$ . Thus, PSWD requires learning the mapping $\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{LR}}_{t}\mapsto\mathcal{Y}^{\mathrm{HR}}_{t}$ to predict a future, higher-resolution, and temporally denser target trajectory.

Why PSWD is challenging. (i) Coupled spatiotemporal prediction. PSWD combines three task axes that are typically handled separately. Each target state is simultaneously a future prediction, a spatially refined field, and a temporally denser sub-step. The model must therefore infer fine-resolution future trajectories from coarse past inputs, making PSWD a coupled spatiotemporal prediction problem rather than a single-axis task shift. (ii) Limited adaptation capacity. While pretrained weather foundation models (WFMs) provide useful large-scale forecasting knowledge, PSWD requires adapting them to a richer output regime with finer spatial grids and denser future time steps. Since modern WFMs are increasingly large, fully fine-tuning them for every target resolution, lead-time structure, or data regime is often impractical. Recent work [ 12 ] therefore emphasizes PEFT as an important paradigm for WFM adaptation. PSWD tests whether limited trainable capacity can support a coupled shift in forecast horizon, spatial resolution, and temporal density.


#### 2.2 Scaffold-and-Correct Pipeline

PSWD requires the model to forecast future atmospheric states while downscaling both spatially and temporally. We therefore introduce a unified scaffold-and-correct pipeline in Figure 1 that organizes prediction into spatial and temporal stages.


##### Overview.

We adopt a scaffold-and-correct architecture: a coarse scaffold built primarily from pretrained or fixed components, followed by a learnable correction head that adds the missing detail. First, a fixed spatial upsampler $U_{s}$ lifts coarse low-resolution inputs to the target HR grid, and a PEFT-adapted WFM backbone $\mathcal{B}$ predicts a spatial scaffold $\mathbf{z}$ at the fixed final lead time $h_{\mathrm{end}}=\max\mathcal{H}_{f}$ . Then, the spatial correction head $C_{s}$ adds a learned residual to produce the refined endpoint. A fixed temporal interpolator $U_{t}$ bridges the upsampled present input and the predicted spatial endpoint to form temporal scaffolds $\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{t+h}$ over the target states at $h\in\mathcal{H}_{f}$ , and the temporal correction head $C_{t}$ adds another residual to produce the final outputs. The entire architecture shares a single backbone and is trained end-to-end under a single loss. The superscripts $S$ and $ST$ denote the spatial-stage and final spatiotemporal predictions, respectively.


##### Spatial scaffold and correction.

Let $\phi_{\mathrm{eff}}=(\phi_{\mathcal{B}},\phi_{\mathrm{PEFT}})$ collect the frozen pretrained backbone parameters and the trainable PEFT parameters, and let $\phi_{s},\phi_{t}$ denote the parameters of the two correction heads $C_{s},C_{t}$ . For the common two-input case $\mathcal{H}_{c}=\{\delta_{c},0\}$ , a fixed spatial upsampler $U_{s}$ (e.g., bilinear interpolation) aligns the coarse inputs to the target HR grid:

[Tableau] $$\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t-\delta_{c}}=U_{s}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{LR}}_{t-\delta_{c}}),\qquad\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=U_{s}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{LR}}_{t}).$$ (2)

The PEFT-adapted WFM backbone $\mathcal{B}$ produces a spatial scaffold $\mathbf{z}$ at the final lead time, and the spatial correction head $C_{s}$ adds a learned residual:

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{z}=\mathcal{B}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t-\delta_{c}},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t};\phi_{\mathrm{eff}}),\qquad\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{S}_{t+h_{\mathrm{end}}}=\mathbf{z}+C_{s}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t},\mathbf{z};\phi_{s}).$$ (3)

Here, $U_{s}$ transports large-scale synoptic structure to the target grid and $\mathcal{B}$ advances it to the final lead time using its pretrained forecasting dynamics. Finally, $C_{s}$ supplies the sub-grid corrections that interpolation alone cannot recover.


##### Temporal scaffold and correction.

The endpoint prediction $\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{S}_{t+h_{\mathrm{end}}}$ then anchors the temporal downscaling. For each $h\in\mathcal{H}_{f}$ , a fixed temporal interpolator $U_{t}$ (e.g., linear interpolation) between the upsampled present input $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t}$ and the predicted endpoint $\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{S}_{t+h_{\mathrm{end}}}$ forms a temporal scaffold $\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{t+h}$ , and the lead-time-conditioned temporal correction head $C_{t}$ adds a residual:

[Tableau] $$\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{t+h}=U_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t},\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{S}_{t+h_{\mathrm{end}}};h),\qquad\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{ST}_{t+h}=\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{t+h}+C_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{t+h},\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{S}_{t+h_{\mathrm{end}}},h;\phi_{t}).$$ (4)

$U_{t}$ preserves the temporal continuity implied by the two endpoint states, while $C_{t}$ learns the sub-step deviations that linear interpolation cannot capture.

The full pipeline thus concentrates the adaptation burden in the WFM backbone $\mathcal{B}$ and the two lightweight correction heads $C_{s}$ and $C_{t}$ , while $U_{s}$ and $U_{t}$ remain non-learnable scaffolds that preserve large-scale spatial and temporal structure.


##### Training objective.

We train end-to-end with the variable-weighted state loss $\ell_{\mathrm{PT}}$ used to pretrain the backbone, applied uniformly to all trajectory outputs:

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{ST}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{f}|}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{f}}\ell_{\mathrm{PT}}\!\left(\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{ST}_{t+h},\,\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{HR}}_{t+h}\right).$$ (5)

No auxiliary loss is attached to the spatial endpoint $\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{S}_{t+h_{\mathrm{end}}}$ : the spatial stage is supervised through gradients flowing back from the temporal stage, ensuring both stages co-adapt for the joint trajectory rather than optimizing toward stage-specific targets. Section 5.1 evaluates these design choices.

### 3 PROLA: PRincipal-Orthogonal Low-rank Adaptation

Low-rank PEFT reduces the number of trainable parameters, but under a fixed budget, performance depends on how the limited capacity is allocated in the pretrained weight space and rescaled during optimization. PROLA addresses these two aspects through rank allocation and gradient-guided update scaling (Figure 2 ). In Section 3.1 , we allocate the rank budget between principal and orthogonal branches, with the former supporting pretrained-aligned adaptation and the latter capturing residual corrections outside the selected principal directions. In Section 3.2 , we further rescale updates from the base optimizer using rank-1 component scores computed from gradient magnitudes. Together, these mechanisms determine where the limited rank budget is allocated and how strongly each component is updated, allowing PROLA to preserve pretrained structure while directing updates toward task-specific corrections.


#### 3.1 Budget Allocation via Dual-Branch Low-Rank Adaptation

We first allocate the fixed low-rank budget in the pretrained weight space. Given a pretrained weight matrix $W_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ , PROLA splits the total rank budget $r=r_{p}+r_{o}$ , with fixed hyperparameters $r_{p},r_{o}>0$ , between a principal branch for pretrained-aligned adaptation and an orthogonal branch for corrections outside the selected principal directions [ 14 , 15 ] . The effective weight is

[Tableau] $$W^{\mathrm{eff}}=W^{\mathrm{res}}+\Delta W^{\mathrm{dual}},\qquad\Delta W^{\mathrm{dual}}=\underbrace{\Delta W_{p}}_{\text{principal update}}+\underbrace{\Delta W_{o}}_{\text{orthogonal update}},$$ (6)

where $W^{\mathrm{res}}$ is a frozen residual weight defined in Eq. ( 9 ). The two branches contain $r(d_{\mathrm{out}}+d_{\mathrm{in}})$ trainable parameters, matching a rank- $r$ adapter.

Principal branch. We compute the SVD $W_{0}=U\Sigma V^{\top}$ and select the top- $r_{p}$ singular directions $U_{p},V_{p}$ and singular values $\Sigma_{p}$ . The principal update is

[Tableau] $$\Delta W_{p}=s_{p}B_{p}A_{p},$$ (7)

where $B_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r_{p}}$ , $A_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{p}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ , and $s_{p}>0$ is a scaling coefficient. We initialize $B_{p,0}=U_{p}\Sigma_{p}^{1/2}$ and $A_{p,0}=\Sigma_{p}^{1/2}V_{p}^{\top}$ . After initialization, both factors are optimized without additional constraints.

Orthogonal branch. The remaining rank $r_{o}$ is assigned to corrections outside the selected principal directions. With $P_{\perp}=I_{d_{\mathrm{out}}}-U_{p}U_{p}^{\top}$ and $Q_{\perp}=I_{d_{\mathrm{in}}}-V_{p}V_{p}^{\top}$ , we define

[Tableau] $$\Delta W_{o}=s_{o}P_{\perp}B_{o}A_{o}Q_{\perp},$$ (8)

where $B_{o}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r_{o}}$ , $A_{o}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{o}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ , and $s_{o}>0$ is a scaling coefficient. By construction, $U_{p}^{\top}\Delta W_{o}=0$ and $\Delta W_{o}V_{p}=0$ , so this branch provides dedicated capacity outside both principal subspaces.

Function-preserving initialization. Following PiSSA [ 14 ] , we residualize the frozen weight:

[Tableau] $$W^{\mathrm{res}}=W_{0}-\Delta W_{p,0},\qquad\Delta W_{p,0}=s_{p}B_{p,0}A_{p,0}.$$ (9)

We set $B_{o,0}=0$ and sample the entries of $A_{o,0}$ from $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{o}^{2})$ , where $\sigma_{o}$ controls the initialization scale. This gives $\Delta W_{o,0}=0$ and $W^{\mathrm{eff}}_{0}=W_{0}$ , preserving the pretrained function.

Role of the rank split. The principal factors remain freely trainable, so a principal-only adapter can also learn corrections outside its initial singular subspaces. PROLA assigns $r_{p}$ factors to the principal branch for pretrained-aligned adaptation and reserves $r_{o}$ factors for a separate projected branch dedicated to orthogonal corrections. The rank split fixes the capacity assigned to these two paths under the same total rank budget, while the learned factors determine their contributions to the effective update. To examine how the resulting update is distributed across the pretrained subspaces, let $\Delta W^{\mathrm{eff}}:=W^{\mathrm{eff}}-W_{0}$ , $P=U_{p}U_{p}^{\top}$ , and $Q=V_{p}V_{p}^{\top}$ , with $P_{\perp}=I-P$ and $Q_{\perp}=I-Q$ . Then

[Tableau] $$\Delta W^{\mathrm{eff}}=\underbrace{P\Delta W^{\mathrm{eff}}Q}_{\text{principal block}}+\underbrace{P\Delta W^{\mathrm{eff}}Q_{\perp}+P_{\perp}\Delta W^{\mathrm{eff}}Q}_{\text{cross blocks}}+\underbrace{P_{\perp}\Delta W^{\mathrm{eff}}Q_{\perp}}_{\text{orthogonal block}}.$$ (10)

Section 5.3 further compares the update energy across these blocks.


#### 3.2 Gradient-Guided Scaling of Optimizer Updates

In Section 3.1 , we allocate the fixed rank budget across the principal and orthogonal branches. We next rescale how strongly each rank-1 component is updated during optimization. For $g\in\{p,o\}$ , where $g$ indexes the principal branch $p$ or the orthogonal branch $o$ , let $r_{g}$ denote the corresponding branch rank. Let $\mathbf{b}_{k}^{g}$ be the $k$ -th column of $B_{g}$ , and let $(\mathbf{a}_{k}^{g})^{\top}$ be the $k$ -th row of $A_{g}$ . Then $(\mathbf{b}_{k}^{g},\mathbf{a}_{k}^{g})$ forms the $k$ -th trainable rank-1 component of branch $g$ , with $B_{g}A_{g}=\sum_{k=1}^{r_{g}}\mathbf{b}_{k}^{g}(\mathbf{a}_{k}^{g})^{\top}$ . For the orthogonal branch, this component is defined before applying the fixed projectors $P_{\perp}$ and $Q_{\perp}$ .

Scaling rank-1 updates. Given this component-wise view, we attach a rank-1 update scale $\alpha_{k}^{g}$ to each factor pair. Let $\tilde{\Delta}\mathbf{b}_{k}^{g}$ and $\tilde{\Delta}\mathbf{a}_{k}^{g}$ denote the raw updates proposed by the base optimizer. The rescaled updates are

[Tableau] $$\Delta\mathbf{b}^{g}_{k}=\alpha^{g}_{k}\tilde{\Delta}\mathbf{b}^{g}_{k},\qquad\Delta\mathbf{a}^{g}_{k}=\alpha^{g}_{k}\tilde{\Delta}\mathbf{a}^{g}_{k}.$$ (11)

This post-optimizer scaling leaves the base optimizer’s raw-update computation unchanged and introduces no additional trainable parameters.

Gradient-guided scale computation. We compute $\alpha_{k}^{g}$ from gradient-based signals at the component and branch levels. The component signal $u_{k}^{g}$ measures the gradient magnitude of the $k$ -th rank-1 component, and the branch signal $\mu^{g}$ averages these signals within branch $g$ :

[Tableau] $$u^{g}_{k}=\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{b}^{g}_{k}}\mathcal{L}_{ST}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}^{g}_{k}}\mathcal{L}_{ST}\right\|_{2},\qquad\mu^{g}=\frac{1}{r_{g}}\sum_{k=1}^{r_{g}}u^{g}_{k}.$$ (12)

These signals are normalized into branch and component scores and converted into the final rank-1 update scale:

[Tableau] $$\pi^{g}_{k}=\frac{u^{g}_{k}+\epsilon}{\sum_{j=1}^{r_{g}}(u^{g}_{j}+\epsilon)},\qquad\rho^{g}=\frac{\mu^{g}+\epsilon}{\sum_{g^{\prime}\in\{p,o\}}(\mu^{g^{\prime}}+\epsilon)},\qquad\alpha^{g}_{k}=r\times\operatorname{sg}\!\left(\rho^{g}\pi^{g}_{k}\right),$$ (13)

where $\pi_{k}^{g}$ is the component score, $\rho^{g}$ is the branch score, $r=r_{p}+r_{o}$ , $\epsilon>0$ is a numerical constant, and $\operatorname{sg}(\cdot)$ denotes stop-gradient.

Since $\rho^{g}$ and $\pi_{k}^{g}$ are normalized across branches and within each branch, respectively, $\alpha_{k}^{g}$ has unit average over all $r$ low-rank components. Thus, the scaling reallocates update magnitude toward high-signal components while preserving the average layer-level update scale. Additional details and diagnostics are provided in Appendix B .

#### 4.1 Experimental Settings

For global PSWD, we adapt Aurora [ 4 ] on WeatherBench 2 [ 16 ] . Given $\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{LR}}_{t}=(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{LR}}_{t-\delta_{c}},\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{LR}}_{t})$ on the $5.625^{\circ}$ grid with $\delta_{c}=6\mathrm{h}$ , we predict $\mathcal{Y}^{\mathrm{HR}}_{t}$ on the $1.5^{\circ}$ grid at leads $\mathcal{H}_{f}=\{1\mathrm{h},\ldots,6\mathrm{h}\}$ , i.e., $\delta_{f}=1\mathrm{h}$ . The 68 target channels comprise three surface variables and five upper-air variables at 13 pressure levels. We report results for five representative variables: 500 hPa geopotential (Z500), 850 hPa temperature (T850), 2m temperature (T2M), and 10m wind components (U10 and V10). Table 1 reports latitude-weighted RMSE and ACC at 3h (an intermediate lead) and 6h (the endpoint) as mean $\pm$ standard deviation over three seeds. All methods train both correction heads within the same scaffold-and-correct pipeline; Full FT also updates the backbone, while Head only keeps it frozen. The low-rank methods adapt the same attention projections. PROLA, LoRA [ 13 ] , DoRA [ 17 ] , PiSSA [ 14 ] , and OPLoRA [ 15 ] use total rank $r=8$ , and AdaLoRA [ 18 ] targets rank 8.

We extend the evaluation to ERA5-to-CERRA downscaling [ 19 , 20 ] , using coarse ERA5 inputs at $t-6\mathrm{h}$ and $t$ to predict high-resolution CERRA fields over Central Europe at leads $\mathcal{H}_{f}=\{3\mathrm{h},6\mathrm{h}\}$ . Table 3 reports latitude-weighted RMSE for the same five variables at 3h. Regional precipitation forecasting follows the protocol in [ 12 ] , using ERA5 inputs at $t-12\mathrm{h}$ and $t$ to predict 6h precipitation totals ending at each forecast valid time. Table 3 reports SEEPS, RMSE, and ACC at 12h. Both tasks compare PROLA with LoRA, PiSSA, and OPLoRA. See Appendices C – F for experimental details and metric definitions.


#### 4.2 Main Results

Table 1 shows that PROLA achieves the lowest RMSE and highest ACC among PEFT methods across all reported variables and leads. Relative to AdaLoRA (36% more trainable parameters), PROLA reduces Z500 RMSE by 5.3% at 3h (50.58 vs. 53.40) and 6.7% at 6h (72.35 vs. 77.51), with reductions of 2.3–4.0% and 2.7–3.5% on the remaining variables. These results suggest that structured low-rank adaptation provides useful implicit regularization in PSWD by limiting backbone update flexibility.

PROLA remains closer to one at shorter wavelengths, supporting improved spatial spectral fidelity. #### 5.3 Update Geometry under a Fixed Rank Budget

We apply Eq. ( 10 ) to compare how PROLA and PiSSA use the same rank budget. Let $\mathcal{A}$ index the adapted attention matrices. For each $\ell\in\mathcal{A}$ , we consider the effective update $\Delta W_{\ell}^{\mathrm{eff}}=W_{\ell}^{\mathrm{eff}}-W_{\ell,0}$ , which includes the learned changes from both branches in PROLA. We decompose this update using the top $r$ left and right singular vectors of the pretrained weight $W_{\ell,0}$ . All methods are evaluated using these same reference subspaces. Let $\Delta W_{\ell}^{b}$ denote the component within both reference subspaces ( $b=\mathrm{prin}$ ), outside both ( $\mathrm{orth}$ ), or within one and outside the other ( $\mathrm{cross}$ , summing both cross blocks). The energy share of block $b$ is obtained by summing its squared Frobenius norm across adapted matrices and normalizing by the total update energy: $\bar{e}_{b}=\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{A}}\|\Delta W_{\ell}^{b}\|_{F}^{2}/\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{A}}\|\Delta W_{\ell}^{\mathrm{eff}}\|_{F}^{2}$ .

> Figure 5: Blockwise update energy. Fractions of total update energy in the principal, cross, and orthogonal blocks.

Figure 5 shows that PROLA more than doubles the orthogonal share of update energy relative to PiSSA (34% vs. 16%). PROLA retains 23% of its update energy in the principal block and 42% in the cross blocks, whereas PiSSA places 18% in the principal block and concentrates 66% in the cross blocks. The principal factors in PiSSA remain freely trainable after initialization, allowing it to learn corrections outside its initial principal subspaces. Under the same rank budget, PROLA achieves a larger orthogonal share, accompanied by a lower cross-block share and a higher principal share. Thus, its increased share of orthogonal corrections is maintained alongside updates within the pretrained principal subspaces. For reference, the orthogonal share is 93% for unconstrained Full FT under the same partition. See Appendix A for the projector definitions and energy aggregation used in this comparison.


### 6 Related Work

Data-driven weather modeling. Data-driven weather forecasting has progressed from early global models [ 21 , 22 ] to systems achieving competitive or state-of-the-art performance on global benchmarks [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 16 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 ] , alongside hybrid physics-ML and probabilistic systems [ 27 , 28 ] . Weather downscaling reconstructs high-resolution fields from low-resolution inputs through spatial statistical or generative refinement [ 5 , 8 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 ] , with fewer studies addressing time-evolving or joint spatiotemporal refinement [ 7 , 34 , 35 ] . Pretrained weather foundation models (WFMs) support diverse atmospheric tasks [ 4 , 36 , 37 , 38 ] , with their growing scale motivating PEFT [ 12 ] . Existing work often treats forecasting, spatial downscaling, temporal refinement, and WFM adaptation separately, whereas PSWD couples forecasting with spatiotemporal refinement in one objective and provides a comprehensive PEFT benchmark for WFMs.

Low-rank parameter-efficient fine-tuning. PEFT avoids costly full fine-tuning by adapting large pretrained backbones with few trainable parameters [ 39 ] . LoRA trains low-rank factors with a frozen backbone [ 13 ] ; variants add quantization, dynamic rank selection, parameter sharing, adaptive rank allocation, factor-wise learning rates, and magnitude-direction decomposition [ 17 , 18 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 ] . Other methods use SVD/decomposition-based initialization [ 14 , 44 , 45 ] to exploit pretrained weight structure, or orthogonality constraints to reduce interference with pretrained or previously learned subspaces [ 15 , 46 , 47 ] . Related approaches distribute principal components across devices [ 48 ] , restrict orthogonal transformations to selected subspaces [ 49 , 50 ] , or combine coefficient updates within a fixed principal basis with orthogonal-complement adaptation [ 51 ] . PROLA splits a fixed rank budget between principal and orthogonal branches, trains its principal factors, and scales optimizer updates using gradient-based branch and rank-1 component scores.


### 7 Conclusion

We formulated Predictive Spatiotemporal Weather Downscaling (PSWD) as predicting future high-resolution global weather trajectories from recent coarse-resolution global states. We addressed PSWD with a spatial-to-temporal scaffold-and-correct pipeline that reuses a shared pretrained backbone and reconstructs target trajectories with fixed numerical scaffolds and lightweight residual heads. For parameter-efficient backbone adaptation, we proposed PROLA (Principal-Orthogonal Low-rank Adaptation), which splits a fixed rank budget across principal and orthogonal branches and rescales rank-1 optimizer updates using gradient signals. Experiments show that PROLA outperforms low-rank PEFT baselines under matched low-rank budgets, indicating that preserving pretrained structure while reserving capacity for orthogonal corrections is effective for PSWD.

[Tableau] $$P_{\ell}=U_{r,\ell}U_{r,\ell}^{\top},\qquad Q_{\ell}=V_{r,\ell}V_{r,\ell}^{\top},\qquad P_{\perp,\ell}=I-P_{\ell},\qquad Q_{\perp,\ell}=I-Q_{\ell}.$$ (15)

The principal, cross, and orthogonal blocks are

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\Delta W_{\ell}^{\mathrm{prin}}$ $\displaystyle=P_{\ell}\Delta W_{\ell}^{\mathrm{eff}}Q_{\ell},$ (16) $\displaystyle\Delta W_{\ell}^{\mathrm{cross}}$ $\displaystyle=P_{\ell}\Delta W_{\ell}^{\mathrm{eff}}Q_{\perp,\ell}+P_{\perp,\ell}\Delta W_{\ell}^{\mathrm{eff}}Q_{\ell},$ (17) $\displaystyle\Delta W_{\ell}^{\mathrm{orth}}$ $\displaystyle=P_{\perp,\ell}\Delta W_{\ell}^{\mathrm{eff}}Q_{\perp,\ell}.$ (18)

PROLA constructs its orthogonal branch using the top- $r_{p}$ directions $U_{p,\ell},V_{p,\ell}$ , with $r_{p}=4$ :

[Tableau] $$\Delta W_{o,\ell}=s_{o,\ell}(I-U_{p,\ell}U_{p,\ell}^{\top})B_{o,\ell}A_{o,\ell}(I-V_{p,\ell}V_{p,\ell}^{\top}).$$ (19)

The branch therefore satisfies

[Tableau] $$U_{p,\ell}^{\top}\Delta W_{o,\ell}=0,\qquad\Delta W_{o,\ell}V_{p,\ell}=0.$$ (20)

These identities concern the top- $r_{p}$ construction subspaces. Under the top- $r$ diagnostic partition, this branch may also contribute to the principal and cross blocks. The freely trainable principal branch may contribute to the orthogonal block. Figure 5 measures blocks of the combined effective update.

Normalized block energy. For each block $b\in\{\mathrm{prin},\mathrm{cross},\mathrm{orth}\}$ , we sum its squared Frobenius norm across adapted matrices and normalize by the total update energy:

[Tableau] $$\bar{e}_{b}=\frac{\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{A}}\|\Delta W_{\ell}^{b}\|_{F}^{2}}{\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{A}}\|\Delta W_{\ell}^{\mathrm{eff}}\|_{F}^{2}}.$$ (21)


### Appendix B Details of Gradient-Guided Scaling of Optimizer Updates

This appendix provides additional details on the gradient-guided scaling of optimizer updates introduced in Section 3.2 . The scaling is applied to the trainable low-rank factors of each adapted layer $\ell\in\mathcal{A}$ . Let $B^{g}_{\ell}$ and $A^{g}_{\ell}$ denote the low-rank factors of branch $g\in\{p,o\}$ at layer $\ell$ , where $p$ and $o$ denote the principal and orthogonal branches, respectively. The $k$ -th rank-1 component is represented by the factor pair $(\mathbf{b}^{g}_{\ell,k},\mathbf{a}^{g}_{\ell,k})$ , where $\mathbf{b}^{g}_{\ell,k}$ is the $k$ -th column of $B^{g}_{\ell}$ and $(\mathbf{a}^{g}_{\ell,k})^{\top}$ is the $k$ -th row of $A^{g}_{\ell}$ . For the orthogonal branch, this component is defined before applying its fixed projectors, as specified in Appendix F .

The component signal and branch signal are

[Tableau] $$u^{g}_{\ell,k}=\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{b}^{g}_{\ell,k}}\mathcal{L}_{ST}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}^{g}_{\ell,k}}\mathcal{L}_{ST}\right\|_{2},\qquad\mu^{g}_{\ell}=\frac{1}{r_{g}}\sum_{k=1}^{r_{g}}u^{g}_{\ell,k}.$$ (22)

They are normalized into the component score and branch score:

[Tableau] $$\pi^{g}_{\ell,k}=\frac{u^{g}_{\ell,k}+\epsilon}{\sum_{j=1}^{r_{g}}(u^{g}_{\ell,j}+\epsilon)},\qquad\rho^{g}_{\ell}=\frac{\mu^{g}_{\ell}+\epsilon}{\sum_{g^{\prime}\in\{p,o\}}(\mu^{g^{\prime}}_{\ell}+\epsilon)}.$$ (23)

The rank-1 update scale is

[Tableau] $$\alpha^{g}_{\ell,k}=r\times\operatorname{sg}\!\left(\rho^{g}_{\ell}\pi^{g}_{\ell,k}\right),\qquad r=r_{p}+r_{o},$$ (24)

where $\epsilon>0$ is a numerical constant and $\operatorname{sg}(\cdot)$ denotes stop-gradient. The stop-gradient operation treats the scores as measurements of the current training signal and prevents gradients from being propagated through the score computation. For single-branch variants, the branch sums and normalizations are restricted to the existing branch, for which $\rho^{g}_{\ell}=1$ .

We apply $\alpha^{g}_{\ell,k}$ after the base optimizer has formed its update. Let $\widetilde{\Delta}\mathbf{b}^{g}_{\ell,k}$ and $\widetilde{\Delta}\mathbf{a}^{g}_{\ell,k}$ denote the optimizer updates before scaling. The applied updates are

[Tableau] $$\Delta\mathbf{b}^{g}_{\ell,k}=\alpha^{g}_{\ell,k}\widetilde{\Delta}\mathbf{b}^{g}_{\ell,k},\qquad\Delta\mathbf{a}^{g}_{\ell,k}=\alpha^{g}_{\ell,k}\widetilde{\Delta}\mathbf{a}^{g}_{\ell,k}.$$ (25)

Thus, gradient-guided scaling rescales the applied parameter updates after the base optimizer computes them, without changing the base optimizer’s raw-update computation.

Mean-preserving property. Since $\sum_{k=1}^{r_{g}}\pi^{g}_{\ell,k}=1$ and $\sum_{g\in\{p,o\}}\rho^{g}_{\ell}=1$ , we have

[Tableau] $$\frac{1}{r}\sum_{g\in\{p,o\}}\sum_{k=1}^{r_{g}}\alpha^{g}_{\ell,k}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{g\in\{p,o\}}\sum_{k=1}^{r_{g}}r\rho^{g}_{\ell}\pi^{g}_{\ell,k}=1.$$ (26)

Therefore, the multiplicative scales have unit mean across the $r$ rank-1 components, while their relative values redistribute update strength.

For the scaling variants in Table 5 , $\rho$ -only scaling uses uniform component allocation within each branch, giving $\alpha^{g}_{\ell,k}=(r/r_{g})\operatorname{sg}(\rho^{g}_{\ell})$ . The $\pi$ -only variant fixes branch allocation at $r_{g}/r$ , giving $\alpha^{g}_{\ell,k}=r_{g}\operatorname{sg}(\pi^{g}_{\ell,k})$ . Both variants retain the two branches with $r_{p}=r_{o}=4$ ; disabling scaling sets all $\alpha^{g}_{\ell,k}$ to one.

Component concentration diagnostic. We examine how gradient-guided scaling distributes optimizer updates across rank-1 components within each layer. Using the mean-preserving property, we define the normalized update-scale distribution as

[Tableau] $$\omega^{g}_{\ell,k}=\frac{\alpha^{g}_{\ell,k}}{r}.$$ (27)

The normalized entropy is

[Tableau] $$H_{\ell}=-\frac{1}{\log r}\sum_{g\in\{p,o\}}\sum_{k=1}^{r_{g}}\omega_{\ell,k}^{g}\log\omega_{\ell,k}^{g}.$$ (28)

At a fixed trained checkpoint, we compute $H_{\ell}$ separately for each validation batch and average over batches. Within each attention block, we then average the entropies of the QKV and output projections.

Figure 6 shows that mean entropy falls below the uniform value of $1$ in every block, with the largest departures in the middle layers. Even the lowest block average, approximately $0.56$ , remains well above the single-component limit of $0$ . These averages indicate that scaling departs from uniform allocation while remaining distributed across multiple rank-1 components, including in the middle layers.

### Appendix D Evaluation Metrics

For the main global PSWD benchmark, we report latitude-weighted root mean squared error (RMSE) and anomaly correlation coefficient (ACC). Let $v$ denote a reported variable, $h\in\mathcal{H}_{f}$ a target lead time, and $t_{n}$ the anchor time of sample $n$ . At latitude index $i$ and longitude index $j$ , $\hat{y}^{(v,h)}_{n,i,j}$ and $y^{(v,h)}_{n,i,j}$ denote the denormalized values of variable $v$ in $\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{ST}_{t_{n}+h}$ and $\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{HR}}_{t_{n}+h}$ , respectively. Here $(H,W)=(H_{\mathrm{HR}},W_{\mathrm{HR}})$ . We use latitude weights

[Tableau] $$w_{i}=\frac{\cos(\varphi_{i})}{\frac{1}{H}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{H}\cos(\varphi_{i^{\prime}})},$$ (29)

where $\varphi_{i}$ is the latitude at row $i$ . Following [ 12 ] , we report the mean of sample-wise latitude-weighted RMSE values:

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{RMSE}_{v,h}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sqrt{\frac{1}{HW}\sum_{i=1}^{H}\sum_{j=1}^{W}w_{i}\left(\hat{y}^{(v,h)}_{n,i,j}-y^{(v,h)}_{n,i,j}\right)^{2}}.$$ (30)

For ACC, we subtract a temporal mean field from both predictions and targets. Let $c^{(v)}_{i,j}$ denote the temporal mean of the denormalized high-resolution ground-truth fields for variable $v$ :

[Tableau] $$c^{(v)}_{i,j}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{clim}}|}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{clim}}}y^{(v)}_{\tau,i,j},$$ (31)

where $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{clim}}$ contains the training-period target valid times from 2007–2016. We compute sample-wise anomaly correlations and then average over samples:

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{ACC}_{v,h}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{H}\sum_{j=1}^{W}w_{i}\left(\hat{y}^{(v,h)}_{n,i,j}-c^{(v)}_{i,j}\right)\left(y^{(v,h)}_{n,i,j}-c^{(v)}_{i,j}\right)}{\max\!\left\{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{H}\sum_{j=1}^{W}w_{i}\left(\hat{y}^{(v,h)}_{n,i,j}-c^{(v)}_{i,j}\right)^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{H}\sum_{j=1}^{W}w_{i}\left(y^{(v,h)}_{n,i,j}-c^{(v)}_{i,j}\right)^{2}},\epsilon\right\}}.$$ (32)

Here, $\epsilon=10^{-8}$ . Weather fields are evaluated after denormalization: RMSE is in the corresponding physical units, and ACC is dimensionless. For CERRA, we use the same sample-wise RMSE aggregation on the regional target grid.

Seasonal and spatial-tail evaluation. Seasonal results group test cases into DJF, MAM, JJA, and SON according to the target valid time. For spatial-tail evaluation, we identify the upper and lower 1% of grid points within each target field using its 99th and 1st percentiles. Tail RMSE uses latitude weighting over the target-selected grid points. For the Brier skill score (BSS), forecast and observed event indicators use the same target-derived threshold, and the reference assigns event probability $0.01$ to every grid point. We report $1-\mathrm{BS}/\mathrm{BS}_{\mathrm{ref}}$ , with latitude weighting for both scores.

Regional precipitation metrics. We report RMSE, ACC, and the Stable Equitable Error in Probability Space (SEEPS) [ 55 ] for 6-hour accumulated precipitation at each evaluated lead time. Precipitation RMSE is the square root of the latitude-weighted mean squared error over all evaluated times and grid points, and is reported in cm. ACC is averaged over samples using a training-period climatology indexed by day of year and hour of day. SEEPS divides precipitation into dry, light, and heavy categories using a dry threshold of $0.1$ mm and the local two-thirds quantile of wet precipitation. We report latitude-weighted mean SEEPS over locations with climatological dry fractions between $0.1$ and $0.85$ . Lower SEEPS indicates better performance.

Spherical harmonic spectra. Following spectral evaluation in global weather forecasting [ 28 , 52 ] , we decompose each predicted and target field into orthonormal spherical harmonics. For fixed $v$ and $h$ , write $\hat{y}_{n}$ and $y_{n}$ for the predicted and target fields of sample $n$ at valid time $t_{n}+h$ . For a field $y_{n}$ with coefficients $a_{n,\ell,m}$ , the power at degree $\ell$ is

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(y_{n})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}|a_{n,\ell,m}|^{2}.$$ (33)

We compute power separately for each case and plot the ratio of mean prediction power to mean target power,

[Tableau] $$\frac{N^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\hat{y}_{n})}{N^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(y_{n})}.$$ (34)

A ratio of one denotes matching power at that spatial scale. Figure 3 uses T2M at 6h from the seed-0 models over all 1,458 test cases in 2018. We evaluate degrees $\ell=1,\ldots,59$ on the native $120\times 240$ grid and convert degree to wavelength as $2\pi R/\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}$ , with Earth’s radius $R=6371$ km.

Text-to-SQL evaluation. For BIRD [ 54 ] , we report execution accuracy (EX), the percentage of predicted SQL queries whose execution results match those of the reference queries on the corresponding databases. We compute EX using a custom SQLite evaluator with unordered result-set comparison.

Aggregation across seeds. For the main comparisons, we compute each test metric separately for seeds 0, 1, and 2, and report the mean and sample standard deviation across these three values. The standard deviation uses denominator $n-1$ .


### Appendix E Implementation Details

The main Aurora training configuration uses two NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs. For PEFT and Head only experiments, we use per-GPU batch size $4$ with gradient accumulation over $4$ steps, giving an effective global batch size of $32$ . For Full FT, we use per-GPU batch size $2$ with gradient accumulation over $8$ steps, giving the same effective global batch size of $32$ . Models in this benchmark are trained for $10$ epochs. We use AdamW [ 56 ] with $(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(0.9,0.999)$ , $\epsilon=10^{-8}$ , and weight decay $0.05$ . The learning rate follows a step-level cosine schedule with linear warmup. For PEFT and Head only experiments, the base learning rate is $5\times 10^{-4}$ , the warmup learning rate is $10^{-6}$ , the minimum learning rate is $10^{-6}$ , and warmup lasts for one epoch. For Full FT, the backbone learning rate is $5\times 10^{-5}$ , with warmup and minimum learning rates $10^{-7}$ . The correction heads in Full FT use the learning rate $5\times 10^{-4}$ . Gradient norm clipping is applied with a maximum norm of $1.0$ . The main comparisons use seeds 0, 1, and 2, with the same seed set for every method. We evaluate the final checkpoint after the prescribed training budget.

Training objective. For the main Aurora benchmark, we use a weighted L1 loss on fields normalized with Aurora’s variable-specific statistics [ 4 ] . At each lead time, the surface term sums the variable-weighted spatial mean absolute errors and is multiplied by $1/4$ . The upper-air term averages spatial mean absolute errors over pressure levels and then takes a weighted sum across variables. The combined loss is divided by the number of variable types, $3+5=8$ . The surface weights for $(T2M,U10,V10)$ are $(3.0,0.77,0.66)$ , and the upper-air weights for $(Z,T,Q,U,V)$ are $(3.0,1.5,0.6,0.77,0.54)$ , shared across pressure levels. We average $\mathcal{L}_{ST}$ in Eq. ( 5 ) over training examples. Its six lead terms supervise the final predictions $\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{ST}_{t+h}$ for $h\in\mathcal{H}_{f}$ . The spatial endpoint $\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{S}_{t+h_{\mathrm{end}}}$ receives supervision through the temporal stage.

Regional weather experiments. ERA5-to-CERRA adaptation uses $10$ epochs, an effective global batch size of $8$ , and a learning rate of $5\times 10^{-4}$ . Regional precipitation forecasting uses $10$ epochs, an effective global batch size of $32$ , and a learning rate of $3.75\times 10^{-4}$ . Both settings use AdamW with $(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(0.9,0.999)$ , weight decay $0.05$ , and a cosine learning-rate schedule with one epoch of linear warmup. These comparisons use seed 0 and the final checkpoint after 10 epochs. Within each task, LoRA, PiSSA, OPLoRA, and PROLA adapt the same attention projections and train the same task-specific heads.

ArchesWeather experiments. We fine-tune the pretrained ArchesWeather-M backbone [ 52 ] for one epoch, corresponding to 7,300 optimizer steps, with an effective global batch size of $2$ and a learning rate of $3\times 10^{-4}$ . We use AdamW with $(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(0.9,0.98)$ , weight decay $0.05$ , and 250 warmup steps followed by cosine decay. All compared methods adapt the attention projections and train the correction heads, using seed 0 and the final checkpoint.

Text-to-SQL adaptation. We compare the unadapted model with LoRA, PiSSA, OPLoRA, and PROLA. All adapters target attention and MLP projections with rank 16 and 40.1M trainable parameters; PROLA uses $r_{p}=r_{o}=8$ . Each adapter is trained for one epoch with a learning rate of $2\times 10^{-5}$ and seed 42.


### Appendix F PROLA and Baseline Configurations

For the main Aurora comparison, PROLA and all LoRA-style PEFT baselines fine-tune the linear projections in the backbone’s self-attention operations. All other pretrained backbone parameters remain frozen unless the method is Full FT. The spatial correction head $C_{s}$ and temporal correction head $C_{t}$ are trained in all methods in this comparison except where explicitly ablated. Thus, PEFT baseline comparisons use the same prediction pipeline and differ only in the backbone adaptation strategy.

The default main comparison uses rank $r=8$ for all fixed-rank PEFT baselines. LoRA [ 13 ] uses the standard zero-output initialization. Unless otherwise stated, all adapters use no adapter dropout and do not train bias terms. DoRA [ 17 ] uses the same rank, target modules, dropout, and bias setting as LoRA, with the additional magnitude parameter trained following the original parameterization. PiSSA [ 14 ] initializes the low-rank factors from the top- $r$ singular components of each pretrained weight and residualizes the frozen base weight to preserve the pretrained function at initialization. OPLoRA [ 15 ] uses fixed projectors onto the complements of the top-8 left and right singular subspaces of each pretrained weight, with the same trainable rank, target modules, dropout, and bias setting as LoRA.

For PROLA, we use the same total low-rank budget $r=8$ , split evenly into $r_{p}=4$ for the principal branch and $r_{o}=4$ for the orthogonal branch. The reference singular directions and orthogonal projectors are computed from the pretrained weights and kept fixed during adaptation, while the principal factors $A_{p},B_{p}$ remain trainable. We residualize the frozen base weight as in Eq. ( 9 ), set $B_{o,0}=0$ , and sample the entries of $A_{o,0}$ from $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{o}^{2})$ with $\sigma_{o}=0.02$ , preserving the pretrained function. With the principal branch removed ( $r_{p}=0$ , $r_{o}=8$ ), the orthogonal-only ablation retains fixed projectors onto the complements of each pretrained weight’s top-8 left and right singular subspaces. We set $s_{g}=\alpha/r_{g}$ for each PROLA branch $g\in\{p,o\}$ and $s=\alpha/r$ for LoRA, DoRA, PiSSA, and OPLoRA, with $\alpha=1$ for all five methods.

### Appendix J Limitations and Future Work

PSWD is constrained by atmospheric chaos and the limited fine-scale information available in coarse inputs. Our weather evaluation covers a global reanalysis benchmark and regional prediction tasks, including ERA5-to-CERRA transfer to a distinct target dataset. Future work could extend this evaluation to broader regions and target sources, and incorporate physical constraints and probabilistic prediction to improve forecast consistency and reliability.


### Appendix K Broader Impacts

This work aims to improve the efficiency and accuracy of high-resolution weather prediction from coarse global states. More accessible spatiotemporal downscaling may benefit applications such as renewable-energy planning, flood-risk assessment, aviation safety, and local weather-risk analysis, where fine spatial and temporal information is important. However, inaccurate or overconfident forecasts could negatively affect downstream decisions if used without proper uncertainty estimates or expert oversight. For real-world use, such models should be integrated with uncertainty estimates, operational validation protocols, and expert oversight to ensure reliable and responsible decision support.
