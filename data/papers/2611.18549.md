## JVT: Just Video Transformer makes pixel-space video diffusion simple

Direct diffusion modeling of high-dimensional raw video data is challenging, and existing pixel-space methods often rely on specialized architectures. We introduce JVT ( J ust V ideo T ransformer), a simple large-patch DiT-style Transformer for pixel-space video diffusion, without a pretrained autoencoder, auxiliary detailers, cascaded stages, or U-Net-style skip connections. We systematically examine video patch embeddings, register tokens, and adaptive layer normalization to identify design choices that balance generation quality and computational efficiency. The resulting JVT design achieves generation quality competitive with latent diffusion baselines in both class-conditioned and unconditional settings. The pixel-space formulation of JVT also naturally supports joint generation of pixel-aligned modalities. We demonstrate this capability through camera-conditioned RGB-disparity generation, which improves camera following relative to RGB-only baselines and yields explicit scene geometry without an additional reconstruction model at inference. These results highlight the potential of simple DiT-style Transformers for pixel-space video diffusion, and we hope our findings offer useful insights and practical guidance for future research.

These outputs yield (e) colored point clouds through geometric unprojection, without an additional reconstruction model.


### 1 Introduction

Diffusion and flow-based models have become leading paradigms for visual generative modeling, driven by rapid advances in algorithm designs and model architectures. Early diffusion models ( Ho et al., 2020 ; Dhariwal and Nichol, 2021 ) operate directly on pixels with classical U-Nets ( Ronneberger et al., 2015 ) , but struggle with efficiency and fidelity at high resolutions. To alleviate this difficulty, latent diffusion moves denoising from pixel space to a compact latent space produced by a separately trained autoencoder ( Rombach et al., 2022 ) . Combined with the Diffusion Transformer (DiT) architecture ( Peebles and Xie, 2023 ) , this formulation has become a standard design for high-resolution visual generation.

Despite its efficiency, latent diffusion is inherently bounded by the autoencoder, including reconstruction error ( Yu et al., 2026 ; Zheng et al., 2026 ) , additional training and inference costs ( Leng et al., 2025 ; Cheng and Yuan, 2025 ; Wang et al., 2026c ) , and limited cross-modal capability for joint modeling ( Bounoua et al., 2024 ; Qiu et al., 2025 ) . Thus recent works revisit pixel-space diffusion and demonstrate competitive quality on image generation ( Li and He, 2026 ; Yu et al., 2026 ; Lu et al., 2026 ; Wang et al., 2026b ) . In contrast, video generation is still dominated by latent DiTs ( Ma et al., 2025 ; Kong et al., 2024 ; Yang et al., 2025 ; Wan et al., 2025 ) , while pixel-space models mostly retain U-Net-style skip connections ( Gu et al., 2024 ; Bar-Tal et al., 2024 ; Hoogeboom et al., 2025 ) or cascaded generation ( Menapace et al., 2024 ; Skorokhodov et al., 2024 ) . This motivates us to investigate how far a simple DiT-style Transformer can go in direct pixel-space video generation.

We introduce JVT, a pixel-space video generative model built on a simple DiT-style Transformer backbone, without detailers, cascaded stages or U-Net-style skip connections. Following JiT ( Li and He, 2026 ) , JVT predicts clean videos from noisy pixels under a rectified-flow objective. To make raw videos tractable, we explore variants of video patch embedding for efficient spatiotemporal downsampling. We further examine the design choices of register tokens ( Darcet et al., 2024 ) and adaptive layer normalization (AdaLN) ( Peebles and Xie, 2023 ) , which can carry noise level or class condition and amplify it by register guidance ( Starodubcev et al., 2026 ) or classifier-free guidance ( Ho and Salimans, 2021 ) . With these design choices, JVT achieves generation quality competitive with latent diffusion baselines in both class-conditioned and unconditional settings.

Pixel-space video modeling also provides a direct interface for jointly generating video and pixel-aligned modalities, which typically requires reusing an RGB-specific autoencoder in latent diffusion ( Ke et al., 2024 ; Zhu et al., 2025 ; Huang et al., 2026a ) . We extend JVT to camera-conditioned joint RGB-disparity generation to demonstrate this cross-modal capability. Without an additional reconstruction model at inference, the generated disparity yields a sequence of point clouds in world coordinates, which enables explicit 4D world modeling. Experiments further show that joint RGB-disparity generation improves camera following relative to RGB-only JVT.

The main contributions of this work are as follows:

- • We present JVT, the first pixel-space video diffusion model built on a simple DiT-style Transformer backbone, without detailers, cascaded stages, or U-Net-style skip connections.

- • We systematically study video patch embeddings and conditioning mechanisms, identifying designs that improve the quality-efficiency trade-off. In class-conditioned and unconditional settings, JVT achieves generation quality competitive with latent diffusion baselines.

- • We formulate pixel-aligned joint generation as a native extension of JVT. We further demonstrate this capability through camera-conditioned RGB-disparity video generation, which enables explicit 4D scene representation and improves camera following relative to RGB-only generation.


### 2 Related Work

High-resolution pixel-space diffusion. Pixel-space diffusion modeling for high-resolution visual data has long relied on specialized architectures, including cascaded diffusion ( Ho et al., 2022a ; Teng et al., 2024 ) , cross-resolution designs ( Gu et al., 2024 ; Chen et al., 2025b ) , multiscale or U-shaped backbones ( Hoogeboom et al., 2023 ; Crowson et al., 2024 ; Hoogeboom et al., 2025 ; He et al., 2026 ; Guo et al., 2026 ) , frequency decomposition ( Ma et al., 2026b ; Lin et al., 2026 ; Ma et al., 2026a ) , and auxiliary detail modules ( Wang et al., 2026a ; Chen et al., 2026 ; Yu et al., 2026 ) . Though effective, these approaches often introduce additional architectural or training complexity, which can limit efficiency and scalability. Recently, JiT ( Li and He, 2026 ) achieves high-resolution image generation competitive with latent diffusion using a simple large-patch DiT-style Transformer. This paradigm is also extended to one-step image generation ( Lu et al., 2026 ) and text-to-image generation ( Wang et al., 2026b ; Jiang et al., 2026 ) , further demonstrating the effectiveness.

Video Transformer architectures. Modern video Transformers largely retain the standard blocks of 2D Vision Transformers (ViTs), making patch embedding the key adaptation to video inputs ( Tong et al., 2022 ; Wang et al., 2023 ; Bardes et al., 2024 ; Assran et al., 2025 ) . A common choice is tubelet embedding ( Arnab et al., 2021 ) , which encodes non-overlapping spatiotemporal patches into tokens via 3D convolution. This architecture is also widely adopted in latent video DiTs, whereas pixel-space video diffusion methods still typically rely on U-shaped architectures ( Gu et al., 2024 ; Bar-Tal et al., 2024 ; Hoogeboom et al., 2025 ) or cascaded generation ( Menapace et al., 2024 ; Skorokhodov et al., 2024 ) . Our work aims to explore whether a simple DiT-style video Transformer can also support direct pixel-space video generation.


### 3 Method


#### 3.1 Preliminaries

Though denoising diffusion models commonly use noise, score or velocity prediction ( Dhariwal and Nichol, 2021 ; Song et al., 2021 ; Karras et al., 2022 ; Salimans and Ho, 2022 ; Lipman et al., 2023 ; Liu et al., 2023 ) , JiT proposes clean data prediction and velocity loss supervision for pixel-space diffusion ( Li and He, 2026 ) . Following this formulation, JVT predicts clean video $\hat{x}_{0}$ from noisy pixels $x_{t}$ :

[Tableau] $$x_{t}=(1-t)x_{0}+t\epsilon,\qquad\hat{x}_{0}=f_{\theta}(x_{t},t,c),\qquad\hat{v}=(\hat{x}_{0}-x_{t})/t,$$ (1)

where noise $\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ , noise level $t\in(0,1]$ , and $c$ denotes the available conditioning. Thus the standard rectified-flow velocity loss can be represented as:

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE}}=\mathbb{E}\left\|\hat{v}-v\right\|_{2}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\|(\hat{x}_{0}-x_{0})/t\|_{2}^{2},\qquad v=x_{0}-\epsilon=(x_{0}-x_{t})/t.$$ (2)

> Figure 2: JVT architecture and register token design . The network predicts a clean video $\hat{x}_{0}$ from a noisy input $x_{t}$ . Register tokens are prepended to visual tokens after the first $L_{1}$ blocks, and then discarded after the remaining $L_{2}$ blocks, where $L=L_{1}+L_{2}$ .


#### 3.2 Patch Embedding

Patch embedding is central to pixel-space DiTs, as it provides the initial downsampling that latent DiTs obtain from an autoencoder. For videos, it also aggregates spatiotemporal information while controlling the token sequence length.

Following standard video Transformer designs, we use plain tubelet embedding as our baseline. As illustrated in Fig. 3 (a), it uses a single 3D convolution to project each non-overlapping patch of temporal size $p_{t}$ and spatial size $p_{h}\times p_{w}$ into a $C$ -dimensional token.

Bottleneck structure. Following JiT ( Li and He, 2026 ) , we introduce a channel bottleneck into tubelet embedding (Fig. 3 (b)). A 3D convolution first projects each patch into a $C_{b}$ -dimensional representation, which is then expanded to $C$ channels by a pointwise projection. We typically choose $C_{b}<C$ to encourage compact, low-dimensional representations ( Tishby et al., 2000 ; Rifai et al., 2011 ; Makhzani and Frey, 2013 ; Alemi et al., 2017 ) .

> Figure 3: Video patch embedding variants (left) and their convolutional components (right) . Assume non-overlapping patches with size $p_{t}\times p_{h}\times p_{w}$ . $T$ , $H$ , $W$ are divisible by $p_{t}$ , $p_{h}$ , $p_{w}$ , respectively.

Spatiotemporal factorization. Inspired by the factorization principle of depthwise separable convolution ( Chollet, 2017 ; Howard et al., 2017 ) , we separate the spatial and temporal operations of tubelet embedding. Specifically, a patch-wise 2D convolution extracts spatial features within each frame, while a frame-wise 1D convolution aggregates temporal information at each spatial location. Applying the spatial operation first yields patch-first embedding (Fig. 3 (c)), while reversing the order yields frame-first embedding (Fig. 3 (d)).


#### 3.3 Register Tokens

Register tokens provide additional computational slots in ViTs ( Darcet et al., 2024 ) and also support in-context conditioning and improve generation quality in pixel-space image DiTs ( Li and He, 2026 ; Starodubcev et al., 2026 ) . We extend this mechanism to JVT and examine its roles in conditioning and sampling guidance.

As illustrated in Fig. 2 , given $L=L_{1}+L_{2}$ Transformer blocks, register tokens are prepended to the visual sequence after the first $L_{1}$ blocks and discarded after the remaining $L_{2}$ blocks. Let $X^{(L_{1})}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times C}$ denote the $N$ visual tokens at the insertion point and $R_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell_{r}\times C}$ the $\ell_{r}$ learnable base registers. With $e_{t},e_{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}$ denoting the noise level and class embeddings, respectively, we construct the register tokens and augmented sequence as

[Tableau] $$R=R_{0}+\operatorname{Repeat}(e_{r},\ell_{r}),\qquad\widetilde{X}^{(L_{1})}=\operatorname{Concat}(R,X^{(L_{1})}),$$ (3)

where $e_{r}\in\{0,e_{t},e_{y},e_{t}+e_{y}\}$ and $\operatorname{Repeat}(e_{r},\ell_{r})$ replicates $e_{r}$ across the $\ell_{r}$ register positions. Setting $e_{r}=0$ preserves $R=R_{0}$ without adding a conditioning embedding, which is different from removing the registers. For $e_{r}\in\{e_{t},e_{y},e_{t}+e_{y}\}$ , the register tokens provide an additional conditioning pathway alongside AdaLN.

Register guidance. We also apply register guidance (RG) ( Starodubcev et al., 2026 ) during sampling. For the same noisy input and condition, let $v_{r}$ and $v_{nr}$ denote the velocity predictions of the same model with and without registers, respectively. RG amplifies the register contribution through

[Tableau] $$v_{rg}=v_{r}+w_{rg}(v_{r}-v_{nr}),\qquad w_{rg}\geq 0.$$ (4)

For conditional generation, let $v_{u}$ denote the unconditional velocity prediction with registers retained. Combining RG with classifier-free guidance (CFG) ( Ho and Salimans, 2021 ) gives

[Tableau] $$v_{cfg+rg}=v_{u}+w_{cfg}(v_{r}-v_{u})+w_{rg}(v_{r}-v_{nr}).$$ (5)

The scales $w_{cfg}$ and $w_{rg}$ separately weight the CFG and RG terms: setting $w_{rg}=0$ recovers CFG, while $w_{cfg}=1$ recovers RG in Eq. ( 4 ). Unlike the original RG procedure ( Starodubcev et al., 2026 ) , which randomly drops registers during training, we keep them active throughout training and remove them only to compute $v_{nr}$ during sampling. Our experiments show that RG remains effective without register dropout during training.


#### 3.4 Pixel-aligned Joint Generation

Pixel-aligned signals about geometry, motion, and semantic structure provide complementary cues for video generation. By channel-wise concatenating RGB frames with these signals ( Ke et al., 2024 ) , diffusion models can jointly generate RGB content and spatially aligned auxiliary modalities ( Zhu et al., 2025 ; Chen et al., 2025a ; Huang et al., 2026a ) .

Pixel-space modeling allows these modalities to be represented on a shared spatiotemporal grid without an RGB-specific autoencoder. After modality-specific normalization, we concatenate an RGB video $x_{\mathrm{rgb}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times 3\times H\times W}$ with $M$ pixel-aligned modalities along the channel dimension:

[Tableau] $$x_{0}=[x_{\mathrm{rgb}};x^{(1)};\ldots;x^{(M)}]\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times C_{\mathrm{joint}}\times H\times W},$$ (6)

where $x^{(m)}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times C_{m}\times H\times W}$ denotes the $m$ -th aligned modality and $C_{\mathrm{joint}}=3+\sum_{m=1}^{M}C_{m}$ is the total number of channels. These modalities may include depth, surface normals, ray maps, segmentation masks, and optical flow. All modalities share the same noise level and are predicted jointly during denoising.

We instantiate this formulation with RGB and disparity, a reparameterization of depth, for camera-conditioned video generation (Sec. 4.3 ). At inference, we convert the predicted disparity back to depth and unproject it using the camera parameters. Combined with the generated RGB frames, this yields a sequence of colored point clouds without an additional reconstruction model.


### 4 Experiments


#### 4.1 Main Results

Datasets. For class-conditioned generation and unconditional generation, we conduct experiments on four datasets: UCF-101 ( Soomro et al., 2012 ) , FaceForensics ( Rössler et al., 2018 ) , SkyTimelapse ( Xiong et al., 2018 ) , and Taichi-HD ( Siarohin et al., 2019 ) . Following settings of previous works ( Skorokhodov et al., 2022 ; Ma et al., 2025 ) , we use both training and test splits of UCF-101, and only the training split of the other datasets. We randomly extract 16-frame video clips with a frame interval of 3, and resize all video frames to $256\times 256$ resolution for training.

Evaluation metrics. We evaluate generation quality with Fréchet Video Distance (FVD) ( Unterthiner et al., 2019 ) , using 2,048 generated samples. For efficiency metrics, we report parameter count, floating point operations (FLOPs), peak allocated GPU memory and inference latency. Additional details about these metrics are given in Appendix ( A.2 , A.4 ).

Baselines. Our main baseline is Latte ( Ma et al., 2025 ) , a representative DiT-based latent diffusion model. Other competing methods include GAN-based methods ( Skorokhodov et al., 2022 ; Yu et al., 2022 ) , autoregressive models ( Yan et al., 2021 ; Wang et al., 2024a ) , U-Net-based latent diffusion ( He et al., 2022 ; Yu et al., 2023b ) and DiT-based latent diffusion ( Liu et al., 2024 ) .

As shown in Tab. 1 , RG works well on UCF-101, but brings no obvious gains on the other 3 datasets. Without register guidance, JVT variants with and without registers exhibit broadly similar FVD scores and intermediate feature-map smoothness. However, as illustrated in Fig. 4 , visual tokens attend strongly to register tokens immediately after their insertion ( $L_{1}=9$ ) on UCF-101, while this interaction is noticeably weaker on FaceForensics, SkyTimelapse, and Taichi-HD.

Adaptive layer normalization. Standard DiTs inject noise level and class information through adaptive layer normalization (AdaLN) ( Peebles and Xie, 2023 ) . However, recent works start to explore AdaLN-free DiTs for image generation, using in-context tokens, cross-attention, or multimodal joint attention for conditioning ( Geng et al., 2026 ; Lu et al., 2026 ; Zeng et al., 2026 ; Wang et al., 2026b ) . In Tab. 4 , we examine whether register-based conditioning offers a reliable alternative to AdaLN in JVT. Relative to the baseline, neither adding class-conditioned registers nor removing noise level conditioning improves FVD. With class-only conditioning, replacing AdaLN with register tokens further worsens FVD to 156.3. We also observe no clear efficiency gain from removing AdaLN. Unconditional ablations show no consistent FVD improvement from jointly removing noise level conditioning and AdaLN (Appendix A.3 , Tab. 11 ). These results support retaining standard AdaLN as JVT’s default conditioning mechanism.


#### 4.3 Camera-conditioned Video Generation

> Figure 5: Joint RGB-disparity generation and point-cloud reconstruction on RealEstate10K . We show generated RGB frames alongside aligned disparity maps. Colored point clouds are obtained by unprojecting depth recovered from disparity using the camera parameters, without an additional reconstruction model at inference.

We instantiate the pixel-aligned joint generation framework in Sec. 3.4 for camera-conditioned video generation. Given an initial RGB frame and a target camera trajectory, we jointly generate RGB frames and aligned disparity maps. After recovering depth from disparity, the camera parameters allow the predictions to be unprojected into a sequence of colored point clouds, providing an explicit 4D representation of the generated scene, as shown in Fig. 5 .

Experimental setting. We conduct 8-frame camera-conditioned video generation experiments on RealEstate10K ( Zhou et al., 2018 ) at a resolution of $256\times 256$ . As dense depth annotations are unavailable, a frozen lightweight 3D reconstruction model, Pi3X ( Wang et al., 2026d ) supplies depth targets online during training. Following prior works ( Song et al., 2026 ; Zhu et al., 2025 ) , we reversibly transform depth $D$ to square-root disparity $d=(D+1)^{-1/2}$ and concatenate it with RGB as a four-channel target.

Evaluation. We evaluate RGB video quality using FVD, and assess camera following by comparing Pi3X-estimated relative poses with the target camera trajectory. Following prior works ( Wang et al., 2024b ; Wang et al., 2025a ; Wang et al., 2026d ) , we report relative rotation accuracy (RRA), relative translation accuracy (RTA), and AUC as camera-following metrics. Specifically, RRA and RTA measure the percentages of evaluated frame pairs whose rotation and translation-direction errors fall below a given threshold, respectively, while AUC summarizes their joint error distribution. We use thresholds of $5^{\circ}$ and $30^{\circ}$ .

We compare JVT with pixel-space U-ViT models ( Song et al., 2025 ; Wu et al., 2026 ) and a latent-space DiT ( Huang et al., 2026b ) . As shown in Tab. 5 , both JVT variants achieve lower FVD than these baselines. Joint RGB-disparity generation achieves the highest RTA and AUC among all compared methods, with only a slight increase in FVD relative to RGB-only JVT. Across methods, RRA is consistently high, whereas RTA remains substantially lower. This gap highlights a key limitation of these camera-conditioned video generators: they can follow prescribed camera rotations relatively reliably, but are less accurate at reproducing camera translations. Joint RGB-disparity generation narrows this gap, suggesting that explicit depth cues help the model relate camera motion to scene geometry. Within a unified pixel-space generative framework, JVT natively supports this joint modeling of RGB and geometry and improves camera following.

### 5 Conclusion

We demonstrate pixel-space video generation with a simple DiT-style Transformer, achieving generation quality competitive with latent diffusion baselines in both class-conditioned and unconditional video generation benchmarks. Our ablations study suggests patch-first embedding for spatiotemporal tokenization and standard AdaLN for conditioning. For the register mechanism, register-free JVT provides an acceptable baseline, while register guidance can offer additional gains, particularly on the more diverse UCF-101 dataset.

Pixel-space modeling makes pixel-aligned joint generation straightforward by representing RGB and aligned modalities on a common spatiotemporal grid, without encoding them into a shared pretrained latent space. When extending JVT to camera-conditioned joint RGB-disparity generation, we find camera following is improved relative to RGB-only baselines, with a small FVD trade-off.

These findings highlight the potential of simple DiT-style Transformers for pixel-space video generation and pixel-aligned joint modeling. We hope our empirical findings help guide future efforts to scale pixel-space video generation to larger model size, more datasets, longer videos and higher resolutions.

##### Register tokens.

As shown in Fig. 4 , the register mechanism brings clear gains on UCF-101 but only limited benefits on the other datasets, so we use register tokens only for class-conditioned and unconditional video generation on UCF-101. The register initialization ablation in Tab. 3 further shows no gain from injecting the timestep or class embedding into the register tokens, and we therefore just set $e_{r}=0$ when registers are used. The base registers $R_{0}$ are learnable tokens initialized i.i.d. from a zero-mean Gaussian with standard deviation $0.02$ . All register-equipped variants are sampled with register guidance, whose scale is selected by a linear search with a step size of 0.1.

Training details. All models are trained from scratch. Following JiT ( Li and He, 2026 ) , we use clean data prediction and velocity MSE loss. For noise sampling, we sample noise level $t_{\mathrm{base}}$ from logit-normal distribution ( Esser et al., 2024 ) and shift it with noise shift $s$ , as shown in Eq. ( 7 ). Our full-scale training of JVT use a global batch size of 256 with 8 NVIDIA 80GB GPUs, while some ablations use a batch size of 40 on a single GPU (Fig. 7 ).

[Tableau] $$t=\frac{s\cdot t_{\mathrm{base}}}{1+(s-1)\cdot t_{\mathrm{base}}}$$ (7)

For camera-conditioned generation, we sample the video noise independently for each frame, following DFoT ( Song et al., 2025 ) . We also adopt the data augmentations of the DFoT dataloader: horizontal flipping and temporal reversal, each applied with probability 0.5, and back-and-forth augmentation with probability 0.1.

Sampling configurations. Tab. 7 specifies the exact sampling configuration of each main result. We use the Euler solver for class-conditioned and camera-conditioned generation, and the Heun solver for unconditional generation. All samplers run for 50 denoising steps, with the timestep schedule shifted by the noise shift $s$ (Eq. ( 7 )). Depending on the task, we apply classifier-free guidance (CFG) ( Ho and Salimans, 2021 ) , register guidance (RG) ( Starodubcev et al., 2026 ) , or history guidance (HG) ( Song et al., 2025 ) . The CFG scale is selected by a linear search with a step size of 1, and the RG scale with a step size of 0.1. For FVD evaluation, we generate 2,048 clips per method and use the same number of real clips as the reference.

Geometry annotation for RealEstate10K. As in Sec. 4.3 , dense depth annotations are unavailable for RealEstate10K, so we use a frozen Pi3X ( Wang et al., 2026d ) to supply depth pseudo-labels online during training. For each training clip, Pi3X is conditioned on the prescribed camera intrinsics and extrinsics as priors. Since Pi3X predicts scale-invariant geometry and affine-invariant cameras, we estimate the global scale of the predicted depth by aligning the predicted cameras with the ground-truth cameras. The aligned depth is clamped to a minimum of $10^{-5}$ and, following prior works ( Song et al., 2026 ; Zhu et al., 2025 ) , reversibly transformed to square-root disparity $d=(D+1)^{-1/2}$ , which is concatenated with RGB as a four-channel target.

#### A.2 Efficiency Evaluation Details

The efficiency experiments use JVT-XL with 28 Transformer blocks, width 1,152, 16 attention heads, and spatial patch size 16. Each case processes one 16-frame $256\times 256$ video in pixel space. With a temporal downsampling factor of 4, the denoiser receives 1,024 visual tokens. The register-enabled configuration inserts 32 in-context register tokens after the first 9 blocks, resulting in 1,056 tokens for the remaining blocks.

Sampling uses Euler integration with 50 steps. Register-enabled cases include no guidance, CFG, RG, and CFG+RG, while register-disabled cases include no guidance and CFG. These four guidance settings use one, two, two, and three denoiser-forward equivalents per step, respectively.

All measurements use bfloat16 with TF32 enabled and torch.compile disabled on one NVIDIA 4090 GPU. Each case runs in a fresh Python process. Denoiser latency uses 20 warm-up iterations followed by 100 timed iterations, and sampling latency uses 5 warm-up samples followed by 20 timed samples. The complete matrix is repeated in three independent sweeps. We record model parameters, FLOPs, baseline and peak allocated GPU memory, incremental peak memory, latency statistics, and throughput. FLOPs for one denoiser forward pass are counted with torch.utils.flop_counter.FlopCounterMode . Sampling FLOPs are computed from the matching denoiser FLOPs, 50 sampling steps, and the number of denoiser-forward equivalents per step.
