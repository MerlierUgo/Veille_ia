## Jailbreak Detection on Large Language Models via Contrastive Prototype Structures

Jailbreak attempts and harmful prompts remain a challenge for the safe deployment of large language models. We study whether contrastively learned representations can support safety detection without training an additional downstream classifier. We propose Jailbreak Detection with Prototype Inference (JDPI), which uses binary safety labels to construct harmful and benign prototypes and makes predictions from relative prototype distances. JDPI uses only binary safety labels for encoder adaptation, prototype construction, and validation. In our setup, it uses fewer labeled examples than the classifier baseline (Method A) by avoiding the separate split used to train the downstream classifier.

Across four benchmarks covering different input formats, contrastive fine-tuning improves JDPI’s detection accuracy by 9.1–18.0 percentage points. Using the same encoder, JDPI achieves slightly higher ROC-AUC on one benchmark and stays within 0.017 of the baseline across all four. With dataset-specific adaptation, JDPI exceeds the released Llama Guard 3-8B baseline by 0.173–0.310 in ROC-AUC across the four benchmarks. In multi-turn replay on TomGibbs, JDPI uses accumulated user history as the conversation unfolds, achieving higher conversation-level accuracy than when it uses the current turn alone. Across 12 directed transfers, JDPI outperforms the classifier by an average of 1.59 percentage points in accuracy, demonstrating stronger cross-dataset robustness.


### 1 Introduction

For cybersecurity and safety, AI agents are typically deployed with policy constraints and safety guardrails. However, the increasing use of external tools and environments also introduces emerging security risks, including indirect prompt injection Zhan et al. (2024) , tool-integrated agent security Debenedetti et al. (2024) , and broader agent attack and defense scenarios Zhang et al. (2025a) . Despite these safeguards, security vulnerabilities and failure modes remain Perez et al. (2022) ; Chao et al. (2024) ; Mazeika et al. (2024) ; Ganguli et al. (2022) and so-called "jailbreaking" techniques are also commonly used to bypass safety alignment Wei et al. (2023) ; Zou et al. (2023) , which can pose risks to current AI-agent applications, starting from LLM use cases.

Many safety guards have been constructed at different levels. One commonly used tool is a classifier, which usually formulates safety detection as a classification problem Inan et al. (2023) ; Sharma et al. (2025) , and the workflow can be: first define or construct a series of policy-violating cases, and then train the classifier to detect whether an input or output is intended to trigger harmful content.

The problem arises from this setup itself: first, the taxonomies are usually designed according to predefined safety policies or risk categories Mazeika et al. (2024) ; Markov et al. (2023) and may not fully cover all cases. Second, attention may be directed toward distinguishing detailed categories rather than the underlying intent of the context itself. Third, the boundaries between detailed categories can be fuzzy and may not correspond directly to the structure of the representation space Inan et al. (2023) ; Sharma et al. (2025) ; Galinkin and Sablotny (2024) . This motivates the question of whether harmful and benign inputs also form exploitable geometric structures in the learned representation space. If such structure exists, it raises two related questions: whether representation-aware structural features can improve conventional classifiers, and whether the classification process can be replaced by direct geometric inference based on the learned representation geometry.

We propose Jailbreak Detection with Prototype Inference (JDPI). We use contrastive learning to improve the separation between harmful and benign inputs in the representation space Gao et al. (2022) ; Khosla et al. (2021) ; Chen et al. (2020) . Using binary safety labels, JDPI constructs harmful and benign prototypes and classifies inputs by their relative distances to these prototypes. This design eliminates the need for a separate labeled split to train a downstream classifier.

Methods B–D test whether adding explicit clustering-derived features improves classifier-based detection. Because the encoder is adapted to labeled data from a particular source, we test cross-dataset robustness by transferring the detector to other datasets without target-dataset adaptation. Our four benchmarks cover prompt-level, question–answer, and multi-turn inputs and include adversarial examples. We assess whether retaining user history improves multi-turn detection on TomGibbs.


### 2 Related Work

A wide range of defense mechanisms has been proposed to improve LLM safety. Some methods enhance safety by prioritizing higher-level instructions over lower-level instructions during inference Wallace et al. (2024) , while others train models to reason about explicit safety specifications before generating responses Guan et al. (2025) . Robustness has also been improved through adversarial training and the incorporation of adversarial objectives Sheshadri et al. (2025) . Additional approaches intervene directly at the representation or activation level through mechanisms such as representation rerouting Zou et al. (2024) . Traditional defense strategies, including input detection, preprocessing, and adversarial training, have also been systematically evaluated Jain et al. (2023) . Input perturbation methods have also been explored, such as SmoothLLM Robey et al. (2024) . Other work investigates internal hidden-layer representations and performs layer-wise safety analysis followed by binary classification Kadali and Papalexakis (2026) . Classifier-based detection is also widely used in safety guard systems Inan et al. (2023) ; Sharma et al. (2025) . Embedding-based jailbreak detectors have combined pretrained sentence representations with conventional classifiers Galinkin and Sablotny (2024) . Despite these advances, many widely used guard models are constructed around predefined safety policies, risk categories, or labeled safety datasets. However, generalization to emerging attack strategies and previously unseen harmful behaviors remains a challenge. Furthermore, the opposite failure mode, namely over-refusal of benign inputs, has received increasing attention and has been studied explicitly in recent work Cui et al. (2025) .

To determine whether latent representations contain meaningful safety signals, several studies have investigated safety-related information encoded within internal model states Ball et al. (2024) ; Jin et al. (2024) . JBShield identifies activated safety-related concepts in internal representations and leverages them for jailbreak detection and mitigation Zhang et al. (2025b) . SIREN further exploits internal safety-relevant features distributed across model layers for harmful-content detection Jiao et al. (2026) . RCS similarly performs jailbreak detection through representational contrastive scoring in latent space Hua et al. (2026) . Collectively, these studies suggest that internal representations contain rich geometric and semantic information that can be effectively leveraged for safety assessment.

A growing number of benchmarks evaluate jailbreak and safety-related behavior using large collections of adversarial and red-teaming-derived inputs, covering both harmful and benign requests. Many of these benchmarks provide comprehensive collections of both harmful and benign prompts, Shen et al. (2024) collect and analyze in-the-wild jailbreak prompts, characterizing their attack strategies and evaluating their effectiveness against LLMs and external safeguards. WildJailbreak and WildGuardMix contain vanilla and adversarial prompts covering harmful and benign requests, including synthetic adversarial prompts generated using tactics mined from in-the-wild interactions Jiang et al. (2024) ; Han et al. (2024) . BeaverTails provides question–answer pairs derived from red-teaming-related prompts designed to elicit unsafe responses, with safety annotations defined over the complete QA pair Ji et al. (2023) . Gibbs et al. (2024) introduce paired single-turn and multi-turn cipher-based jailbreak inputs, alongside benign controls, and examine how prompting structure and conversational context affect guardrail performance. Evaluation frameworks such as StrongREJECT further emphasize that jailbreak assessment should account for whether harmful behavior is actually elicited rather than only whether an attack prompt is present Souly et al. (2024) .

Contrastive learning Chen et al. (2020) improves representation structure by increasing the separation between semantically different samples. This principle has been applied to sentence representation learning Gao et al. (2022) and supervised class-aware representation learning Khosla et al. (2021) . Sentence-transformer models provide fixed-dimensional sentence representations that are suitable for efficient similarity and distance computation Reimers and Gurevych (2019) , extending pretrained Transformer encoders such as BERT Devlin et al. (2019) . In our work, an MPNet-based sentence-transformer encoder is used to obtain semantic representations for subsequent structure analysis Song et al. (2020) .

Prototypical Networks introduced prototype-based few-shot classification, where samples are classified according to distances from representative class prototypes in a learned metric space Snell et al. (2017) . Prototype-oriented Conditional Transport Tanwisuth et al. (2021) addresses source–target alignment by aligning target features with class prototypes. Recent safety detection methods have similarly explored geometric signals in representation space. RCS distinguishes benign and malicious inputs through contrastive scoring over internal model representations Hua et al. (2026) , while T3 models the distribution of acceptable prompts in semantic space and formulates safety detection as out-of-distribution detection without requiring harmful training examples Ganguly et al. (2026) . The Semantic Codebooks approach compares multilingual query embeddings against a fixed codebook of jailbreak prompts, providing a training-free external guardrail based on semantic similarity Alanova et al. (2026) .

Different from these approaches, our focus is not to construct a stronger embedding model or to model only a predefined safe or malicious reference distribution. Instead, we investigate whether geometric structure learned under binary harmful benign supervision can reduce the dependence on an additional downstream classifier. Using the same contrastively adapted encoder, we compare embedding-based classification and variants augmented with clustering-derived features against direct inference from harmful and benign prototype sets, without requiring fine-grained risk annotations. But how finer cluster structure and its refinement affect binary harmful–benign detection is still examined.


### 3 Method

The proposed framework consists of two main stages. First, contrastive representation learning is employed to increase the separation between harmful and benign inputs in the embedding space, thereby inducing a safety-aware geometric structure. Second, the resulting representation space is exploited for downstream detection through a series of increasingly structure-aware approaches, ranging from conventional classifier-based methods to direct prototype-based inference. Figure 1 provides an overview of the framework.

> Figure 1: Overview of the proposed framework. Contrastive fine-tuning first learns safety-aware representations that increase the separation between harmful and benign inputs. Method A serves as the embedding-based classifier baseline. Methods B–D progressively incorporate richer structural information, whereas JDPI performs direct prototype-based inference without relying on an additional downstream classifier.


#### 3.1 Problem formulation

Given a labeled dataset $\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}$ with $y_{i}\in\{0,1\}$ , where $1$ denotes harmful and $0$ denotes benign, we encode each input as $\mathbf{z}_{i}=f_{\theta}(x_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ Song et al. (2020) ; Reimers and Gurevych (2019) .


#### 3.2 Contrastive Representation Learning

We fine-tune the sentence encoder through contrastive learning to improve harmful–benign separation Khosla et al. (2021) ; Gao et al. (2022) ; Tunstall et al. (2022) ; Li et al. (2020) . For each training pair $p=(x_{p}^{(1)},x_{p}^{(2)})$ , we compute the cosine similarity $s_{p}=\operatorname{sim}(f_{\theta}(x_{p}^{(1)}),f_{\theta}(x_{p}^{(2)}))$ . In the first phase, harmful–harmful and benign–benign pairs are treated as positive pairs and assigned label $1$ , while harmful–benign pairs are treated as negative pairs and assigned label $0$ Khosla et al. (2021) ; Tunstall et al. (2022) . The encoder is optimized using CoSENTLoss Huang et al. (2024) :

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CoSENT}}=\log\!\left(1+\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}^{+}}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{P}^{-}}\exp\!\left[\lambda\left(s_{n}-s_{p}\right)\right]\right),$$ (1)

where $\mathcal{P}^{+}$ and $\mathcal{P}^{-}$ denote the sets of positive and negative training pairs in a mini-batch, respectively, and $\lambda$ controls the scale of pairwise similarity differences.

We also evaluate an iterative refinement stage for harmful subclusters, described in Appendix D .


#### 3.3 Structure-Aware detection method

Methods A–D examine whether clustering-derived structural features improve supervised detection beyond using the adapted embeddings alone.

Method A is the embedding-based baseline Galinkin and Sablotny (2024) . It predicts harmfulness directly from the embedding: $p_{A}(y=1\mid\mathbf{z})=\sigma(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{z}+b)$ . The decision threshold $\tau_{A}$ is selected on the validation split.

Method B augments the embedding with distances to harmful cluster centroids. We apply $K$ -means to harmful examples in the structure split Lloyd (2006) , obtaining centroids $\{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{h}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K_{h}}$ . The augmented representation is

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathbf{d}^{h}(\mathbf{z})$ $\displaystyle=\bigl[\|\mathbf{z}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{h}_{1}\|_{2},\ldots,\|\mathbf{z}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{h}_{K_{h}}\|_{2}\bigr],$ (2) $\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}^{(B)}$ $\displaystyle=[\mathbf{z};\mathbf{d}^{h}(\mathbf{z})].$

This comparison tests whether harmful-cluster geometry adds predictive information to the embedding.

Method C adds features describing regions with elevated false-negative rates. For each harmful cluster $k$ , $r_{k}^{\mathrm{FN}}$ is estimated from Method B predictions on the structure split. Clusters whose false-negative rate exceeds the overall harmful-class rate and whose size is at least $n_{\min}$ form the high-FN set $\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FN}}$ . If no cluster satisfies the high-FN criterion, we use the two clusters with the highest false-negative rates as $\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FN}}$ . The remaining harmful clusters form $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FN}}$ . If this set is empty, $\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FN}}$ is also used as the reference set for the low-FN features.

For an input representation, $d_{H}$ and $d_{L}$ denote its minimum distances to these two sets, while $s_{H}$ and $s_{L}$ are their aggregated distance-based soft-membership scores. The indicator $I_{H}(\mathbf{z})$ records whether the nearest harmful centroid belongs to $\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FN}}$ . These quantities form

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{FN}}(\mathbf{z})=\bigl[$ $\displaystyle d_{H},d_{L},d_{H}-d_{L},$ (3) $\displaystyle s_{H},s_{L},s_{H}-s_{L},I_{H}(\mathbf{z})\bigr].$

Method C uses $\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}^{(C)}=[\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}^{(B)};\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{FN}}(\mathbf{z})]$ .

Method D incorporates benign structure and cross-class relationships through three feature groups. The first, $\mathbf{r}_{HB}(\mathbf{z})$ , contains distance and soft-membership statistics for high-FN harmful regions, all harmful clusters, and benign clusters, together with their differences and ratios. For the second group, $\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{mirror}}(\mathbf{z})$ , each high-FN harmful centroid is paired with its nearest benign centroid in Euclidean distance. Features include the input’s distances to both centroids, their difference and ratio, and the difference between distance-based similarity scores computed using exponential decay.

The third group, $\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{z})$ , contains nine features derived from Methods A–C: each method’s harmful probability $p$ , distance from the decision midpoint $|p-0.5|$ , and uncertainty score $1-2|p-0.5|$ . Training features use five-fold out-of-fold predictions; validation and test features use the corresponding models fitted on the training split. The final representation is

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}^{(D)}=\bigl[$ $\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}^{(C)};\mathbf{r}_{HB}(\mathbf{z});$ (4) $\displaystyle\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{mirror}}(\mathbf{z});\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{z})\bigr].$


#### 3.4 Jailbreak Detection with Prototype Inference

We refer to prototype-based inference (Method E) as JDPI. JDPI examines whether the geometry of the same representation space can instead support detection directly. It makes the harmful–benign decision from relative distances to harmful and benign prototypes, without training an additional downstream classifier.

Following prototype-based metric learning Snell et al. (2017) , we independently cluster harmful and benign examples in the structure split to obtain $\mathcal{P}_{h}=\{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{h}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K_{h}}$ and $\mathcal{P}_{b}=\{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{b}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K_{b}}$ . Both sets are constructed using binary safety labels.

For an input representation $\mathbf{z}$ , its distances to the two prototype sets are

[Tableau] $\displaystyle d_{h}(\mathbf{z})$ $\displaystyle=\min_{k}d_{\cos}(\mathbf{z},\boldsymbol{\mu}^{h}_{k}),$ (5) $\displaystyle d_{b}(\mathbf{z})$ $\displaystyle=\min_{j}d_{\cos}(\mathbf{z},\boldsymbol{\mu}^{b}_{j}),$

where $d_{\cos}$ denotes cosine distance. A two-class softmax converts these distances into a harmfulness score:

[Tableau] $$p_{E}(y=1\mid\mathbf{z})=\frac{\exp\!\left(-d_{h}(\mathbf{z})/T\right)}{\exp\!\left(-d_{h}(\mathbf{z})/T\right)+\exp\!\left(-d_{b}(\mathbf{z})/T\right)}.$$ (6)

The temperature $T$ controls the sharpness of the score. An input is classified as harmful when $p_{E}(y=1\mid\mathbf{z})\geq\tau_{E}$ . Both $T$ and $\tau_{E}$ are selected on the validation split.

JDPI constructs prototypes from each class in the structure split and scores new inputs using the cosine distances $d_{h}$ and $d_{b}$ to the nearest prototypes of the two classes. ### 4 Experiments


#### 4.1 Datasets and Splits

We evaluate safety detection on four benchmarks using individual prompts, question–answer pairs, and multi-turn conversations. The evaluation includes adversarial prompts from WildGuardMix and WildJailbreak and multi-turn jailbreak attempts from TomGibbs. Class proportions below refer to our experimental test splits. We abbreviate WildGuardMix, WildJailbreak, BeaverTailsQA, and TomGibbs as WGM, WJB, BTQA, and TG, respectively. We derive the binary safety labels from the released annotations or categories of each benchmark and match the unit of evaluation to the unit those labels describe.


##### WildGuardMix.

WildGuardMix Han et al. (2024) contains vanilla and adversarial prompts with harmful and benign labels. We use the prompt text and its prompt-harmfulness annotation. The test split contains 53.10% harmful and 46.90% benign prompts.


##### WildJailbreak.

WildJailbreak Jiang et al. (2024) contains vanilla and adversarial versions of harmful and benign requests, including benign prompts that resemble attacks but have no harmful intent. Our classifier-training split combines 30,000 vanilla and 6,000 adversarial examples; the structure, validation, and test splits contain adversarial examples. The test split contains 51.77% harmful and 48.23% benign prompts.


##### BeaverTailsQA.

BeaverTails Ji et al. (2023) provides a safety label for each question–answer pair. The label depends on the answer in the context of the question, so the same question can receive different labels when paired with different answers. We therefore concatenate the user question and the assistant response as a single input. Unsafe pairs are mapped to the harmful class and safe pairs to the benign class, accounting for 55.42% and 44.58% of the test split, respectively.


##### TomGibbs.

We use the input-only Multi-Turn Jailbreak Attack Datasets of Gibbs et al. (2024) . Each example provides a complete single-turn prompt and a corresponding sequence of user turns. The attacks use word-substitution ciphers and distribute their instructions across turns.

The dataset includes harmful, completely benign, and semi-benign examples. Semi-benign examples contain harmful or toxic vocabulary but pursue benign goals; both benign subsets are assigned label 0. These three groups account for 63.39%, 18.75%, and 17.86% of our test split, respectively. For the separate static evaluation, we use the corresponding complete prompts from the same held-out examples.


##### Data splits.

We divide each dataset into four splits. The structure split is used to fine-tune the encoder and construct clusters and class prototypes. The training split supplies the labeled examples for fitting the downstream classifiers. We use the validation split to select detector settings, including decision thresholds. The test split is held out throughout model development and used only for final evaluation.

For WildGuardMix and BeaverTailsQA, we pool the released training and test partitions, sample 60,000 examples stratified by the binary label, and construct new experimental splits. For TomGibbs, we assign entire goal groups to splits and downsample the training and structure splits to balance the binary labels. Table 1 summarizes the split sizes.

#### 4.2 Baselines and Evaluation Metrics

We compare JDPI with an embedding-based classifier (Method A) using the same encoder. Methods B–D provide additional comparisons using structure-derived features. Llama Guard 3-8B serves as an external baseline without task-specific fine-tuning. We report accuracy and ROC-AUC, supplemented by F1, false-negative rate, and false-positive rate. We also report silhouette scores to characterize harmful–benign separation and clustering structure within the harmful class.

By avoiding the separate classifier-training split, JDPI uses 65.9–75.0% fewer labeled examples for model development than classifier A across the four benchmarks (Table 8 ). In cross-dataset evaluation, the encoder, classifier or prototypes, and selected parameters are obtained from the source dataset and applied to the target test split without target-dataset adaptation.

For fixed multi-turn replay on TomGibbs, we compare scoring the current user turn alone with scoring the accumulated user history up to and including that turn. Each method uses the detector and threshold selected in the complete-prompt setting throughout replay. Following the any-turn blocking criterion used by Gibbs et al. (2024) , we count a conversation as blocked if the detector flags the input at any evaluated user turn.


#### 4.3 Implementation Details

We use a random seed of 42. The sentence encoder is all-mpnet-base-v2 from Sentence Transformers, with an encoding batch size of 64 for downstream evaluation. Contrastive fine-tuning uses CoSENTLoss. Phase 1 uses five epochs and 200 warm-up steps. Phase 2 runs for at most five iterations, with two epochs and 50 warm-up steps per iteration. Appendix C reports the encoder training configurations and contrastive pair counts.

For clustering, $K$ is selected from $\{4,\ldots,12\}$ using the cosine-distance silhouette score. K-means uses 10 initializations. The maximum iteration counts for classifiers A–D are 3,000, 4,000, 5,000, and 6,000, respectively. Methods A–C use balanced class weights. Method D constructs its training prediction features through five-fold out-of-fold prediction.

For Methods A–C and JDPI, decision thresholds are selected on the validation split from $\{0.10,0.15,\ldots,0.90\}$ . JDPI additionally selects its temperature from $\{0.1,0.3,0.5,0.7,1.0,1.5,2.0\}$ . Threshold and temperature selection for JDPI maximizes validation accuracy. Methods B–D use Euclidean cluster-distance features, while JDPI uses cosine distances to prototypes.

For the external baseline, we use Llama-Guard-3-8B with greedy decoding and at most 10 generated tokens. Outputs beginning with unsafe are mapped to the harmful class; remaining outputs are mapped to the benign class. Continuous unsafe scores are used for ROC-AUC calculation, separately from these binary decisions.

### 6 Conclusion

This paper investigates how the structure of learned representations can support jailbreak and harmful-input detection. We examine two related questions: whether explicit structural information improves a conventional embedding-based classifier, and whether the learned geometry can support detection without training an additional downstream classifier.

Contrastive fine-tuning improves the separation between harmful and benign examples in the embedding space, providing a clearer structure for constructing class prototypes. It also improves JDPI’s detection accuracy across all four benchmarks. The clustering-derived features in Methods B–D yield small, dataset-dependent gains in the in-domain experiments. This suggests that classifier A already captures much of the signal needed for detection directly from the fine-tuned embeddings. On WildGuardMix, WildJailbreak, and BeaverTailsQA, JDPI achieves accuracy close to that of classifier A while saving the labeled data and computation required to train a downstream classifier. With dataset-specific adaptation, JDPI exceeds the released Llama Guard 3-8B baseline in accuracy and ROC-AUC on all four benchmarks. Together, these results support representation adaptation as a basis for effective detection with a simple downstream decision rule.

Across the 12 directed transfers, JDPI achieves higher mean accuracy than classifier A, suggesting greater robustness to dataset shift on this metric. In multi-turn replay on TomGibbs, retaining user history improves JDPI’s conversation-level accuracy, which exceeds that of classifier A under the same input setting. Together, these results show that contrastively adapted representations can support competitive prototype-based detection. JDPI uses binary safety labels for encoder adaptation, prototype construction, and validation. JDPI avoids the separate labeled split used to train classifier A and shows greater robustness to dataset shift as measured by mean accuracy across the evaluated transfers.


### Limitations

Although JDPI removes the need for a trained downstream classifier, binary labels are still required for contrastive representation learning, class-specific prototype construction, and validation-based parameter selection. In our experimental setup, it avoids the additional labeled training split used by the classifier, but does not eliminate dependence on labeled data.

How larger fine-tuning datasets or greater encoder training budgets affect the relative performance of JDPI and the classifier baseline remains an open question. Although the decision threshold and temperature of JDPI are selected on the validation split, the search covers a finite set of candidate values. Sensitivity to these parameters, training duration, clustering configurations, and distance metrics has not been fully characterized.

Cross-dataset experiments show that transfer performance varies across source–target pairs and involves different false-positive and false-negative trade-offs.

##### Method D configuration.

We fixed the benign class weight at 1.0 and tested harmful class weights of 1.0, 1.2, and 1.5. For each weight, we searched thresholds in $\{0.10,0.15,\ldots,0.90\}$ on the validation split. Among thresholds with precision of at least 0.70 and an FPR no more than one percentage point above Method B’s reference FPR, we selected the threshold with the highest recall. If no threshold met both constraints, we selected by recall over the full grid. Recall ties were broken by F1, balanced accuracy, and accuracy, in that order.

We computed Method B’s reference FPR and ranked the candidate class weights on the validation split. The ranking prioritized fewer false negatives, followed by lower FPR.


### Appendix D Ablation Study: Harmful-Subcluster Refinement

In the second phase, we further refine the internal structure of the harmful representations through an iterative clustering procedure. At each iteration, harmful samples are re-encoded using the current encoder and partitioned using $K$ -means clustering. The number of clusters is selected from $K=4,\ldots,12$ by maximizing the silhouette score computed using cosine distance. The resulting cluster assignments are used as pseudo-labels to construct new contrastive pairs. Harmful samples belonging to the same cluster form positive pairs, while harmful samples from different clusters form negative pairs. Harmful-benign pairs are additionally retained as negative pairs to preserve the global separation between the two classes.

Using these newly constructed pairs, the encoder is further optimized with the same CoSENTLoss. The updated encoder is then used to re-encode harmful samples, and the clustering process is repeated for up to a predefined maximum number of iterations. By progressively refining substructures within the harmful class, this stage allows us to examine whether finer cluster separation improves binary harmful–benign detection.

Thus, improved separation among harmful clusters does not translate into improved binary detection in these experiments.

These trends are consistent with a mismatch between the refinement and detection objectives. Phase 1 explicitly separates harmful and benign examples, whereas Phase 2 additionally separates subgroups within the harmful class. This can alter the geometry used by nearest-prototype inference without improving the harmful–benign decision boundary. A learned classifier may accommodate these changes more effectively by fitting a decision direction across embedding dimensions. The results therefore favor evaluating refinement through downstream detection performance, rather than harmful-cluster silhouette alone.

Future work could jointly optimize harmful–benign separation and harmful-subcluster separation, with their relative weights selected on validation data for the intended detection protocol.
