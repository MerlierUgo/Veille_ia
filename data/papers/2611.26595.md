## Coda-DaVinci: Reimagining Video Generation with Code2Video

Code2Video uses agent-authored 3D proxies to guide video generation, enabling explicit control over structure and dynamics. However, how to construct proxies and prompts for optimal downstream quality remains underexplored, as do the capabilities and limitations of this paradigm. Through controlled experiments and blinded human evaluation, we study proxy granularity, physics simulation, and prompting. The results reveal task-dependent trade-offs: coarse proxies favor natural motion, while medium-grained proxies favor consistency. Physics simulation and proxy-aligned prompting further improve average quality. Guided by these findings, we introduce Coda-DaVinci , a task-adaptive agentic framework for Code2Video generation that selects between proxy-guided and direct generation, adapts modeling granularity to each entity, and coordinates simulation with proxy-grounded prompting. Its multi-agent harness preserves scene assets and guides repairs through visual inspection and independent review. Experiments demonstrate leading instruction following on VTR and scene consistency on R2M, improved physical plausibility over direct generation, and higher visual realism than the compared Code2Video baselines. Long-video evaluations further show gains in event structure and consistency across successive interactions, while revealing limitations in transition stability and adherence to scene settings.


### 1 Introduction

Recent video generation models can synthesize realistic and visually appealing videos from text and reference media [ 22 , 11 , 27 , 7 , 24 ] . Yet following detailed instructions, preserving scene state over time, and producing physically plausible interactions remain challenging. Code2Video approaches, including VideoCoCo [ 13 ] and Code Video Model [ 14 ] , address these challenges through executable scene programs. In this paradigm, a coding agent constructs and renders a 3D proxy whose structure and motion guide a video model in synthesizing the final video. This explicit scene representation helps maintain scene state across camera revisits, as shown in Figure 1 (a).

The benefits of Code2Video depend on the task and on how the proxy is constructed and used. Scene programs provide precise control over layout and trajectories, but detailed modeling can introduce geometric or motion errors that may carry over to the final video. When motion naturalness is central and explicit scene control offers limited benefit, an imperfect proxy may yield less natural results than direct generation. Proxies for the same task can differ substantially in their level of detail. Physical interactions also require choosing between scripted motion and physics simulation. The synthesis prompt must then align the intended appearance and finer actions with the rendered proxy. These considerations raise two central questions: How can we achieve the best video generation performance within the Code2Video paradigm, and how far can Code2Video extend the capabilities of video generation?

> Figure 1: Selective 3D modeling. Each method pairs 3D proxy frames with generated video frames at 3, 5, and 7 seconds. Codex + Blender and VideoCoCo explicitly model hands and arms. Coda-DaVinci models the objects involved in cutting and unwrapping, while leaving detailed hand articulation to the video generator.

We study these questions through controlled comparisons with blinded human evaluation on tasks involving scene revisitation, complex motion, and physical processes. The benefits of proxy guidance vary by task: improved consistency and instruction following can come at the cost of motion naturalness. Under matched settings, coarse proxies preserve more natural motion than detailed alternatives on motion-intensive tasks, while medium-grained proxies provide stronger consistency when physics simulation is required. Prompts written by the coding agent outperform the tested external enhancement strategy in pooled scores. Among the tested configurations, medium-grained proxies combined with physics simulation and agent-authored prompts achieve the highest pooled performance.

Guided by these findings, we introduce Coda-DaVinci , a task-adaptive agentic framework for Code2Video generation as shown in Figure . Coda-DaVinci selects 3D modeling when precise scene or motion control is needed and favors direct generation for natural body movement when explicit control is unnecessary and proxy animation risks stiffness. For modeled scenes, it uses medium-grained proxies for environments and structured objects, coarse proxies for human subjects, and Blender simulation for supported physical interactions, as shown in Figure 1 (b). The agent inspects the proxy before writing a synthesis prompt that links appearance references to proxy entities and specifies finer human actions. A multi-agent harness preserves the scene workspace across revisions and successive requests, supports visual inspection, and uses an independent acceptance agent to identify unmet requirements for repair.

Experiments show that Coda-DaVinci leads the compared methods in instruction following on VTR [ 9 ] and scene consistency on R2M [ 6 ] . It also improves physical plausibility over direct generation on World Models’ Last Exam in Physics [ 5 ] and outperforms Code2Video baselines in visual realism on the T2AV-Compass subset [ 2 ] , approaching direct generation. We also examine agent–generator pairings and rendering engines; Blender offers higher quality than Three.js at greater token cost. Long-video and multi-turn evaluations show gains in long-form structure and scene consistency over direct continuation, but lower transition stability and setting adherence.

Our main contributions are threefold:

- • We systematically analyze proxy granularity, physics simulation, and prompting, deriving task-dependent guidelines for proxy construction and video synthesis.

- • We introduce Coda-DaVinci , a task-adaptive agentic framework that coordinates selective 3D modeling and proxy-grounded synthesis, supported by a persistent scene workspace and visual feedback.

- • We empirically characterize Code2Video’s capabilities and limitations across tasks, agent–generator pairings, rendering engines, and long-video settings, revealing trade-offs in visual quality, consistency, and token cost.


### 2 Related Work

Video Generation. Recent advances in video generation have substantially improved visual fidelity, motion quality, and text–video alignment [ 22 , 11 , 27 , 7 , 10 , 30 , 23 , 26 , 25 , 17 , 31 ] . Building on this progress, recent work increasingly emphasizes reliable instruction following, spatiotemporal consistency, and physical plausibility. TempAct [ 28 ] improves adherence to temporally complex instructions through planner–executor reinforcement learning. To maintain visual consistency over longer sequences, Context Forcing [ 3 ] combines long-context supervision with slow–fast memory, while LongLive-RAG [ 8 ] retrieves previously generated video latents to mitigate identity drift. MMPhysVideo [ 15 ] improves physical plausibility by jointly modeling RGB video with semantic, geometric, and trajectory cues. Despite these advances, reliably following complex instructions, maintaining spatiotemporal consistency, and generating physically plausible dynamics remain challenging.

Code2Video. Code2Video generation uses executable programs to control visual content and motion, enabling targeted editing. The Code2Video framework [ 4 ] targets educational videos, and MoVer [ 19 ] guides motion graphics refinement through spatiotemporal verification. StoryBlender [ 12 ] produces consistent 3D storyboards using persistent scene memory and engine-based verification, while SimWorlds [ 16 ] constructs dynamic Blender scenes through planning, coding, and verification. For proxy-guided synthesis, GPT4Motion [ 18 ] uses Blender-simulated motion cues to guide diffusion models. VideoCoCo [ 13 ] guides video generation with Blender-rendered spatiotemporal drafts, while Code Video Model [ 14 ] combines Three.js proxies with early-step conditioning to preserve structure and reduce synthetic-appearance leakage.


### 3 Empirical Analysis


#### 3.1 Code2Video Pipeline Formulation

Given an input prompt $x$ with optional image or video references $r$ , a coding agent $\mathcal{A}$ constructs a proxy video $\tilde{v}$ . A video-generation model $\mathcal{G}$ then produces the final video $v$ conditioned on the proxy and a generation prompt $q$ :

[Tableau] $$\tilde{v}=\mathcal{A}(x,r),\qquad v=\mathcal{G}(\tilde{v},q,r),$$ (1)

where $q$ is either the original prompt $x$ or an enhanced version produced by the coding agent $\mathcal{A}$ or an external model.

To address the central questions raised in Section 1 , we examine how different proxy construction and prompting strategies affect video quality across tasks.


#### 3.2 Evaluation Setup

Analyzed Components and Baselines. We analyze three design factors and combine their labels to denote experimental configurations (e.g., G1/P1/PE2 ). Implementation details are provided in the supplementary material.

- 1. Proxy granularity ( G ): G0 , G1 , and G2 denote coarse, medium, and fine geometry and motion, respectively.

- 2. Physics requirements ( P ): P1 requires Blender’s built-in physics simulation; P0 does not.

- 3. Prompt enhancement and proxy alignment ( PE ): PE0 applies no additional enhancement; PE1 uses MiniMax H3-Context-IR [ 20 ] to enrich the prompt; and PE2 requires the coding agent itself to specify subject–color bindings in the prompt and add complementary appearance details based on the constructed proxy.

We use Seedance 2.5 as the direct generation baseline, conditioned on the original prompt and optional references.

Visualization of different implementations of the analyzed components. Figure 2 presents qualitative comparisons of proxy videos and their corresponding generated outputs. The G0 – G2 rows illustrate changes in geometric structure and articulation as proxy granularity increases. The P0 – P1 rows compare two configurations, highlighting differences in the represented physical motion and its realization in the generated video. The PE0 – PE2 rows compare the original prompt, independent text-only enhancement by MiniMax H3-Context-IR, and agent-guided proxy-aware enhancement with explicit subject–color bindings. For each configuration, we uniformly sample eight timestamps over the shared video interval and align the proxy and generated frames at identical timestamps. These paired sequences allow direct inspection of how subject placement, motion, interactions, and appearance are transferred from the proxy to the final output.

> Figure 2: Qualitative visualization of component configurations. The groups labeled P0 and P1 correspond to G1_P1_PE1 and G0_P1_PE0 , respectively. Both enable explicit physics simulation and differ in geometry and prompt enhancement.

Tasks. Our evaluation covers three task categories:

- 1. WorldMemBench: Scene revisitation, evaluating consistency on return visits.

- 2. VGIF-Bench: Complex instruction following involving human or object motion and interactions.

- 3. Principia: Physical process simulation, which evaluates videos depicting physical phenomena.

Evaluation metrics and protocol. Experts score each video on four dimensions under a blinded protocol using $\{0,1,2,3\}$ (higher is better).

- 1. Consistency: Temporal consistency within the generated video and consistency with the input reference.

- 2. Instruction following: The extent to which the generated video satisfies the input instruction.

- 3. Physical plausibility: The physical plausibility of the depicted motion, interactions, and processes.

- 4. Naturalness and visual realism: The perceived naturalness of motion and overall visual realism.

The implementation details of the empirical analysis are in Appendix B.1 .

#### 3.4 Empirical Answers and Workflow Insights

The above empirical observations provide the following answers to the two central questions.

Workflow Design Insights. These findings motivate three coordinated workflow choices: adapt modeling and motion-control granularity to each entity, favoring coarse proxies for human motion and medium-grained proxies where structural consistency matters; selectively invoke Blender’s built-in physics engine for interactions the proxy can meaningfully simulate; and coordinate proxy construction with prompt enhancement by establishing subject–color bindings during modeling and explicitly describing these bindings alongside the proxy’s geometry, motion, and simulated behavior in the generation prompt.


### 4 Method

We introduce Coda-DaVinci , a task-adaptive agentic framework for code2video generation. Coda-DaVinci selects a generation path based on task requirements and applies the corresponding modeling and prompting skills. An execution harness coordinates scene construction and video synthesis, using visual feedback to guide revisions, as shown in Figure 5 .


#### 4.1 Task-Adaptive Proxy Construction

Route selection. Based on the input instruction and reference media, Coda-DaVinci selects 3D modeling for tasks requiring precise control over scene structure or motion. For tasks centered on natural body movement, it selects direct video generation when explicit control is unnecessary and simplified proxy animation could introduce stiffness. Both routes support video synthesis, with Blender tools additionally enabled for 3D modeling.

Modeling granularity. We encode the entity-wise policy from the preceding analysis in modeling skills: G1 for environments and structured objects, and G0 for human subjects. The scene code specifies overall human movement, while the synthesis prompt describes finer actions. The agent simulates requested physical interactions when they can be meaningfully represented in Blender.


#### 4.2 Proxy-Grounded Video Synthesis

From proxy to synthesis prompt. After constructing the proxy, the agent inspects the rendered sequence and revises the scene code when the depicted events deviate from the request. Guided by the synthesis skill, it then uses the inspected proxy to write a prompt describing the intended final video. The prompt links subject descriptions to the corresponding proxy entities and appearance references, follows the actions depicted in the proxy, and specifies the visual details and subtle movements to be synthesized.

Video synthesis. Before synthesis, the harness checks that the proxy video and its shot description correspond to the same render and resolves subject references. The generation tool then passes the prompt, proxy video, and associated reference media to the video model, which produces a candidate video. The direct-generation path uses the same synthesis stage without a proxy, supplying a task-derived action and camera prompt with the available references.


#### 4.3 Harness Design and Visual Feedback

Workflow execution. The harness provides a shared Blender workspace and tool interfaces. A lead generation agent coordinates execution and can delegate separable modeling tasks to coding subagents. Each assignment specifies how a component should fit into the scene, and returned assets are then integrated and inspected in context. The workspace preserves this work across revisions, allowing subagents to continue refining their components and supporting asset reuse in later video extensions.

Visual feedback. Visual inspection covers both the proxy and the generated video. For code2video outputs, the harness displays generated frames alongside the corresponding proxy views to help identify the source of discrepancies. Proxy errors lead to scene revisions, while discrepancies introduced during synthesis guide changes to the prompt or reference inputs. An independent acceptance agent reviews each candidate against the original task and its registered criteria. The harness links the review to the submitted video and returns unmet requirements for repair.

#### 5.2 Main Results

Coda-DaVinci leads on both instruction following and scene consistency. Coda-DaVinci achieves the highest scores across the reported VTR and R2M metrics and improves physical plausibility over direct generation. On VTR, it surpasses the strongest agentic baseline in checklist satisfaction and reduces WER from 59.21% for direct generation to 11.08%. These gains also extend to preserving scene structure and object states across views.

Explicit scene modeling strengthens physical fidelity. Each code2video method outperforms direct generation on World Models’ Last Exam in Physics. Coda-DaVinci ranks second to Claude + Blender, supporting the use of executable scenes for physical interactions. While claude also outperforms Codex with the same Blender tools, suggesting that the coding model’s ability to implement physical behavior contributes to the remaining performance gap.

Adaptive routing improves visual quality. On T2AV-Compass, Coda-DaVinci leads the code2video methods in realism and instruction following, with realism approaching direct generation and conventional agentic methods. This suggests that direct video generation of complex body motion reduces stiffness from proxy animation.

#### 5.3 Ablation Experiments

We conduct the ablations on a 33-task diagnostic subset. Gemini 3.8 Flash scores each video from 1 to 5 on four dimensions: physical plausibility, instruction following, visual consistency, and joint aesthetics and fluency. All configurations use the same task-specific checklists, derived from the original instructions and references. We average the four scores to obtain the overall score.

Coda-DaVinci is robust across coding agents. We evaluate Coda-DaVinci with four coding agents and three video generators in Table 5 . Overall scores remain similar across coding agents, suggesting that our task-adaptive design works with different models. The video generator also affects the result: Supplementary Material shows that even with correct proxy actions or camera movements, the video may deviate if the model does not follow the reference.

Task adaptation and independent review improve video quality. We ablate three components in Coda-DaVinci with GPT-6-Astra and Seedance 2.5: independent acceptance, adaptive routing, and entity-wise modeling granularity. Each ablation lowers scores on all four dimensions as shown in Figure 6 (b). Independent acceptance is particularly useful for consistency, which drops most when this review is removed. Fixing routing to 3D modeling in all tasks or applying G1 to all subjects has its largest effect on instruction following and aesthetics. These results suggest that fulfilling the task depends on both when to use explicit modeling and how much detail to represent.

Blender improves video quality at a higher token cost. We replace Blender with Three.js while keeping the coding agent and other settings fixed. As shown in Figure 6 (c), both modeling engines improve overall quality over direct generation. Blender scores higher on all four dimensions, suggesting that it provides a better basis for representing these tasks. It also consumes roughly twice as many agent tokens per case. Three.js thus offers a less costly option, with a modest loss in video quality on this subset.


#### 5.4 Long-Video Consistency Study

A reusable 3D scene preserves objects and spatial relationships across clips, providing persistent memory for long-video generation.

We compare Coda-DaVinci with direct Seedance 2.5 on 29 LongAV-Compass and 50 WBench cases. LongAV-Compass sequences span 25–90 seconds and are generated event by event, using the preceding tail frame (T2AV/I2AV) or clip (V2AV) as reference. WBench evaluates successive interactions within a scene, with each turn continuing from the previous tail frame.

As shown in Figure 6 (d,e), Coda-DaVinci improves long-form structure on LongAV-Compass, with its largest WBench gains in interaction adherence and consistency. These findings support scene reuse for maintaining state across successive instructions. However, direct generation performs better in transition stability on LongAV-Compass and setting adherence on WBench, revealing remaining limitations in cross-clip continuity and scene fidelity.


#### 5.5 Qualitative Analysis

Scene modeling supports instruction following and state persistence. In Figure 7 , the camera is instructed to turn away from a person clearing leaves and then return to the steps. Seedance keeps the steps largely in view, with many leaves still present near the end. Both Codex + Blender and Coda-DaVinci complete the camera departure and return to cleared steps. The ongoing action therefore advances across the unseen interval and leaves a lasting change in the scene. This example illustrates how explicit modeling helps coordinate camera instructions with event progression while preserving the resulting state across a revisit.

> Figure 7: Camera instruction following and state persistence. The camera turns away while leaves are being cleared, then returns to the steps. Each method occupies two rows, read consecutively from left to right. Frames are sampled at matching timestamps, with denser sampling during the return. Both code2video methods show cleared steps on revisitation, while Seedance retains substantial leaf coverage.

Selective modeling preserves natural hand motion. Figure 8 compares a close-up of hands flattening dough. Codex + Blender keeps the hands in nearly the same pose throughout the sequence, giving the interaction a static appearance. In Coda-DaVinci, the wrists turn and the fingers adjust through successive presses as the dough changes shape. These coordinated changes make the action more fluid and the contact more convincing, retaining the lively motion visible in native Seedance generation. ### 6 Conclusion

We investigated how coding agents and video-generation models can be coordinated within the Code2Video paradigm. Our analysis shows that effective proxy guidance requires matching modeling granularity, physics simulation, and prompting to task requirements. These findings inform Coda-DaVinci, which assigns essential structure and dynamics to coding agents while leaving appearance and fine motion to video models, supported by persistent scene assets and visual feedback. Experiments demonstrate leading performance on VTR and R2M, improved physical plausibility over direct generation, and further gains in long-form structure and consistency across successive interactions. Remaining gaps in motion naturalness, transition stability, and adherence to scene settings highlight the need to better preserve explicit control during synthesis as video generation extends to longer and more complex scenarios.

### Appendix B Implementation Details


#### B.1 Implementation Details for Empirical Analysis

We implement the three experimental components through explicit instructions to the coding agent and controlled selection of the final generation prompt. The agent constructs and renders Blender proxies under the selected configuration. Across configurations, we preserve task-required entity counts, identities, spatial layout, event order, endpoint states, and camera requirements. Background coverage, lighting, shadows, and rendering settings follow the same policy.


##### Proxy granularity ( G ).

The three levels control the geometry and motion of subjects and interaction-relevant objects:

- • G0 (coarse). Each human is represented by a cylindrical body and a spherical head, without limbs or articulated joints. Animals use simple head and body volumes, and objects primarily use boxes, spheres, or cylinders. Animation represents whole-entity translation and rotation. Invisible logical anchors express possession and object transfer, while task-critical openings or passages may use a few additional primitives.

- • G1 (medium). Humans have a simple torso, head, and four unsegmented limbs with a fixed relative pose and recognizable front/back orientation. Root motion and interaction objects are animated, but local anatomical articulation is excluded. Objects use small assemblies of primitives that retain their coarse silhouettes, major openings, and necessary supports. Contacts are represented through coarse endpoints or logical anchors.

- • G2 (fine). Subjects use proportionate segmented bodies and task-relevant articulated joints. Animation explicitly represents local poses, contact, release, support, and visible state changes. Objects include recognizable components, thickness, cavities, openings, and moving parts at the rendered viewing scale. Facial, finger, and clothing geometry is added only when required by the task; refinement focuses on structure and motion rather than surface textures.

Task-required material changes, including cloth deformation, liquids, particles, and smoke, are permitted at every granularity level using sufficient simulation geometry. These exceptions do not relax the anatomical articulation limits of G0 and G1 . Revisions remain within the selected granularity.


##### Physics requirements ( P ).

- • P0 (no mandatory simulation). The agent is instructed to produce physically reasonable motion without a mandatory explicit solver. It may use authored trajectories or keyframes. Physics simulation remains permitted when independently selected by the agent.

- • P1 (explicit simulation). The agent is required to execute task-appropriate physics simulation in Blender. Interacting bodies and boundaries use collision geometry with actual contact, non-penetration, support, and collision response. Cloth motion uses cloth simulation; liquid flow, pouring, and splashing use fluid simulation; discrete grains use particle systems; and airborne dust or smoke uses gas simulation. Only processes relevant to the original task are introduced.

Required simulations are executed or baked over the relevant interval, and their evaluated states, deformed meshes, particles, or volumes drive the rendered proxy. Merely configuring a collider or simulation domain is insufficient. The agent records initial conditions, physical parameters, solver settings, and cache paths, together with failed or unsupported simulation attempts.


##### Prompt enhancement and proxy alignment ( PE ).

- • PE0 (no enhancement). The original task instruction is retained verbatim as the base generation prompt. Subjects and objects use neutral gray/white proxy materials and are identified through geometry, position, and semantic roles. The controller appends the proxy-interpretation instructions and reference mappings, indicating that final appearance should follow the task and authorized references.

- • PE1 (independent enhancement). MiniMax H3-Context-IR [ 20 ] receives only the unchanged original dataset prompt, without the proxy, reference media, agent history, or benchmark-added camera and duration instructions. Its complete response becomes the base generation prompt and is reused across revisions of the same run. The proxy retains the neutral gray/white appearance used in PE0 .

- • PE2 (agent-guided enhancement). Before construction, the coding agent assigns each tracked subject a stable entity identifier, an unambiguous semantic description, and a distinct identity color. Colors remain consistent across frames and changes of possession. After inspecting the proxy, the same agent explicitly states the observed color–subject bindings in the generation prompt. These colors serve as identity markers; final colors and materials follow the original task and references. The agent additionally describes compatible appearance details absent from the proxy, including materials, surface properties, lighting, and natural anatomy or motion. Shot descriptions follow the constructed proxy’s actions, camera behavior, timing, and persistent states. Incorrect represented geometry or motion must be corrected in the proxy before generation.

Thus, PE2 jointly changes prompt construction and proxy identity encoding, whereas PE0 and PE1 share the neutral-clay representation.


##### Experimental configurations.

We evaluate all six G $\times$ P combinations under PE0 , and all three PE levels at fixed G1 under both physics settings, yielding ten unique Code2Video configurations. The direct-generation baseline uses Seedance 2.5 with the original prompt and optional original references.


##### Human evaluation.

We recruit 10 human evaluators to assess the generated videos under a blinded protocol, with model and configuration identities hidden. Each evaluator scores consistency, instruction following, physical plausibility, and naturalness and visual realism on a four-point scale $\{0,1,2,3\}$ , where higher scores indicate better quality. For each video and dimension, we average the scores from the 10 evaluators. Benchmark-level results average these video-level scores over the evaluated samples, and the overall results pool samples from the three benchmarks.


##### Benchmarks.

We evaluate on three complementary benchmarks. WorldMemBench assesses world consistency during scene revisitation, including the preservation of identities, object appearances, spatial geometry, and evolving states. VGIF-Bench evaluates complex spatio-temporal instruction following, including entity attributes, actions, interactions, and event ordering. Principia assesses physical consistency through motion relationships between paired objects across eight Newtonian phenomena. For this analysis, we retain only samples that produce valid outputs under every evaluated configuration, ensuring that all configurations are compared on the same inputs. This filtering is necessary because Seedance’s privacy policy prevents some samples from returning valid results. The resulting evaluation set contains 33 WorldMemBench samples, 29 VGIF-Bench samples, and 32 Principia samples, totaling 94 samples. Figure 27 shows their distribution.

> Figure 27: Composition of the 94-sample evaluation set retained across all evaluated configurations. Each sector shows the benchmark name, percentage, and sample count.


##### Metrics.

We assess generated videos along four dimensions. Consistency measures temporal and cross-shot coherence, including whether scenes, spatial layouts, character identities, and object attributes remain consistent except where changes are explicitly required. It also evaluates fidelity to input reference images when applicable, such as preserving a specified first frame or faithfully reproducing the appearance of a reference subject. Instruction following measures whether the video completely and correctly fulfills the original input requirements, including events, character actions, interactions, environmental layouts, lighting, and camera movements, with the specified temporal order and spatial relationships. Physical plausibility evaluates whether depicted physical processes visually conform to physical laws, including plausible trajectories, gravity, collisions, contact, support, and material responses. Naturalness and visual realism assesses the perceptual quality of appearance and motion, including plausible anatomy, fluid movement, and convincing rendering, while penalizing malformed limbs, stiff or jerky actions, and other visible artifacts. Ten expert evaluators independently rate the videos along these dimensions in a blind evaluation, without access to method identities or configuration labels. Evaluators are provided with the original instructions and any applicable reference images to assess adherence and reference fidelity. We report the mean score across the ten evaluators for each dimension.
