## Stranger Things: When Objects Appear Without Their Typical Neighbors

Visual context is a powerful cue for object recognition: scenes inform what, where, and at what scale objects are likely to appear. Yet over-reliance on context becomes a failure mode when models predict objects based on their typical neighbors rather than the objects’ own visual features, a phenomenon we refer to as typicality bias . While this issue has been extensively studied in the pre-Transformer era, it has often been assumed that architectural and scale improvements would naturally solve it. Despite recent attempts in isolated settings, a systematic diagnostic across the modern vision stack remains absent. In this work, we provide such a diagnostic framework. Specifically, we measure typicality bias via normalized pointwise mutual information (NPMI) over object pairs, and introduce STRANGE-Bench ( S ynthetic T ypicality- RAN ked G rounded E valuation Bench mark): a photorealistic, layout-controlled benchmark spanning 17 sub-tiers from typical to never-co-occurring object pairs. Across five model families (specialist detectors, open-vocabulary detectors, self-supervised, vision-language, and multimodal large language models) and three recognition tasks (supervised detection, open-vocabulary detection, and classification), we find the bias to be highly prevalent: gaps of 5–11 p.p. persist when objects appear outside their typical company, and they do not consistently shrink with scale. This persistence motivates a complementary question: how to mitigate it? Data augmentation is a natural lever, since it directly shapes the training distribution that produces the bias; yet existing augmentations are designed for visual diversity, not for breaking co-occurrence shortcuts: they sample from the very distribution that produces typicality bias, preserving rather than perturbing it. We therefore propose Anchor-Atypicality Sampling (AAS), a simple sampling rule for paste-based augmentations that uses the NPMI signal to deliberately tilt the training-time co-occurrence distribution toward atypical partners. AAS reduces the typicality gap by up to 1.6 p.p. on natural-image COCO splits and 23.0 p.p. on synthetic STRANGE-Bench across three representative model families (specialist detection, open-vocabulary detection, self-supervised classification), without sacrificing overall performance. Dataset: huggingface.co/datasets/sidgairo18/strange-bench


### 1 Introduction

Modern recognition models are trained on large-scale datasets of natural images. Such images capture objects in their typical surroundings, e.g. , a cow on grass, a boat in water, or a stove in a kitchen. These models can exploit co-occurrence cues that go unnoticed on standard benchmarks, since training and test sets draw from the same patterns. Failures surface only when objects appear in atypical contexts, e.g. , a cow on a beach [ 3 ] . While context is itself informative for recognition [ 48 , 35 ] , models can come to use it as a shortcut around the object’s visual features. This over-reliance on co-occurring context has been studied through context-based interventions [ 43 , 45 , 12 , 40 ] , controlled benchmarks [ 31 , 38 , 27 ] , and dedicated out-of-context detection [ 1 , 7 ] . All these studies converge on the same conclusion: classifiers exploit co-occurrence shortcuts and fail when they are absent.

It is unclear whether these findings still hold across the modern vision stack. Recent architectures and training paradigms such as end-to-end detection transformers [ 9 , 61 , 56 ] , open-vocabulary detectors that ground predictions in language [ 30 , 33 , 34 ] , self-supervised vision foundation models [ 11 , 36 , 44 ] , vision-language models pretrained on billions of image-text pairs [ 37 , 14 , 55 , 49 ] , and large multimodal models that fuse vision and language at scale [ 25 ] are expected to alleviate context reliance through enhancements such as deformable attention, language grounding, as well as web-scale data diversity [ 52 , 30 , 37 ] . Recent work has examined whether such shortcuts persist for specific subsets of the stack: segmentation and detection through explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) methods [ 16 ] , and nuisance-based robustness benchmarks [ 58 , 17 ] . However, a systematic diagnostic across diverse model families that evaluates on object pairs never co-occurring in natural data remains underexplored.

(Co-DINO Swin-L) to ${\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.1523,0.1563}\Delta=11.0}$ p.p. (OpenCLIP).

In this work, we revisit this phenomenon systematically and at scale across modern vision models. We refer to it as typicality bias : the tendency of recognition models to base predictions on familiar co-occurring context rather than the target object’s own visual evidence. As shown in Fig. 1 , a model exhibiting typicality bias performs well when objects appear in typical contexts and degrades when they appear alone or in atypical contexts.

To diagnose typicality bias systematically, we introduce a framework based on normalized pointwise mutual information (NPMI) over object pairs. For each anchor class, we use NPMI to construct paired evaluation splits in which the anchor (object category under examination) appears alongside either typical or atypical partners. To extend this natural-data evaluation with co-occurrence regimes that natural data do not cover (see Fig. 2 ), we further release STRANGE-Bench ( S ynthetic T ypicality- RAN ked G rounded E valuation Bench mark), a photorealistic, layout-controlled synthetic benchmark spanning the full spectrum from typical to never-co-occurring object pairs.

A complementary question is how to mitigate typicality bias. Data augmentation is a natural lever for this, since it directly shapes the training distribution that produces the bias. In practice, existing augmentations are designed to add visual diversity, not to break co-occurrence shortcuts: both non-generative [ 6 , 57 , 21 ] and generative [ 59 , 24 , 53 ] methods sample from the natural training distribution, preserving rather than perturbing it. We present the first systematic study of how popular data augmentations interact with typicality bias across representative model families, and propose a simple context-aware sampling rule that reduces the bias while preserving overall accuracy.

Our findings reveal three patterns. First, typicality bias persists across diverse model families and tasks, with gaps of $\mathbf{5}$ – $\mathbf{11}$ p.p. between typical and atypical splits. Second , the bias does not consistently shrink with scale: more pretraining data does not narrow the gap, and larger models narrow it without closing it, indicating that scale alone is not a solution. Third , most non-paste augmentations leave the gap largely unchanged or narrow it by lowering both sides, while paste-based ones narrow it on STRANGE-Bench. AAS lifts atypical scenes further without hurting typical ones, and reduces the gap of standard training by up to $\mathbf{1.6}$ p.p. on natural-image COCO [ 29 ] splits and $\mathbf{23.0}$ p.p. on synthetic STRANGE-Bench, without sacrificing overall performance.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

- 1. Typicality bias persists across the modern vision stack. We provide a unified diagnostic of typicality bias spanning five model families (specialist detection, open-vocabulary detection, self-supervised representations, vision-language models, and multimodal LLMs) and three recognition tasks. Our key finding is that typicality bias persists across diverse tasks and scales.

- 2. A photorealistic synthetic benchmark for tiered typicality. We present STRANGE-Bench , a layout-controlled synthetic benchmark spanning the full spectrum from typical to never-co-occurring object pairs, enabling controlled evaluation in low-NPMI regimes that natural data cannot cover; blinded user studies confirm that its gaps are not rendering artifacts.

- 3. Anchor-Atypicality Sampling for mitigation. We present the first systematic study of how popular data augmentations interact with typicality bias across representative model families, and propose Anchor-Atypicality Sampling (AAS), a simple sampling rule for paste-based augmentation that reduces typicality bias while preserving overall performance.


### 2 Related Work

Contextual and co-occurrence bias in recognition models. Reliance on co-occurring context as a predictive shortcut is well-documented for older architectures, but less so for the modern stack. Causal interventions demonstrate the dependence by manipulating features or inputs: via causal inpainting on convolutional neural network (CNN) features [ 43 ] , and via feature-space splitting on COCO-Stuff [ 8 , 45 ] . Out-of-context benchmarks measure model behavior when objects appear in atypical scenes [ 1 , 7 ] . Concept-graph approaches discover and mitigate co-occurrence biases via concept-graph clique sampling [ 12 ] or open-set foundation-model tagging [ 40 ] . More recent work has examined context bias in modern models, but only in narrower settings: XAI for segmentation and detection [ 16 ] , nuisance-based robustness benchmarks [ 58 ] , referring expression generation [ 32 ] , and compositional action recognition [ 2 ] . In contrast to these narrower studies, we apply one diagnostic across five model families, complemented by STRANGE-Bench , which covers co-occurrence regimes absent from natural data, and we also test mitigations.

Data augmentation for visual recognition. Data augmentation shapes the training distribution that models see, but existing methods were not designed with co-occurrence shortcuts in mind. Non-generative methods perturb individual images (flip, crop, color jitter) or stitch existing instances together (MixUp [ 57 ] , Mosaic [ 6 ] , Simple Copy-Paste [ 21 ] ). Generative methods synthesize new instances using text-to-image diffusion (XPaste [ 59 ] , InstanceAug [ 24 ] , MosaicFusion [ 53 ] ). Across both families (see Table 1 ), pasted or generated instances are sampled from the natural co-occurrence distribution. Whether this preserves typicality bias, and whether targeted modifications can reduce it, have not been systematically studied. We present such a study on representative model families, and propose Anchor-Atypicality Sampling (AAS) , a sampling rule that plugs into any paste-based augmentation and reduces typicality bias while preserving overall performance.

Modern visual recognition models. The vision recognition stack has evolved rapidly along architectural and supervision axes. Specialist detection transformers such as DETR [ 9 ] , Deformable DETR [ 60 ] , and Co-DETR/Co-DINO [ 61 ] formulate detection end-to-end via (deformable) attention. Open-vocabulary detectors such as GLIP [ 26 ] and Grounding DINO [ 30 ] extend detection to text-conditioned localization. Self-supervised foundation models such as DINOv2 [ 36 ] and DINOv3 [ 44 ] learn general-purpose visual representations from hundreds of millions of unlabeled images. Vision–language models (CLIP [ 37 ] , OpenCLIP [ 14 ] ) and large multimodal models (LLaVA-OneVision [ 25 ] ) use web-scale image–text pretraining for zero-shot recognition and image understanding. Each of these design choices, from deformable attention focusing on object regions to language grounding and web-scale data diversity, is expected to be resistant to context shortcuts. Yet none is trained or evaluated with such shortcuts in mind, leaving their susceptibility to typicality bias underexamined, a gap our diagnostic, benchmark, and mitigation directly address.


### 3 Diagnosing and Mitigating Typicality Bias

In this section, we present a framework for diagnosing and mitigating typicality bias, built around normalized pointwise mutual information (NPMI). We first use NPMI to construct paired typical and atypical evaluation splits on real images (Sec. 3.1 ). To probe co-occurrence regimes that real data cannot reach, we then introduce STRANGE-Bench , a layout-grounded synthetic benchmark spanning 17 sub-tiers from natural co-occurrence to never-co-occurring object pairs (Sec. 3.2 ). Finally, we present Anchor-Atypicality Sampling (AAS) , a sampling rule for paste-based augmentations that perturbs training-time co-occurrence using the same NPMI signal (Sec. 3.3 ). STRANGE-Bench is an evaluation instrument and AAS a training-time intervention.


#### 3.1 NPMI Diagnostic on Natural Data

To quantify typicality bias on real data, we measure how a model’s behavior changes between paired scenes where an anchor class (the target object category we examine) appears alongside its typical co-occurrence partners versus its atypical ones. This requires a typicality measure that is comparable across object categories of widely different frequencies, since we aggregate gaps over all anchor classes ( e.g. , person co-occurs with almost everything, while toaster with almost nothing). We use normalized pointwise mutual information (NPMI), which is bounded in $[-1,1]$ and corrects for marginal class frequency, making typicality directly comparable across rare and common categories.

NPMI definition. For categories $c_{i},c_{j}$ , we compute pointwise mutual information from image-level co-occurrence ( i.e. , whether both classes appear in an image, ignoring count and location) and normalize it:

[Tableau] $$\text{NPMI}(c_{i},c_{j})\;=\;\frac{\log\big[p(c_{i},c_{j})/p(c_{i})\,p(c_{j})\big]}{-\log p(c_{i},c_{j})}\;\in\;[-1,1].$$ (1)

The numerator is positive when $c_{i}$ and $c_{j}$ co-occur more often than chance, negative when they co-occur less, and zero when they are independent; normalizing by $-\log p(c_{i},c_{j})$ bounds the score to $[-1,1]$ , with the bound saturated when $c_{i}$ and $c_{j}$ always or never co-occur. Typical pairs (high NPMI) include toaster–microwave ; atypical pairs (low NPMI) include toaster–car , and some pairs, such as toaster–giraffe , never co-occur (black cells in Fig. 2 , left). We compute the matrix on COCO train2017 ( $N={}$ $117{,}266$ images with at least one annotation, $80$ categories), which provides clean instance annotations across diverse everyday objects [ 29 ] . The same matrix drives split construction here and the AAS sampler in Sec. 3.3 .

Hot (red) cells correspond to high-NPMI (typical) pairs, cool (blue) cells to low-NPMI (atypical) pairs, and black cells to category pairs that never co-occur in training. Paired typical/atypical splits. For each anchor class $c$ , we build paired evaluation splits. Both splits contain images where $c$ is present alongside at least one partner, such that typical partners appear only in one split and atypical partners only in the other. Concretely, for anchor $c$ and a quantile parameter $\alpha\in(0,0.5)$ , we define typical and atypical partner sets via per-anchor NPMI quantiles:

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{N}^{\text{typ}}(c)=\{\,j:\text{NPMI}(c,j)\geq q_{1-\alpha}^{(c)}\,\},\qquad\mathcal{N}^{\text{aty}}(c)=\{\,j:\text{NPMI}(c,j)\leq q_{\alpha}^{(c)}\,\},$$ (2)

where $q_{p}^{(c)}$ is the $p$ -quantile of NPMI in row $c$ , restricted to partners with sufficient train-set co-occurrence with $c$ . 1 1 Specifically, typical candidates require $N_{ij}\geq 5$ ; atypical candidates require $N_{ij}\geq 1$ and at least one val image containing both $c$ and $j$ , and the atypical quantile is taken over these candidates. The count floors keep wide quantile cuts on noisy long-tail rows from drifting into the neutral region. Per-anchor quantiles, rather than a fixed top- $K$ , calibrate each anchor’s partner set to its own NPMI distribution: a fixed $K$ would over-include partners for common anchors (which have many high-NPMI pairs) and under-include for rare ones. The choice of $\alpha$ trades partner-set sharpness against split sample size. A val image $I$ joins the Typical split for $c$ iff $c\in I$ , $I\cap\mathcal{N}^{\text{typ}}(c)\neq\emptyset$ , and $I\cap\mathcal{N}^{\text{aty}}(c)=\emptyset$ (and symmetrically for the Atypical split). This scene-purity requirement prevents typical and atypical partners from leaking across splits when an image contains both. The choice of $\alpha$ , the val-coverage floor for long-tail anchors, and additional implementation details are provided in Appendix B.2 . Figure 2 shows the NPMI matrix used here, alongside example scenes from STRANGE-Bench at representative NPMI tiers; we describe its construction next.


#### 3.2 STRANGE-Bench: Tiered Synthetic Evaluation

The diagnostic in Sec. 3.1 covers only the portion of the NPMI spectrum that exists in natural data, i.e. , pairs from typical (high NPMI) to atypical (low NPMI). It cannot reach pairs that never co-occur in training, where NPMI is undefined and no natural images exist. Nor can it vary contextual load while controlling for typicality, since natural images do not allow for adding or removing partners. To examine these regimes, we build STRANGE-Bench ( S ynthetic T ypicality- RAN ked G rounded E valuation Bench mark), a layout-grounded photorealistic benchmark of $5{,}523$ scenes with $17{,}332$ annotations across 17 sub-tiers (see Fig. 3 ). Each scene contains an anchor category and $k\in\{0,\dots,5\}$ partners, all uniformly typical or atypical with respect to the anchor. We call $k$ the contextual load and a ( $k$ , typicality) cell a tier : $T_{1}$ to $T_{6}$ cover $k=0$ to $5$ ( $T_{1}$ varies only the background), and App. C.3 lists every sub-tier. $k\leq 4$ covers $91\%$ of COCO train2017 images; $k$ is set by design rather than matched to natural statistics. A separate $T_{\text{never}}$ tier pairs the anchor with categories that never co-occur with it in COCO train2017 ; atypical tiers also contain such pairs, but $T_{\text{never}}$ requires every pair in the scene, partners included, to have zero co-occurrences. This sweeps bias as a function of both typicality and contextual load.

> Figure 3 : Overview of the STRANGE-Bench generation pipeline , illustrated with outputs of the actual build. (1) Typicality-based sampling : partners are drawn by their COCO NPMI with the anchor, high for the typical arm and low or never co-occurring for the atypical arm. (2) Layout and caption : a planner places the categories in a layout and writes a caption from it; each category keeps its color in panels 2 and 3. (3) Rendering and annotation : SD 3.5 Large renders the caption up to three times until SAM3 finds every category, and the boxes of the SAM3 masks are the annotations. (4) Audit : Grounding DINO rejects scenes with unintended objects (here, a laptop), and SAM3 and OWLv2 turn the remaining ones into ignore regions or drop the scene. (5) Assembly : typical and atypical sub-tiers at each contextual load $k$ , plus $T_{\text{never}}$ , form STRANGE-Bench.

Generation pipeline. Each scene is generated by a five-stage pipeline (Fig. 3 ) that turns a set of categories into an annotated photorealistic image. (1) Typicality-based sampling. For a target sub-tier, we sample an anchor and $k$ partners from the COCO label set, preferring partners with high NPMI (typical arm) or low NPMI (atypical arm) with the anchor and with each other (App. C.3 ). (2) Layout and caption. A Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct [ 54 ] planner (or, for $21\%$ of the scenes, a rule-based solver) places the categories in a bounding-box layout and writes from it one wide-shot caption that names every object with its position in the image; the renderer sees the layout only through this caption. The caption asks for a minimalist scene with every object fully visible, which targets the two failure modes of our pilot renders: cropped objects and unprompted clutter. (3) Rendering and annotation. Stable Diffusion 3.5 Large [ 46 , 18 ] renders the caption, and SAM3 [ 10 ] segments each intended category from a text prompt ( e.g. , “a giraffe”); the boxes of its instance masks become the annotations. We render up to three times and keep the first render in which SAM3 finds every intended category ( $89.5\%$ of the categories are found on the first render, $95.9\%$ within three). (4) Audit. An object that was not asked for would turn its detections into false positives and could bring typical context into an atypical scene. A Grounding DINO [ 30 ] auditor therefore rejects scenes with unintended or duplicate objects, and a second audit with SAM3 and OWLv2 [ 34 ] turns each remaining one into an ignore region, where predictions are not counted, or drops the scene if it is atypical and the object co-occurs with an intended category more often than chance (NPMI $>0$ ). (5) Assembly. Scenes that miss an intended category are dropped, and the typical and atypical sub-tiers at each $k$ are cut to the same size; $44.6\%$ of the rendered scenes enter STRANGE-Bench. Prompt templates, audit thresholds, the per-tier composition, and the yield of each stage are provided in Appendix C .


#### 3.3 Anchor-Atypicality Sampling (AAS)

Standard Copy-Paste [ 21 ] and X-Paste [ 59 ] sample paste classes uniformly (or by inverse frequency), so the trained co-occurrence distribution mirrors the source distribution and reinforces typicality bias. AAS replaces uniform sampling with an anchor-conditioned, atypicality-tilted distribution that shifts the training-time joint toward low-NPMI pairs while preserving overall class coverage.

For each training image with present classes $\mathcal{P}$ , we take as anchor $a$ the class of the largest ground-truth object. We then sample each paste class $j$ from an instance pool $\mathcal{C}_{\text{pool}}$ (pre-computed real and synthetic individual instances over all categories as in [ 59 ] ) with probability

[Tableau] $$P(j\mid a)\;\propto\;\pi(j)\,\exp\big(\beta\,A(a,j)\big),$$ (3)

where $A(a,j)=(1-\text{NPMI}(a,j))/2\in[0,1]$ is the atypicality of the pair, set to $1$ for pairs that never co-occur in COCO train , $\beta\geq 0$ controls the sharpness of the tilt ( $\beta=0$ recovers the baseline), and $\pi(j)$ is uniform. We use $\beta=3.5$ throughout and ablate it in Appendix A.5 . Pasted instances are scaled per-class via $S_{r}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{c},\sigma_{c}^{2})$ statistics estimated from the pool, randomly placed, and composited with occlusion-aware mask updates following X-Paste [ 59 ] . We apply AAS as a late stage: a paste-trained model (SCP or XPaste) resumes its last epochs on COCO plus a small number of atypical scenes generated by the pipeline of Sec. 3.2 , and the resulting weights are averaged 1:1 with those of the base model (App. B.1.4 ). These scenes come from the generator of STRANGE-Bench but are separate renders, never benchmark images; App. A.6 tests this link and the shared NPMI statistics.

AAS explicitly perturbs the training-time NPMI structure: the model sees anchors paired with their bottom-NPMI partners far more often than the natural distribution would allow. This directly attacks the shortcut behind typicality bias (“predict $j$ because $a$ is present”) by making that shortcut unreliable during training. Since the tilt is anchor-conditional rather than global, typical co-occurrences in the original data are largely preserved, so the intervention is asymmetric: gap-closing without overall-performance regression.


### 4 Experiments

In this section, we evaluate typicality bias across the modern vision stack and study its mitigation. We first provide our experimental setup in Sec. 4.1 , including datasets, evaluation metrics, models, and augmentation suites. We then survey five model families on the COCO NPMI splits and the synthetic STRANGE-Bench in Sec. 4.2 , establishing that the bias persists across the stack and does not consistently shrink with scale. We finally run the systematic mitigation study on three representative families in Sec. 4.3 , showing that AAS reduces the typicality gap while preserving overall performance. Sec. 4.4 checks with human raters that the gaps come from the models and not from the rendered scenes.


#### 4.1 Experimental Setup

Datasets and metrics. We evaluate on COCO val , Objects365v2 val [ 42 ] (mapped to COCO’s 80-class label space for cross-domain transfer), and the synthetic STRANGE-Bench of Sec. 3.2 . On COCO and O365, we report overall performance as a sanity check (typicality-bias mitigation should not regress overall accuracy) and the typicality gap $\Delta^{\text{T-A}}=M^{\text{T}}-M^{\text{A}}$ on the per-anchor NPMI quintile ( $\alpha=0.2$ ) splits of Sec. 3.1 , where $M$ is AP for detection and micro-averaged F1 at a $0.5$ threshold (F1 ${}_{\text{micro}}$ ) for DINOv3 classification. We compare rows within a family only, since the metrics (AP, F1) and their operating ranges differ. On STRANGE-Bench, we report the per-tier average $T_{1\text{-}5}^{\text{T}},T_{1\text{-}5}^{\text{A}}$ across tiers $T_{1}$ to $T_{5}$ ( $k=0\dots 4$ ) and the single-point $T_{\text{never}}$ score, the most extreme atypical case by construction (see Appendix A for more details). The main paper uses $11$ of the $17$ sub-tiers ( $T_{1}$ to $T_{5}$ in both arms and $T_{\text{never}}$ ); Tab. 8 and Fig. 5 report the others.

Models. We evaluate typicality bias on a representative model from each of five families spanning the modern vision stack: specialist detection (Co-DINO [ 61 ] R-50, Swin-L), open-vocabulary detection (Grounding DINO [ 30 ] Swin-B), self-supervised classification (DINOv3 [ 44 ] ViT-B, both linear-probe and fine-tuned), vision–language models (CLIP [ 37 ] ViT-B/16, OpenCLIP [ 14 ] ViT-B/16 LAION-2B), and multimodal LLMs (LLaVA-OneVision-7B [ 25 ] ). For the diagnostic survey, we use the public pretrained checkpoints officially released by the authors. These families span the modern vision stack along two axes: supervision regime (fully supervised, language-grounded, self-supervised, contrastive image–text, and instruction-tuned) and pretraining data scale (from $\sim 100$ K images for COCO-trained specialists to $\sim 2$ billion image–text pairs for OpenCLIP and LLaVA-OneVision). Any persistence of bias across this set is therefore unlikely to be an artifact of a single paradigm or scale.

For the systematic mitigation study, we select one representative from each of the three task settings, spanning supervision regime, architecture, and task: Co-DINO R-50 (specialist closed-vocabulary detection), Grounding DINO Swin-B (open-vocabulary, text-conditioned), and DINOv3 ViT-B/16 fine-tuned (SSL foundational backbone fine-tuned for classification). All representative models are trained on COCO train2017 (initialized from the public pretrained checkpoints) under the augmentation variants below. Full training recipes, checkpoint sources, hyperparameters, and compute budgets are provided in Appendix B.1 . We leave vision–language models and multimodal LLMs out of the mitigation study, since their co-occurrence prior is set during web-scale pretraining.

Augmentation suite. We compare AAS against a representative suite of seven augmentations spanning two axes, static / dynamic and non-generative / generative : Color jitter , MixUp [ 57 ] , Mosaic [ 6 ] , YOLOX [ 20 ] (MixUp + Mosaic), SCP [ 21 ] , XPaste [ 59 ] , and InstAug [ 24 ] . We additionally report a Std. baseline using only standard augmentation (flip, crop, resize). None of the existing augmentations are designed to break co-occurrence shortcuts; whether they incidentally affect typicality bias is an empirical question we answer below. AAS hyperparameters are selected once on a $25\%$ COCO subset and held fixed across architectures and tasks; per-augmentation hyperparameters are detailed in Appendix B.1.4 .


#### 4.2 Typicality Bias Persists Across the Modern Vision Stack

To isolate the bias contained in the pretrained representation from any introduced by downstream task adaptation, we evaluate each family with the pretrained representation frozen : zero-shot for Grounding DINO, CLIP, and OpenCLIP; linear probing for DINOv3; and the released checkpoint for Co-DINO. We survey the five model families on the COCO quintile split (Fig. 1 , right) and on the STRANGE-Bench per-tier breakdown (Fig. 4 ). The COCO quintile gap $\Delta^{\text{T-A}}$ is non-zero for every family we examine, ranging from 5.4 p.p. (Co-DINO Swin-L $1\times$ ) to 11.0 p.p. (OpenCLIP ViT-B/16); concretely, specialist detection shows $\Delta^{\text{T-A}}_{\text{AP}}=$ 9.5 p.p. (Co-DINO R-50), 5.4 p.p. (Swin-L $1\times$ ), and 5.6 p.p. (Swin-L $3\times$ ), open-vocabulary detection $\Delta^{\text{T-A}}_{\text{AP}}=$ 6.3 p.p. (Grounding DINO Swin-B), self-supervised classification $\Delta^{\text{T-A}}_{\text{F1}}$ $=$ 8.6 p.p. (DINOv3 ViT-B linear probe), and vision–language models $\Delta^{\text{T-A}}_{\text{mAP}}=$ 9.5 p.p. (CLIP ViT-B/16) and 11.0 p.p. (OpenCLIP ViT-B/16 LAION-2B). The Co-DINO Swin-L checkpoint, pretrained on a multi-dataset blend [ 61 ] , also exhibits $\Delta^{\text{T-A}}_{\text{AP}}=$ 5.7 p.p. on COCO val, indicating the bias is a property of the training distribution rather than dataset overfitting. From CLIP (400M image-text pairs) to OpenCLIP (LAION-2B), more pretraining data widens rather than shrinks the gap ( 9.5 $\rightarrow$ 11.0 p.p.); scale alone is not a solution (App. A.7 separates data from capacity). STRANGE-Bench amplifies this picture (Fig. 4 ): the gap grows with contextual load $k$ at every tier for the DINOv3 linear probe, CLIP and OpenCLIP, reaching $\Delta=37.2$ for the DINOv3 linear probe at the highest tier; Co-DINO R-50 rises to about $8.0$ by T3 and then stays flat, and the LLaVA-OV gaps (F1 macro) are smaller and do not grow with $k$ . Per-tier breakdowns are provided in App. A .

### 5 Conclusion

We revisited typicality bias , the tendency of recognition models to anchor predictions on familiar co-occurring context rather than the object itself. Across the modern vision stack, the bias persists across diverse model families and pretraining scales, without consistently shrinking with size. To diagnose it on a continuous scale, we introduced an NPMI-based diagnostic and the photorealistic STRANGE-Bench. To mitigate it, we further proposed Anchor-Atypicality Sampling (AAS), a sampling rule for paste-based augmentation that closes the typicality gap by lifting atypical-context performance without degrading typical-context performance.

Our diagnostic relies on annotated co-occurrence statistics, and AAS requires training pipelines that allow for paste-based augmentations, leaving contrastive image-text and instruction-following pretraining, where the bias is most pronounced, as open directions for future work. These findings suggest that contextual robustness should be reported alongside in-distribution performance in the standard evaluation of foundation-scale recognition models.

#### A.4 DINOv3 ViT-B/16 Linear Probe: Full Results

We additionally evaluate typicality bias under the linear-probe (LP) protocol on DINOv3 ViT-B/16, where only a linear classification head is trained on top of the frozen pretrained backbone. This isolates the contribution of the backbone’s representations from end-to-end fine-tuning: any gap that survives the LP setup is encoded in the backbone itself and must be read out of features the backbone already provides, while any gap reduction obtained by augmentation must operate purely through the head’s training signal. We restrict the LP study to Std. and three paste augmentations of the FT sweep (§ A.3 ), SCP, XPaste and XPaste + AAS, since the goal of this section is to characterize the ceiling on what augmentation can achieve in the frozen-backbone regime, not to repeat a full sweep. Unlike in the fine-tuned tables, the AAS row here (XPaste + AAS † ) applies AAS sampling over the whole LP schedule rather than as the late stage of Sec. 3.3 , and every row is a single training draw.

Tab. 13 reports the in-domain results together with mean STRANGE-Bench statistics; Tab. 14 reports the cross-domain transfer to Objects365; and Tab. 15 reports the full per-tier STRANGE-Bench breakdown. Three observations stand out. First , the standard augmentation recipe ( Std. ) attains the highest in-domain F1 on COCO val and on the typical split, but also exhibits the largest typicality gap ( $\Delta^{\text{T-A}}=8.6$ ); paste-based methods trade a small amount of in-domain F1 ( $1.5$ to $2.3$ p.p.) for substantially smaller gaps ( $5.7$ to $5.9$ ). Second , on STRANGE-Bench, XPaste + AAS † is the strongest mitigator under the LP protocol: it achieves the lowest mean tier gap ( $\Delta_{1\text{-}5}=6.0$ ), the highest atypical mean ( $68.1$ ) and $T_{\text{never}}$ score ( $70.1$ ), and the smallest gap at T2 to T5; XPaste is second on these three metrics ( $8.1$ , $67.3$ , $67.9$ ) and SCP third ( $9.2$ , $57.5$ , $55.7$ ). Third , the STRANGE-Bench ordering is the same as under end-to-end fine-tuning (XPaste + AAS, then XPaste, then SCP; Tab. 4 and § A.3 ): the AAS tilt lowers $\Delta_{1\text{-}5}$ from $8.1$ to $6.0$ and raises $T_{\text{never}}$ from $67.9$ to $70.1$ over XPaste even when only the head is trained, but the residual gap stays larger than after fine-tuning ( $-0.8$ ).

Why augmentation hits a ceiling under LP. The residual gap that no LP augmentation closes ( $\Delta_{1\text{-}5}\geq 6.0$ , $T_{\text{never}}\leq 70.1$ ) points to the frozen features: DINOv3 is pretrained without labels on about $1.7$ billion images (LVD-1689M [ 44 ] ), and the gap of the Std. probe grows with the pretraining data (DINOv1 $19.3$ , DINOv2 $21.5$ , DINOv3 $23.9$ ; App. A.7 ), which is consistent with co-occurrence structure that is already present in the features. #### A.5 Selecting the AAS Atypicality Temperature $\beta$

We select the AAS atypicality temperature $\beta$ once via a small ablation and hold the selected value fixed across all model families and full-COCO runs. Tab. 16 reports the sweep on Co-DINO R-50 (AP), trained on the $25\%$ subset of COCO train2017 (mini-COCO, $29{,}571$ images), and on DINOv3 ViT-B/16 fine-tuned on full COCO (mAP). $\beta=3.5$ achieves the best or second-best score on every metric for both models, simultaneously balancing overall accuracy, in-domain typicality gap, and STRANGE-Bench coverage, and we adopt it for all main-paper experiments.

Tradeoff intuition. The sampling distribution $P(j\mid a)\propto\pi(j)\exp\big(\beta A(a,j)\big)$ of Sec. 3.3 has two endpoints. At $\beta=0$ , AAS samples paste classes uniformly and independently of the anchor, i.e. the XPaste baseline, and provides no debiasing signal; as $\beta\to\infty$ , AAS concentrates almost all paste mass on the most atypical partners, which over-distorts the training-time joint and starts to hurt typical-pair coverage and overall accuracy. The sweep traces this tradeoff: $\beta=2$ leaves residual gap ( $\Delta^{\text{T-A}}=8.1$ for Co-DINO, $9.5$ for DINOv3 FT, both worse than $\beta=3.5$ ); $\beta=5$ matches or beats $\beta=3.5$ on a few metrics (Co-DINO typical AP, DINOv3 $\Delta_{1\text{-}5}$ on STRANGE-Bench) but drops on others (Co-DINO val AP, DINOv3 val mAP and gap), reflecting the over-tilting regime. $\beta=3.5$ sits near the sweet spot for both families.

#### A.8 Spatial Arrangement as a Typicality Axis

NPMI scores whether two classes co-occur, not how they are arranged. To test whether models also rely on the usual arrangement of a pair, we measure how stereotyped it is: for each ordered COCO train2017 pair with at least $10$ co-occurring images, we take the direction from the largest anchor box to the largest partner box in every such image, bin it into eight $45^{\circ}$ sectors, and compute the normalized entropy $H\in[0,1]$ of the histogram. Low $H$ means one dominant arrangement, such as a rider above a horse. STRANGE-Bench layouts are generated without regard to these arrangements, so a model that relies on them should recognize the anchor less often in stereotyped pairs. We use the single-partner tier $T_{2}$ (both arms) and the DINOv3 ViT-B linear probe, count an anchor as recognized when its score is at least $0.5$ , and keep pairs with at least two scenes.

#### A.9 Partner Masking

Instead of changing which objects appear together, a mitigation can remove the objects around a target. On COCO train2017 images with exactly two classes ( $35{,}858$ of $117{,}266$ ), partner masking picks one class as the anchor, removes the other with a precomputed LaMa [ 47 ] inpainting and keeps only the anchor label; other images are unchanged. We fine-tune DINOv3 ViT-B/16 with it for 20 epochs, the recipe of the submitted runs, and compare it with the Std. run of the same recipe (Tab. 23 ). Partner masking narrows the STRANGE-Bench gap only from $20.0$ to $18.2$ , while the COCO gap widens from $4.8$ to $5.2$ and both $T_{\text{never}}$ ( $67.2\rightarrow 65.8$ ) and COCO val ( $80.9\rightarrow 80.1$ ) drop. A model learns the partners it is shown with each anchor. Partner masking shrinks that set, since the anchor is now often seen alone, so an anchor among atypical partners at test time remains unlike the training images; paste-based augmentation (Sec. 4.3 ) widens the set instead. The invariance to context comes from varying the context, not from deleting it.

AAS sampler. The AAS sampler of the AAS rows is shared across all model families and is summarized in Alg. 1 . It takes the class of the largest ground-truth object of each training image as anchor and then samples paste classes from an anchor-conditioned, atypicality-tilted distribution $P(j\mid a)\propto\pi(j)\exp(\beta A(a,j))$ with $A(a,j)=(1-\text{NPMI}(a,j))/2$ , $A=1$ for pairs that never co-occur, and uniform $\pi$ . Zero-shot VL inference (CLIP / OpenCLIP ViT-B/16). Each class name is wrapped in the standard 80-template ensemble from [ 37 ] (templates such as “a photo of a {class}.”). Per-class text embeddings are mean-pooled across templates and L2-normalized. Per-image embeddings come from the CLIP / OpenCLIP image encoder, also L2-normalized. The score for class $c$ on image $i$ is the cosine similarity, used directly as the ranking score for per-class mAP. No threshold sweep is needed for mAP; for F1 we use a fixed threshold (which is why F1 is uninformative for the zero-shot models and is omitted from the headline tables).

LMM inference (LLaVA-OneVision 0.5b / 7b). Two-pass “gated” prompting. Both prompts are issued as chat-template user turns with the image attached: (i) a main-objects prompt asks the model to list up to $12$ unique visible object categories as a JSON array; (ii) a small-objects prompt then asks for up to $8$ small handheld items (utensils, containers, etc.).

Pass 1 (main objects). List the object categories that are actually visible in the image. Return ONLY a JSON array of at most $12$ UNIQUE short nouns ( $1$ – $2$ words). If you are unsure, omit the object. No explanations. No extra text.

Pass 2 (small objects). List ONLY small objects that are CLEARLY visible (utensils / containers / handheld items). Return ONLY a JSON array of at most $8$ UNIQUE short nouns ( $1$ – $2$ words). If you are not $100\%$ sure an object is present, DO NOT include it. Do NOT guess utensils. No explanations. No extra text.

The text output of each pass is regex-parsed (multiple JSON arrays / dicts / quoted strings, plus a comma-split fallback), normalized (lower-cased, basic plural $\to$ singular), and aliased onto the COCO-80 vocabulary ( e.g. “sofa” $\to$ “couch”, “mobile phone” $\to$ “cell phone”). The resulting predicted class labels are converted to a binary multi-hot vector over the $80$ COCO classes and compared against per-image GT. Greedy decoding ( do_sample=False ), repetition penalty $1.2$ , max_new_tokens $=96$ (main pass) / $64$ (small pass).

Note : this hand-crafted pipeline only estimates which everyday classes an LMM names in a scene. It is applied identically to the typical and atypical sets, so the gap compares the two sets under the same pipeline.

#### B.2 Quantile Choices for the Paired Diagnostic

The paired splits in Sec. 3.1 are parameterized by a single quantile $\alpha\in(0,0.5)$ : for each anchor $c$ , typical partners are the top- $\alpha$ NPMI quantile of $c$ ’s row and atypical partners are the bottom- $\alpha$ quantile. The headline numbers reported in Sec. 4 use the quintile cut ( $\alpha=0.2$ ). In this subsection we describe the split construction (partner-eligibility floors, image-balancing protocol), report split-quality statistics across five candidate cuts, justify the quintile choice, and present the full $\alpha$ -sweep across the augmentation suite of Sec. 4 .

Trade-off. The choice of $\alpha$ trades partner-set sharpness against split sample size. Smaller $\alpha$ yields a sharper typical/atypical contrast but smaller per-anchor partner pools and consequently smaller per-anchor splits; larger $\alpha$ yields larger and more stable splits at the cost of admitting partners closer to the neutral region.

Implementation floors. All quantile settings share the same partner-eligibility constraints: train-support floors ( $N_{ij}^{\text{typ}}\geq 5$ , $N_{ij}^{\text{aty}}\geq 1$ ) prevent wide quantile cuts on noisy long-tail anchor rows from drifting into the neutral region; and a val-observability filter keeps only atypical partners that co-occur with the anchor in at least one val image, so anchors with an empty atypical split are dropped ( $16$ of $80$ at the quintile cut). No sign constraint is applied: at the quintile cut $54$ of $850$ typical partner slots have NPMI $\leq 0$ and $3$ of $670$ atypical slots have NPMI $\geq 0$ , while the pooled means remain $+0.21$ vs. $-0.23$ .

Image balancing. After the partner-eligibility filters above, the typical and atypical sides of each cut are highly imbalanced in the natural distribution: typical pairs are roughly an order of magnitude more common than atypical ones, so the unfiltered typical-side image pool is much larger than the atypical-side pool (e.g., $3{,}501$ vs. $389$ at the quintile cut). To prevent this asymmetry from biasing per-anchor AP estimates, we keep the atypical side intact (it is already the bottleneck) and down-sample the typical side per-anchor so that, for each anchor class, the number of typical-matched images is approximately equal to the number of atypical images for that anchor. The resulting post-balancing counts are reported in Table 30 and feed directly into the eval suite of § B.1.1 .

Split-quality summary. Table 30 summarizes the per-cut split quality on COCO val: the partner-set NPMI separation ( $\mu^{\text{typ}}-\mu^{\text{aty}}$ ), the post-balancing typical-matched / atypical image counts, and the number of anchor classes that survive both the implementation floors and the balancing step. Three observations support the quintile choice. First, the quintile cut achieves the highest anchor coverage of any cut ( $64/80$ , vs. $56$ for $\alpha=0.5$ and $61$ for the decile), which is the relevant quantity for stable per-anchor AP estimates. Second, its pooled NPMI means lie on opposite sides of zero ( $+0.21$ vs. $-0.23$ , a gap of $0.44$ ), while the wider cuts admit partners closer to zero (gap $0.24$ at $\alpha=0.5$ ). Third, it keeps about $1.5\times$ the images of the decile ( $465/389$ vs. $300/259$ ); the decile separates the partner sets more sharply (gap $0.55$ ) but keeps fewer anchors and images. The quintile cut therefore balances anchor coverage, NPMI separation, and sample size.

Why is natural data not enough? Even at the standard cuts we use, the COCO paired-real splits comprise only $465/389$ typical-matched / atypical images at the quintile cut ( $\alpha=0.2$ ) and $300/259$ at the decile ( $\alpha=0.1$ ) (Tab. 30 and Tab. 25 ), spread across $64$ and $61$ anchor classes. Per-anchor coverage is therefore modest in absolute terms and very uneven across the long tail; anchors whose atypical partners never appear with them in val are dropped ( $16$ of $80$ at the quintile cut), and $28$ of the remaining $64$ anchors have fewer than three atypical images. More fundamentally, no natural cut can reach the regime where partners never co-occur with the anchor. As Fig. 7 shows, on train2017 the never-co-occur cells form a small minority of the $80\times 80$ NPMI matrix ( $982$ of the $6{,}320$ off-diagonal cells, $15.5\%$ ), but on val2017 the empty fraction jumps to $49.1\%$ : nearly half of all category pairs that we might want to probe simply have no images in val. The empty-cell rate is highest in the long-tail $\times$ long-tail quadrant ( $78.5\%$ vs. $39.5\%$ elsewhere), exactly where typicality bias should be most pronounced and stress-tests are most informative. By construction, no quantile cut on val can recover these cells, and the never-co-occur regime ( $T_{\text{never}}$ ) is inaccessible from natural data altogether. This is the gap STRANGE-Bench (§ C ) is designed to close: rendering scenes from sampled category sets lets us produce paired typical / atypical scenes and a clean $T_{\text{never}}$ tier with balanced per-anchor coverage that natural validation sets structurally cannot offer.

#### C.3 Sub-Tier Composition

STRANGE-Bench varies two axes: partner-count $k\!\in\!\{0,\dots,5\}$ and pair-typicality (typical: high-NPMI partners; atypical: low-NPMI or never-co-occurring partners; never-co-occurring: $N_{ij}\!=\!0$ for every pair of the scene, with the fallback below). The anchor-only setting ( $k\!=\!0$ ) splits into a natural-background and an atypical-background variant, giving two sub-tiers at $k\!=\!0$ , two each at $k\!=\!1,\dots,4$ , and one tier_never sub-tier for a total of eleven, the grid of the main tables. Six more sub-tiers appear only in App. A : the typical and atypical pair at $k\!=\!5$ ( $T_{6}$ ), a $k\!=\!1$ pair whose anchor box covers at most $4\%$ of the image, and never-co-occurring cliques with $k\!=\!3$ and $k\!=\!4$ .

Anchors are allocated evenly over the $80$ COCO categories ( $74$ to $80$ anchors per sub-tier of the main grid after filtering). Partners are added one at a time, and each new partner must meet the arm’s rule with the anchor and with every partner already chosen. The rule uses $A_{ij}=(1-\text{NPMI}_{ij})/2$ on COCO train2017 : typical pairs need $A_{ij}\leq 0.45$ (NPMI $\geq 0.1$ ) and at least $10$ co-occurrences with the anchor; atypical pairs need $A_{ij}\geq 0.55$ (NPMI $\leq-0.1$ ). Pairs that never co-occur get $A_{ij}=0.8$ , above every co-occurring pair. Each partner is drawn by a softmax over the $15$ highest-scoring candidates; if no candidate meets the rule, the sampler takes the highest-scoring one.

For tier_never we attempt a strict $3$ -clique (every pair among $\{\text{anchor},p_{1},p_{2}\}$ has $N_{ij}\!=\!0$ ), or a pair when the anchor has a single never-co-occurring partner; for ubiquitous anchors that admit no $3$ -clique ( person , car , chair , …), we pad the largest strict sub-clique with atypical partners. Of the $466$ tier_never scenes ( $397$ with two partners, $69$ with one), $83.7\%$ are strict cliques; the rest are fallback scenes whose strict-clique status can be derived from labels_intended together with the COCO-train co-occurrence matrix. The cliques with $k\!=\!3$ and $k\!=\!4$ are strict by construction and use only pairs whose expected count under independence, $Np_{i}p_{j}$ , is at least $10$ , so that a zero count reflects absence rather than two rare classes; anchors without such a clique are skipped ( $46$ and $28$ anchors remain). #### C.4 Sanity Diagnostics

Three checks test the typicality structure and two nuisance variables, object scale and position. (i) Tier semantics. Per-tier intended-pair NPMI on COCO-train separates the typical and atypical sub-tiers at every quartile: the lower quartile of every typical sub-tier is at least $+0.05$ and the upper quartile of every atypical sub-tier at most $-0.20$ (typical means $+0.18$ to $+0.24$ , atypical $-0.27$ to $-0.31$ ; pairs that never co-occur have no NPMI and are left out; Fig. 16 ). (ii) Object-scale balance. Median relative bbox area within typical/atypical pairs at fixed $k$ is matched to within $0.15$ – $0.66$ pt for $k\!=\!1,\dots,5$ (Fig. 17 ); the largest gap is at $k\!=\!1$ ( $0.66$ pt), and the single anchor of $T_{1}$ is $3.0$ pt larger on the natural than on the atypical background. (iii) Spatial layout. Box centers sit below the image center on average in every sub-tier (mean vertical position $0.60$ to $0.67$ of the image height, $0$ at the top), and the typical and atypical sub-tiers at the same $k$ match within $0.013$ in mean position (Fig. 18 ), so the two arms share the same center bias.
