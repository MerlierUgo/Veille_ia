## Learning the Next Best Move with Visual Diffusion Models

In this paper, we show that visual diffusion models can learn to predict the next best move (NBM) in tasks requiring sequential reasoning and lookahead, without explicitly exploring future steps or exhaustive searching at inference. Previous work demonstrated that these models can generate complete solutions to geometric and reasoning problems. Here, we show that simple fine-tuning on visual state–NBM pairs enables a pretrained model to select actions whose value depends on consequences several steps ahead. Each move is predicted directly in image space from the current state, without an explicit symbolic representation or search procedure. Repeated NBM predictions then construct a solution one decision at a time.

We demonstrate this capability on chess puzzles and tile-based pathfinding, and show that it extends to Connect Four, Go and billiards. In chess, the model predicts legal moves and finds the best move far more often than by recalling similar training positions, including piece sacrifices whose benefit emerges only through later tactical continuations. In pathfinding, it constructs shortest paths on uniform-cost maps and cost-optimal routes across terrain tiles with different traversal costs, choosing longer routes when they are cheaper overall. These costs are learned implicitly from examples, without numerical cost supervision. These results show that direct visual move prediction can support globally informed decisions and successful sequential problem solving.


### 1 Introduction

“I see only one move ahead, but it is always the correct one.” — attributed to José Raúl Capablanca

Visual generative models have demonstrated a surprising ability to solve hard problems by generating their solutions as images. Beyond synthesizing realistic appearance, visual diffusion models can produce geometric configurations, complete puzzles, and construct paths that satisfy complex constraints [ 7 , 22 , 8 , 5 ] . These findings suggest that visual generation can support forms of reasoning traditionally addressed by specialized algorithms. In this work, we investigate whether visual diffusion models can also learn to predict the next best move (NBM): choosing an action that advances a task toward its solution, without searching over future states. It’s worth mentioning that our goal is not to develop a task-specific solver (e.g., a competitive chess player), but to demonstrate and analyze an intriguing capability of visual diffusion models. We use chess, pathfinding, and other tasks as testbeds to investigate and better understand this capability.

Choosing the next move introduces a further challenge: its quality depends on what can happen afterward. A valid action needs only to satisfy the rules of the current state; the best action must also account for consequences several steps ahead. Generating a complete solution allows its parts to be represented and refined together. NBM prediction instead commits to a single action without producing the continuation that justifies it. The challenge is therefore to capture the benefit of lookahead through direct visual prediction, without explicitly exploring future steps at inference.

Chess puzzles provide a concrete setting for this challenge. From a board image alone, the model must predict a move that is both legal and directed toward solving the puzzle against the opponent’s best responses. Piece sacrifices are particularly striking: their benefit emerges only through later consequences. Section 2 develops this example alongside cost-aware pathfinding, illustrating the decisions we seek to learn.

We show that a pretrained visual diffusion model can learn to make such decisions by fine-tuning on pairs of visual states and their next best moves. Given the current state as an image, the model directly generates a visual prediction of the NBM, without explicit search or symbolic reasoning modules. After the move is applied, the updated state serves as input for the next prediction. Repeated application constructs a solution one decision at a time, selecting actions whose benefit depends on later consequences. The same formulation extends to predicting a short sequence of future moves at once. The number of moves predicted is commonly referred to as the prediction horizon. A video diffusion model marks successive moves and generates their resulting states, alternating between the solver and the opponent in chess.

In our work, we show that, under the same training budget, predicting several moves together improves the accuracy of the next move, even when the remaining predictions are not executed. Generating a continuation therefore offers a benefit beyond producing a single action. In deterministic tasks, the model can also execute several predicted moves before being called again. Against an opponent, whose replies may differ from those predicted, we execute only the first move and make a new prediction after observing the reply.

Our study of next best move (NBM) prediction reveals a surprising finding: predicting the move from the first denoising step is more accurate than using a fully denoised sample. At this early stage, several possible moves appear as overlapping marks in a single image. The strongest marks often reveal the correct move. As denoising proceeds, the model settles on one candidate, which may differ from the move most strongly indicated at the first step. We can thus read out the model’s preferred move before denoising is complete.

#### 2.1 Chess Puzzles

A chess puzzle presents a board position and asks the player to find a sequence of moves that achieves a tactical objective, such as checkmate or a decisive material advantage. Solving it requires choosing moves that succeed against the opponent’s best responses. Many moves may be legal, but only a few advance toward the solution.

Our model receives the board as an image and predicts a move by marking its source and destination squares. It receives no symbolic board encoding, piece identities, or explicit movement rules. A bishop, for example, is represented only by its visual appearance; the model must learn from visual examples how it moves and when moving it is useful. Fine-tuning pairs show board images and their next best moves, providing supervision for both valid movement patterns and effective action selection.

Figure 3 presents a striking example of a decision whose value becomes apparent only through its later consequences. In this held-out puzzle, White can capture an undefended rook with Qxe3, gaining material immediately. Instead, the model predicts Qf7+, offering its queen to force the black king into the corner. After the reply Kh8 is applied and the new board is fed back, it predicts Qf8+, sacrificing the queen a second time to draw the black rook onto f8, and after Black’s Rxf8 it delivers mate with Rxf8#. The sacrifice is particularly revealing as it gives up the most valuable piece despite an immediate material gain.

Each move is predicted separately from the current board. The model commits to the queen sacrifice before generating any subsequent move, without constructing future board states, evaluating alternative continuations, or performing explicit tree search. Successive NBM predictions recover the displayed tactical sequence, with each prediction matching the puzzle’s prescribed move. This example illustrates how direct visual prediction can select an action whose justification depends on a coordinated continuation several moves ahead, without explicitly generating that continuation at inference. The example is not an isolated case: on the 1,001 held-out puzzles tagged as sacrifices, the video model finds the next best move 47.9% of the time (57.3% with voting), against 16.4% for copying the move of the nearest training position ( Tab. 1 ).


#### 2.2 Cost-Aware Pathfinding

Figure 2 shows two examples of navigation on held-out tile maps. The agent moves between adjacent traversable tiles, and each terrain type has a fixed cost for entering a tile. The objective is to reach the goal while minimizing the sum of tile costs along the route. With uniform tile costs, this reduces to finding a shortest path; with terrain-dependent tile costs, a longer route can be cheaper. Both settings can be solved by Dijkstra’s algorithm, which explicitly expands candidate states and maintains cumulative distance estimates. Our model instead predicts one move at a time, without explicitly exploring future routes.

The model receives no numerical terrain tile costs during training, learning only from examples of optimal next moves. Through successive NBM predictions, it constructs cost-optimal routes, taking additional steps when these avoid sufficiently expensive tiles. Each move is predicted from the current visual state, without generating the remaining route or comparing alternative future continuations. These examples illustrate decisions whose quality depends on costs several steps ahead: entering a cheap tile can lead to an expensive continuation, whereas an initially indirect move can enable a cheaper route overall. This holds beyond the two examples: on held-out maps where the cheapest route either detours around or crosses an expensive band, the model makes the right choice on $85.0$ % of maps against $50$ % for chance, and it finds the cheapest route on $87.5$ % of test maps against $59.2$ % for the fewest-steps route ( Tab. 2 ).


### 3 Method

We turn a pretrained visual generative model into a decision maker without changing its architecture: the state is an image, the action is an edit of that image, and decisions are made by sampling.


#### 3.1 Next best move prediction

A task is a sequence of states $s_{0},s_{1},\dots$ observed only through renderings $x_{t}=R(s_{t})$ . Taking action $a_{t}$ in state $s_{t}$ leads to $s_{t+1}=E(s_{t},a_{t})$ , where in two-player games, the environment $E$ also applies the opponent’s reply. Training data provide, for each state, a reference next best move $a^{\star}_{t}$ taken from a known solution. We write $M(x,a)$ for the image $x$ with action $a$ drawn on it ( Sec. 3.2 ) and train a conditional generator $p_{\theta}$ to produce $M(x_{t},a^{\star}_{t})$ from $x_{t}$ and a fixed text prompt $c$ . At inference, a deterministic decoder $D$ reads the action from the generated image,

[Tableau] $$\hat{a}_{t}=D(\hat{y}_{t},x_{t}),\qquad\hat{y}_{t}\sim p_{\theta}(\,\cdot\mid x_{t},c),$$ (1)

the environment returns $s_{t+1}=E(s_{t},\hat{a}_{t})$ , and the model is called again on $x_{t+1}$ . A solution is thus built one decision at a time, in closed loop.

No search. Each call draws a single noise sample and runs a fixed number of denoising steps. The model never enumerates, simulates or scores alternative actions, and neither the environment nor any engine is queried within a call. Whatever the decision anticipates about later steps must therefore be carried by a single generation.


#### 3.2 Actions as image edits

An action moves an entity from cell $u$ to cell $v$ of a grid, such as a chess piece between squares or an agent to a neighbouring tile, or places an entity on a cell $v$ , such as a stone in Go. $M(x,a)$ marks $u$ in blue and $v$ in red (only $v$ for placements) and leaves all other pixels unchanged ( Fig. 3 ), so input and target differ only where the action takes place, and the output space is the image itself rather than a task specific action vocabulary. The prompt is fixed per task and contains only what the image cannot show, such as which side should move and how the predicted move should be marked on the image. Rules, move generators and costs are never provided as input; in pathfinding with terrain, the cost of each tile type is neither drawn nor stated. What the model knows about them is learned from the training targets alone.

Decoding. $D$ inverts the marking. For each cell, it fits the difference between $\hat{y}_{t}$ and $x_{t}$ as a blend toward the blue and the red tint, giving strengths $\alpha_{b}(u)$ and $\alpha_{r}(v)$ , and returns the pair of the strongest blue and the strongest red cell. This readout uses no rules, and an action that cannot be executed counts as a failure.


#### 3.3 From one move to a horizon

The closed loop of Eq. 1 makes one NBM prediction per call. A horizon model makes $H$ of them in a single generation: it predicts the NBM on the current state, the state that move leads to, the NBM on that state, and so on. For a reference continuation $a^{\star}_{t},\dots,a^{\star}_{t+H-1}$ with visual states $x^{\star}_{t+k}=R(s^{\star}_{t+k})$ and $x^{\star}_{t}=x_{t}$ , the target is

[Tableau] $$\big(\,M(x^{\star}_{t+k-1},a^{\star}_{t+k-1}),\;x^{\star}_{t+k}\,\big)_{k=1}^{H},$$ (2)

rendered as a video that starts from $x_{t}$ and alternates Decide frames, the current state with the next move marked, and Imagine frames, the visual state after that move. For a single agent, the horizon consists of the agent’s successive NBMs; in two-player games, it alternates between the sides, and the NBM predicted for the opponent is its best reply. In chess, the video model’s Decide frames also mark the moving piece’s legal destinations with green dots, which the decoder does not read (Appendix B ) and which serve to check whether the model has learned how each piece moves. Each move is decoded from the generated frames, $\hat{a}_{t+k-1}=D(\hat{m}_{k},\hat{x}_{k-1})$ with $\hat{x}_{0}=x_{t}$ , so every predicted move is an explicit, checkable decision on an explicit state.

Executing a horizon. How many predicted moves to execute depends on how much of the future the current state determines. In a deterministic single-agent task, they can be executed directly: this replaces $H$ calls with one, and when $H$ equals the solution length $L$ , the entire solution is drawn in a single image. Against an opponent, moves beyond the next depend on replies that may differ from the predicted ones, so we use a receding horizon [ 15 ] : only the first predicted move is executed, the true state is observed, and the model is called again. This recovers the closed loop of Eq. 1 , but each decision now comes with the continuation the model expects to follow from it. One formulation thus covers single decisions ( $H=1$ ), decisions with an explicit continuation (receding horizon) and complete solutions ( $H=L$ ); Sec. 4 compares them.

### 4 Analysis and Ablations

Setup. Chess positions are drawn from the Lichess puzzle database and split by game to prevent overlap between training and evaluation. Our main models are trained on up to three million puzzles and evaluated on 10,000 held-out positions (Appendix A.1 ). The image model predicts the next move of each puzzle solution, while the video model predicts up to eight plies, including the opponent’s replies. The remaining tasks are generated with exact solvers or engines, used only to construct training targets and score predictions (Appendix A ). Unless stated otherwise, we use $20$ Euler steps for the image model and $30$ UniPC steps for the video model, and invalid actions count as failures. We report next-best-move accuracy for chess and the fraction of optimal routes for pathfinding.

Recall or play? To test whether these decisions reflect the position rather than recalled training examples, we compare against retrieval from the training set, evaluate on boards transformed by exact symmetries of the game, and alter single pieces so that the best move changes: the model’s choices track these changes, while retrieval does not.

> Figure 5 : “top”: Two similar board configurations, where the only difference is an added white bishop on c3. “bottom”: how much our model’s confidence in the move drops when each piece is removed.

Is the model relying on the right pieces? Figure 5 shows the same effect on a single held-out puzzle (rated 1932). On the original board, our model plays the rook sacrifice Rxh6+, which forces mate in three. Adding a white bishop on c3 leaves the position almost unchanged visually, yet it changes the best move: the bishop now guards h8, so Qh8# mates in one, and the model switches to exactly this move. To see what each decision rests on, we remove one piece at a time and measure how much the model’s confidence in its move drops. On the original board, the sacrifice depends on the pieces that build the later mating net, namely the queen that delivers the final checks and the bishop and pawn that cover the king’s escape squares. With the added bishop, the decision depends most on the two pieces that make the new mate work: the bishop guarding h8 and Black’s own pawn on g6, which blocks the king’s only escape. Removing either of them indeed makes Qh8 no longer mate. The model thus separates two boards that differ in a single piece as the position requires, and Table 1 shows that this holds over thousands of such changes.

Implicit policy. A diffusion model can also score outputs it did not sample, through its denoising error, as in diffusion classifiers [ 12 , 3 ] . For this analysis only, we enumerate the legal moves, draw each into the output image, measure how well the model denoises it under shared noise, and turn the errors into a policy with a softmax. Its most likely move is more often correct than any sampled readout of the same model, and it ranks the remaining moves in agreement with Stockfish (Appendix D.2 ). In Figure 4 , every knight move gives check, yet most of the policy goes to Nh6+, the only one that wins.

One step reads the decision. Training gives the model a distribution $\pi(m\mid c)$ over the moves $m$ of a board $c$ , and the number of denoising steps only decides how this distribution is read out. The reason lies in the training objective: the best prediction of the clean output from a noisy one is the mean of all clean outputs that could have produced it, and at full noise, where the input carries no information about the move, this mean is the average of the move images $I_{m}$ weighted by their probabilities,

[Tableau] $$\hat{x}_{0}\;=\;\mathbb{E}[\,x_{0}\mid c\,]\;=\;\textstyle\sum_{m}\pi(m\mid c)\,I_{m}\qquad\text{(full noise)}$$ (5)

(Tweedie’s formula [ 4 ] ; Proof in Appendix D.1 ). A single step therefore returns the whole distribution at once, and its strongest marks pick out the most likely move. This is why one step is the more accurate readout in every task that asks for a single decision (Figure 6 , Table 1 ), and it also sets its limit: because the average hardly depends on the noise, repeated one-step outputs agree, and eight of them contain the answer for only 69.2% of chess puzzles. Full sampling instead follows each noise draw to a single move, chosen in proportion to its probability. One sample is right less often, but eight of them contain the answer for 85.4%. One step is thus the better answer and sampling the better source of candidates, and the gap between the two is headroom that no readout without the answer yet exploits. Averaging also explains why routes need full sampling: the average of several routes is not itself a route. In every task the decision is fixed within the first steps of denoising (Appendix D.3 ), as observed for video models solving mazes [ 16 ] ; the remaining steps refine one candidate rather than search among several (Appendix D ).

Going beyond chess. The same formulation applies without modification to tasks with substantially different action spaces (Table 2 ). What these tasks share is what the formulation needs: what the player observes can be shown in one image, and the action can be expressed as an edit of that image, for instance by marking the piece to move and its destination, the point to play, or the direction and strength of a shot. Within this class, the model must infer the properties of each task that are never stated. In pathfinding it is the cost of terrain: with costs hidden, the closed-loop image model finds the cheapest route on $87.5$ % of maps, against $59.2$ % for breadth-first search, which ignores terrain, while on uniform maps the video model, which plans several moves per clip, generalises better to longer paths. In Connect Four it is gravity: the disc lands in the correct row in all but one of 2,000 test positions for the image model and all but eight for the video model. In Go it is the rules of play: stones land on a legal point $99.8$ % of the time, and the model matches KataGo’s move on $31.1$ % of positions, against $1.7$ % for copying the nearest training position. In billiards it is the physics of the table: the video model pots the ball on $47.9$ % of held-out layouts, against $19.0$ % for reusing the nearest training shot (Appendix A ).

Learning from the continuation. Drawing the future also helps the model decide the present. A video model trained to draw the whole solution line, replies included, predicts the next move more accurately than the same model trained on the first move alone, although only the first move is scored (24.6% against 21.4% in chess, with the same 400,000 puzzles and training budget), and the same holds on terrain for both models. Controlled experiments on a purpose-built pathfinding task show why (Appendix K ): when the first move is decided by a single wall $k$ cells down one of two otherwise identical corridors, with equal training a model only trained on the first move ignores the wall beyond two cells, while one trained to draw the route uses it up to twelve cells away; the gain comes from training rather than from reading the drawn continuation at inference. Whether the drawn moves should also be played depends on the task. In deterministic tasks such as shortest paths, executing a whole clip is as accurate as predicting again after each move, with far fewer model calls. Against an opponent, a new prediction on the observed board is better than following the drawn continuation (52.5% against 41.2% for the next move on identical boards). Predicting the future can make each decision better, and against an opponent, each decision is best made on the board as it actually is.


### 5 Related Work

Having presented our formulation and results, we now place them in the context of visual problem solving, sequential generation, and learned decision-making. Our focus is on learning task rules and action preferences from visual examples, and using this knowledge to select the next best move (NBM).

Goren et al. [ 7 ] show that visual diffusion models can be used to generate complete solutions to geometric problems in pixel space, including the inscribed square, Steiner tree, and maximum-area polygon. We extend visual solution generation to sequential decisions: in chess and pathfinding, successive predicted actions construct solutions, even when an action’s value depends on later consequences.

DiffThinker [ 8 ] fine-tunes a pretrained image-editing model to generate complete visual solutions to tasks including mazes, TSP, Sudoku, and jigsaws. Video models have also shown signs of reasoning by generating maze trajectories, both zero-shot [ 23 ] and after supervised fine-tuning [ 26 ] . These works establish visual generation as a means of producing complete solutions or their unfolding trajectories. Our single-move model makes a different commitment: it selects an action without generating its future continuation. Our horizon model connects these formulations by generating short continuations, allowing us to compare decisions made with and without an explicit visual trajectory.

Wewer et al. [ 22 ] are closely related where they show that sequential generation can improve reasoning in denoising models. Their Spatial Reasoning Models use uncertainty to order the generation of spatial variables in visual Sudoku and other constraint tasks. Their approach selects which unknown part of a solution to resolve next, prioritizing variables that can be predicted with greater confidence. Our experiments address action selection according to its contribution to the eventual outcome. The sacrifice in chess and the longer but cheaper terrain route illustrate choices that cannot be judged by immediate gains alone.

Ezquerro and Özdenizci [ 5 ] improve logical constraint satisfaction through object-centric relational abstractions that guide the generation of complete solution images. They demonstrate improvements on tasks including colored Sudoku, Tangram, and logic-based face generation. Their contribution centers on adding representations of objects and their relationships. Our models acquire their decision-making capabilities through fine-tuning pretrained visual diffusion models, without additional object-discovery or relational modules.

Learning strong actions without inference-time search has also been studied in chess using symbolic representations. Ruoss et al. [ 18 ] train on engine-annotated positions to reach grandmaster-level play without inference-time search. DiffuSearch [ 27 ] uses discrete diffusion to predict future states and actions alongside the next move, using the generated continuation as a form of implicit search. These methods use chess-specific encodings and orders of magnitude more engine-labelled positions. We ask whether a general visual generator can learn valid and effective actions from pixels across tasks ranging from chess to billiards. Unlike a constrained chess output vocabulary, our pixel readout must learn legal and effective movements from examples alone. Our horizon variant further connects our approach to DiffuSearch by expressing predicted actions and future states visually.

Our horizon-based execution also relates to diffusion models for planning and control. Diffuser [ 11 ] generates trajectories through denoising using receding-horizon planning, while Diffusion Policy [ 2 ] predicts action sequences and executes part of each sequence before predicting again. Our horizon model follows the same execution principle: execute the first predicted action, observe the resulting state, and predict again. Here, states and action markings share a visual representation, and actions are decoded from generated pixels. In adversarial tasks, the generated continuation also includes predicted opponent replies. This connection places our comparison of single-move and horizon-based prediction within the broader study of learning to act through generative models.


### 6 Discussion and Conclusions

In this work, we show that visual diffusion models can learn to decide what to do next by learning to draw the next move. This simple formulation supports a surprisingly wide range of decisions: sacrificing a rook for a payoff several moves later, taking a longer route to avoid costly terrain, or selecting a shot in billiards. In sequential tasks, the model builds a solution one decision at a time, without explicitly searching through future continuations.

A natural question is why we should generate a move rather than classify it. In chess, a classifier with an output designed to score pairs of board squares can outperform our model. The advantage of generation is that the same output representation works across tasks: the model draws a chess move, a route, or a billiard shot directly on the image. This goes beyond reusing an architecture. When we train a single model on Connect Four and pathfinding together, with the same prompt for both, it matches the single-task model on Connect Four and stays within a few points of it on pathfinding. The appendix reports these comparisons in detail.

The most intriguing finding is that the first denoising step can be more useful for choosing a move than a fully denoised sample. It shows several possible moves as overlapping marks. In our experiments, reading the strongest marks gives a more accurate single decision than drawing one fully denoised sample, without any additional training. Full denoising remains useful when we want diverse candidates. For a model that learns to act by drawing, the first sketch can already be the better guide to what to do next.

### Appendix A Tasks and data

Each task presents a rendered state and asks the model to draw an action. The video model also draws subsequent actions and states. Prompts never state the rules, costs, or physics. Except for chess and Go, the prompt is simply “Continue.” An exact solver or strong engine supplies the labels used to build the data and score predictions. Below, we specify each task, its rendering, data and splits, and evaluation.


#### A.1 Chess

Problem. Positions come from the Lichess puzzle database. Each puzzle starts after the opponent’s last move and has an engine-verified solution line in which only the first move of the side to move is forced to be unique. The target is that first move.

Rendering. Boards are drawn from White’s side at $256{\times}256$ pixels, without coordinates. The model marks a move by tinting the origin square blue and the destination square red. The image model’s prompt is “ White to move. Highlight the best move: the origin square in blue and the destination square in red. ” (Black analogously). The video model additionally draws green dots on the legal destination squares of the moving piece and then shows the board after the move, as described in Appendix B .

Data and splits. Puzzles are assigned to splits by a hash of the game they come from, so all puzzles of one game share a split. Any puzzle whose start position appears in validation or test (same pieces, side to move, castling and en passant rights) is removed from training, which also excludes transpositions. Validation and test keep only well-established puzzles (rating deviation at most 90 and at least 200 plays) and are stratified over eight rating bands, from below 1000 to above 2500. This gives 5,808,734 training, 2,000 validation and 10,000 test puzzles. The models of Tab. 1 train on the first three million training puzzles. The variants and analyses in this supplement use the first million unless stated otherwise. For the video model, each puzzle becomes a line of up to eight solution plies (mean 3.6).

Engine labels. For the implicit-policy analysis and for contrastive guidance we score every legal move with Stockfish 19 (all moves at depth 12, at most 4M nodes per position, one thread). Scores are converted to a win probability for the side to move with the Lichess formula, and forced mates rank above every centipawn score. The engine’s best move equals the puzzle answer on 99.8% of test positions.

Metrics and baselines. A prediction is correct when the move read from the generated image ( Appendix C ) equals the puzzle answer. Promotion pieces are ignored. We report two subsets: sacrifices (1,001 test puzzles tagged as such by Lichess) and far from training , the test puzzles whose nearest training position differs on 13 to 16 of the 64 squares (1,757 puzzles against the three million training puzzles, 2,403 against the first million). Retrieval baselines and their construction are in Appendix E .

#### A.2 Shortest paths and terrain

Problem. An agent on an $N{\times}N$ grid ( $N\leq 12$ ) must reach a goal. In the uniform variant every step costs the same and the target is a shortest path. In the terrain variant, cells are floor, mud or sand, entering them costs 1, 2 or 4, and the target is a cheapest path. Walls cannot be entered. The costs are never shown and must be inferred from examples.

Rendering. Maps are drawn at $384{\times}384$ pixels with 32-pixel cells, so a cell is exactly one video-model token and $2{\times}2$ image-model tokens. The agent is a blue disc, the goal a red ring, and visited cells are green. Mud has stripes and sand has dots, so the two can be told apart without colour alone. A rule-free decoder reads every cell by matching its centre, ring and corner colours to the known palette.

Formats. The image model operates in a closed loop: it marks the next cell with a red disc, the move is applied, and the resulting state becomes the next input. The video model generates segments of up to eight moves, each with a decision frame and the resulting state. Longer routes chain these segments. The receding-horizon variant executes only the first decision from each segment ( Appendix C ).

Data. Maps come from three families: random blocks (15 to 32% wall density), walls with one or two gaps, and rooms made by recursive division. Terrain maps cover 25 to 45% of free cells with mud or sand. Targets are computed by breadth-first search (Dijkstra for terrain), breaking ties by fewest turns, and evaluation accepts any optimal path. Each variant has 200,000 training maps and 600 validation maps. The uniform variant has 1,000 in-distribution test maps and three out-of-distribution tests of 500 maps each: larger maps ( $N$ = 11 or 12), longer paths (21 to 32 steps) and mazes, a family never seen in training. The terrain variant has 1,000 test maps and a probe set of 500 maps on which every cheapest path either crosses a band of costly terrain (250 maps) or goes around it (250 maps). No evaluation map appears in training, and the evaluation sets are pairwise disjoint.

Metrics and baselines. A route is valid if it is connected, avoids walls and revisits, and changes nothing off the path. It is optimal if it is valid and has the optimal length (terrain: the optimal cost). On the probe set, a route is correct if it is valid and crosses the band exactly when the cheapest paths do. The terrain-blind baseline follows a shortest path in steps. It is cost-optimal on 59.2% of terrain test maps and, by construction of the probe set, correct on half of the probe maps. The pretrained models without fine-tuning never produce a valid route. On the maze test, the image and video models are optimal on 78.4 and 84.4% of maps.


#### A.3 Connect Four

Problem. Standard $7{\times}6$ Connect Four: the model chooses a column for the side to move. That a disc falls to the lowest free row is never stated.

Rendering. Boards are $256{\times}256$ pixels with 32-pixel cells. Red moves first and blue second, and a disc in the top margin shows whose turn it is. A move is drawn as the mover’s disc in its landing cell, tinted green.

Data. Positions come from self-play games in which each player plays a random legal move with a probability drawn from $\{0,0.05,0.1,0.2,0.35,0.5\}$ and otherwise a best or value-preserving move. We keep only critical positions, in which not every column has the same game-theoretic value. Labels come from Pascal Pons’ exact solver. The label is a best column, and evaluation accepts any best column. There are 200,000 training, 1,000 validation and 2,000 test positions, plus a hard test set of 1,000 positions with neither an immediate win nor an immediate threat. Positions are deduplicated up to mirror symmetry within and across splits, and each evaluation set takes at most one position per game.

Metrics and baselines. Legal, correct landing row : the marked column is not full and the disc is drawn in the row where it would land. Keeps the win : on the hard test set, the chosen move preserves the game-theoretic value of the position, so a won position stays won and a drawn one stays drawn. Of the 1,000 hard positions, 936 are won. The one-move heuristic wins immediately if it can, otherwise blocks the opponent’s immediate win, otherwise plays the most central legal column.


#### A.4 Go

Problem. Full $19{\times}19$ Go. The model marks the move KataGo prefers.

Data and labels. We take games between strong amateurs (7 dan, or both players at least 6 dan) from the public KGS archive, keeping even games of at least 80 moves played up to 2015 without bots. Positions are sampled from the middle of each game. Each position is searched by KataGo (network b11c768 , 400 visits, Chinese rules, komi 7.5), and the label is the move with the most visits. Positions whose outcome is already clear are filtered out. The smaller training set has 100,000 positions from 44,191 games, and the larger one has 1,000,000 positions from 91,253 games. Both share 1,000 validation and 2,000 test positions, and no game or board position is shared between splits.

Rendering and prompt. Boards are $384{\times}384$ pixels, and the move is a red disc on the chosen point. The prompt is “ Black to play. Mark the best move with a red dot. ” (White analogously).

Metrics and baselines. A prediction is correct when the decoded point equals KataGo’s move. With about 296 legal points per position, a random legal move is correct 0.35% of the time in expectation. The single random draw reported in Tab. 2 is correct on 15 of the 2,000 positions (0.7%). Copying the move of the nearest training position is correct 1.7% of the time. For reference, KataGo’s raw policy network alone (no search) matches its own searched move 74.0% of the time, and the move played in the game matches 39.5%.


#### A.5 Billiards

Problem. A table holds the cue ball, a red target ball and up to four other balls. The model chooses a shot, a cue direction and a power, and succeeds if the red ball is potted and the cue ball is not.

Simulator and solver. Shots are simulated by a deterministic 2D simulator without spin: rolling friction, elastic ball collisions (restitution 0.95), cushions (restitution 0.8) and six pockets. For each layout we simulate 12,960 shots (every $0.25^{\circ}$ and nine powers) and take as label the centre of the widest successful range of angles that is robust to small changes in power. Shots include direct pots, banks, kicks, kisses and combinations.

Rendering. Tables are $512{\times}288$ pixels. A shot is drawn as a cyan aiming line from the cue ball to where its centre would first meet a cushion, drawn under the balls, together with a magenta cue stick behind the cue ball and a power bar under the table. A rule-free decoder reads the angle from the line and stick and the power from the filled part of the bar. On clean renders its angle error has a median of $0.003^{\circ}$ . In the video model’s clip, the decision frame is followed by the simulated motion of the balls.

Data and metrics. There are 200,000 training, 1,000 validation and 2,000 test layouts, and a mirrored copy of every test layout. No layout is shared between splits up to the table’s symmetries. A shot counts as potted if the decoded shot succeeds in the simulator. The nearest-shot baseline finds the training layout whose cue and red balls are closest (including mirrored layouts) and reuses its shot. It pots 19.0% of test layouts, and a random shot 0.7%. On the mirrored layouts the one-step image and video models pot 28.2 and 49.1%, against 28.0 and 47.9% on the originals.


#### A.6 N-queens

Problem and rendering. On a partly filled $N$ -queens board, the model places the next queen so that the board can still be completed with no two queens sharing a row, column or diagonal. Boards with $N\leq 12$ are drawn on a fixed $12{\times}12$ grid. Qwen-Image-2.1, fine-tuned with rank-64 LoRA, receives the prompt “Continue.” and marks the next queen in red. The action is read from the strongest red mark, without a minimum-strength threshold.

Data and evaluation. We train on 200,000 boards for 24k steps, using sizes $N=8,9,10$ . Whole solution families related by rotation or reflection are held out from training for validation and test: 4 of 12 families at $N=8$ , 16 of 46 at $N=9$ , and 30 of 92 at $N=10$ . Sizes 11 and 12 are never seen in training. No test board or any of its rotations or reflections occurs in training. Exact enumeration supplies the labels: any placement that still permits a complete solution is correct. We select one sampling step on validation.

Results. On held-out solution families, the model trained on the first move only makes a completable move on 85.2% of boards and a legal placement on 99.4%. A random non-attacked cell is completable on 71.2% and copying a move from the nearest training board on 62.3%. The copier falls from 96.8% on training families to 62.3% on held-out families, while the model’s advantage over the random non-attacked choice remains similar (+15.5 and +14.0 points). On the never-trained sizes 11 and 12, the model reaches 79.2% against 72.6% for that random baseline, with 97.0% legal placements. On hard boards, it reaches 62.0% against 49.1%. At 20 sampling steps, held-out-family accuracy is 78.8%, below the validation-selected one-step result. The controlled horizon test is in Appendix K .

### Appendix C Inference and evaluation

Sampling. The image model is sampled with a 20-step Euler flow-matching sampler, the video model with UniPC for 30 steps (flow shift 3, no classifier-free guidance).

Reading the action. We decode actions from pixels without using task rules. In chess, we fit the blue and red tint added to each square and select the strongest blue square as the origin and the strongest red square as the destination. The other tasks use the same principle with their respective palettes ( Appendix A ). The strongest mark of each kind determines the action regardless of its strength. We do not constrain the readout to legal actions, so an illegal action counts as a failure. For video, we average each block’s two central frames and check each decision against the board the model imagined immediately before it.

Closed loop and receding horizon. To play a full line or route, the image model is called once per move: its move is applied and the true new state is rendered for the next call. The video model generates up to eight moves at a time. In the receding-horizon variant, only the first decision of each clip is executed and a new clip is generated from the resulting state.

Contrastive guidance. Guided sampling is described and evaluated in Appendix M . With $w=0$ the sampler is the unguided one.

Majority voting. We draw 8 samples with different noise in one batch and keep the most frequent decoded move, breaking ties by sample order. For scoring, samples whose drawing is not a legal move are pooled into a single “no move” vote, which is never correct, so a prediction fails whenever this vote wins. Legality is thus checked by the evaluation only, and pooling is the stricter convention compared with the mode over raw decoded moves of Eq. 4 . In billiards, the vote is the circular median of the decoded angles and the median power. On the transformed and changed boards of Appendix E we use 4 samples.

Few-step sampling. We also sample with fewer denoising steps. For Tab. 1 and Tab. 9 , the number of steps is selected on the validation set only: among 1, 2, 3 and 4 steps and the default, the most accurate setting, with ties going to fewer steps. This selects one step for both models of Tab. 1 , and two steps for the image and one for the video model at one million puzzles. In Tab. 2 , every row uses full sampling, and the “+ one-step” rows of the single-decision tasks (Connect Four, Go, billiards) use exactly one step, without selection, and Tab. 7 reports every step count. With contrastive guidance, fewer steps do not help ( Tab. 6 ), so we report no few-step setting with guidance.


### Appendix D Train with diffusion, decide in one step

Few-step sampling, majority voting and the implicit policy all beat a single full sample ( Tab. 1 ). This section shows that there are three ways of reading one object: the distribution over actions that the model learns in training. The model’s estimate of the clean output at full noise is the average of the action images, each weighted by its probability, so its strongest marks give the most likely action. One denoising step returns this average. Many steps instead turn it into a single draw from the distribution, which is right less often. We derive this from the training objective ( Sec. D.1 ), measure the distribution directly ( Sec. D.2 ), and test the consequences in chess ( Sec. D.3 ) and in the other tasks ( Sec. D.4 ). Section D.5 discusses what follows for inference.

Left: for every noise draw, the first estimate of the clean output is the probability-weighted average (star). During 20 steps, the estimate travels from there to one corner (lines). Right: the move returned over 2,000 noise draws.

Figure 8 shows the idea on an exact toy model with three moves of probability 0.5, 0.3 and 0.2. From every noise draw, the first estimate is the same point, the weighted average of the three move images, and its strongest mark is the most likely move. Twenty steps instead carry each noise draw to one of the three moves, in proportion to their probabilities. If the answer follows the model’s probabilities, the first estimate is right half of the time and a 20-step sample $0.5^{2}+0.3^{2}+0.2^{2}=0.38$ of the time, since both the ground truth and the model must independently choose the same answer.


#### D.1 Why one step reads the most likely action

Setup. For a board $c$ and a noise level $\sigma\in(0,1]$ , training corrupts the clean output $x_{0}$ into $x_{\sigma}=(1-\sigma)\,x_{0}+\sigma\epsilon$ with $\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ , and the network $v$ predicts the velocity $\epsilon-x_{0}$ . Its estimate of the clean output is $\hat{x}_{0}=x_{\sigma}-\sigma v$ . We write $I_{m}$ for the target image of action $m$ and $m^{\star}$ for the correct action, and treat the targets of a board as a mixture: given $c$ , the clean output is $I_{M}$ with $M\sim\pi(\cdot\mid c)$ , where $\pi_{m}=\pi(m\mid c)$ is the probability the model assigns to action $m$ . This ignores variation within the image of one action.

Lemma 1 (the model predicts a conditional mean). The training loss $\mathbb{E}\,\|v(x_{\sigma},c)-(\epsilon-x_{0})\|^{2}$ is minimised by $v^{\star}=\mathbb{E}[\epsilon-x_{0}\mid x_{\sigma},c]$ , and then

[Tableau] $$\hat{x}_{0}(x_{\sigma},c)=\mathbb{E}[\,x_{0}\mid x_{\sigma},c\,].$$ (6)

Proof. Write $X=(x_{\sigma},c)$ for the input, $Y=\epsilon-x_{0}$ for the target and $g(X)=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X]$ . For any predictor $v$ ,

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|v(X)-Y\|^{2}$ $\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\|v(X)-g(X)\|^{2}+\mathbb{E}\|g(X)-Y\|^{2}$ $\displaystyle\quad+2\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[(v(X)-g(X))^{\top}(g(X)-Y)\bigr].$

The last term is zero: conditioned on $X$ , the factor $v(X)-g(X)$ is fixed and $g(X)-Y$ has mean zero. The second term does not depend on $v$ , so the loss is smallest when $v=g$ . Since $x_{\sigma}=(1-\sigma)\,x_{0}+\sigma\epsilon$ , the target can be written as $Y=(x_{\sigma}-x_{0})/\sigma$ , so

[Tableau] $\displaystyle v^{\star}$ $\displaystyle=\bigl(x_{\sigma}-\mathbb{E}[x_{0}\mid X]\bigr)/\sigma,$ $\displaystyle\hat{x}_{0}$ $\displaystyle=x_{\sigma}-\sigma v^{\star}=\mathbb{E}[x_{0}\mid X].\qquad\square$

This is Tweedie’s formula [ 4 ] written for flow matching.

Proposition 1 (the first estimate is a weighted average of actions). At $\sigma=1$ , for every noise draw,

[Tableau] $$\hat{x}_{0}(x_{1},c)=\sum_{m}\pi_{m}\,I_{m}.$$ (7)

Proof. At $\sigma=1$ the input $x_{1}=\epsilon$ is pure noise, independent of $x_{0}$ given $c$ , so by Eq. 6

[Tableau] $$\hat{x}_{0}(x_{1},c)=\mathbb{E}[x_{0}\mid c]=\sum_{m}\pi_{m}I_{m}.\qquad\square$$

A one-step sampler outputs exactly this estimate, $x_{1}-v(x_{1},c)=\hat{x}_{0}(x_{1},c)$ . The image model’s two-step schedule ( $\sigma=1\to 0.02\to 0$ ) returns nearly the same image, since its second step starts almost at $\sigma=0$ .

Reading the average. Suppose the marks add up, $I_{m}=B+\beta\,e_{f(m)}+\rho\,e_{t(m)}$ , where $B$ is the board, $e_{s}$ the tint of square $s$ , and $f(m)$ and $t(m)$ the origin and destination of move $m$ . By Eq. 7 , square $s$ is then blue with strength $\pi_{\mathrm{from}}(s)=\sum_{m:\,f(m)=s}\pi_{m}$ and red with strength $\pi_{\mathrm{to}}(s)=\sum_{m:\,t(m)=s}\pi_{m}$ , and reading the strongest blue and the strongest red square returns the most likely move whenever that move holds more than half of the probability. Below that, the readout can pair the origin of one move with the destination of another when several moves share a square. In practice this is rare ( Sec. D.3 ).

Reading the most likely action beats sampling. A sampler that is integrated to the end draws an action from $\pi$ , which is correct with probability $\pi_{m^{\star}}$ . Reading the most likely action is correct if $\arg\max_{m}\pi_{m}=m^{\star}$ . If the model is calibrated, i.e. among the boards to which it assigns probabilities $\pi$ the correct action is $m$ on a fraction $\pi_{m}$ of them, the two accuracies over boards are $\mathbb{E}[\sum_{m}\pi_{m}^{2}]$ and $\mathbb{E}[\max_{m}\pi_{m}]$ , and since every $\pi_{m}\leq\max_{m^{\prime}}\pi_{m^{\prime}}$ ,

[Tableau] $$\sum_{m}\pi_{m}^{2}\;\leq\;\max_{m^{\prime}}\pi_{m^{\prime}}\sum_{m}\pi_{m}\;=\;\max_{m}\pi_{m},$$ (8)

with equality only when $\pi$ is uniform over the actions it allows. The gap vanishes for a certain and for a uniform model and is largest in between. A majority vote over samples converges to the most likely action, slowly when the leading actions are close, and at least one of $n$ samples is correct with probability $\mathbb{E}[1-(1-\pi_{m^{\star}})^{n}]$ , which approaches one even when $m^{\star}$ is not the most likely action.


#### D.2 Measuring the distribution: the implicit policy

To test these predictions we need $\pi$ itself. A conditional diffusion model can score outputs it did not sample, through how well it denoises them. Diffusion classifiers use this to pick the class whose prompt makes an image easiest to denoise [ 12 , 3 ] . We score every legal move in this way. Unlike the readout of Appendix C , this analysis is given the legal moves.

Scoring a move. We encode $I_{m}$ with the VAE into the latent $x_{0}^{(m)}$ , add noise, $x_{\sigma}^{(m)}=(1-\sigma)\,x_{0}^{(m)}+\sigma\,\epsilon_{k}$ , and average the error of the predicted velocity,

[Tableau] $$E(m)=\frac{1}{|\Sigma|\,K}\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{d}\,\bigl\|v\bigl(x_{\sigma}^{(m)},c\bigr)-\bigl(\epsilon_{k}-x_{0}^{(m)}\bigr)\bigr\|^{2},$$

over $\Sigma=\{0.8,0.9\}$ and $K=8$ noise draws, where $d$ is the size of the latent. All moves of a board are scored with the same draws $\epsilon_{k}$ , so differences in $E(m)$ come only from the moves. The implicit policy is $q(m)\propto\exp(-E(m)/T)$ over the legal moves.

The error reads $\pi$ . Fix a move $m$ and one noise draw $\epsilon$ , and write $z=(1-\sigma)\,I_{m}+\sigma\epsilon$ for the noisy version of the move’s image. The velocity the model is trained to predict is $\epsilon-I_{m}=(z-I_{m})/\sigma$ , and the velocity it predicts can be written as $v=(z-\hat{x}_{0})/\sigma$ , where $\hat{x}_{0}$ is its estimate of the clean output. Subtracting the two, the error of the move becomes

[Tableau] $$\bigl\|v-(\epsilon-I_{m})\bigr\|^{2}\;=\;\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\,\bigl\|I_{m}-\hat{x}_{0}\bigr\|^{2},$$ (9)

for any network: the error of a move is the distance between the move and the model’s estimate.

For the optimal model, Eq. 6 makes this estimate a weighted average of all moves,

[Tableau] $$\hat{x}_{0}=\sum_{k}w_{k}I_{k},\qquad w_{k}\;\propto\;\pi_{k}\,\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{\|z-(1-\sigma)I_{k}\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Bigr),$$

where $w_{k}$ is the probability of move $k$ given the noisy input, by Bayes’ rule: the prior $\pi_{k}$ times how well move $k$ explains $z$ .

With two candidate moves $m$ and $k$ at distance $d=\|I_{m}-I_{k}\|$ , the error of $m$ is $(1-w_{m})^{2}d^{2}/\sigma^{2}$ , and

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\log\frac{w_{m}}{w_{k}}$ $\displaystyle=\underbrace{\log\frac{\pi_{m}}{\pi_{k}}}_{\text{belief}}+\underbrace{\frac{(1-\sigma)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\,d^{2}+\frac{1-\sigma}{\sigma}\,\langle\epsilon,\,I_{m}-I_{k}\rangle}_{\text{evidence in }z}.$ (10)

For a fixed noisy input, a larger $\pi_{m}$ gives a larger $w_{m}$ and therefore a smaller error. Each move is scored on its own noisy input, so between two moves the last term enters with opposite signs, and the comparison holds on average over the noise rather than for every draw. This is why we average over $K$ draws, and the move with the lowest average error is then the one the model believes in most. With more than two candidates, moves that share a square lie closer to the average than isolated ones, so the errors follow $\pi$ only approximately, and Sec. D.3 measures how closely. The noise level sets the balance between the two parts: near $\sigma=0$ the evidence dominates and every move is denoised perfectly, while at $\sigma=1$ the evidence vanishes and the error is the distance to the average of Eq. 7 . Intermediate levels such as ours keep both.

Results. On the first 1,000 test puzzles, the move with the lowest error is the answer for 67.0% of the puzzles and among the three lowest for 88.1% (a random legal move: 5.6% and 16.1%, the pretrained model scored with four noise draws: 6.7% and 15.5%). Within each puzzle, the errors also rank the other moves in agreement with Stockfish, among quiet moves as well and after removing the effects of piece type, captures and checks.

#### D.5 What this means for inference

Majority voting. Samples from different noise can propose different moves, and a vote keeps the most frequent one. Voting therefore helps in proportion to how much the samples differ. At one million puzzles, at least one of eight samples is correct on 79.0% of the puzzles for the video model, 70.0% for the image model and 62.4% with guidance, which concentrates the samples, and voting gains 10.4, 8.2 and 2.7 points ( Tab. 9 ). Few-step samples are the extreme case: they all read the same average, so they barely differ, and voting adds little to them ( Tab. 1 ).

No search at inference. Where iterative denoising performs optimisation, more steps improve the solution and harder instances need more of them [ 19 ] . We observe the opposite on both counts: accuracy falls with more steps, and harder positions gain less, not more, from them. At equal compute, samples are worth more than steps: on the 10,000 test puzzles, one step reaches 62.5% with only one network evaluation, 20 steps 56.0% with 20, and a vote over eight 20-step samples 63.8% with 160. Look-ahead is acquired in training, as in chess models trained without search [ 18 ] , and extra computation at inference can only select among the model’s own candidates. That is also where most of the remaining headroom lies: the correct move is among the eight 20-step samples for 85.4% of the test puzzles and among the implicit policy’s three most likely moves for 88.1%, while no readout that does not use the answer exceeds 67.0%. The same holds for whole puzzles: one or more of eight 20-step samples solves 69.0% of them ( Appendix F ).

Training at full noise only. Equation 7 makes a prediction that can be tested directly. If one step returns the average over actions, then the full-noise part of the training loss, which is plain regression of the marked image, should be enough to produce the decision. We test this on Connect Four with the same backbone, LoRA, data, prompt and steps, but with every training example at $\sigma=1$ . Read in one step, this model keeps the win on 83.5% of the hard positions, against 76.4% for the diffusion-trained model, as expected from a model that spends all of its training on the one noise level the readout uses. What it cannot provide is alternatives. Its outputs for eight noise draws agree on 98.4% of positions, so they contain a value-preserving move on only 83.9%, whereas eight 20-step samples of the diffusion-trained model, which agree on 3.7%, contain one on 94.4%. The one-step decision therefore comes from the full-noise term of the objective, as Eq. 7 states, and training across the other noise levels is what gives the same model a distribution to draw candidates from ( Fig. 11 ).

Scope. The derivation assumes an optimal model and marks that add up. In practice the marks blur and the image decoder is nonlinear, so two-step outputs are more often illegal (8.6%) than the mispairing of Sec. D.1 explains. With guidance the model no longer predicts a conditional mean, and fewer steps do not help. The implicit policy is somewhat overconfident (expected 71.4% against a measured 67.0%), and we measured calibration on one model only. The gain of the one-step output over a single sample is larger where the samples disagree, on every model, but not significantly.

Symmetries. Mirroring the board left to right, or swapping the colours together with flipping the board top to bottom, maps a puzzle onto an equivalent one whose answer is the transformed original answer. We apply each transformation to 2,000 test puzzles and compare accuracy before and after. Colour swaps leave accuracy unchanged for every model (all differences within 1.2 points). Mirroring lowers it by 0.5 to 4.0 points, so this symmetry is learned only approximately.

One-piece changes. The strongest test changes the position so that the best move may change. For each test puzzle we remove one piece other than a king, or move it to an empty neighbouring square, without touching the squares of the original answer. Stockfish 19 at depth 18 then decides whether the change is decisive , meaning another move is now clearly best, or a control , meaning the original move is still clearly best. A model that recalls a pattern should keep its move in both cases, while a model that evaluates the new position should keep it on control boards and abandon it on decisive ones. Our image model does the latter ( Tab. 1 ). It keeps its move on 82.3% of control boards but only on 35.3% of decisive ones, and on the decisive boards it finds the new best move 39.5% of the time, against 5.1% for a random legal move. Retrieval barely reacts: the difference between control and decisive boards is 4.1 points for copying and 8.4 for the majority of 16 nearest positions, against 47.0 for our image model and 58.8 with few-step sampling. The video model at one million puzzles behaves in the same way (41.2 points, Tab. 9 ).

### Appendix H Attention and occlusion

Figure 5 in the main paper shows which pieces a chess decision depends on. We test this over many positions with the models at one million puzzles, on puzzles of two kinds: an enemy piece can be won, or the enemy king can be attacked. For the video model, attention from the decision frame to the piece that can be won is 1.13 times chance, against 0.86 for the other enemy pieces, and it stays higher after controlling for visual saliency (which squares hold pieces and which squares the move itself uses). In mate puzzles, the enemy king’s zone receives 1.00 times chance against 0.89 in the other puzzles, whereas the pretrained model gives it 1.10 in both: fine-tuning makes attention depend on what the puzzle is about. Removing pieces confirms this causally: removing the piece that can be won lowers the strength of the chosen move by 0.61, removing another enemy piece by 0.02. For the image model, removing pieces shows the same dependence (0.67 against 0.05), but its attention does not. The piece that can be won receives more attention than the other pieces (1.15 against 1.05 times chance), but the pretrained model shows the same contrast, and attention to the king’s zone does not differ between the two kinds of puzzle. The image model’s decisions thus depend on the right pieces, but its attention maps do not show it.

Pathfinding allows a cleaner test because relevance is easy to define. Attention from the decision to the cells of the chosen route is above chance and above matched off-route cells for both video models (ratios 1.10 to 1.14), and the effect comes from fine-tuning. For the image models, attention to the route is at chance. On terrain maps, all three models attend more to the costly cells their route avoids than to matched floor cells, which shows that they look at the obstacles that shape the route. The counterfactual tests of Appendix E show the causal side: a wall on the chosen cell, or removing the terrain the route avoids, changes the decision, while the same change elsewhere does not.


### Appendix I Probing for lookahead

The implicit policy shows that the image model distinguishes good moves from bad ones. Because move quality often depends on what follows, we ask whether the model represents future moves internally even though training asks it to draw only its own move.

Features. We run the unguided image model at one million puzzles exactly as at evaluation time and record the output of transformer blocks 8, 16, 24 and 32 (of 32) at sampling steps 0, 4 and 10 ( $\sigma$ = 1.00, 0.86 and 0.60). Each board square covers $2{\times}2$ image tokens, and we average them into one 4,096-dimensional vector per square.

Targets. The puzzle’s solution gives three sets of squares: those of our first move, which the model draws, of the opponent’s reply and of our follow-up. For the reply we leave out the squares of the first move, and for the follow-up those of the first two moves. Otherwise a probe could succeed simply by finding the painted mark, for example when the reply recaptures on the destination square of our move.

Probe and selection. The probe is a single logistic-regression layer, shared by all squares, that maps a square’s standardised vector to the probability that the square belongs to the target move. For each position, it ranks the candidate squares, and we score this ranking by its average precision. Probes are trained on 20,000 puzzles from training rows the model never saw. The block, the noise level and the regularisation are chosen on 1,834 validation puzzles, and only the chosen probe is evaluated, once, on 3,679 test puzzles.

Baselines. A probe could also succeed because the reply is easy to guess. To rule this out, we train the same probe on four other inputs. The first describes the board: for each square, the piece on every square of a $15{\times}15$ window around it, and the side to move. The second adds the true first move to this description, so that the probe knows what the model would play. The third uses the pretrained model’s features, and the fourth pairs our model’s features with the labels of a different position.

Why the continuation helps: a delayed-cue fork. We isolate the role of future supervision with maps containing two equal-length, mirror-image corridors. A single wall $k\in\{1,2,3,5,8,12\}$ cells down one corridor determines the first move. Every map has a twin with the wall in the other corridor, so ignoring it yields chance accuracy (50%). Four video models train on the same 40,000 maps, steps, and seed. Their targets differ only after the correct first move: no continuation, the next eight route moves, the next three route moves, or seven moves that wander off-route. We score first moves on 320 held-out maps per $k$ ( Fig. 16 ). With this budget, the first-move-only model responds to walls one and two cells away (99.4 and 92.8%), falls to 57.8% at three cells and is at chance from five cells onward. With the route target, accuracy is 99 to 100% through eight cells and 91.6% at twelve, beyond the drawn continuation. Three route moves help through three cells, while the equally long wandering continuation helps at three but not from five. The gain therefore comes from learning to follow the route toward the decisive wall, rather than clip length alone.

More labels or more training? Two controls separate the continuation from what comes with it. First, the continuation supervises eight decisions per map instead of one. A first-move-only model trained on every position along the same eight-move routes as separate examples, with the same 2,500 steps, stays at chance for walls three to eight cells away (53.4%, against 54.2% for the first move only and 99.7% with the continuation). Only one in eight of its examples shows the fork decision, so it is also weaker at near walls (43.1% at two cells). With eight times the steps, when it has seen each fork as often as the first-move model, it reaches 94.2% but remains below the continuation model at distant walls (85.3 against 99.1% at eight cells and 70.0 against 91.6% at twelve). Second, the first-move target can learn the rule, but it needs more training. With twice the steps on the same maps it reaches 84.7% at three to eight cells and 70.9% at twelve, still below the continuation model at every distance, and with eight times the steps it solves every test fork ( Fig. 17 ). The reach of the continuation is also bounded by what is drawn: with the wall 10, 14 and 16 cells away, beyond the eight drawn moves, its accuracy falls to 96, 87 and 71%. This is the pattern expected from intermediate supervision. A target that draws each step toward the cue lets every frame be supervised by evidence next to it, so a long-range dependency is learned as a chain of local ones, which makes composite rules learnable from far fewer examples [ 24 , 6 ] . The continuation thus lowers the amount of training a distant cue requires rather than making learnable what otherwise is not.

Training on the continuation. In the with continuation condition, the training target draws every missing queen, and in first move only it draws just the next queen. Evaluation reads only the first move in both conditions. With the same training boards, recipe and seed, continuation training raises first-move accuracy to 90.4, 80.2 and 58.7% at dead-end depths 0, 1 and 2 ( Fig. 18 , bottom right). The depth-2 gain is 10.2 points ( $p<0.001$ ). It also improves the first move on held-out solution families (92.6 versus 85.2%) and hard boards (82.0 versus 62.0%). Using the drawn solution. We evaluate the model trained with continuation in two ways: draw a complete board once, or execute only its first queen and generate again from the resulting board. On 300 held-out half-filled boards, the closed loop solves 55.7% with two sampling steps per call, against 34.7% for the best one-pass result (20 steps). Across 512 empty-board trials, it solves 38.1% against 17.0% for the best one-pass result (50 steps, Fig. 18 , top right). On the example at left, the first four loop drawings are invalid as complete solutions, yet each supplies a completable next queen. Subsequent drawings become valid and the loop solves the board. The comparison changes how the drawing is used as well as the sampling schedule. It shows that the model’s continuation is more useful when its first decision can be applied and revised than when the entire initial drawing is taken as the answer.

Changing the board. In a separate intervention, the environment adds a queen after the first move that contradicts the model’s initially drawn solution. At two steps per call, a plan-breaking change is possible on 145 of the 300 half-filled boards. The loop solves 55.2% of these changed episodes, against 48.3% on the same boards without a change. A control adds the queen where the model had planned it. The plan-breaking condition outperforms this control on half-filled boards at two steps (55.2 versus 42.1%, $p=0.011$ ), but underperforms it from empty boards (43.2 versus 51.9%, $p=0.0007$ ). At 20 steps, the control comparisons are 46.3 versus 43.4% on half-filled boards and 43.5 versus 40.8% from empty boards, and neither difference is significant. Against no change, the plan-breaking condition is no worse numerically in the same four settings (55.2 versus 48.3, 43.2 versus 38.5, 46.3 versus 44.1, and 43.5 versus 40.8%). These results show that the loop can continue from an unexpected state without an observed average penalty, but not that breaking its plan helps.

The continuation is not read at inference. If the model consulted its generated continuation to choose the first move, changing that continuation should change the decision. During video generation, we fix the opponent-reply frames to show either the engine’s refutation of the model’s first move or a weak reply, while keeping that first move visible. Under either condition, the first move changes on only 0.8% of 1,000 validation puzzles (exact McNemar $p=1$ ). Together with the fork experiment, this points to a training benefit: predicting future states shapes the learned decision, but the drawn continuation is not consulted to make it.

A parallel in language models. Language models show a related effect under the name of multi-token prediction. Training a model to predict several future tokens at each position, instead of only the next one, improves sample efficiency, with the extra predictions serving as an auxiliary training task [ 6 ] . The authors suggest that the gain comes from choice points, the positions whose outcome shapes what follows, which receive a stronger training signal when the future is predicted as well. A next best move is such a point. Our results show the same principle in a visual generator, where the predicted future consists of drawn states and opponent replies instead of tokens, and they allow two further tests: the drawn future can be changed at inference without changing the decision, and the same decisions given as separate examples do not replace it at the same compute.

Executing the drawn moves. In deterministic tasks, a predicted clip can be executed in full. On terrain maps, whole-clip execution finds a cheapest route on 82.8% of test maps, compared with 83.0% when predicting again after every move, and the probe-set rates are 76.8 and 78.8%. On uniform maps, clips solve 94.6% of test maps, versus 96.0% for a receding horizon on the first 500. Each clip executes up to eight moves per call, rather than one, yielding 3.6 versus 19.4 seconds per executed move ( Tab. 21 ). Against an opponent, predicted replies may differ from actual ones. On the 1,411 chess puzzles where the video model correctly drew its first move and the opponent’s reply and went on to draw a next move, that move is correct 41.2% of the time, while a fresh prediction on the observed board after those same moves reaches 52.5%. Table 15 gives 39.5% for the drawn move because it also counts the 62 clips that stop after the reply. Against the exact Connect Four solver, receding-horizon play converts 28.7% of won starts, compared with 22.7% when drawn moves are played regardless of the opponent’s replies ( $p=0.004$ , 150 games).

Second, the advantage of imagining the full line over drawing a single decision grows from 2.3 to 3.2 and 8.0 test points at 80k, 400k and one million videos ( Appendix K ). Go shows the same trend: the model trained on one million positions matches KataGo’s move on 20.9% of test positions, against 11.3% when trained on 100,000 ( $p<10^{-20}$ ), and 27.9% with majority voting.


### Appendix M Contrastive guidance

Method. A conditional diffusion model can be steered away from outputs it has learned to associate with a different prompt. For the one-move image model, the LoRA is additionally trained on two auxiliary prompts: one asking for any legal move, and one asking for a plausible but losing move $c^{-}$ , labelled offline by the same engine that labels the reference moves ( Sec. A.1 ). At inference, the velocity is steered away from the losing move,

[Tableau] $$\tilde{v}=v_{\theta}(\cdot,c)+w\,\big(v_{\theta}(\cdot,c)-v_{\theta}(\cdot,c^{-})\big),$$ (11)

i.e. classifier-free guidance [ 9 ] with the unconditional branch replaced by a learned negative prompt. We use $w=3$ . In Go, $c^{-}$ asks for a move that loses at least 20 points of win rate. No engine is queried at inference, and the cost is one extra network evaluation per denoising step ( Tab. 21 ). The horizon model always uses its plain sampler, since guidance needs a second pass over a whole video.

Results. Contrastive guidance improves the mixture-trained model, although the main results use unguided models. At one million chess puzzles, guidance raises single-sample accuracy from 31.8 to 40.9% ( Tab. 9 ). Much of this gain offsets the prompt mixture: training on the best-move prompt alone reaches 39.9% without guidance, and training on three times as much data reaches 56.0% ( Tab. 1 ). Voting narrows the guidance gain from 9.1 to 3.6 points with eight samples (40.0 versus 43.6%). Go shows a similar pattern at 100,000 positions: guidance raises single-sample accuracy from 9.3 to 14.0%, versus 11.3% for best-move-only training. With eight votes, the variants reach 14.1, 17.4, and 17.8%. We therefore treat guidance as optional rather than using it in the main tables. Its advantage over unguided sampling grows with training data ( Appendix L ).

The two language models achieve similar accuracy and show similar failure patterns whether the input is an image or text. Compared with answering in text, drawing the move on the board yields higher accuracy with matched data, especially where the correct action depends on the current position rather than a familiar pattern.

The output decides. A generic transformer on frozen DINOv2-L features reaches 50.0% after eight passes with a 4,096-way move output, or 48.1% when the board is misaligned with its patches. Changing only the output to score moves at their origin and destination squares raises accuracy to 78.5%, nearly identical to a head given the full board structure (78.6%). The spatial output thus accounts for much of the classifier’s strength. Drawing provides a spatial output across tasks without designing a separate head for each. After the same one pass and batch 8 used for our image model, the generic classifier reaches 21.8%, versus 45.5% for our model.

Learning from each example. With training to convergence, classifiers solve the delayed-cue fork (100%) and produce optimal routes more often than our whole-route model. After one pass over the fork maps, matching our models’ exposure, they respond to the wall only when it is one or two cells away (67.2%, or 58.7% with route supervision). Our route-drawing model reaches 98.4%.

Transfer to a new layout. We also test forks whose corridors meet at a right angle, a layout absent from training. The converged classifiers reach 58.0 and 49.5% and often move into a wall by choosing the direction favoured in the training layout. Our model retains 94.0%. This suggests that the classifiers learned a layout-specific shortcut, whereas the route-drawing model transfers to the new geometry.

### Appendix P Legality of the drawn actions

Because no model receives the rules, its raw drawing may be illegal, and all reported results count such actions as failures. Measured directly on the test sets, almost all raw drawings are legal. In chess, the image model trained on three million puzzles draws a legal move on 97.0% of positions (88.3 to 88.7% at one million puzzles), and the video model trained on one million on 93.4%. After legal drawn moves, 97.3% of imagined video boards are correct on all 64 squares, with 99.95% of individual squares correct. In Go, the marked point is legal 99.8% of the time, versus 43% for the base model. Connect Four discs occupy a non-full column and the correct landing row in 100.0% of image outputs and 99.6% of video outputs, although gravity is never stated. For the image and video models respectively, routes are valid on 94.7 and 97.4% of shortest-path maps and on 98.5 and 95.0% of terrain maps, and 99.2 and 98.2% of billiards outputs are readable shots.

Inference cost depends chiefly on the number of denoising steps and on whether guidance adds a second forward pass ( Tab. 21 ). One-step sampling ( Appendix C ) is the cheapest setting and, without guidance, also the more accurate one. Voting multiplies the cost by the number of samples. The execution protocol matters on move-by-move tasks: a video clip executes up to eight moves per call and solves a terrain map in about the time of the image model’s closed loop, while the receding horizon generates a new clip for every executed move and is about five times slower.
