## CodeQuant2: Enhanced Outlier Mitigation and Adaptive Clustering for Low-Precision LRMs

Large reasoning models (LRMs) improve task performance by generating longer intermediate reasoning traces, but this substantially increases inference costs and makes low-precision quantization increasingly important. However, aggressively quantized models often perform poorly on reasoning tasks. We observe larger activation fluctuations during the first decoding steps of reasoning. Calibration that omits these early states may miss their activation fluctuations. To address these reasoning-specific quantization challenges, we propose CodeQuant2 , a clustering-based LUT quantization framework for low-precision large reasoning models. CodeQuant2 mitigates activation outliers with rotation and scaling, uses clustering to represent non-uniform weight distributions, and adapts codebook sizes to the quantization difficulty of each layer. Guided by our observation of reasoning-time activation behavior, CodeQuant2 further improves calibration sample selection to better match actual decoding statistics. Combined with our customized LUT CUDA kernel, CodeQuant2 delivers substantial accuracy improvements on reasoning benchmarks while achieving up to $2.85\times$ speedup over BF16.


### 1 Introduction

Large reasoning models (LRMs) Guo et al. (2025) ; Yang et al. (2025) ; Agarwal et al. (2025) have become important for structured, multi-step tasks such as mathematical reasoning, code generation, and tool-augmented decision making. By spending more computation on reasoning, these models achieve stronger performance but incur higher inference costs from longer generation traces, larger memory footprints, and increased compute demand. Low-precision quantization is therefore a key strategy for efficient deployment. Recent hardware support further strengthens this trend: NVIDIA Hopper ( NVIDIA Corporation, 2022b ) and Ada GPUs ( NVIDIA Corporation, 2022a ) support FP8 arithmetic, while Blackwell extends it to FP4 Alvarez et al. (2025) , making low-precision LRM inference increasingly practical.

However, activation outliers remain a major obstacle to low-bit quantization in LRMs, as in conventional foundation models Dettmers et al. (2022b) ; Sun et al. (2024b) . These extreme values enlarge the quantization range, leaving limited effective precision for most activations and causing large approximation errors. In reasoning generation, such errors are even more harmful because they can accumulate across autoregressive decoding steps Liu et al. (2025) ; Li et al. (2025) ; Liang et al. (2025) . Since reasoning tasks often involve long multi-step traces, errors introduced early can be amplified later and degrade final reasoning quality Zhao et al. (2025) ; Liu et al. (2025) . Therefore, despite progress in outlier-smoothing post-training quantization methods Tseng et al. (2024) ; Chee et al. (2024) ; Ashkboos et al. (2024a) ; Xiao et al. (2023) ; Xiang and Zhang (2024) ; Liu et al. (2024b) ; Liang et al. (2025) ; Yin et al. (2026) , aggressive low-bit quantization still struggles to maintain stable performance on complex reasoning tasks.

Existing methods address these challenges from complementary directions. Outlier-smoothing techniques, including scaling and rotation, mitigate activation quantization errors Xiao et al. (2023) ; Ashkboos et al. (2024a) ; Liu et al. (2024b) ; Liang et al. (2025) . Meanwhile, clustering-based quantization represents weights using learned centroids, better capturing non-uniform weight distributions Kim et al. (2023) ; Yin et al. (2026) . Moreover, clustering-based representations naturally support lookup-table (LUT) inference, offering opportunities for efficient low-bit deployment. Building on these complementary approaches, we adopt clustering-based LUT quantization as the foundation of our framework and identify two key considerations for accurate and efficient inference.

Although existing outlier-mitigation techniques improve activation quantization, these gains do not always translate into comparable improvements on reasoning tasks. To understand this gap, we examine activation behavior during reasoning and observe higher activation fluctuations and channel-wise outliers in early decoding steps, which gradually weaken as generation progresses. Existing outlier-mitigation techniques do not naturally account for these reasoning-specific decoding-stage-dependent activation patterns. Thus, we argue that outlier mitigation should explicitly emphasize high-variance early-decoding activations to better preserve reasoning quality. This motivates us to design a calibration strategy that better captures these early-stage activation patterns.

Beyond activation calibration, clustering-based quantization offers another degree of freedom: the number of clusters assigned to each weight matrix. Allocating more clusters to sensitive matrices can improve quantization quality, but also increases LUT inference latency. Thus, effective cluster allocation should balance quantization performance and hardware-aware inference latency, rather than optimizing accuracy alone. We therefore design a performance–latency-balanced cluster allocation strategy based on matrix sensitivity and profiled execution costs.

Building on these two design principles, we present CodeQuant2 , a clustering-based low-bit quantization framework for LRMs. Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

- • We propose CodeQuant2 , a clustering-based quantization framework that combines reasoning-aware calibration, fine-tuned rotation and scaling transformations, and differentiable assignment tuning, enabling learned cluster assignments to handle outlier-heavy distributions.

- • We further introduce Adaptive Cluster Allocation (ACA), which adaptively assigns cluster numbers by balancing accuracy and hardware efficiency for each target matrix.

- • We design a GPU kernel that directly executes on the clustered representation by recasting decode-time matrix multiplication as table-lookup computation. In the evaluated GPU settings, CodeQuant2 achieves up to $2.85\times$ speedup while improving reasoning accuracy.


### 2 Related Work


#### 2.1 Large Reasoning Model

Chain-of-thought prompting and related methods show that explicitly unfolding intermediate reasoning steps can improve performance on complex tasks Wei et al. (2022) ; Wang et al. (2022) ; Zelikman et al. (2024) . Recent large reasoning models internalize this capability through longer reasoning traces or dedicated thinking modes, enabled by additional test-time compute Shao et al. (2024) ; Guo et al. (2025) ; Yang et al. (2025) ; Agarwal et al. (2025) . Their training increasingly relies on reinforcement-learning-based post-training, from earlier PPO-style methods Luong et al. (2024) ; Xie et al. (2025) to recent GRPO-based approaches and variants Shao et al. (2024) ; Guo et al. (2025) ; Yu et al. (2025) ; Gao et al. (2025) . Thus, explicit reasoning traces, extra test-time computation, and RL-driven post-training have become defining features of current LRMs. Despite their strong reasoning ability, LRMs are more fragile under aggressive low-bit quantization than standard language models. Prior work shows that quantization can cause much larger degradation on reasoning benchmarks, even when general-task performance remains relatively stable Liu et al. (2025) ; Li et al. (2025) ; Zhao et al. (2025) . This is largely because quantization errors during autoregressive generation can accumulate over long reasoning traces, making low-bit LRM quantization more challenging than quantizing conventional foundation models Liu et al. (2025) ; Liang et al. (2025) ; Zhao et al. (2025) .


#### 2.2 Outlier Aware Quantization

Activation outliers are a major challenge for low-bit LLM quantization. Prior work has shown that the input activations to self-attention weight matrices often exhibit pronounced channel-wise outliers Dettmers et al. (2022a) ; Sun et al. (2024a) ; Xiang and Zhang (2024) , where a small number of channels have much larger magnitudes than the rest. These outliers dominate the quantization range and introduce large quantization errors when low-bit quantization is applied. One solution is to isolate or retain outliers in higher precision, as in mixed-precision methods such as LLM.int8() Dettmers et al. (2022a) and SPQR Dettmers et al. (2023b) . While effective, these methods often introduce irregular computation and memory access, reducing hardware efficiency.

To preserve uniform low-precision execution, many methods instead apply computational invariant transformations before quantization. Scaling-based methods, such as SmoothQuant Xiao et al. (2023) , use channel-wise scaling to redistribute quantization difficulty between activations and weights, while rotation-based methods, such as QuaRot Ashkboos et al. (2024a) , SpinQuant Liu et al. (2024b) , and ParoQuant Liang et al. (2025) , spread outliers through orthogonal transformations. Let $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ be the hidden states and $\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ an invertible transformation along the hidden dimension. For a linear projection $\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ , inserting $\mathbf{T}\mathbf{T}^{-1}$ preserves the output given that $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^{\top}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{\top}=(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{T})(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}^{-\top})^{\top}$ .

The transformation $\mathbf{T}$ can be instantiated as an orthogonal rotation or a diagonal scaling matrix. These transformations reduce outlier concentration and improve robustness under low-precision quantization. More recently, CodeQuant Yin et al. (2026) combines rotation with clustering-based non-uniform quantization. However, aggressive low-bit quantization remains challenging, particularly for reasoning tasks.


#### 2.3 Non-uniform Quantization

Non-uniform quantization provides a more flexible alternative to fixed uniform bins for the highly irregular distributions encountered in aggressive low-bit LLM compression. Existing work in this direction can be roughly divided into two categories. The first adjusts scalar quantization levels to better match the underlying distribution, as exemplified by NF4 in QLoRA Dettmers et al. (2023a) . The second adopts stronger clustering-based representations to improve expressiveness in the low-bit regime, as explored in SqueezeLLM and CodeQuant Kim et al. (2023) ; Yin et al. (2026) . Related work has also investigated vector quantization as an even stronger low-bit representation Liu et al. (2024a) . Overall, non-uniform quantization has shown clear advantages over uniform quantization, but existing methods still focus on static reconstruction quality and general compression settings, leaving low-bit quantization for LRMs underexplored.


#### 2.4 GEMM with Lookup Table-based Implementation

GEMM with clustered multiplicands requires efficient Lookup Table (LUT) support for practical deployment. Without hardware-friendly LUTs, centroids must be stored and loaded as floating-point values during computation, introducing substantial memory and execution overhead. Existing work has approached this problem from both CPU and GPU settings. On CPUs, DeepGEMM ( Ganji et al., 2023 ) , LUTIN ( Lin et al., 2024 ) , and T-MAC ( Wei et al., 2025 ) explore LUT-oriented execution paths for low-precision inference, with an emphasis on kernel design, memory efficiency, and reformulating mixed-precision GEMM as table lookup. On GPUs, LUT-GEMM ( Park et al., 2022 ) , FLUTE ( Guo et al., 2024 ) , and LUT Tensor Core ( Mo et al., 2025 ) develop increasingly specialized kernels and software-hardware co-design techniques to reduce lookup overhead and better integrate LUT computation into high-throughput matrix multiplication pipelines.


### 3 Methodology

In this section, we present the methodology of CodeQuant2 , as shown in Figure 1 . We first motivate the design by showing that decoding on reasoning tasks induces activation behaviors not well captured by conventional calibration data. We then introduce CodeQuant2’s main components, including fine-tuned rotation and scaling for outlier mitigation, adaptive cluster allocation, and differentiable clustering quantization. Finally, we describe the LUT-based GPU kernel for efficient inference.


#### 3.1 Outlier Behavior in Large Reasoning Models

To better characterize outlier behavior in LRMs, we analyze the distributions of self-attention activations on reasoning benchmarks, including GSM8K Cobbe et al. (2021b) and AIME25 Zhang and Math-AI (2025) . As shown in Figure 2 , models generating reasoning traces exhibit substantially larger activation fluctuations during the early decoding stage. In particular, the average activation standard deviation over the first 30 decoding steps is about $20\%$ higher than that of non-thinking decoding, while non-thinking statistics remain relatively stable across steps. The heatmaps on the right of Figure 2 show the channel-wise activation standard deviation across layers at decoding steps 1 and 400 for LRMs. At step 1, strong channel-wise outliers appear from shallow layers and persist toward deeper layers. By contrast, at later decoding steps, the outlier pattern becomes noticeably weaker.

> Figure 2: Activation behavior during reasoning decoding on GSM8K Cobbe et al. (2021b) , measured on Qwen3-4B-Thinking Team (2025) and averaged over 128 samples. Left: Averaged activation standard deviation across decoding steps with and without thinking. Right: Activation heatmaps at representative decoding steps.

Because quantization errors introduced early in reasoning can propagate through later decoding steps and affect subsequent token generation, reducing error at the beginning of the reasoning process is especially important. This observation motivates our reasoning-aware calibration strategy. During local fine-tuning, we combine activations from early tokens decoded by the full-precision model with general-text samples. Section 4 gives the sampling details. Additional results across reasoning models and datasets appear in Appendix A .


#### 3.2 Fine-tuned Rotation and Scaling for Outlier Smoothing

Motivated by the outlier patterns in Figure 2 , we aim to smooth input activations further before low-precision quantization. To this end, we jointly apply rotation and scaling for outlier mitigation. Specifically, we introduce a shared orthogonal rotation matrix $\mathbf{R}$ and layer-wise diagonal scaling matrices $\mathbf{S}_{\ell,t}$ , where $\ell$ denotes the layer index and $t\in\{{\mathrm{QKV},\mathrm{FFN}}\}$ denotes the projection group. We apply rotation, scaling, and quantization only to the query, key, value, gate, and up projections. Given an input activation $\mathbf{X}$ and its corresponding weight matrix $\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ , the transformed activation and weight are defined as $\mathbf{X}_{RS}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}_{\ell,t}$ and $\mathbf{W}_{RS}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}_{\ell,t}^{-1}$ , respectively. This transformation preserves the original linear operation since $\mathbf{X}_{RS}\mathbf{W}_{RS}^{\top}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^{\top}$ . The transformed weights can be computed offline. We optimize $\mathbf{R}$ and $\mathbf{S}_{\ell,t}$ through local fine-tuning by minimizing the activation quantization error over the calibration set:

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{R}^{\star},\{\mathbf{S}_{\ell,t}^{\star}\}=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\mathbf{R},\{\mathbf{S}_{\ell,t}\}}\left\|\mathbf{X}_{RS}-\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{RS}\right\|^{2}$$ (1)

where $\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{RS}$ denotes the quantized activation. We parameterize $\mathbf{R}$ with the Cayley transform ( Nishimori and Akaho, 2005 ; Li et al., 2020 ; Liu et al., 2024b ) to preserve orthogonality during optimization.


#### 3.3 Adaptive Cluster Allocation

After the input activations are transformed as described in Section 3.2 , activation quantization error is reduced, but preserving the full-precision layer output still requires careful weight clustering. In CodeQuant2, each weight value is mapped to one of the $K$ centroids in a learned codebook, with details described in Section 3.4 . Therefore, the cluster number $K$ directly determines the codebook size and directly affects both quantization accuracy and system latency. Since $K=2^{b}$ corresponds naturally to a $b$ -bit index, existing clustering-based methods typically choose $K$ based on the target bitwidth and use the same cluster number for all target linear layers Kim et al. (2023) ; Yin et al. (2026) . However, such uniform assignment is often suboptimal. Empirically, matrices exhibit different sensitivities to clustering. Some can be represented with fewer clusters while introducing limited output perturbation, whereas others require more clusters to preserve accuracy.

Motivated by the effectiveness of mixed precision, we propose to adaptively assign the cluster number $K$ to balance matrix importance and hardware performance. For simplicity, we adjust $K$ at the matrix level, treating it as a capacity parameter. We restrict $K$ to a predefined candidate set $\mathcal{K}$ . In our experiments, $\mathcal{K}=\{8,16,32\}$ . We initialize all target matrices with the original cluster number $K_{\mathrm{ori}}$ , and then estimate the effect of changing $K$ for each matrix before selecting which matrices to update.

The impact of cluster-number adjustment on accuracy is estimated through the local reconstruction error. Given the original cluster number $K_{\mathrm{ori}}$ and the corresponding clustered weight $\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{RS}^{(K_{\mathrm{ori}})}$ , the clustered output $\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}^{(K_{\mathrm{ori}})}$ under this cluster assignment is written as $\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}^{(K_{\mathrm{ori}})}=\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{RS}(\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{RS}^{(K_{\mathrm{ori}})})^{\top}$ . When changing the cluster number from $K_{\mathrm{ori}}$ to $K_{\mathrm{tar}}\in\mathcal{K}$ , the corresponding local output perturbation is $\Delta\mathbf{Y}_{K_{\mathrm{ori}}\rightarrow K_{\mathrm{tar}}}=\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{RS}((\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{RS}^{(K_{\mathrm{ori}})})^{\top}-(\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{RS}^{(K_{\mathrm{tar}})})^{\top})$ . The full-precision output of this layer on the calibration inputs is $\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{fp}}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^{\top}=\mathbf{X}_{RS}\mathbf{W}_{RS}^{\top}$ . To estimate the accuracy impact of this change, we use a first-order approximation of the local reconstruction loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}}=||\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{fp}}-\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}^{(K_{\mathrm{ori}})}||^{2}$ . This gives the following importance score:

[Tableau] $$I_{K_{\mathrm{ori}}\rightarrow K_{\mathrm{tar}}}=\frac{1}{N}\left\|\Delta\mathbf{Y}_{K_{\mathrm{ori}}\rightarrow K_{\mathrm{tar}}}\odot\nabla_{\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}}\right\|_{F}^{2}.$$ (2)

where $\nabla_{\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}}$ denotes the gradient of the local reconstruction loss with respect to the clustered output. The derivation is provided in Appendix B . For efficiency, CodeQuant2 computes this score using the local reconstruction loss, although the same formulation can also be extended to the final training loss of LRMs. This choice avoids full model-level backpropagation and only requires gradient computation for the local GEMM operation, substantially reducing the computational overhead.

Considering only the importance score leads to a trivial solution, since it would assign the maximum number of clusters to every group. On the other hand, increasing $K$ also introduces higher LUT-based processing latency. To balance quantization quality and system performance, we profile the LUT execution latency of each target matrix under different cluster numbers on the target GPU platform. Let $T_{m}(K)$ denote the profiled lookup latency of weight matrix $m$ when using $K$ clusters. All matrices are initialized with $K_{\mathrm{ori}}$ , with latency $T_{m}(K_{\mathrm{ori}})$ . Starting from 16 clusters (4-bit indices), we pair a change to 32 clusters (5-bit indices) for one matrix with a change to 8 clusters (3-bit indices) for another. Each pair has an average index width of 4 bits across the two matrices. We also account for their profiled latency. We increase $K$ for the matrix with the largest estimated benefit and decrease $K$ for the matrix with the smallest estimated loss, subject to the latency budget. Figure 3 (a) summarizes this allocation process.


#### 3.4 Learnable Clustering for Enhanced Performance

> Figure 3: Adaptive cluster allocation and clustering. Left: Importance scores and latency guide cluster allocation. Right: K-means initializes the centroid matrix and per-row binary assignment matrices.

To better align the quantized model with the full-precision model, CodeQuant2 fine-tunes the clustered weights by optimizing a centroid matrix and learning soft assignment matrices while keeping the initial K-means assignments fixed. Let $\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ be a weight matrix. Its $i$ -th row is $\mathbf{W}_{i,:}$ , where $0\leq i<d_{\mathrm{out}}$ . Figure 3 (b) shows an example weight matrix $\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 4}$ clustered with $K=3$ . We initialize the clustering by applying K-means to each row. For row $i$ , the clustered weight is

[Tableau] $$\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{i,:}^{\top}=\mathbf{A}_{i}\mathbf{c}_{i}.$$ (3)

The centroid matrix $\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times K}$ stores one codebook per output row. We write its $i$ -th row as $\mathbf{c}_{i}^{\top}$ , where $\mathbf{c}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}$ . The binary matrix $\mathbf{A}_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}\times K}$ records the initial K-means assignments for row $i$ of $\mathbf{W}$ . Its $j$ -th row selects the centroid for $W_{ij}$ . We use $\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{fp}}$ , defined before Eq. 2 , as the reference output for the local reconstruction loss:

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}}=\left\|\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{fp}}-\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{RS}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{RS}^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}.$$ (4)

During fine-tuning, we keep the initial K-means assignments fixed and learn soft assignments through low-rank updates. We stack the one-hot assignment matrices $\mathbf{A}_{i}$ into $\mathbf{A}\in\{0,1\}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}\times K}$ . To reduce the number of trainable parameters, we apply LoRA to the first two dimensions:

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{A}^{\prime}=\operatorname{softmax}\left(\frac{\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}^{d}\mathbf{B}^{u}}{\tau}\right),$$ (5)

where $\mathbf{B}^{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r\times K}$ and $\mathbf{B}^{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\mathrm{in}}\times K}$ are the low-rank factors, with $r\ll\min(d_{\mathrm{out}},d_{\mathrm{in}})$ . Their multiplication is performed independently for each centroid, while the softmax operates along the centroid dimension. The resulting assignments reconstruct each weight row as $\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{i,:}^{\top}=\mathbf{A}^{\prime}_{i}\mathbf{c}_{i}$ .

The temperature $\tau$ controls the sharpness of the soft assignments and is annealed during fine-tuning:

[Tableau] $$\tau(t)=\tau_{\max}-\frac{t}{T}(\tau_{\max}-\tau_{\min}),\quad t=0,\ldots,T,$$ (6)

where $t$ denotes the current fine-tuning step, $T$ is the total number of steps, and $\tau_{\max}$ and $\tau_{\min}$ are the initial and final temperatures, respectively. During the final stage of fine-tuning, we convert soft assignments to hard assignments, allowing the centroids to adapt to the discrete assignments.


#### 3.5 Lookup Table CUDA Kernel Design

> Figure 4: (a) The steps of the LUT kernel, including a one-time LUT precomputation and table lookup. (b) The precomputed LUT is stored in the shared memory in the Streaming Multiprocessors.

CodeQuant2 applies linear quantization to input activations and clustering-based quantization to weights. A simple deployment strategy is to reconstruct the clustered weight matrix from centroid indices and then invoke a standard CUDA GEMM kernel Kim et al. (2023) . However, this reconstruction-based approach introduces additional dequantization and memory traffic before matrix multiplication, reducing the practical benefit of the low-bit weight format. To avoid this overhead, we design a GPU kernel that directly operates on the clustered representation by reformulating decode-time matrix multiplication as a table-lookup computation.

An example kernel design is illustrated in Figure 4 (a), where we consider 4-bit activation quantization and weight clustering with 16 centroids. The same principle can be extended to other bit-widths for activation and codebook sizes for weight clustering. In step 1 in Figure 4 (a), with 4-bit activations, each activation element is mapped to one of 16 quantized levels, while each weight element is stored as a 4-bit assignment index selecting one centroid from the row-wise codebook. The key idea is to precompute the products between quantized activation levels and weight centroids once, so that each multiplication between a quantized activation and a clustered weight can be replaced by a table lookup indexed by the activation level and the weight assignment, as shown in Step 2 of Figure 4 (a).

We further improve the GPU kernel for decode-time execution with three optimizations. First, activation min/max computation, 4-bit quantization, and bit packing are fused, so the activation vector is read only once and directly converted into packed indices consumed by the kernel. This avoids writing intermediate quantized activations to global memory. Second, the LUT is stored in GPU shared memory within each streaming multiprocessor (SM) and reused during decoding, as illustrated in Figure 4 (b), reducing dequantization overhead and improving execution efficiency. Third, because decode-time GEMM exposes limited batch-level parallelism, we tune the matrix tile sizes to better match CUDA core resources and improve hardware utilization. Together, these optimizations enable clustered low-bit models to execute efficiently through table lookup, avoiding the higher latency of reconstruction-based matrix multiplication.

### 4 Experiment

We evaluate our method on LRMs of different scales, including Qwen3-4B-Thinking ( Yang et al., 2025 ) , Qwen3-8B with thinking mode enabled ( Yang et al., 2025 ) , and Phi-4-Reasoning ( Abdin et al., 2025 ) . Our evaluation covers language modeling, general question answering, mathematical reasoning, and code generation. For language modeling, we report perplexity on WikiText2 ( Merity et al., 2016 ) and C4 ( Raffel et al., 2023 ) . For general question answering, we report ARC ( Clark et al., 2018 ) , PIQA ( Bisk et al., 2020 ) , and WinoGrande ( Sakaguchi et al., 2021 ) results in the appendix. For mathematical reasoning, we evaluate on MATH500 ( Hendrycks et al., 2021 ) , GSM8K ( Cobbe et al., 2021a ) , and AIME ( Zhang and Math-AI, 2025 ; Zhang and Math-AI, 2026 ) under both pass@1 and pass@8. To assess code generation after quantization, we use HumanEval ( Chen et al., 2021 ) and LiveCodeBench ( Jain et al., 2024 ) .

All downstream evaluations are performed using EvalScope ( Team, 2024 ) . We use zero-shot evaluation for all benchmarks except GSM8K, where we adopt a 4-shot setting. For AIME, we report pass@1 and pass@8. During generation, we follow the official inference settings for each reasoning model. For Qwen3-4B-Thinking and Qwen3-8B, we use temperature $0.6$ , top- $p$ $0.95$ , top- $k$ $20$ , min- $p$ $0$ , and a maximum model length of $32768$ . Qwen3-8B is evaluated with thinking mode enabled.

We compare CodeQuant2 with representative post-training quantization and clustering baselines, including QuaRot ( Ashkboos et al., 2024b ) , ParoQuant ( Liang et al., 2025 ) , SqueezeLLM ( Kim et al., 2024 ) , and CodeQuant ( Yin et al., 2026 ) , with BF16 as the full-precision reference. All methods are evaluated under A4W4 and A8W4 settings, where activations use 4 or 8 bits and weights use 4-bit-equivalent capacity, using a 4-bit average assignment-index width across target matrices for CodeQuant2. For fairness, we use the same granularity across methods: per-channel weight quantization and per-token activation quantization. For weight-only baselines such as SqueezeLLM and ParoQuant, we extend them to weight-activation quantization by applying rotation before activation quantization to mitigate activation outliers.

For system evaluation, we measure autoregressive decoding latency on three types of single GPUs: NVIDIA A6000, NVIDIA L40s, and NVIDIA A100. We compare against BF16, QuaRot ( Ashkboos et al., 2024b ) , and SqueezeLLM ( Kim et al., 2024 ) under the A4W4 setting. SqueezeLLM provides a clustered quantization baseline for the latency comparison. Performance is assessed using average prompt and decoding lengths profiled from a subset of examples sampled from WikiText2, GSM8K, and AIME25, with the (prompt, decode) lengths set to (1024, 100), (100, 4000), and (150, 6000), respectively.

We further specify the method-specific settings used by CodeQuant2. For calibration, we use WikiText2 as the general-text source and generate reasoning-decoding samples from GSM8K training split. We evaluate GSM8K accuracy on the held-out test split. For each GSM8K sample, we first generate 256 tokens using the full-precision model, concatenate the prompt with the generated tokens, and truncate or pad the resulting calibration sequence to 512 tokens. We use a 9:1 mixture of WikiText2 samples and RAC-selected GSM8K samples, ensuring that the calibration set is strictly disjoint from all test sets. The mixed samples are then randomly shuffled to reduce ordering bias. For fine-tuning, we use SGD with momentum $0.9$ and zero weight decay, with separate learning rates of $1\times 10^{-4}$ for centroids and $1\times 10^{-5}$ for assignment. For adaptive cluster allocation, we use the candidate set $\mathcal{K}=\{8,16,32\}$ . All training stages can be conducted on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU. Rotation and scaling fine-tuning requires approximately 30 minutes, adaptive cluster allocation requires around 15 minutes, and the clustering fine-tuning takes about 30 minutes.

#### 4.1 Accuracy Evaluation

Table 1 reports the A4W4 results across Qwen3-4B-Thinking, Qwen3-8B, and Phi-4-Reasoning. On Qwen3-8B and Phi-4-Reasoning, the gains are more moderate but remain consistent, indicating that CodeQuant2 generalizes across different reasoning models. Importantly, the improvements on reasoning and code-generation benchmarks do not sacrifice general language modeling quality, as CodeQuant2 maintains competitive perplexity on WikiText2 and C4. We further test quantized reasoning models on challenging mathematical benchmarks, including AIME25 ( Zhang and Math-AI, 2025 ) and AIME26 ( Zhang and Math-AI, 2026 ) . As shown in Table 2 , CodeQuant2 consistently improves mathematical reasoning robustness under the challenging A4W4 setting. CodeQuant2 also improves pass@8, so at least one of eight sampled responses is more likely to be correct. Under A4W4, CodeQuant2 maintains strong code performance while improving reasoning-heavy tasks. The largest gain appears on LiveCodeBench for Qwen3-4B-Thinking, where CodeQuant2 outperforms CodeQuant by 15.0% points. These results show gains on coding as well as math benchmarks under A4W4.

Although CodeQuant2 targets reasoning-oriented quantization, it preserves strong general-task performance. On language understanding and commonsense reasoning benchmarks such as ARC, PIQA, and WinoGrande, CodeQuant2 performs comparably to or better than existing quantized baselines. ##### Comparison of Cluster Allocation Strategies

Table 4.1 reports decoding throughput under different cluster allocation strategies. We compare fixed cluster counts of 8, 16, and 32 with our adaptive cluster allocation (ACA) method from Section 3.3 , whose average assignment-index width across target matrices is 4 bits, as in the fixed 16-cluster setting. The fixed settings reveal a clear efficiency trade-off: throughput drops from 47 tokens/s with 8 clusters to 42 tokens/s with 16 clusters and 33 tokens/s with 32 clusters on the GSM8K dataset. ACA achieves 44 tokens/s, outperforming the fixed 16-cluster setting and approaching the efficiency of the fixed 8-cluster setting. ACA is also faster on AIME25. Its accuracy gain in Table 5 and its higher throughput are consistent with reallocating clusters toward sensitive matrices under a profiled-latency budget.


### 5 Conclusion and Limitations

In conclusion, CodeQuant2 combines early decoded tokens with general samples to build a reasoning-aware calibration set that captures the activation fluctuations at the start of reasoning. Using this calibration set, CodeQuant2 learns rotation and scaling transformations that smooth activation outliers. For the weights, it allocates cluster budgets across weight matrices to balance accuracy and latency, and then fine-tunes the centroid values and assignments to better match the full-precision outputs. Finally, a LUT-based CUDA kernel operates directly on the clustered representation during inference.

Although CodeQuant2 performs well on the evaluated reasoning tasks, further study of its generated reasoning trajectories could provide a more complete picture of the model’s reasoning behavior after quantization. Such an analysis could examine whether a correct answer is accompanied by repetition or unnecessarily long reasoning. It could also explore how quantization interacts with behaviors learned during post-training beyond those measured by our benchmarks, as well as potential safety implications such as unreliable or overconfident outputs. We have not evaluated these questions in this work and believe addressing them is a promising direction for future work.

### Appendix B Adaptive Cluster Importance Score

In adaptive cluster allocation, we estimate the importance of each candidate cluster number change by measuring how much its induced output perturbation affects the local reconstruction loss. Let the local reconstruction loss be

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}})=\left\|\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{fp}}-\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}\right\|^{2}.$$

When the cluster number is changed from $K_{\mathrm{ori}}$ to $K_{\mathrm{tar}}$ , the clustered output changes from $\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}^{(K_{\mathrm{ori}})}$ to $\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}^{(K_{\mathrm{tar}})}$ , producing the output perturbation

[Tableau] $$\Delta\mathbf{Y}_{K_{\mathrm{ori}}\rightarrow K_{\mathrm{tar}}}=\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}^{(K_{\mathrm{ori}})}-\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}^{(K_{\mathrm{tar}})}.$$

Using a first-order Taylor approximation around $\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}^{(K_{\mathrm{ori}})}$ , the change in reconstruction loss can be estimated as

[Tableau] $$\Delta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}}\approx-\left\langle\nabla_{\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}},\Delta\mathbf{Y}_{K_{\mathrm{ori}}\rightarrow K_{\mathrm{tar}}}\right\rangle.$$

where $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ denotes the inner product. To obtain a stable matrix-wise score, we use the squared element-wise contribution and normalize it by the number of calibration tokens $N$ :

[Tableau] $$I_{K_{\mathrm{ori}}\rightarrow K_{\mathrm{tar}}}=\frac{1}{N}\left\|\Delta\mathbf{Y}_{K_{\mathrm{ori}}\rightarrow K_{\mathrm{tar}}}\odot\nabla_{\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}}\right\|_{F}^{2}.$$

This score measures how strongly the output perturbation caused by changing the cluster number affects the local reconstruction loss.
