## Gamer: A Generalist Vision-Language-Action Foundation Model for Long-Horizon Real-Time Game Agent

We introduce Gamer, a generalist agent framework built for long-horizon tasks in complex interactive environments. Its end-to-end architecture combines perception, memory management, and multi-step reasoning. A shared training and deployment pipeline supports interaction with both conventional 2D graphical interfaces and complex 3D simulated worlds. We first continue pre-training on a dataset constructed from tens of thousands of hours of device-level keyboard and mouse interaction. These trajectories span simulated games and embodied environments, allowing the model to learn environmental dynamics across both physical and virtual domains. Sparse-thinking instruction tuning then substantially reduces redundant computation while preserving the reasoning needed for complex decisions. We train the policy for long-horizon interaction with a stable multi-turn reinforcement learning method that addresses finite context windows, accumulated rollout errors, and sparse rewards. For real-time interaction, a cloud–edge inference engine serves numerous simulated game environments concurrently, supports unbounded-context execution, and keeps online inference latency low. Across multiple challenging 3D open-world games, Gamer demonstrates solid generalization and operational efficiency comparable to skilled players. It also achieves competitive results on a range of conventional 2D game benchmarks. These results suggest that even repetitive keyboard and mouse trajectories can support substantial gains from scaling data and learning algorithms. Training on these trajectories helps Gamer learn complex skills more steadily in environments with both physical and digital properties. This approach offers a path toward generalist agents that can interact across digital and physical worlds.


### 1 Introduction

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) [ 45 , 2 , 13 , 7 , 6 , 25 ] are rapidly evolving from static input processors into interactive agents that can operate in digital and physical environments. In particular, vision-language-action (VLA) models [ 46 , 28 , 24 , 58 ] offer a promising path toward embodied artificial general intelligence. However, high trial-and-error costs and the lack of environmental controllability significantly hinder both the evaluation and algorithm scaling of real-world physical agents.

Modern large-scale games [ 11 , 4 , 60 , 47 , 61 , 59 , 32 ] provide an ideal testbed for developing generalist VLA agents and studying their scaling behaviors because they combine realistic complexity with rigorous experimental control. Compared with traditional 2D games, web interfaces, and simplified simulators, contemporary games require agents to parse complex visual scenes, navigate intricate 3D layouts, and advance multi-stage quests. Agents must continually make strategic decisions and manage resources under partial observability and delayed feedback, all through fine-grained, low-level keyboard and mouse actions. Tasks may last hours or longer, requiring reliable memory, planning, and decision-making throughout. Such environments therefore serve as an uncompromising benchmark for coordinating perception, reasoning, memory, and control.

Honkai: Star Rail (HSR) exemplifies this setting by condensing these demands into a continuously evolving game environment. Its gameplay combines narrative-driven exploration across diverse regions with turn-based combat, squad management, resource allocation, and multi-level UI operation. This environment features nested objectives and long-term progression that challenge an agent’s cross-task generalization, long-term memory, and closed-loop execution. Developing agents in this system compels MLLMs to transition from passive visual understanding to sustained, proactive interaction. Mastering such challenges paves a concrete pathway toward generalist agents capable of operating across both complex digital worlds and embodied physical environments. As illustrated in Figure 1 , Gamer is designed to operate across visually and mechanically diverse environments via a single screen-to-action interface.

> Figure 1 : Overview of Gamer. A generalist game agent framework operates across a broad range of visually and mechanically diverse games. At each step, the agent uses the current screen, task context, recent interaction history, long-term memory, and reasoning to generate native keyboard and mouse actions in real time. The agent interacts continuously through this loop. Cross-game training, multi-level supervision, and persistent memory support transferable control and sustained task execution.

Although games have long served as standard testbeds for AI, extending these successes to modern, complex games remains challenging. With domain-specific policy optimization, prior agents have matched or exceeded human performance in benchmarks such as Atari [ 27 ] , Minecraft [ 11 ] , and StarCraft [ 43 ] . However, these methods typically use privileged internal states, tailored action spaces, or dedicated APIs, which generalize poorly beyond their narrow training domains. As summarized in Table 1 , existing game agents differ substantially in their training recipes, game coverage, and real-time execution capabilities. Because modern games rarely expose internal states or dedicated interaction APIs, a true generalist agent should operate like a human player [ 41 , 51 , 33 ] : perceiving the world through raw visual pixels and acting through standard keyboard-and-mouse interfaces. Unifying transferable control, long-horizon decision-making, and non-invasive execution within a single generalist model remains largely unaddressed.

Unlike static benchmarks, dynamic game environments evolve continuously while an agent observes, reasons, and acts. Therefore, real-time interaction introduces a critical system bottleneck. Conventional inference engines introduce high inference latency, causing actions to be executed after the observed game state has changed. Over long trajectories, these delays can lead to control errors, unnecessary interactions, and early failures. This latency penalty also affects online reinforcement learning (RL): delayed rollouts not only inflate wall-clock collection times but also dilute the quality of policy feedback. Consequently, real-time inference is not merely a deployment requirement, but an important part of effective online learning.

To address these challenges, we present Gamer, a scalable generalist agent framework for complex interactive games. Gamer observes raw screen pixels and executes native keyboard and mouse controls. This unified device-level interface enables non-invasive interaction across diverse environments, eliminating reliance on game-specific APIs or customized action spaces. We first conduct continued pre-training (CPT) on thousands of hours of multi-source gameplay trajectories and map heterogeneous control formats into a unified keyboard-and-mouse representation to establish a transferable control prior. A scalable data-construction pipeline is built to transform raw gameplay trajectories into multi-level instruction data. Using LLM-assisted annotation, the pipeline adds task instructions, reasoning at critical decisions, and memory summaries while preserving the original observation-action sequences. We produce over a thousand hours of instruction data covering local operations, intermediate subgoals, and complete tasks. We leverage this corpus for sparse-thinking instruction tuning, enabling the policy to organize executable behavior around long-horizon objectives while retaining task-relevant memory. Finally, we introduce Temporal-Aware Group Relative Policy Optimization (TA-GRPO) to further optimize the game policy through online environment feedback and address accumulated execution errors and sparse rewards over extended interactions.

We support large-scale training and real-time deployment through a unified training, evaluation, and inference infrastructure. We extend Verl [ 40 ] to support CPT, SFT, and online RL within a shared training framework, with self-developed Streaming-vLLM serving as the inference engine for interactive evaluation and rollout generation. Streaming-vLLM combines stateful KV-cache reuse, architecture-aware execution, FSM-guided speculative decoding with BF16 verification, and streaming action dispatch to reduce inference latency. Equipped with a sandbox supporting concurrent game instances and a data pipeline that collects trajectories and identifies failures, this system enables iterative policy improvement under real-time interaction constraints. Gamer demonstrates solid generalization and operational efficiency comparable to skilled human players on several challenging 3D game tasks and achieves competitive performance on a range of traditional 2D game benchmarks in real-time. These results demonstrate that a unified keyboard-and-mouse action space which is coupled with cross-domain trajectory scaling and real-time online learning can offer a viable pathway toward generalist interactive agents.

We make the following contributions:

- • A generalist agent framework for complex games. Gamer interacts with complex game environments non-invasively, using screen images as input and standard keyboard and mouse operations as a unified action space. We demonstrate how learning in this shared action space generalizes across diverse game.

- • A scalable pipeline for long-horizon instruction data construction. We develop a pipeline that transforms multi-source and multi-format gameplay trajectories into instruction data. Through LLM-assisted annotation and quality validation, the pipeline enriches recorded interactions with task instructions, reasoning at critical decisions, and persistent memory summaries. Applied to tens of thousands of hours of gameplay data, it produces over a thousand hours of high-quality instruction data spanning operations, subgoals, and complete quests, providing supervision for long-horizon reasoning and control.

- • Stable and scalable training, evaluation, and real-time inference. We extend Verl into a unified training and evaluation framework spanning CPT, SFT, and TA-GRPO and introduce Streaming-vLLM to support efficient online rollouts and deployment. Our RL approach optimizes memory-linked trajectory segments using verifiable environment feedback at scale.

- • Strong empirical results. Gamer demonstrates solid generalization and operational efficiency comparable to skilled human players on challenging 3D commercial game tasks, and achieves competitive performance on several traditional 2D game benchmarks. These results support its potential for industrial-scale deployment.


### 2 Continued Pre-Training for Cross-Game Control

Games differ in world, mechanics, and objectives, but most games share a unified device-level interface: screen observations and native keyboard and mouse inputs. As shown in Figure 2 , CPT adapts a pretrained multimodal model to this interface and learns transferable control from trajectories collected across environments. We first unify action representations across games and reduce biases caused by key frequencies and repeated control states. We then summarize findings from GameScaling [ 58 ] on how model capacity, gameplay data, and game composition affect control learning and cross-game generalization.

> Figure 2 : CPT for unified keyboard-mouse control. Synchronized gameplay observations and device events are mapped to a shared action representation. Random key remapping reduces reliance on key identity, while repeat-aware loss decay reweights temporally persistent actions. The resulting control prior is used by the subsequent task-conditioned training stages.


#### 2.1 Unified Observation-Action Representation

Training across games requires a common action format that preserves the meaning of each game’s controls. We represent each gameplay trajectory as interleaved screen observations and native keyboard-mouse actions. For each game, a control specification $\mathcal{B}$ maps physical inputs to their logical functions:

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{B}=\{(k_{i},d_{i})\}_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{B}|},$$

where $k_{i}$ is a keyboard key or mouse button and $d_{i}$ describes its in-game function. We include this specification in the policy input so that the model can interpret the shared input vocabulary using the target game’s controls.

Following previous works [ 41 , 51 ] , we decouple visual decision making from low-level action execution. Observations are sampled every $200$ ms ( $5$ Hz), and each observation interval is divided into six keyboard-mouse action blocks. Let $\mathcal{E}_{t}$ denote as the set of mouse movement events in interval $t$ , where each event is represented by $\{(\delta x_{i},\delta y_{i},\delta z_{i})\}$ , corresponding to horizontal movement, vertical movement, and scrolling, respectively. We define the action for interval $t$ as

[Tableau] $$a_{t}=\left(\Delta\mathbf{P}_{t};\mathcal{K}_{t,1},\ldots,\mathcal{K}_{t,6}\right),\qquad\Delta\mathbf{P}_{t}=\sum_{\mathcal{E}_{t}}(\delta x_{i},\delta y_{i},\delta z_{i}),$$ (1)

where $\Delta\mathbf{P}_{t}$ is the accumulated mouse movement in the interval $t$ , and $\mathcal{K}_{t,j}$ ( $j=1,\dots,6$ ) is the set of keyboard keys and mouse buttons pressed in action block $j$ . We show more details of the action format in Figure 3 . Each block lasts approximately $33$ ms, yielding a $30$ Hz action rate. Keys and buttons shared by consecutive blocks remain pressed, while those absent from the next block are released. This representation supports held movement, brief taps, dragging, and simultaneous input combinations without increasing the visual observation rate. Actions are serialized using the pretrained language model’s output vocabulary, without an additional output head.

> Figure 3 : Serialized device-level action. For each observation interval, the policy predicts accumulated mouse movement and six sequential sets of active keys and buttons.

Given $\mathcal{B}$ and a recent interaction history $\mathcal{H}_{t}$ containing the current observation and preceding observation-action pairs, the policy autoregressively generates an output sequence $y_{t}=[r_{t},a_{t}]$ :

[Tableau] $$\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid\mathcal{B},\mathcal{H}_{t})=\prod_{j=1}^{|y_{t}|}\pi_{\theta}(y_{t,j}\mid\mathcal{B},\mathcal{H}_{t},y_{t,<j}),$$ (2)

where $r_{t}$ is an optional reasoning sequence preceding the executable action $a_{t}$ , and $y_{t,j}$ is the $j$ -th output token. For a small fraction of CPT turns, $r_{t}$ contains retrospective reasoning annotated from the observation and recorded action. These annotations preserve the backbone’s optional reason-then-act format without changing the action targets. The same executable action representation is retained during SFT, online RL, and deployment.


#### 2.2 Trajectory Debiasing and Temporal Reweighting

Gameplay data exhibit two biases that can limit transferable control learning. Uneven key frequencies encourage the model to associate actions with specific inputs rather than their functions, while long runs of repeated actions can dominate the token-level supervision. We address these biases with random key remapping and repeat-aware loss decay, respectively.

For each trajectory, we sample a random bijection $\sigma:\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bind}}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{bind}}$ over rebindable keys. Inputs with fixed semantics remain unchanged. The same mapping is applied to the control specification and the actions:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{\sigma}=\{(\sigma(k_{i}),d_{i})\}_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{B}|},\quad\mathcal{K}_{t,j}^{\sigma}=\{\sigma(k)\mid k\in\mathcal{K}_{t,j}\},\quad a_{t}^{\sigma}=\left(\Delta\mathbf{P}_{t};\mathcal{K}_{t,1}^{\sigma},\ldots,\mathcal{K}_{t,6}^{\sigma}\right).$ (3)

Historical actions are remapped consistently to obtain $\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\sigma}$ , whereas visual observations and mouse displacements remain unchanged. The policy is trained to predict $a_{t}^{\sigma}\sim\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}^{\sigma}\mid\mathcal{B}^{\sigma},\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\sigma})$ . The mapping remains fixed within a trajectory, preserving its control semantics while reducing shortcuts based on frequent key identities.

More than $85\%$ of the action steps in our interaction data repeat the preceding action, which is common in gameplay since players often hold the same inputs across multiple time steps to perform sustained behaviors such as moving or waiting. These repetitions record how long such behaviors last, so we retain the full trajectories while reducing the contribution of prolonged repetitions. Following the decaying-loss formulation in Game-TARS [ 51 ] , we down-weight consecutive repeated actions. Specifically, the action-token component of the CPT objective we used is

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{action}}$ $\displaystyle=-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\omega_{t}\sum_{j=1}^{|a_{t}|}\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t,j}\mid\mathcal{B},\mathcal{H}_{t},r_{t},a_{t,<j}),\quad\omega_{t}=\gamma^{n_{t}-1},$ (4)

where $n_{t}$ counts consecutive repetitions of the current action and resets to $1$ whenever the action changes. We use $\gamma=0.9$ and omit the remapping superscript for clarity. When present, retrospective reasoning tokens are additionally supervised as part of the same autoregressive response. Eq. ( 4 ) specifies the reweighting applied to action tokens, not the full mixed-token objective.

A newly issued action receives full weight, while successive repetitions contribute progressively less. This preserves action duration while emphasizing behavior transitions, such as stopping, turning, and interacting. Random key remapping discourages reliance on key identity, while repeat-aware decay shifts supervision toward changes in behavior.

The CPT objective combines the reweighted action-token loss with a standard autoregressive loss for the optional reasoning tokens:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CPT}}$ $\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{action}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reason}},$ (5) $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reason}}$ $\displaystyle=-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{j=1}^{|r_{t}|}\log\pi_{\theta}(r_{t,j}\mid\mathcal{B},\mathcal{H}_{t},r_{t,<j}).$

Here, $r_{t}=\varnothing$ for turns without reasoning, so the corresponding reasoning loss is zero. The repeat-aware weight $\omega_{t}$ is applied only to action tokens. For clarity, we omit the remapping superscript; when key remapping is applied, the control specification, interaction history, and action targets are remapped consistently.


#### 2.3 Scaling Laws and Cross-Game Generalization

Although a shared control interface makes cross-game CPT possible, the benefits of additional gameplay data depend on how well the training data cover the target environment. GameScaling [ 58 ] examines this dependence by varying model size, gameplay budget, and game composition. We summarize its findings here to contextualize Gamer’s CPT stage.

To characterize these effects, a composition-aware scaling relation based on D-CPT Law [ 35 ] is adopted. Let $N$ be model size, $D$ the gameplay budget in hours, and $q\in[0,1]$ the proportion of gameplay relevant to a specified validation distribution. The validation loss is modeled as

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}(N,D,q)=E+\frac{C_{N}}{N^{a}}+\frac{C_{D}q^{\eta}}{D^{b}}+\frac{C_{q}}{(q+\epsilon)^{c}},$$ (6)

where $E$ , $C_{N}$ , $C_{D}$ , $C_{q}$ , $a$ , $b$ , $c$ , and $\eta$ are fitted variables. The small constant $\epsilon>0$ keeps the expression finite when the target game’s share is zero. In view of the widespread use of Minecraft in prior work [ 51 , 11 ] , Gamescaling [ 58 ] varies its proportion in the gameplay mixture to study the effect of data composition. We use $q=w_{\mathrm{MC}}$ for Minecraft validation and $q=1-w_{\mathrm{MC}}$ for validation over the remaining training-game distribution, where $w_{\mathrm{MC}}$ is the Minecraft fraction of the gameplay mixture.

The data term describes how validation loss changes with the gameplay budget, and the final term captures differences between data mixtures. The scaling relation fits the training-game distributions and supports held-out prediction within the measured range. However, distant unseen games do not exhibit sustained gains from additional CPT data. Sec. 8.2 provides the controlled study and its empirical limits. CPT thus provides transferable control, while more distant worlds and longer tasks motivate additional task-conditioned supervision and online interaction.


### 3 Supervised Fine-Tuning for Sparse Reasoning and Cross-Context Memory

CPT learns transferable action control from visual observations, while supervised fine-tuning (SFT) teaches the model to follow explicit task objectives. We train on tasks at different difficulty levels, from local operations and intermediate subgoals to complete objectives, with reasoning and memory summaries at key decision points. The need for reasoning is inherently sparse in long-horizon gameplay. Routine movement and repeated attacks favor direct action, while route failures, target switches, and resource decisions often require replanning. Gamer therefore uses a single autoregressive policy to alternate between direct control, sparse reasoning, and memory updates. Persistent summaries carry task state across context boundaries. Figure 4 illustrates how adaptive reasoning and memory compaction are integrated into an interaction sequence.

> Figure 4 : Sparse reasoning and cross-context memory in supervised fine-tuning. The shared autoregressive policy integrates visual observations, task memory, optional reasoning, and executable keyboard-mouse actions. Context memory compaction preserves task progress across long interactions, while adaptive reasoning allows the policy to act directly at routine steps and generate explicit reasoning when needed.


#### 3.1 Multi-level Task Conditioning and Sparse Reasoning

SFT retains the control specification $\mathcal{B}$ and recent interaction history $\mathcal{H}_{t}$ introduced during CPT, and additionally conditions the policy on a task instruction $I$ and long-term memory $\mathcal{M}_{t}$ . The resulting context is

[Tableau] $$(\mathcal{B},I,\mathcal{M}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t}),$$

where $\mathcal{H}_{t}$ contains the current observation together with recent observations, actions, and previously generated reasoning. To expose the policy to task structure at different temporal scales, the instruction $I$ is instantiated at three difficulty levels:

[Tableau] $$I\in\left\{I^{\mathrm{op}},I^{\mathrm{sub}},I^{\mathrm{task}}\right\},$$

in which $I^{\mathrm{op}}$ , $I^{\mathrm{sub}}$ , and $I^{\mathrm{task}}$ correspond to local operations, intermediate subgoals, and complete tasks, respectively. Training data at all three levels use the same observation–action interface and are used to train a single policy that produces executable keyboard and mouse actions.

At each interaction step, the model first generates an optional reasoning sequence $r_{t}$ and then produces the executable action $a_{t}$ . Their joint distribution follows the autoregressive generation order:

[Tableau] $$\pi_{\theta}\left(r_{t},a_{t}\mid\mathcal{B},I,\mathcal{M}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t}\right)=\pi_{\theta}\left(r_{t}\mid\mathcal{B},I,\mathcal{M}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t}\right)\cdot\pi_{\theta}\left(a_{t}\mid\mathcal{B},I,\mathcal{M}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t},r_{t}\right).$$ (7)

The reasoning sequence takes one of three forms:

[Tableau] $$r_{t}\in\left\{\varnothing,r_{t}^{\mathrm{cur}},r_{t}^{\mathrm{mem}}\right\},$$ (8)

when $r_{t}=\emptyset$ , $\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid\mathcal{B},I,\mathcal{M}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t},r_{t})$ naturally simplifies to $\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid\mathcal{B},I,\mathcal{M}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t})$ . $r_{t}^{\mathrm{cur}}$ denotes reasoning for the current decision and $r_{t}^{\mathrm{mem}}$ denotes a memory summary for future contexts. At routine control steps, the model sets $r_{t}=\varnothing$ and generates an action directly. At decision-critical or memory-update steps, the model generates reasoning before the action. Current reasoning captures immediate judgment, next subgoal, or short-term plan, such as revising a route, selecting an interaction target, or switching combat strategy. Memory summaries, by contrast, retain information needed beyond the current context, including task progress, important locations, resource states, failed attempts, and remaining plans. The policy learns when to generate reasoning within the same autoregressive process, without a separate classifier or routing head. Here, $r_{t}$ refers only to the explicitly supervised reasoning text generated by the policy and does not characterize all latent computation within the model.

SFT trains current reasoning, memory summaries, and executable actions within the same autoregressive output space. Let

[Tableau] $$y_{t}=[r_{t},a_{t}]$$

denote the complete output sequence at step $t$ , and let $y_{t,<j}$ denote the generated prefix before its $j$ -th token. The SFT objective is

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}=-\sum_{t}\sum_{j=1}^{|y_{t}|}\log\pi_{\theta}\left(y_{t,j}\mid\mathcal{B},I,\mathcal{M}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t},y_{t,<j}\right).$$ (9)

The objective uses the same token-level autoregressive formulation as CPT, with the target extended from the action $a_{t}$ to the reasoning-action sequence $[r_{t},a_{t}]$ . It also introduces the task instruction $I$ and long-term memory $M_{t}$ into the conditioning context.


#### 3.2 Summary-Based Cross-Context Memory

Long-horizon tasks require the agent to retain relevant information after earlier observations and actions leave the active context. We update long-term memory $\mathcal{M}_{t}$ when the task changes stage, a subgoal is completed, or older interactions are about to leave the active context. Consider a memory-update step in which the short-term history $\mathcal{H}_{t}$ is partitioned as

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{H}_{t}=\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{old}}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{keep}},$$

where $\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{old}}$ contains earlier interactions that will leave the active context, $\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{keep}}$ contains recent interactions that remain directly accessible, and $\oplus$ denotes temporal concatenation.

Before $\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{old}}$ is discarded, the model generates a memory summary from the complete information currently available. The long-term memory and short-term history are then updated as

[Tableau] $$r_{t}^{\mathrm{mem}}\sim\pi_{\theta}\left(\cdot\mid\mathcal{B},I,\mathcal{M}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t}\right),\qquad\left(\mathcal{M}_{t}^{+},\mathcal{H}_{t}^{+}\right)=\left(\bar{\mathcal{M}_{t}}\oplus r_{t}^{\mathrm{mem}},\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{keep}}\right).$$ (10)

Here, $\mathcal{M}_{t}^{+}$ and $\mathcal{H}_{t}^{+}$ denote the updated long-term memory and short-term history. $\bar{\mathcal{M}_{t}}$ denotes the portion of the previous memory retained after reserving space for the new summary. When the memory budget is exceeded, the oldest complete summaries are removed in temporal order.

The update preserves overall task progress in memory and recent interaction details in the active history. Task-relevant information contained in $\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{old}}$ is carried forward through $r_{t}^{\mathrm{mem}}$ , while fine-grained recent evidence remains directly accessible in $\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{keep}}$ . When memory is updated at a task boundary without context eviction, we set $\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{old}}=\varnothing$ and $\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{keep}}=\mathcal{H}_{t}$ .

A memory-update step remains an ordinary interaction step. After generating $r_{t}^{\mathrm{mem}}$ , the policy still produces the corresponding executable action $a_{t}$ . Memory generation remains part of the reasoning–action process, allowing closed-loop control to continue without help from another memory model. During training, each step uses only the information that would be available at inference time. Each memory summary is generated only from interactions available at the corresponding update point. Subsequent trajectory segments are conditioned on the updated memory together with the retained recent history. The policy thus learns to continue a task after parts of its interaction history are no longer directly accessible.


### 4 Temporal-Aware GRPO for Long-Horizon Policy Optimization

CPT and SFT provide cross-game control and task-following capabilities, while online RL improves the policy on states encountered during its own interactions. Online RL in real-time games faces two challenges: long interactions can exceed the model’s context window, and delayed model responses can change the environment transitions observed during rollout collection. We address the first using Temporal-Aware GRPO (TA-GRPO) and the second using the shared Streaming-vLLM infrastructure (Sec. 5 ). Together, they enable policy optimization over long sequences of real-time keyboard and mouse interaction.

As illustrated in Figure 5 , TA-GRPO divides long rollouts into finite segments linked by the same memory compaction mechanism used during SFT. It scores segments from separate rollouts of the same task using verifiable outcomes and assigns group-relative advantages [ 39 ] to the decisions within each segment. The policy can therefore learn from long interactions without fitting a full trajectory into one context or repeatedly restoring an intermediate game state. The policy retains the same input and output format, generating reasoning, memory updates, and executable actions as needed.

> Figure 5 : Memory-linked long-horizon optimization with TA-GRPO . A long interaction may exceed the model’s context budget (top). Memory compaction divides online rollouts into finite segments whose task states remain connected by generated summaries (bottom). Segments at corresponding stages are compared using environment-verifiable outcomes, and temporal credit assignment distributes their group-relative advantage across decisions. The figure is schematic, and model execution uses the real-time serving backend in Sec. 5 .


#### 4.1 Memory-Linked Rollouts

For a task instruction $I$ , the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}$ samples $G$ long-horizon rollouts from the same task condition. Memory compaction partitions rollout $g$ into $J_{g}$ consecutive segments $\tau_{g,1},\ldots,\tau_{g,J_{g}}$ , each containing $T_{g,m}$ interaction steps. At step $t$ of segment $m$ , the policy receives $x_{g,m,t}=(\mathcal{B},I,\mathcal{M}_{g,m,t},\mathcal{H}_{g,m,t})$ and generates $y_{g,m,t}=(r_{g,m,t},a_{g,m,t})$ . The memory produced at a segment boundary carries completed objectives, failures, and remaining plans into subsequent segments. Earlier detailed observations may leave the active context without resetting the task.

For each segment index $m$ , we form a comparison group from rollouts that reach that stage, with index set $\mathcal{V}_{m}$ and size $G_{m}=|\mathcal{V}_{m}|$ . Unlike state-level branching, these segments are not required to start from identical intermediate game states. Grouping rollouts by task condition and compaction stage avoids costly game-state restoration. The shared action format keeps output structure and interaction granularity consistent. This grouping makes computation more regular, although the segments may still differ in difficulty and state distribution. Environment-verifiable feedback determines the relative outcomes used for learning.


#### 4.2 Verifiable Rewards and Temporal Credit Assignment

Long-horizon tasks often expose sparse terminal feedback, although some intermediate objectives admit direct verification. We use the verified outcome $R_{g,m}^{\mathrm{env}}$ of the segment’s current objective as its score $S_{g,m}=R_{g,m}^{\mathrm{env}}$ . When milestone completion is verifiable, it can provide feedback before the entire quest ends. Otherwise, the score remains sparse. No learned process reward model or value critic is required. Environment-side verification may access task state for training and evaluation, while the deployed policy still observes only the screen and issues keyboard-mouse actions.

Within the group at stage $m$ , scores are normalized to obtain a relative segment advantage:

[Tableau] $$A_{g,m}=\frac{S_{g,m}-\mu_{m}}{s_{m}},\qquad\mu_{m}=\frac{1}{G_{m}}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{V}_{m}}S_{g,m},\quad s_{m}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{G_{m}}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{V}_{m}}(S_{g,m}-\mu_{m})^{2}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{grp}}}.$$ (11)

This critic-free estimate follows GRPO [ 39 ] . Because verification typically occurs at a segment boundary, the segment score does not identify the contributions of individual decisions. TA-GRPO assigns larger weights to later steps, which are temporally closer to that outcome, while normalizing the weights to preserve their mean:

[Tableau] $$\widetilde{d}_{g,m,t}=\frac{\beta^{T_{g,m}-1-t}}{\frac{1}{T_{g,m}}\sum_{u=0}^{T_{g,m}-1}\beta^{T_{g,m}-1-u}},\qquad A_{g,m,t}=\widetilde{d}_{g,m,t}A_{g,m},\quad\beta\in(0,1).$$ (12)

The temporal weights average to one, redistributing the segment-level advantage across steps while preserving the mean. This weighting encodes a temporal prior, and it does not imply that later actions contribute more to the outcome.


#### 4.3 Joint Reasoning-Action Policy Update

TA-GRPO updates the autoregressive policy over the complete output $y_{g,m,t}=(r_{g,m,t},a_{g,m,t})$ , where $r_{g,m,t}$ may be empty, contain reasoning for the current decision, or provide a memory summary. The low-level action $a_{g,m,t}$ is always generated. For output token $j$ , we define the ratio between the current and old policy, conditioned on the preceding output tokens:

[Tableau] $$\rho_{g,m,t,j}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{g,m,t,j}\mid x_{g,m,t},y_{g,m,t,<j})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{g,m,t,j}\mid x_{g,m,t},y_{g,m,t,<j})}.$$ (13)

We use the standard token-level clipped GRPO surrogate, with $\rho=\rho_{g,m,t,j}(\theta)$ and $A=A_{g,m,t}$ for readability:

[Tableau] $$\ell_{g,m,t,j}(\theta)=\min\!\left\{\rho A,\,\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\rho,1-\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}},1+\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}\right)A\right\}.$$

The final objective averages this surrogate over valid segments, interaction steps, and output tokens:

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TA\text{-}GRPO}}=-\frac{1}{\sum_{m}G_{m}}\sum_{m}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{V}_{m}}\frac{1}{T_{g,m}}\sum_{t=0}^{T_{g,m}-1}\frac{1}{|y_{g,m,t}|}\sum_{j=1}^{|y_{g,m,t}|}\ell_{g,m,t,j}(\theta).$$ (14)

Token-level clipping avoids a single sequence-wide probability ratio whose scale would depend strongly on the combined reasoning and action length. The same learning signal can update memory summaries, sparse reasoning, and executable control. Streaming-vLLM generates these rollouts across concurrent game instances and dispatches actions promptly.


### 5 Streaming-vLLM for Real-Time and Scalable Gameplay

Real-time gameplay introduces timing constraints that are absent when evaluation pauses the game during inference. The results in Sec. 8.2 show that a policy can perform well in a paused game but degrade sharply when the game state changes before its actions arrive. This temporal mismatch also affects online policy optimization: delayed actions change subsequent states and the verified feedback in collected trajectories. At the same time, long-horizon training requires many independent environments to advance concurrently. Practical gameplay and efficient online rollout collection therefore require low-latency serving that preserves state across turns.

We develop Streaming-vLLM as a common execution backend for training, evaluation, and deployment. The backend retains KV states across turns under memory compaction and reduces model execution cost. It also verifies structural tokens proposed by a fast finite-state machine (FSM) and streams verified action blocks before the full packet is complete. The model-facing interface is shared across Qwen3-VL [ 3 ] and Qwen3.5 [ 36 ] , including dense and mixture-of-experts (MoE) backbones. Architecture-specific kernels and schedules support each backbone while preserving the same observation–action protocol. Appendix C reports representative Dense and MoE serving configurations on Ampere (NVIDIA A800) and Hopper (NVIDIA H200).

Paged allocation and memory-aware KV-cache reuse avoid repeated context processing, while incremental rotary position embedding (RoPE) refresh corrects retained keys after compaction. The runtime combines architecture-aware execution, BF16-verified FSM speculation, and early action dispatch to serve concurrent real-time interactions.


#### 5.1 Memory-Compacted Streaming Context

At each interaction step, the model consumes the control specification, task instruction, long-term memory, and recent observation-action history $(\mathcal{B},I,\mathcal{M}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t})$ . Re-encoding the visual and textual history at every turn would repeat the same prefill work. As shown in Figure 6 , Streaming-vLLM instead preserves KV states of retained context and computes new states only for newly appended tokens, following stateful streaming inference [ 53 , 54 ] . A short-term queue holds recent complete interaction rounds, while a long-term queue stores generated memory summaries. At a compaction boundary, the policy summarizes history before eviction. The discarded rounds release their KV pages, and recent rounds retain cached states. Each environment keeps its own logical-to-physical block table over the paged allocator [ 16 ] , allowing requests with different history lengths to coexist.

Compaction also changes the logical token positions of retained states. We update the rotary phase of the corresponding cached keys using the positional shift, without recomputing their projections or modifying the value cache. The retained cache preserves state across turns, so it need not be numerically equivalent to a full re-encoding of the shortened context. The precise RoPE refresh and its scope are detailed in Appendix C.2 . The same bounded history and persistent summaries are used in SFT and to link TA-GRPO trajectory segments.


#### 5.2 Architecture-Aware Execution Across Backbones

Frequent short requests incur costs beyond prefill, including decoder launches, inter-device communication, and architecture-specific operations. Streaming-vLLM separates a common serving interface from specialized execution paths. Dense and MoE variants share action serialization, context compaction, and client scheduling, while the compute backend accommodates their respective attention, expert, and recurrent or gated-delta-network operations. We combine operator fusion, tensor-parallel execution, communication overlap, and CUDA Graph replay to reduce serial overhead. We apply tensor parallelism selectively. It can reduce decoder compute time, but communication and synchronization may outweigh the savings for the smaller ViT workload. The measured tradeoff is analyzed in Table 9 . On NVIDIA H200, a C++ and bare-PTX BF16 backend further reduces kernel and orchestration costs on the critical path. Optional W8A8 paths are evaluated independently because lower precision does not always improve latency at a given model size or tensor-parallel degree.

We assess portability across model families and hardware configurations. We support Qwen3-VL [ 3 ] dense models at 2B, 4B, 8B, and 32B and its 30B-A3B MoE model, as well as Qwen3.5 [ 36 ] dense models at 0.8B, 2B, 9B, and 27B and its 35B-A3B MoE model. Our experiments and analysis cover representative Qwen3-VL Dense and Qwen3.5 MoE workloads across A800 and H200. The appendix compares numerical precision and tensor-parallel settings on each GPU.


#### 5.3 Verified FSM Decoding and Streaming Actions

After execution overhead is reduced, autoregressive generation remains sequential. A fast finite-state-machine (FSM) drafter proposes predictable structural tokens in the reasoning–action output. The FSM tracks <think> , </think> , <|action_start|> , and <|action_end|> boundaries. It proposes closing an empty reasoning span or starting an action packet after reasoning ends. Within the action, it drafts whitespace after mouse-coordinate numbers, the separator following the Z coordinate, and predictable delimiters and empty-key patterns across the six keyboard-mouse groups. Mouse-coordinate values remain model-generated. The target model verifies FSM proposals using BF16 computation, and only accepted prefixes are committed. The policy retains control over action selection, while verification reduces the number of sequential decoding iterations. See Appendix C.1 for more details.

Streaming-vLLM also reduces the time to the first executable action. Once the mouse fields and first verified key block are ready, the server sends that prefix to the client while the remaining blocks continue decoding. The client executes the six blocks in order, maintaining key press-hold-release semantics. Action execution can therefore begin while the remaining blocks are still being generated. The theoretical block-generation rate reported in the system ablation and the nominal 30 Hz client control protocol are distinct measurements, as clarified in Appendix C.4 .


#### 5.4 Concurrent Serving for Online Rollouts

Real-time rollouts require scheduling that accounts for both action deadlines and throughput. Each game environment maintains its own short-term history, long-term memory, KV pages, and timing deadlines. A shared scheduler batches context extension and decoding while limiting queueing delays that could make actions stale. This balances aggregate throughput against the progress rate of each active environment. Real-time evaluation and TA-GRPO training share the same serving path, so training rollouts are collected under the same execution protocol while the game continues to advance.

Stateful cache reuse reduces the cost of long contexts, architecture-specific backends support different models and GPUs, and verified FSM drafting reduces action-generation cost. Combined with early action dispatch, these components enable responsive, high-frequency control and parallel RL rollouts linked by memory summaries. Section 8.6 reports latency and closed-loop evaluations, and Appendix C.4 provides representative GPU, precision, and parallelism settings.


### 6 Data Construction for Cross-Game Learning

Raw gameplay videos provide rich visual and control experience, but they rarely specify the player’s objective, the decisions that require reasoning, or the information that should be retained beyond the recent context. We develop a scalable annotation pipeline that turns heterogeneous recordings into supervision for long-horizon task following, as shown in Figure 7 . Starting from tens of thousands of hours of raw gameplay collected across different sources, we filter invalid or misaligned recordings and balance coverage across gameplay scenarios. For SFT, we curate approximately 1K hours of gameplay trajectories. GPT-5.6-Luna [ 31 ] generates sparse action reasoning, task segments with instructions and scene descriptions, and task-state memories. We organize these annotations into three instruction levels: local operations, intermediate subgoals, and extended task stages.

#### 6.1 Trajectory Preparation and Temporal Alignment

Data sources, filtering, and scenario balancing. We collect raw gameplay recordings with associated keyboard and mouse events from multiple sources. Within this collection, the HSR recording track contains roughly 68K sessions and 11K hours of video. Rather than annotating all available videos, we first select recordings with reliable visual and action supervision. We reject corrupted or duplicated sessions, missing or discontinuous frames, and trajectories whose video and action timestamps cannot be aligned consistently. We also remove invalid action traces, including mouse coordinates outside the valid screen or control bounds, malformed action states, and events that cross recording or interaction boundaries. Long inactive periods and recordings without meaningful interaction are filtered, while valid held inputs and task-related waiting periods are retained.

Filtering alone would leave the instruction corpus dominated by frequently recorded activities. We therefore balance scenario coverage by limiting repeated behaviors and retaining less frequent interactions and longer task transitions. After these quality and coverage checks, we obtain approximately 3.2K sessions and 1K hours of video , which we use as the source for SFT annotation.

Action-observation alignment. We retain the original ordering of keyboard and mouse events and associate each action block with its corresponding frame using corrected recording timestamps. Each device-level action interval spans approximately $200$ ms and contains the six finer control blocks defined in Sec. 2.1 . Source videos are sampled to obtain the visual observations used for annotation. When frame indices are discontinuous, or timestamps show a recording gap, an action window is not allowed to cross that boundary. Short button presses are kept as short pulses rather than being dropped by temporal aggregation. This preserves movement duration and interaction timing while producing the same executable action interface used by the policy.


#### 6.2 Sparse Reasoning and Visual Task Segmentation

The annotation process separates local action reasoning from longer visual task understanding. Short video-action windows identify key decisions, while longer visual clips reveal task structure and progress. We use GPT-5.6-Luna for both annotation passes and align their outputs by the original frame IDs and recording timestamps.

Decision-focused Thinking. We divide the action stream into windows of 50 device-level action intervals, corresponding to roughly 10 seconds of interaction. Each window contains 25 ordered game observations and the associated 50 action intervals. GPT-5.6-Luna identifies a sparse set of decision points, typically when the player selects or corrects a route, handles a new interaction prompt, changes objective, recovers from an unsuccessful attempt, or responds to a significant scene transition. Each Think annotation describes the observed outcome, the current decision, and an intended next action. Continuous movement, repeated controls, and minor camera corrections usually receive no separate reasoning target. Windows with neither meaningful input nor visual change can be skipped. This process yields approximately 1.2M timestamp-aligned Think annotations .

Long-context instruction and scene annotation. We divide each session into the minimum number of equal-length visual clips of at most 288 seconds. Frames are selected at approximately one-second intervals and arranged into $3\times 2$ timestamped mosaics, yielding at most 49 mosaic images per clip. This lets GPT-5.6-Luna inspect continuous visual context without exceeding the image-input budget. The model identifies contiguous task segments and assigns each a task instruction , a gameplay label , and a short scene description of the visible evidence. These task segments provide the temporal and semantic basis for the instruction hierarchy.

We prompt the model to segment gameplay by the player’s current task. Brief movements and reward collection are grouped with the task they support, while an explicit challenge restart begins a new segment. Postprocessing adjusts adjacent segment boundaries to form a continuous timeline and converts clip-relative start and end times to timestamps in the original recording. This yields approximately 63K task segments , with a median duration of about 40 seconds. The activity labels cover seven gameplay categories: navigation, NPC dialogue, combat, minigames, system menus, Currency Wars, and other activities.


#### 6.3 Memory Annotation from Continuous Visual Context

After task segmentation, we reuse the clip images and cleaned task segments in a second conversational turn to generate memory annotations. The model returns all summaries for the clip in one response, typically one per 16-second window. Each summary briefly describes, in the first person, the cumulative accomplishments and outcomes within the clip up to the window end. A final window shorter than 8 seconds is merged into the preceding window. Postprocessing associates each summary with the active task instruction and the latest image frame at or before the window end.

The dataset contains approximately 223K memory annotations . At each training step, we condition the policy on the latest summary whose end timestamp is no later than the current observation. The annotation model receives the complete visual clip and is prompted to summarize only events up to each window end.


#### 6.4 Three-Level Instruction Construction

Building on the first-pass task segments, we construct easy, medium, and hard instructions according to the scope of the objective and the number of annotated decisions it covers. These levels correspond to local operations, intermediate subgoals, and extended task stages, respectively. The same recorded interval can support instructions at multiple levels.

Easy: local operations. Easy instructions cover one or two annotated decisions, such as approaching an NPC, selecting a menu option, or collecting an item. Medium: intermediate subgoals. Medium instructions combine related operations into a subgoal spanning three to five annotated decisions, such as navigating around an obstruction and completing the required interaction. Hard: extended task stages. Hard instructions connect multiple subgoals into a task stage spanning six or more annotated decisions, such as navigating through several areas and resolving intervening challenges.

We construct the instruction hierarchy bottom-up from temporally aligned annotations. Task-segment instructions and scene descriptions establish the activity and its boundaries, Think annotations identify key decisions, and memories track task progress. We group adjacent operations that advance a common subgoal, then combine related subgoals into extended task stages. Human-annotated examples guide the wording and scope of the generated instructions. All three levels share the recorded actions and Think annotations , and each provides an instruction and memory context appropriate to its objective.

Horizon-dependent training targets. For a selected supervision horizon $h\in\{\mathrm{op},\mathrm{sub},\mathrm{task}\}$ , an example is represented as

[Tableau] $$x_{t}^{h}=\left(\mathcal{B},I^{h},\mathcal{M}_{t}^{h},\mathcal{H}_{t}\right),\qquad y_{t}=[r_{t},a_{t}],$$ (15)

where $I^{h}$ denotes the task instruction at level $h$ , $\mathcal{M}_{t}^{h}$ denotes its task memory, and $\mathcal{H}_{t}$ contains the current observation and recent interaction history. The reasoning text $r_{t}$ and action target $a_{t}$ are drawn from the same recorded trajectory and its annotations. Every step includes action supervision, including steps without Think annotations.


### 7 Implementation Details


#### 7.1 Model and Training Configuration

Gamer supports both dense and mixture-of-experts (MoE) architectures across Qwen3-VL and Qwen3.5 families. The trained models share the same autoregressive keyboard-mouse action interface without an additional output head. We first analyze CPT scaling across 120 controlled Qwen3.5 Dense configurations, referred to as GameScaling . Guided by these findings, we train Gamer from Qwen3-VL-8B with additional HSR gameplay data.

Training follows three stages while preserving the model architecture and action representation. CPT learns transferable control with key remapping and repeat-aware loss decay. SFT introduces multi-level task supervision, sparse reasoning, and cross-context memory. TA-GRPO then improves long-horizon behavior through online interaction, using environment-verifiable rewards and temporal credit assignment. Gameplay trajectories are sampled at 5 Hz, with six action blocks per step providing approximately 30 Hz control and a local visual history of 20 frames.

We extend Verl [ 40 ] into a unified training and evaluation framework, using Streaming-vLLM for interactive evaluation and online RL rollouts. A sharded inference service pool backed by 64 NVIDIA H200 GPUs serves 32 parallel game environments. Each RL iteration collects 128 rollouts in groups of 8, with environment-verifiable rewards and no learned process reward model. Updated policy weights are synchronized across the service pool before the next rollout collection, keeping policy versions aligned throughout online training. Streaming-vLLM also supports dense and MoE backbones on NVIDIA A800 and H200 GPUs. Further configuration details are provided in Table 15 and Appendix F.1 .

#### 7.3 Automated RL Environment

A shared infrastructure for learning, evaluation, and testing. Many existing game-RL benchmarks run as lightweight, self-contained simulators that can be reset cheaply and queried directly for rewards. Modern commercial games present a substantially harder setting. In particular, Honkai: Star Rail depends on a full game client, supporting online services, GPU-accelerated rendering, and complex game-state dependencies. Reproducing its native mechanics in a lightweight simulator would change the interaction setting, while simply replicating game clients does not provide controlled initialization or objective verification. These challenges extend beyond online RL to large-scale automated evaluation and gameplay testing, where hundreds of executions must start from comparable states and be judged against consistent success criteria. We therefore build a dedicated, engine-instrumented sandbox that makes this resource-intensive game reproducible, verifiable, and scalable without changing the agent’s visual-control interface.

Game-native rules as a unified source of feedback. We adapt the game engine to expose task-relevant state transitions and completion conditions to the environment backend. The resulting rule-based verifiers serve three complementary purposes. During online RL, they provide environment-verifiable rewards for TA-GRPO without a learned process reward model. During evaluation, they measure task completion objectively across models and policy checkpoints. For automated gameplay testing, the same checks provide reproducible pass/fail criteria for interactions and quest progression. This shared verification layer avoids relying on subjective assessments of generated text or inferring complex task outcomes from individual screenshots.

GM-controlled initialization and reproducible execution. We integrate game-master (GM) commands to prepare predefined task states and restore the corresponding conditions before an episode. Each task pairs an initialization procedure with a target state, a completion check, and a termination rule. This allows the same objective to be executed repeatedly for policy optimization, fair comparison, and systematic testing of game behaviors. It also makes failed executions easier to reproduce and inspect. Crucially, all privileged engine access remains within the environment backend. The policy receives only screen observations and task context, and its actions are executed through the same keyboard–mouse interface used in real gameplay.

Scalable execution across use cases. We construct verifiable environments spanning navigation, dialogue and interaction, and game-specific puzzle mechanics. For online RL, 32 parallel Windows game environments are served by a sharded Streaming-vLLM inference pool backed by 64 NVIDIA H200 GPUs. Each iteration collects 128 trajectories in groups of eight, with policy checkpoints synchronized before the next collection round. The same initialization, execution, and verification interfaces also support batched evaluation and automated regression tests without manually resetting or judging each attempt. Beyond enabling real-time policy optimization, this infrastructure provides a practical foundation for scalable assessment and testing in modern commercial games. Sec. 8.1 introduces the held-out side-quest and minigame evaluations.


### 8 Experiments

Our evaluation examines three complementary questions. First, we summarize the study in GameScaling, which evaluates 120 CPT configurations to characterize the effects of model scale, gameplay budget, and data composition. We then assess the executable cross-game performance of CPT policies built on Dense and MoE backbones. Next, we evaluate the separately trained HSR-augmented Gamer-CPT and its SFT and RL successors on HSR side quests and minigames, separating broad interaction skills from long-horizon task execution. Finally, we test whether real-time rollout collection supports online learning and whether Streaming-vLLM delivers the accuracy and latency needed for sustained interaction. All interactive evaluations use the shared keyboard-mouse interface.


#### 8.1 Evaluation Setup

We evaluate game-control policies on MCU [ 63 ] , ViZDoom [ 52 ] , OmniGameArena [ 23 ] , and GameWorld [ 32 ] . These benchmarks vary in how far they differ from the gameplay data used for CPT. Specifically, MCU and ViZDoom evaluate game domains represented during CPT and primarily measure control learning within training-covered environments. OmniGameArena evaluates transfer to unseen three-dimensional games with related interaction semantics. GameWorld provides a more distant cross-game transfer setting with larger shifts in visual appearance, control mechanics, and task structure.

We follow the task definitions, environment settings, and native scoring protocols of the corresponding benchmarks. Each benchmark retains its original evaluation metric. We distinguish idealized time-paused (TP) evaluation from real-time (RT) interaction. TP pauses the environment during model inference, whereas RT allows the game to continue advancing. In the public game comparison, we report the RT score as the main value and its signed difference from TP (RT $-$ TP) in parentheses.

Across game evaluations, we use the same set of strong multimodal baselines wherever they support the required visual-control interface. These include GPT-6.1-Sol [ 30 ] , Gemini-3.8-Flash [ 12 ] , GPT-5.6-Sol [ 31 ] , GPT-5.6-Luna [ 31 ] , Claude-Opus-5.0 [ 1 ] , GLM-5.3-Flash [ 57 ] , and DeepSeek-V4-Flash-Vision [ 9 ] . The same baseline pool is reused in the Honkai: Star Rail side-quest and minigame evaluations to provide a consistent comparison across control, instruction following, and gameplay generalization.

We construct two complementary evaluation sets in Honkai: Star Rail, comprising side quests and minigame tasks held out from training (Figure 9 ). Although the final CPT model has seen other gameplay from the same game, it has not been trained on these evaluation tasks or their target completions. This setting tests generalization to new tasks within a familiar game, rather than zero-shot transfer to an unseen game.

The first set contains two long-horizon side quests that test sustained progress on complex instructions. Each quest may combine navigation, dialogue, combat, puzzles, and item interactions across several intermediate objectives. To finish a quest, the agent must remember quest information, track subgoal progress, and use that task state to guide low-level control.

The two evaluated quests are Opportunity Makes the Thief (Part I) and Bestial Ferocity . They contain 30 and 55 evaluation milestones, grouped into 22 and 40 parent steps, respectively. The first quest combines navigation, dialogue, combat, and UI interaction. The second also includes three automaton-repair puzzles and repeated visits to earlier locations. The total time budgets across milestones are 30 minutes and 100 minutes, respectively.

Each quest has a fixed initial state, task objective, milestone sequence, verifier, and termination condition. We report accuracy as the fraction of evaluated task executions that succeed, both overall and for each interaction type. The milestone inventory covers navigation, dialogue, combat, UI interaction, and, where applicable, puzzle solving. On the remaining training games, multi-game mixtures continue to improve while Minecraft-only training eventually plateaus or degrades. Adding the same amount of gameplay data can therefore produce different gains, depending on its coverage of the target distribution.

Larger models learn more efficiently early in training, but their gains still depend on data composition. Under single-game training, stronger adaptation to Minecraft can coincide with degraded performance on other games. Multi-game mixtures alleviate this effect. For distant unseen games, validation loss typically improves during early interface adaptation and then plateaus or increases even while training loss continues to decrease. These observations motivate considering model capacity, interaction budget, and target-game coverage jointly.

Evaluation results on game benchmarks complement the validation-loss analysis with executable gameplay outcomes. As shown in Figure 14 , additional CPT experience generally improves performance in training-covered and behaviorally related environments, particularly at smaller model scales. The comparison across S1-S5 also shows why the amount of gameplay alone is an incomplete description of the training signal. Mixtures that expose the model to multiple interaction patterns provide broader coverage than single-game training and support transfer to related environments. For Gamer, these findings motivate scaling game diversity alongside data volume rather than treating all recorded gameplay hours as equally informative for every target game.

#### 8.5 Impact of Real-Time Rollouts on Online RL

Real-time interaction is critical for online RL in modern games. Unlike lightweight simulators, modern games involve continuously evolving environments, complex gameplay mechanics, and state-dependent interactions. In this setting, inference latency affects not only the responsiveness of the deployed agent but also the quality and efficiency of online policy learning. Delayed control can cause subsequent actions to operate on outdated observations, making it harder to obtain timely feedback from the environment. We therefore examine the effect of rollout frequency on RL training, comparing real-time Streaming-vLLM against standard vLLM rollouts. The game continues to advance under both settings.

> Figure 11 : Real-time inference improves online RL learning across eight navigation tasks. Task labels indicate navigation targets. For example, “Pom-Pom” refers to the task of finding the NPC Pom-Pom. Orange solid lines show rewards under the original real-time Streaming-vLLM setting, while blue dashed lines show the 0.6-FPS control representing standard vLLM rollout speed. Real-time inference typically achieves earlier reward improvement and stronger learning over the first 50 plotted training iterations.

Timely control supports more effective online policy learning. As shown in Figure 11 , real-time Streaming-vLLM consistently demonstrates more favorable learning dynamics than the standard vLLM setting across eight navigation tasks. First, real-time rollouts achieve higher rewards from the early stages of training. Timely observation and action execution reduce accumulated interaction errors, allowing the agent to reach intended states more reliably and obtain informative reward feedback more efficiently. Second, real-time training achieves higher reward levels, particularly on tasks involving time-sensitive navigation and interactions, where delayed control can prevent the agent from completing the required action sequences. More importantly, several low-FPS runs exhibit reward collapse in the later stages of training. This behavior suggests that accumulated execution errors can introduce increasingly biased trajectories into online optimization, undermining subsequent policy updates and training stability. In contrast, real-time interaction maintains more reliable environment feedback and supports sustained policy improvement. These observations highlight that low-latency inference is not merely a deployment optimization but an important prerequisite for effective and stable RL training in complex commercial games.

### 9 Conclusion

Gamer learns to control diverse and complex games through a shared keyboard-and-mouse interface. It combines large-scale continued pre-training, task-conditioned supervised fine-tuning, and temporal-aware reinforcement learning to learn transferable control, long-horizon reasoning, and persistent memory. Streaming-vLLM supports both training and deployment, enabling real-time interaction and efficient online rollouts while reducing latency-induced bias in reinforcement learning. At inference time, Gamer acts through visual observations and device-level controls without privileged game state or game-engine modifications. Environment-side task verification is used separately to provide online RL rewards. These capabilities could effectively reduce development costs through automated testing, faster game iteration, and new gameplay mechanics. By supporting real-time interaction without game-specific infrastructure, Gamer offers a basis for agents that learn and improve across complex 3D games and other interactive environments.


### 10 Limitations and Future Work

Several main directions remain for extending Gamer’s scope and accessibility. First, our evaluation covers a selected set of environments and task structures. The shared observation-action interface provides a basis for extending joint post-training and systematic evaluation to broader interactive settings. Our preliminary results suggest the potential for improving performance and generalization by scaling relevant gameplay data across a broader range of environments. These benefits depend on the coverage and composition of the training experience.

Second, Streaming-vLLM enables real-time interaction on the evaluated GPU platforms, while further reductions in serving costs would support wider deployment. Model compression and additional runtime optimizations offer promising directions for extending this efficiency to more resource-constrained settings while preserving responsiveness and task performance.

Third, although Gamer improves the trade-off between response latency and task performance in the evaluated settings, reasoning capability may remain a bottleneck in environments that demand deeper deliberation. Fast, continuous interaction alone does not ensure the ability to solve complex strategic problems. Our framework provides a foundation for exploring hierarchical game agents that combine a larger model for deliberative System 2 reasoning with a fast model for reactive System 1 execution. Such architectures could coordinate longer-term planning with timely control, extending the range of tasks that agents can handle under real-time constraints.

### Appendix A Related Work


#### A.1 Generalist Game Agents

Research on game-playing agents has expanded from environment-specific policies to general models combining perception, reasoning, memory, and executable control. Large-scale behavioral pretraining provides one route: VPT [ 4 ] learns Minecraft control from internet videos labeled by an inverse dynamics model, while STEVE-1 [ 21 ] extends this paradigm to language-conditioned behavior. Other systems, including DEPS [ 48 ] , Voyager [ 44 ] , JARVIS-1 [ 49 ] , Optimus-1 [ 20 ] , and OmniJARVIS [ 50 ] , incorporate planning, reusable skills, and memory to support increasingly complex tasks.

Recent work further targets generalization across tasks and worlds. Gato [ 38 ] and SIMA [ 37 ] study shared policies across heterogeneous environments, while Game-TARS [ 51 ] and Lumine [ 41 ] combine reasoning, memory, and native keyboard–mouse control for extended gameplay. NitroGen [ 26 ] and OpenP2P [ 56 ] instead emphasize scalable visual policy learning from broad gameplay data. These approaches address different aspects of generalist gameplay, typically emphasizing cross-environment reactive control or extended reasoning under more specialized training settings. We combine cross-game continued pre-training with task-oriented policy optimization for sustained interaction.


#### A.2 Scalable Gameplay Learning

Progress in game agents increasingly relies on large and heterogeneous interaction data. MineRL [ 14 ] and MineDojo [ 11 ] provide structured demonstrations and multimodal resources, while VPT [ 4 ] scales behavioral learning through inverse dynamics. D2E [ 8 ] , NitroGen [ 26 ] , and OpenP2P [ 56 ] broaden supervision through desktop trajectories, visible control overlays, and inverse-dynamics-labeled gameplay. GameHorizon [ 47 ] further introduces multi-level annotations spanning operations, subgoals, and strategies.

Transfer also depends on model size and data composition. General scaling and continual-pretraining studies show that performance depends jointly on model capacity, data budget, and domain mixture. In gameplay, VPT [ 4 ] demonstrates the value of large-scale behavioral pretraining, while OpenP2P [ 56 ] studies scaling through both predictive performance and causal behavior. GameScaling varies model size, interaction budget, and game composition to distinguish between gains obtained by learning a shared control interface and gains that depend on target-relevant gameplay. Scale alone is not sufficient for task-oriented gameplay. Effective policy learning also requires supervision that connects local actions with higher-level objectives. GameHorizon [ 47 ] introduces temporal hierarchy into gameplay supervision, while OmniJARVIS [ 50 ] and Lumine [ 41 ] combine reasoning, memory, and staged training. We train the policy with supervision at the operation, subgoal, and task levels, and retain compact task summaries across context boundaries.


#### A.3 Memory and Long-Horizon Interaction

Sustained gameplay also requires agents to retain task-relevant information after earlier interactions leave the active context. Existing agents address this through reusable skills, retrieved experience, or compressed interaction histories. Voyager stores executable skills, JARVIS-1 [ 49 ] and Optimus-1 [ 20 ] retrieve accumulated experience, and Game-TARS [ 51 ] combines recent interactions with compressed textual history. OmniJARVIS [ 50 ] and Lumine [ 41 ] further integrate memory with reasoning and action generation. We explicitly supervise task summaries and use them as persistent task state during supervised training, online policy optimization, and streaming inference.

Feedback-based optimization provides a complementary mechanism for improving extended behavior. GRPO [ 39 ] optimizes relative advantages across groups of sampled outputs, while GUI-R1 [ 24 ] and Game-RL [ 42 ] adapt reinforcement learning to executable GUI actions and multimodal game reasoning. Game-TARS [ 51 ] also refines thought–action trajectories through action-consistency-based filtering. We optimize finite trajectory segments linked by compressed memory, updating task-state maintenance and executable behavior together. Long-horizon interaction must also operate under bounded context and latency. StreamingLLM [ 53 ] maintains stable inference with a fixed attention window, while InfiniPot-V [ 15 ] , DSCache [ 34 ] , and StreamingVLM [ 54 ] explore compressed or structured memory for continuous visual streams. Serving techniques such as PagedAttention [ 16 ] and speculative decoding further reduce autoregressive inference cost. These directions are largely studied separately from long-horizon policy optimization. Our streaming engine uses bounded memory, cache reuse, and the task representation learned during training to sustain interaction within context and latency limits.

Gamer combines cross-game behavioral scaling, multi-level task supervision, and memory-supported long-horizon interaction in one policy.

#### B.3 Composition-Aware Scaling-Law Fitting

This subsection summarizes the fitting procedure and results reported in GameScaling [ 58 ] , using the notation of Sec. 2.3 . The composition-aware scaling relation is fitted separately to Minecraft validation loss and validation loss over the remaining training-game distribution:

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}(N,D,q)=E+\frac{C_{N}}{N^{a}}+\frac{C_{D}q^{\eta}}{D^{b}}+\frac{C_{q}}{(q+\epsilon)^{c}}.$$ (17)

The fitted parameter vector is

[Tableau] $$\bm{\phi}=\left(E,C_{N},C_{D},C_{q},a,b,c,\eta,\epsilon\right).$$

Model size $N$ is measured in billions of parameters and data budget $D$ in hours of gameplay interaction. For Minecraft validation, we use

[Tableau] $$q=w_{\mathrm{MC}},$$

where $w_{\mathrm{MC}}$ is the Minecraft fraction of the gameplay mixture. For validation over the other training games, we use

[Tableau] $$q=1-w_{\mathrm{MC}}.$$

The complete fit uses the 120 controlled configurations described in Sec. 8.2 . All validation losses are measured from checkpoints that complete the corresponding training budget and decay phase.

Fitting objective. Let $\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{fit}}$ denote the set of configurations used for fitting and let $L_{j}^{\mathrm{obs}}$ denote the observed validation loss of configuration $j$ . We define the log residual as

[Tableau] $$e_{j}(\bm{\phi})=\log\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}\left(N_{j},D_{j},q_{j},\bm{\phi}\right)-\log L_{j}^{\mathrm{obs}}.$$

The fitted parameters minimize a Huber objective:

### Appendix C Streaming-vLLM Implementation Details


#### C.1 FSM-Guided Drafting and BF16 Verification

FSM grammar coverage. The SM90 fast FSM recognizes the boundaries <think> , </think> , <|action_start|> , and <|action_end|> . It can draft the end of an empty reasoning span or the beginning of an action packet after a finished reasoning span. Within the XYZ mouse-coordinate fields, the FSM drafts formatting tokens following numerical values, not the coordinate values themselves. In the six subsequent key groups, it drafts predictable delimiters, blank-key templates, and the action terminator. Candidate tokens are proposed opportunistically when the current grammar state supports them.

Target-model verification. The drafts are checked by the BF16 target model before being committed. Under greedy decoding, only a candidate prefix that agrees with target-model token selection is accepted. Rejected suffixes and their speculative cache states are discarded. FSM output is therefore a fast proposal, not constrained decoding or a replacement for the policy’s action decisions. The implementation should report the drafting window, prefix acceptance, and verified output consistency under the selected inference configuration.

Stochastic decoding. If stochastic sampling is used, preserving the target sampling distribution requires a proposal-aware acceptance rule and residual correction rather than unconditionally accepting FSM predictions [ 17 ] . We do not assume that such a correction is enabled in every deployment configuration. The SM90 results correspond to the implemented BF16 target-model verification path. Distribution-preserving stochastic decoding requires the appropriate acceptance rule.


#### C.2 Incremental RoPE Cache Refresh

Figure 18 illustrates the incremental positional refresh used for retained KV states after context compaction. A cached key originally associated with logical position $p$ may be reassigned to a new position $\widehat{p}$ . Rather than recomputing its key projection, Streaming-vLLM applies only the positional difference

[Tableau] $$\Delta p=\widehat{p}-p.$$

> Figure 18 : Incremental RoPE refresh for retained KV states. A cached key moved from logical position $p$ to $\widehat{p}$ is updated by an additional rotation determined by $\Delta p=\widehat{p}-p$ . The same positional offset is applied independently to each RoPE frequency pair and written back in place, while the value cache remains unchanged. The figure illustrates $\Delta p<0$ , corresponding to a leftward shift after context compaction.

For a pair of cached key components $(u,v)$ associated with rotary frequency $\omega_{i}$ , let

[Tableau] $$\phi=\Delta p\,\omega_{i}.$$

The incremental update is implemented as

[Tableau] $\displaystyle u^{\prime}$ $\displaystyle=u\cos\phi-v\sin\phi,$ (19) $\displaystyle v^{\prime}$ $\displaystyle=u\sin\phi+v\cos\phi.$

Both updated components are computed from the original pair before being written back to the cache. The implementation follows the component-pair layout used by the underlying model and applies the corresponding update independently to all dimensions participating in RoPE.

Tokens whose logical positions remain unchanged have $\Delta p=0$ and therefore require no modification. The update is performed in place on the retained key cache. No unrotated key representation is stored, and the value cache is left unchanged. For RoPE variants with multiple positional axes, the same correction is applied independently to the component groups associated with each axis.

This update corrects only the rotary positional phase. As discussed in Sec. 5.1 , retained KV states continue to follow the stateful streaming semantics of Streaming-vLLM rather than being reconstructed through full re-encoding after memory compaction.

### Appendix D Honkai: Star Rail Evaluation Protocol and Taxonomy


#### D.1 World Diversity and Interactive Complexity

Honkai: Star Rail provides a useful setting for studying generalist game agents because its complexity comes from combining familiar interactions across a large, varied world, rather than from a single difficult mechanic. Figure 20 brings together 171 floor maps from 85 areas across the game’s major worlds. The maps range from compact rooms and branching corridors to large open layouts, interconnected structures, and multi-floor environments. Even when presented at a common coordinate scale, their differences are apparent. An agent cannot assume that a route learned in one area will work in another. It must adjust to new geometry, identify transitions between areas or floors, and preserve its destination as the scene changes.

> Figure 20 : Exploration diversity in Honkai: Star Rail . Map layouts vary in geometry, connectivity, and floor structure, and point icons indicate resources and other interactive locations. Red paths illustrate the possible exploration routes.

The many point icons also reveal how exploration and task execution are interleaved. Individual areas contain resources and other points of interest alongside locations relevant to progression and interaction. A route can pass several optional objects before reaching the target, and the shortest visible path need not be a feasible sequence of game actions. The red paths in Figure 20 are schematic exploration routes , not gameplay-verified optimal solutions. They illustrate the need to plan through branching spaces, but do not establish that any particular route is the fastest or most reliable. The annotated maps are provided only to describe the environment and are not given to the evaluated agents as observations or privileged navigation input.

Accessible gameplay does not imply easy autonomous control. For a human player, quest objectives, map markers, and familiar interaction prompts make many individual steps understandable. A vision-language-action agent receives only the rendered screen and must turn this guidance into timed keyboard and mouse inputs. It must recognize where it is, distinguish relevant objects from nearby distractions, navigate while the camera and scene change, and determine when an interaction has actually completed. Longer tasks add another difficulty: navigation, dialogue, UI manipulation, combat, and local puzzles may appear within the same quest. Failure at one step can invalidate a later plan, and an agent must remember earlier progress even after those observations leave its active context. The challenge is therefore not simply to play any one minigame, but to coordinate heterogeneous skills over time.

This combination makes the game useful for both research and practical evaluation. Short interactive challenges probe whether a policy can adapt to different local rules, while side quests test whether those skills support sustained, instruction-conditioned progress. The same interface also reflects conditions faced by automated game testing: new content introduces routes, objects, objectives, and mechanics that cannot all be anticipated by hand-written scripts. A generalist agent able to execute these tasks through screen observations and device-level actions could support exploration, quest verification, and regression testing without modifications to the game engine. These are potential applications , not deployment benefits measured by the map itself. Our experiments cover a defined subset of this broader environment. Although Gamer-CPT is trained on other Honkai: Star Rail gameplay, neither of the two evaluated side quests nor any of the 40 scored minigame tasks is included in training. This split tests generalization to held-out tasks within a familiar game world, not zero-shot transfer to a game unseen during training.


#### D.2 Combat Mechanics and Strategic Decision-Making

> Figure 21 : Combat mechanics and character roles in Honkai: Star Rail . The examples illustrate (1) the combat interface, including action order, HP and Toughness, team status, skill points, and available abilities, (2) a Weakness Break during a boss encounter, (3) the Action Value display, and (4) a character Data Bank snapshot. The lower panel summarizes the nine Paths by representative combat roles, including damage, support, sustain, and more specialized mechanics. Path groupings summarize representative combat roles, and the figure provides game-mechanics context rather than a measured policy rollout.

Exploration is only one part of Honkai: Star Rail . Combat adds a different decision-making problem within the same game interface. The game uses turn-based encounters, but choosing an action still depends on information that changes throughout the fight. As shown in Figure 21 , the combat screen combines the action-order queue with character HP, enemy Toughness, team status, skill points, and available abilities. The action-order display and Action Value indicate when combatants will act, while the Toughness system creates opportunities for Weakness Break. The agent must use these cues together, rather than treating each turn as an isolated image-to-action decision.

Combat decisions involve coupled constraints over multiple turns. Attacking a vulnerable target may advance a Weakness Break, but the agent must also consider the available skill points, the health and status of its team, and which combatant acts next. Character roles introduce further variation. The Paths shown in Figure 21 span single-target and multi-target damage, buffs and debuffs, protection and healing, summons, and other specialized combat effects. A useful action can therefore depend on the team composition and on how one character’s choice changes the options available to others. Although human players can read these cues from a familiar interface, an autonomous policy must recognize them visually, choose a suitable action, and issue the corresponding executable inputs.

This makes combat complementary to the game’s navigation, dialogue, and puzzle mechanics. Navigation requires spatial grounding and route execution, whereas combat requires state-dependent target selection and coordination of limited resources across turns. Both demand that the agent preserve its objective while the available actions and relevant observations change. The held-out side-quest benchmark includes combat milestones alongside other interaction types. The atlas in Figure 21 provides context for those milestones and the broader game setting, rather than constituting a separate combat benchmark or evidence of a model’s combat performance. Together with the exploration maps, it illustrates why generalist gameplay requires more than a collection of fixed action scripts: the same visual-control policy must adapt to different rules, time scales, and notions of progress within one evolving game world.
