## Learning When Diffusion Language Models Should Regenerate

Remasking allows diffusion language models to revisit generated text, but effective correction requires knowing when and what to revise. Our diagnosis on Sudoku reveals two gaps. Hidden states contain error information that token confidence does not reliably expose, and remasking an isolated error usually reproduces it when the surrounding context remains fixed. These findings motivate DiVR , a verifier that predicts whether a partially generated sequence will reach a correct answer. Trained on the final correctness of the model’s own trajectories, DiVR decides when to regenerate a block and which alternative to retain, while the generator itself stays unchanged. On five reasoning benchmarks with LLaDA-8B-Base, DiVR raises the average accuracy of the fine-tuned generator from 35.3% to 43.4% at $1.9$ – $3.5\times$ the cost of greedy decoding, above search baselines that use $3.8$ – $7.4\times$ . It also complements post-training such as supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning, and generalizes to other generators, including Dream.


### 1 Introduction

Masked diffusion language models (MDLMs) generate text by repeatedly filling in the masked positions of a partially completed canvas, predicting every position with the whole response in view ( Sahoo et al., 2024 ; Nie et al., 2025 ; Ye et al., 2025b ) . This joint generation helps on planning tasks such as Sudoku and Countdown ( Ye et al., 2025a ) , reduces the number of sequential steps ( Inception Labs et al., 2025 ; Wu et al., 2026 ) , and in principle allows generated tokens to be masked again and revised ( Wang et al., 2025 ) .

In practice, standard MDLM decoding never revisits a committed token ( Nie et al., 2025 ) , so a wrong answer committed early with high confidence tends to remain ( Zhao et al., 2026 ) . Remasking samplers and search methods add revision, but decide what to revise by a schedule, the model’s confidence or likelihood, a planner or prompted self-verification ( Wang et al., 2025 ; Hong et al., 2026 ; Peng et al., 2025 ; Luo et al., 2026 ; Bai et al., 2026 ) , none of which is trained to predict whether the final answer is correct. Learned alternatives act on individual tokens, through scorers trained against a reference sequence, unmasking policies or uniform-noise training ( Kim et al., 2026 ; Huang et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2026 ; Jazbec et al., 2026 ; von Rütte et al., 2025 ) , although in free-form reasoning a token that departs from the reference may still belong to a correct solution. Others borrow an external process reward model trained on autoregressive solutions ( Mounier and Idehpour, 2025 ; Dang et al., 2026 ; Misaki and Akiba, 2026 ; Miles et al., 2026 ) , a second large model that never sees the generator’s states.

Recent work shows that MDLM hidden states carry correctness information about the sequence as a whole. The final answer is often identifiable before decoding ends ( Li et al., 2026 ) , correct intermediate answers are sometimes overwritten ( Wang et al., 2026 ) , and hidden states predict whether the output will be correct ( Chiu and Liu, 2026 ; Zhao et al., 2026 ) . It remains open whether this information pinpoints which tokens are wrong, and whether locating an error is enough to repair it.

We answer both questions on Sudoku, where the unique solution labels every cell, and find two gaps. Error information is present at the level of individual cells, but confidence does not expose it, as a linear probe on hidden states separates wrong cells from correct ones better than confidence does (AUROC 0.838 versus 0.685). Locating an error, in turn, does not repair it. Re-predicting exactly the wrong cells fixes only 6 of 77 failures because the retained context keeps supporting the mistake, and reopening part of the preceding reasoning raises this only to 13. Repair therefore requires revising the context together with the error.

Guided by this diagnosis, we propose DiVR ( Di ffusion V erifier for R egeneration). 1 1 Code: https://github.com/minhaeoh/DiVR DiVR shares the generator’s backbone, scores the partially masked canvas after each block, and regenerates the whole block when the score is low, keeping the best-scoring alternative or the original. It is trained only on the generator’s own decodes labelled by final-answer correctness, so it needs answer checks but no token-level labels ( Cobbe et al., 2021 ; Yu et al., 2024 ) . Our contributions are as follows.

- • A diagnosis on Sudoku showing that MDLM errors are readable from hidden states but persist when only the erroneous tokens are re-predicted (Section 2 ).

- • DiVR, an outcome-supervised verifier that shares the generator’s weights and decides when to regenerate a block and which alternative to keep (Section 3 ).

- • Experiments on five math and code benchmarks and three generators, in which DiVR outperforms the search baselines SR-SMC and Prism at a fraction of their cost, transfers to benchmarks and generators it was not trained on, and adds to supervised and reinforcement-learning post-training. Most of its repairs begin in the first block of a solution, as the diagnosis predicts (Section 4 ).


### 2 Diagnosis: Readable Errors, Persistent Mistakes

We ask whether an MDLM’s errors are represented in its hidden states, whether its confidence exposes them, and whether reopening an erroneous position repairs it. Sudoku answers these questions with token-level ground truth, because every cell of a uniquely solvable puzzle has exactly one correct digit.


##### Setup.

We study LLaDA-8B-Instruct after one epoch of supervised training on Sudoku propagation traces, on the 190 uniquely solvable $4{\times}4$ evaluation puzzles of d1 ( Zhao et al., 2025 ) , with a 128-token canvas, 128 steps and temperature zero. We analyze two decoders. Commit-once fills masked positions in confidence order and never changes them, and confidence-based remasking may mask filled positions again, so that the model can revise a committed mistake. Linear probes on answer-position hidden states are compared with confidence, scored as $1-p_{\mathrm{top1}}$ , on the same observations (Appendix A ).


##### Finding 1. Representations expose errors more reliably than confidence.

On remasking trajectories, the final-layer probe separates wrong from correct cells with AUROC 0.838 at the final state, against 0.685 for confidence (Figure 1 a). The information is already present when a digit is first chosen. At the forward pass that first fills a cell, while the cell is still masked, the probe reaches 0.866 against 0.732. Commit-once trajectories show the same ordering, and the probe also ranks the wrong cells of a puzzle above its correct ones, so it locates errors rather than only recognizing difficult puzzles.

> Figure 1: Diagnosis on uniquely solvable $4\times 4$ Sudoku. (a) Wrongness AUROC of hidden-state probes and confidence on matched observations. (b) After re-masking a wrong cell, the original digit stays highly probable and the gold digit rarely outranks it. (c) Gold-guided repair on 77 failures: reopening the wrong cells alone fixes few, and the per-puzzle best setting is an oracle upper bound.


##### Finding 2. Locating an error does not guarantee repair under its context.

We mask one committed wrong cell, keep the rest of the canvas, and let the model predict it again. The original wrong digit keeps a mean probability of 0.869, rising to 0.962 when the cell is masked sixteen steps after its commit, and the gold digit rarely outranks it (Figure 1 b). On 77 failed puzzles, a gold oracle that reopens every wrong answer cell repairs only 6 (Figure 1 c). Reopening the reasoning written after the first error as well helps, but the best fixed rollback depth repairs 13, and even the best depth chosen for each puzzle with the gold answer repairs 15.


##### Implication.

Error information can be read from the model’s states, but re-predicting the erroneous tokens under their context rarely repairs them. Instead of locating an error and fixing it in place, a method should regenerate a region jointly and judge the alternatives by their eventual outcome, which is what DiVR does.


### 3 Method


#### 3.1 Learning an outcome criterion on masked states

The diagnosis motivates two design choices. The criterion for revision should be learned rather than read from token confidence, and the unit of revision should be a region regenerated jointly rather than an isolated token. DiVR implements both with an outcome-supervised verifier and block re-decoding, as Figure 2 illustrates.

Let $q$ be a prompt, $L$ the response-canvas length, and $B$ the block length. For a sampled response $y_{i}$ , the state after block $b$ (numbered from one) is

[Tableau] $$s_{i,b}=[q;\,y_{i,1:bB};\,\mathrm{MASK}^{L-bB}].$$ (1)

We label every block-boundary state of a sampled response with the binary outcome $r_{i}$ of the completed response. Following outcome-supervised verification ( Cobbe et al., 2021 ) , this needs only an answer check on the finished response, not gold reasoning steps or token-level labels. A negative label says that the trajectory through a state failed, not that the state itself is wrong. The training states are block prefixes with masked suffixes, exactly the states that decoding visits, and they come from the generator’s own samples, so the verifier learns the error modes of the generator it guides.

> Figure 2: DiVR-guided block re-decoding. DiVR scores the canvas, masked suffix included, after each block ( $B=4$ here, 32 in the experiments). A score below $\theta$ triggers four alternatives of the block from the same prefix, and the best-scoring state ( $s_{1}$ , 0.91) replaces the original. Text and scores are illustrative.


#### 3.2 A verifier that shares the generator’s weights

The verifier runs the generator’s backbone on a state and maps the pooled final-layer states to a score with a small head. Its own parameters $\phi$ are this head and a low-rank adapter (LoRA) on the backbone’s attention and feed-forward projections ( Hu et al., 2022 ) , trained together as one model, while the backbone weights stay frozen and shared with the generator. For a state $s$ , the verifier computes

[Tableau] $\displaystyle h_{\phi}(s)$ $\displaystyle=f_{W+\Delta W_{\phi}}(s),$ (2) $\displaystyle z_{\phi}(s)$ $\displaystyle=\operatorname{MLP}_{\phi}\!\left(\frac{1}{L}\sum_{t=1}^{L}h_{\phi}(s)_{t}\right),$ $\displaystyle v_{\phi}(s)$ $\displaystyle=\sigma(z_{\phi}(s)),$

where $f_{W}$ is the generator’s backbone with frozen weights $W$ , $\Delta W_{\phi}$ is the adapter’s low-rank update, and $h_{\phi}(s)_{t}$ is the final-layer state at response position $t$ . Pooling averages all $L$ response positions, including those still masked, and excludes the prompt. The head $\operatorname{MLP}_{\phi}$ is a two-layer perceptron, and $\sigma$ is the logistic function. Training the adapter and the head together on the outcome objective lets the verifier reshape the backbone’s representation of a state so that its information about the outcome is easier to use. The verifier’s trainable parameters amount to about 1% of the backbone.


#### 3.3 Classification and within-question ranking

Each training step draws one question and one block index $b$ and forms the state $s_{i,b}$ of each of its four sampled trajectories, using the final boundary of a trajectory that ended earlier. The loss has one term for each decision of Section 3.4 . A class-balanced binary cross-entropy $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}}$ trains each score to predict its trajectory’s outcome, and a pairwise logistic loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rank}}$ trains correct trajectories to score above incorrect ones. With $z_{i}=z_{\phi}(s_{i,b})$ , we minimize

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}}$ $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i}w_{r_{i}}\,\ell_{\mathrm{BCE}}(z_{i},r_{i}),$ (3) $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rank}}$ $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{P}}\log(1+e^{z_{j}-z_{i}}),$ (4) $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}$ $\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rank}},$ (5)

where $w_{1}=1/(2\pi)$ and $w_{0}=1/[2(1-\pi)]$ give correct and incorrect trajectories equal total weight, $\pi$ is the fraction of correct trajectories in the sampled pool, and $\mathcal{P}$ contains the correct–incorrect pairs of the question. The classification term places the scores on an absolute scale, on which $\theta=1/2$ asks whether a state is less promising than an average sampled trajectory. The ranking term trains the within-question ordering that selecting among alternatives requires.


#### 3.4 Selection and block re-decoding

For best-of- $k$ , the generator samples $k$ complete responses, and we return the one whose final state scores highest. For guided decoding, the generator fills each block in its ordinary way and the verifier scores the canvas. If the score is below $\theta$ and the regeneration budget is not exhausted, we remask the whole block, sample $K$ alternatives from the same prefix, and keep the original unless an alternative scores higher. Decoding then continues, and every regeneration event counts against the budget. Because the whole block is resampled, its tokens change together, as the diagnosis requires, and the procedure needs neither to locate the error nor to know what caused it.

#### 4.1 Experimental setup and baselines


##### Models and benchmarks.

We evaluate three models, LLaDA-8B-Base and LLaDA-8B-Instruct ( Nie et al., 2025 ) and Dream-v0-Instruct-7B ( Ye et al., 2025b ) . LLaDA-8B-Base is evaluated both untuned (Base) and after supervised fine-tuning on GSM8K (SFT). The instruction-tuned models are used as released, and Appendix Table 6 reports them after the same fine-tuning. We use three math benchmarks, GSM8K (1,319 problems) ( Cobbe et al., 2021 ) , MATH-500 (500) ( Hendrycks et al., 2021 ; Lightman et al., 2024 ) and Countdown (500 three-number tasks) ( Pan et al., 2025 ) , and two code benchmarks, HumanEval (164) ( Chen et al., 2021 ) and MBPP (500) ( Austin et al., 2021 ) , and report their unweighted average. The prompts are listed in Appendix G . Each generator has one verifier per domain, trained on four of its own responses, sampled at temperature 0.7, for each of 2,000 GSM8K training questions for math and for each MBPP training and validation problem for code. A verifier serves every benchmark of its domain, and the untuned Base model is guided by the SFT generator’s verifiers. The epoch and the threshold $\theta$ are chosen on development data, and the remaining hyperparameters are given in Appendix B .


##### Decoding and baselines.

Canvases contain 256 tokens, or 512 for MATH-500, decoded in 32-token blocks with 16 steps per block. Guided decoding uses $\theta=0.5$ and $K=4$ alternatives at temperature 0.7, with at most three regeneration events per problem. Cost counts forward passes, verifier reads included, relative to a greedy decode of the same problems, which is also the reference for accuracy. We compare against two search methods for diffusion decoding, reimplemented on the same generators and decoder (Appendix C ). SR-SMC ( Luo et al., 2026 ) keeps four particles and resamples them after each block by their accumulated log-confidence, and Prism ( Bai et al., 2026 ) starts sixteen trajectories, prunes them to four with the generator’s own prompted Yes/No judgment, and answers by majority vote, as in its paper (Appendix Table 5 reports its released top-score rule). Both search with the generator’s own judgment and keep several trajectories for every problem, whereas guided decoding branches only at flagged blocks. DiVR best-of-4 reranks four complete samples by the verifier’s score at about the baselines’ budget, which isolates the verifier’s selection quality from block regeneration.


#### 4.2 Main results

Table 1 summarizes the results. Across the three generators, guided decoding is the most accurate method on average for every generator, and with best-of-4, which uses about the search baselines’ budget, DiVR is the most accurate on every math and code benchmark. On the SFT generator, guided decoding raises GSM8K from 68.7% to 78.8% and MBPP from 24.2% to 33.6%, and best-of-4 raises GSM8K further to 82.2%. On GSM8K with the SFT generator, guided decoding is also more accurate than the search baselines at a lower cost, reaching 78.8% at $2.1\times$ the cost of greedy decoding against 72.6% at $4.0\times$ for SR-SMC and 71.9% at $5.9\times$ for Prism. On LLaDA-8B-Instruct, guided decoding raises the five-benchmark average from 39.4% to 44.8%, and best-of-4 reaches 49.8%, 10.4 points above greedy decoding. These results show that DiVR is an effective inference-time revision method for MDLMs.


##### DiVR is cost-effective.

Over the five benchmarks of the SFT generator, guided decoding raises the average from 35.3% to 43.4% at $1.9$ – $3.5\times$ the cost of greedy decoding, whereas SR-SMC reaches 39.1% at $3.8$ – $4.0\times$ and Prism 37.3% at $5.2$ – $7.4\times$ . On average, guided decoding thus uses about 70% of SR-SMC’s computation and less than half of Prism’s. Best-of-4 with the same verifier is the most accurate row on most benchmarks, and guided decoding comes close to it at as little as 40% of its cost. Guided decoding retains most of the gain of best-of-4 because it samples alternatives only for the blocks the verifier flags, which Table 2 shows to be concentrated at the start of a solution. The gap between the two DiVR rows is small next to the gap between either of them and the baselines, so the benefit comes from the verifier’s judgment rather than from the amount of sampling.


##### DiVR complements post-training.

We test whether DiVR adds to post-training of the generator on two post-trained variants, supervised fine-tuning of LLaDA-8B-Base and diffu-GRPO reinforcement learning of LLaDA-8B-Instruct, the model for which d1 published its recipe ( Zhao et al., 2025 ) , trained at about one eighth of d1’s GSM8K budget. DiVR improves both. Fine-tuning raises Countdown from 10.6% to 35.6%, and guided decoding raises it further to 47.2%. For the RL generator, guided decoding raises the five-benchmark average from 40.1% to 45.4%. RL training moved the Instruct run’s reward, with its correctness term rising from 0.76 to 1.35 of a possible 2, but greedy accuracy moved little (the rows marked $\ddagger$ ). With guidance, the RL generator gains 6.0 points on GSM8K and 11.2 on Countdown over its own greedy decoding, more than the verifier adds to Instruct itself (3.8 and 7.4). RL changes what the generator writes, the verifier decides when a block is discarded, and the two gains add. Appendix Table 7 gives the training details.


##### DiVR generalizes across benchmarks and generators.

Each verifier is trained on a single benchmark per domain, GSM8K for math and MBPP for code, yet it also improves benchmarks it never saw. The GSM8K-trained verifier raises the SFT generator on MATH-500 from 24.6% to 33.8% and on Countdown from 35.6% to 47.2%, and the MBPP-trained verifier raises HumanEval, which has no training split, for LLaDA-8B-Instruct and Dream (34.1% to 39.0% and 45.1% to 53.0%) (Appendix Table 8 compares the math and code verifiers on code). The verifiers also transfer across generators. Without any training on the untuned Base model’s decodes, the SFT generator’s verifiers raise its average from 29.3% to 38.0%, above the 35.3% of fine-tuning alone. Trained on its own decodes, the same recipe raises Dream-v0-Instruct-7B, a second model family with a different tokenizer, from 45.0% to 54.8%. The gains are largest where the generator’s own signals are least reliable, as on Countdown, where Dream’s accuracy doubles from 18.0% to 37.2%.

To see where DiVR triggers the regenerations that repair an answer, we locate the repairs on the three math benchmarks. For every problem that greedy decoding gets wrong and guided decoding gets right, we record the block at which an alternative first replaced the original, which is where the guided trajectory departs from the greedy one. As Table 2 shows, the repairs are concentrated at the start of the solution. On GSM8K, 74% of the 154 repaired problems depart in the first block and 92% within the first two. On Countdown, 58 of the 59 repairs start in the first block, because the generator writes its whole candidate expression there and the next block only copies it into the answer. Appendix F shows three such repairs.

This concentration has two sources, and the table separates them. The first is detection. Among greedy decodes, the first-block score falls below $\theta$ for 76% of the eventually wrong GSM8K solutions and 22% of the eventually right ones, for 99% and 1% on Countdown, and for 50% and 14% on MATH-500. One block is therefore enough for the verifier to separate most trajectories by their eventual outcome. The second source is repairability, which the diagnosis predicted. The first block fixes the interpretation of the problem, such as which quantity is asked for or how the equation is set up, and later tokens are predicted from that context and tend to confirm it. A fresh sample of the first block starts from the prompt alone and can take a different route, whereas a fresh sample of a later block inherits the mistaken setup.

##### Controlling the evaluation signal.

The main results combine a learned score with a regeneration procedure, so we first ask whether the improvement comes from the verifier or from the opportunity to regenerate. On GSM8K with the SFT generator, we hold the regeneration procedure and its budget fixed and replace only the signal that decides when to regenerate and which block to keep. As shown in Table 3 , three signals serve as controls. Commitment confidence is the geometric mean of the probabilities with which the visible tokens were placed, the signal on which confidence-based remasking relies. Prompted Yes/No asks the generator itself, through a judgment prompt, whether the partial solution is correct, the signal that Prism uses to prune its search. A head-only verifier (V0) is DiVR without its adapter, so its head is trained with the same outcome labels on the generator’s unchanged hidden states. Each signal uses the threshold that maximizes its accuracy on the development split.

The ordering of the signals mirrors the diagnosis. Confidence and self-judgment reach 72.2% and 67.9%, so regenerating on the generator’s own signals recovers a small part of the gain over the 68.7% of greedy decoding or none of it. The head-only verifier reaches 74.2%, so an outcome-trained score helps even on the generator’s unchanged states. The full verifier reaches 78.8% at the lowest cost, $2.10\times$ against $2.45$ – $3.62\times$ , which is consistent with a sharper signal spending fewer regeneration events on blocks that were already adequate. The improvement therefore comes from the verifier’s learned criterion. The gap between the head-only verifier and DiVR also shows that reshaping the representation with the adapter, so that the outcome information is easier to use, matters.

##### Selection and ranking on fixed candidates.

To separate the verifier’s ranking quality from the regeneration procedure, Table 4 compares selectors on the same four sampled responses per problem, so that every selector chooses from an identical pool. DiVR exceeds majority voting on GSM8K (82.2% against 75.7%), MATH-500 (34.2% against 28.8%) and Countdown (49.2% against 38.2%). Mean log-probability reaches only 68.5%, 23.6% and 34.6%, close to picking a candidate uniformly at random (68.7%, 24.6% and 33.7%), so the generator’s likelihood carries almost no information about which of its own samples is correct. On Countdown, where voting groups equivalent expressions, DiVR approaches the pool’s oracle ceiling of 49.8%, which means that it recognizes a correct expression whenever the pool contains one.

Guiding decoding requires a score that ranks partial solutions, not only finished ones. On the same candidates, DiVR’s pair accuracy on GSM8K is already 0.705 after the first block and rises to 0.919 for the finished responses, and on Countdown it reaches 0.957 after the first block (Table 4 ). Useful discrimination thus appears after the first block, where most repairs begin (Table 2 ).


##### Sensitivity to the threshold.

The threshold $\theta=0.5$ was chosen on the GSM8K development split, and Figure 3 varies it on the test sets with everything else fixed. On GSM8K, accuracy stays within 77.5–79.2% for every $\theta$ from 0.1 to 0.9 while the cost rises from $1.79\times$ to $2.61\times$ , so the threshold is primarily a cost control. On MATH-500, which lies outside the domain the threshold was chosen on, accuracy nonetheless peaks at the development-chosen $\theta=0.5$ . A low threshold misses errors, and a high threshold regenerates correct work more often. The threshold chosen on in-domain development data is thus also the best setting out of domain. Appendix E evaluates the scores as probabilities of success and the regenerate-or-continue decision at each threshold on paired states.


### 6 Related Work


##### Refinement and search in MDLMs.

MDLMs can revise intermediate states during iterative unmasking ( Nie et al., 2025 ; Ye et al., 2025b ) . ReMDM remasks generated tokens to spend additional inference computation on refinement ( Wang et al., 2025 ) , and PRISM learns token-quality estimates to choose which positions to remask ( Kim et al., 2026 ) . Search methods weight and resample trajectories by their accumulated log-probabilities ( Luo et al., 2026 ) or prune them with prompted self-verification ( Bai et al., 2026 ) . Others bring an external process reward model into diffusion decoding to remask low-scoring blocks ( Mounier and Idehpour, 2025 ) , resample particles ( Dang et al., 2026 ) , guide a tree search over unmasking actions ( Misaki and Akiba, 2026 ) or stitch high-scoring steps across traces ( Miles et al., 2026 ) . Reinforcement learning with outcome or denoising-process rewards also improves reasoning in these models ( Zhao et al., 2025 ; Xie et al., 2026 ) , and Section 4 shows that DiVR’s gains add to d1’s recipe. DiVR differs from these methods in its signal, an outcome criterion learned on the generator’s own partially masked states.


##### Error signals in MDLMs.

In autoregressive LLMs, hidden states encode the correctness of outputs beyond what output probabilities reveal ( Orgad et al., 2025 ) . For MDLMs, corrective post-training supervises incorrect tokens directly so that confidence-guided refinement targets them ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) , linear probes on hidden states predict whether the final output will be correct ( Chiu and Liu, 2026 ) , and LOCKR detects answers that settle early on a wrong value from hidden-state trajectories and repairs them by remasking an answer region ( Zhao et al., 2026 ) . Concurrently, Zhan et al. (2026) train a verifier of a similar form, a LoRA-adapted MDLM backbone with an MLP head, and use it to prune candidates at every scoring step. Our work differs in three respects. Our diagnosis shows that a correctly located error is rarely repaired while its context is kept, which motivates regenerating whole blocks rather than answer tokens. Our verifier triggers regeneration only below a threshold and keeps the original block unless an alternative scores higher. Finally, we evaluate one procedure across three generators and five math and code benchmarks against confidence, self-verification and search baselines.


##### Outcome supervision and verifier-guided reasoning.

Cobbe et al. (2021) train verifiers on sampled solutions labelled by final-answer correctness and use them to select among candidates, and process supervision labels individual steps instead ( Lightman et al., 2024 ) . Outcome labels also support evaluating partial solutions, through values learned for step-level planning ( Yu et al., 2024 ) or process rewards derived from outcome supervision ( Yuan et al., 2025 ) , and verifiers can steer generation before a solution is complete ( Liao et al., 2025 ) . For masked diffusion outside text, MDM-VGB regresses a verifier of masked states onto the terminal rewards of the model’s own rollouts to guide unmasking and remasking ( Jeon et al., 2026 ) . DiVR brings such a state evaluator to partially masked text, trained on the generator’s own decodes instead of a reward model trained on complete solutions, and uses it to decide block regeneration.


### 7 Conclusion

Our diagnosis shows that an MDLM can carry information about its errors and still fail to repair them in place, because the surrounding context keeps reproducing the mistake. DiVR therefore decides when a block should be written again, using a verifier learned from the generator’s own outcomes. This single decision, made after each block, improves three generators on five math and code benchmarks, outperforms search at a fraction of its cost, and adds to supervised and reinforcement-learning post-training. Revision in MDLMs needs a learned criterion of when to start over more than a way to find the error.

### Appendix A Diagnosis Details

All Sudoku results in the main text use the 190 uniquely solvable puzzles from a fixed 256-puzzle sample of the d1 $4{\times}4$ test set ( Zhao et al., 2025 ) . Overall solve rates, probe datasets, localization subsets, and repair-eligible failures have different denominators. The final-state probe analyses contain 1,416 eligible cells for remasking and 1,279 for commit-once, distinct from the 190-puzzle solve-rate denominator. Single-layer probes use standardization, PCA, and class-balanced logistic regression with five-fold cross-validation grouped by puzzle. Bootstrap intervals resample puzzles rather than cells, because repeated cell observations from one puzzle are not independent. The reported bootstrap intervals condition on fitted out-of-fold probe predictions, and they do not rerun probe training in each resample. For the remasking probe they are $[0.793,0.881]$ at the final state and $[0.836,0.892]$ at first commit, from 500 resamples. Probes read answer-position hidden states at layers 8, 16, 24 and 32 at every step, excluding given clues and echoed blank digits, and reported AUROCs pool out-of-fold cell scores. The within-puzzle detector of Finding 1 is trained with puzzle-grouped folds on decoding states from the first quarter of decoding onward and is distinct from the single-layer probes. On the 73 remasking final states that contain both correct and incorrect eligible cells, it reaches a mean within-puzzle AUROC of 0.817, against 0.489 for confidence, and its median number of correct cells ranked before the last erroneous cell is one, against three for confidence. The gold-guided repair of Finding 2 uses the 77 of the 84 unsolved remasking outputs that have eligible erroneous answer cells. Rollback depth $d\in\{0,8,16,32,64\}$ additionally reopens the non-answer tokens first committed at or after step $\max(0,\tau-d)$ , where $\tau$ is the earliest first-commit step among the final wrong cells. The one-cell re-prediction summary of Finding 2 pools 1,292 wrong-commit observations from three balanced rounds at offset zero, not 1,292 independent puzzles, and observation counts vary across offsets, so the comparison across offsets is descriptive rather than a paired estimate of how each error evolves.


### Appendix B Verifier Training and Evaluation


##### Verification and generation.

The primary verifier uses four samples for each of 2,000 GSM8K training questions, decoded with the canvas, block length and step schedule of the main experiments. Every verifier uses a rank-32 adapter with scaling $\alpha=64$ on the attention and feed-forward projections, and its head maps the hidden dimension through 1,024 SiLU units to a single logit. Training runs for two epochs with gradients accumulated over four questions. It pools the full response canvas, including unresolved masks. An already-terminated sample contributes its final boundary when a later training index is drawn. For the SFT generator, the SFT adapter is merged into the backbone before the verifier adapter is added, so that disabling the verifier adapter leaves exactly the SFT generator. Pair accuracy averages correct–incorrect ranking accuracy within each problem, then across problems with both outcomes, and ties receive half credit. After block $b$ , a candidate that has already terminated contributes its final state, and 488 of the 1,319 GSM8K test problems have both outcomes among their four candidates. For each guided run, the paired greedy baseline uses the same process and decoding configuration. Cost counts forward passes weighted by batch rows.


##### Seed sensitivity.

The primary GSM8K run fixes 154 failures and breaks 20 correct answers. Changing only the verifier-training seed gives 77.5% success, and changing only the re-decoding seed gives 77.6%, against 78.8% in the primary run and 68.7% for paired greedy decoding. These are separate sensitivity checks rather than independent end-to-end replications.


##### Evaluation subsets.

MATH-500 is the 500-problem subset of MATH used in process-verification research ( Hendrycks et al., 2021 ; Lightman et al., 2024 ) , and the implementation loads its public dataset release. For Countdown, the sampler filters three-number tasks from the training partition of the public Countdown-Tasks-3to4 collection ( Pan et al., 2025 ) and selects 500 for evaluation.


##### Code evaluation.

The code results use all 500 MBPP test tasks and 164 HumanEval tasks. MBPP prompts expose the same tests used by its grader. HumanEval runs its test harness on the prompt and extracted program. Programs have a 10-second timeout and a 2-GiB address-space limit, and extraction or execution failures count as incorrect.


##### Regeneration locations.

The repair block of Table 2 is the first block at which the guided decode’s recorded score departs from the paired greedy decode’s, which identifies the first accepted alternative exactly.

##### diffu-GRPO from the untuned Base model.

The Base RL generator applies the same recipe, seed and step count to the untuned LLaDA-8B-Base with two changes that its cold start requires.

Prompt prefix. The prompt ends with the <reasoning> prefix that every Base decode in this paper uses (Appendix G ), passed to d1’s trainer as an opening assistant turn so that the rendered prompt is identical to ours. Without the prefix the untuned model does not produce d1’s answer tags and receives no correctness reward.

Correctness reward. The correctness reward is our numeric grader (the first <answer> block, else a #### line, else the last boxed expression, else the last number, compared numerically) in place of d1’s exact string match, with d1’s four format rewards unchanged. The untuned model writes sentences inside the answer tags, so the exact match rewards 4% of its samples where our grader scores 53% right, and groups of six generations then carry almost no reward variance for the policy gradient to use.

Training. Training took 52 GPU-hours on the same four GPUs, its correctness reward rose from 1.03 to 1.19 of 2, and its completions fill the 256-token canvas because the Base model writes no end token, as in every untuned-Base decode of the paper. The Instruct run keeps d1’s reward.

Results. The Base RL generator’s greedy accuracy stays at the untuned level, 29.7% against 29.3% averaged over the five benchmarks and 61.7% against 61.8% on GSM8K (199 problems gained and 200 lost), with $+5.0$ on Countdown ( $p=0.022$ ), $+4.3$ on HumanEval ( $p=0.09$ ), $-2.6$ on MATH-500 and $-4.2$ on MBPP ( $p=0.022$ ), and 5.6 points below one GPU-hour of GSM8K SFT on average (Table 7 ).

Verifiers. Its verifiers repeat the recipes of this appendix on its own decodes (65.1% of the GSM8K samples correct, held-out pair accuracy 0.938 and 0.949, final-state pair accuracy 0.927 on the test pools against 0.919 for the SFT generator’s verifier, and for MBPP 24.4% passing with 0.776 and 0.908).

Guidance. With guidance it gains 14.5 points on GSM8K, 16.0 on Countdown, 4.8 on MATH-500 ( $p=0.015$ ), 9.6 on MBPP and 3.0 on HumanEval ( $p=0.06$ ), its GSM8K guidance is 4.5 points above the SFT verifier’s guidance of the untuned generator ( $p=0.0005$ ), and best-of-4 reaches 82.7% on GSM8K, the level of the SFT generator with its verifier (82.2%). The lenient reward function is included with our code.

### Appendix E Calibration and Paired Intervention Analysis

We evaluate the probability of final-answer success under the continuation policy that generated each trajectory. Every observed block boundary inherits that trajectory’s outcome. Sampled-policy evaluation uses 20,744 states from four responses per GSM8K test problem (5,276 responses in total, temperature 0.7). Greedy-policy evaluation uses 1,209 states from a 300-problem test subset. Commitment confidence, prompted Yes/No, and DiVR are evaluated on the same greedy states with the same outcome labels.

For each policy and signal separately, we fit positive-slope affine-logit Platt scaling ( Platt, 1999 ) by unweighted NLL on development states, with 7,671 states for the sampled-policy verifier and 957 for each greedy-policy signal. Fitted parameters are frozen for test evaluation. ECE uses ten equal-width bins, and Brier score and NLL retain the natural class ratio. We report state-weighted metrics, so longer trajectories contribute more observations. Final states form a separate group, and block-index groups contain only non-final states. No terminated trajectory is padded into later groups, unlike the pair-accuracy analysis in Section 5 .

##### Decision calibration.

Zhao et al. (2021) call predictions decision-calibrated for a family of decision rules if, for every rule, the loss the predictions anticipate for the chosen action equals the realized loss. Their predictions concern an outcome that the decision does not change. Here each action leads to a different state, so we predict the outcome of each action separately and require the prediction for the chosen action to match its realized outcome. We consider rules that regenerate when the raw score is below $\theta$ .

- • Predicted success of an action. We apply the greedy-policy Platt map, fitted on development states (Table 9 ), to the raw score of the state the action leads to, which is the current block when continuing and the retained block when regenerating.

- • Realized success. The outcome of the greedy continuation after that action.

Table 10 compares the two on the 436 paired states.

- • Chosen action. Predicted and realized success differ by at most 2.4 points at every threshold.

- • Regeneration gain. The predicted gain matches the realized gain. At $\theta=0.5$ the difference is 0.8 points, with interval $[-4.1,5.8]$ .

- • Selection. Choosing the best of five candidates by score adds no optimism beyond a shared 2-point offset. After regenerating everywhere, predicted success is 72.1% versus 70.2% realized, and when continuing everywhere it is 67.8% versus 65.8%. The offset reflects the development-fitted map on this test subset, not selection.

All intervals are 95% problem-level bootstrap intervals. The test covers one regeneration event per state, with candidates selected by raw score. It does not cover the sequential three-event policy or per-state repair probabilities.

### Appendix G Prompt Templates

Every prompt is a single user turn in the generator’s own chat template, with the generation prompt appended. A fixed response prefix follows it and belongs to the prompt, not to the decoded canvas. Line breaks are as used, and long lines are wrapped for display ( $\hookrightarrow$ ). Braces mark inserted fields. LLaDA-8B-Base, LLaDA-8B-Instruct and their SFT adapters share one template. Dream-v0-Instruct-7B uses its ChatML template with the default system message, as listed below.

The verifier adds no prompt of its own. It reads the same prompt followed by the current canvas. SR-SMC also uses only the generation prompt.


##### GSM8K and MATH-500.

The d1 instruction ( Zhao et al., 2025 ) precedes the question, and the prefix is <reasoning> . The same content is used for the verifiers’ training decodes.


##### Countdown.

The d1 Countdown instruction, verbatim, including its missing space before “Respond”. The question lists the three numbers and the target. The prefix is <reasoning> .


##### HumanEval and MBPP.

The prefix opens a Python code fence followed by a newline. The program is the response up to the closing fence. HumanEval appends its prompt field, the function signature and docstring. MBPP lists the task text and its test assertions.


##### Sudoku diagnosis.

The d1 $4{\times}4$ instruction, verbatim, which begins with a newline. The trace SFT used the same prompt. The prefix is <reasoning> .


##### Prompted Yes/No and Prism’s verifier.

Both use the generator itself as judge.

- • Fields. The judge prompt is filled with the question and the solution decoded so far. A solution longer than 2,000 characters keeps its first 500 and last 1,500 characters around a ...[truncated]... marker.

- • Readout. The prompt is wrapped in the chat template and one mask token is appended. The score is the two-way softmax of the largest Yes-variant logit ( Yes , Yes , YES ) against the largest No-variant logit, both read at that mask position.

- • Prompts. Mathematical tasks use Prism’s judgment prompt. Code tasks use our shorter reviewer adaptation, the second listing.
