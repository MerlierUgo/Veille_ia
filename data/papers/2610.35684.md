## O m n i S h o w c a s e : Omnidirectional Subject-Consistent Video Generation from Multi-View Images

Generating videos of a specific subject from a few reference images remains challenging when the camera reveals surfaces that are not visible in the input. Early subject-to-video (S2V) methods condition on a single reference image and must therefore infer the subject’s unseen appearance and geometry as the viewpoint changes. To address this limitation, recent methods extend S2V to multiple reference views, providing more information about the subject from different viewpoints. However, they do not explicitly specify the 3D structure that connects these observations. In addition, existing training data do not guarantee that viewpoint changes preserve the same underlying 3D subject. We present OmniShowcase, built on two components: (i) a fully automated data synthesis pipeline that produces training videos along diverse camera trajectories, with viewpoint variation driven by underlying subject geometry, even spanning full 360-degree orbits; and (ii) a geometry-conditioning method that provides the video model with a turntable of surface-normal maps reconstructed from the reference views without requiring temporal alignment with the generated frames. We construct Showcase-4K , a corpus of 3D-asset-based videos covering twelve camera motions across $88$ trajectory configurations with reconstruction-derived surface-normal conditioning, and ShowcaseBench , a held-out evaluation set with the source meshes and camera parameters used to render each video. Experiments show state-of-the-art subject identity and 3D consistency while maintaining competitive video quality and motion. Project page: justin4ai.github.io/OmniShowcase

### 1 Introduction

Subject-to-video (S2V) generation aims to create videos of a specified subject from reference images while preserving its appearance across time and viewpoints. Recent video diffusion models generate photorealistic videos from text or a single image ( Yang et al., 2025 ; Kong et al., 2024 ; Wan Team, 2025 ; Brooks et al., 2024 ) , and S2V methods use reference images to preserve subject identity in generated videos ( Liu et al., 2025 ; Deng et al., 2026 ; Jiang et al., 2025 ; Yuan et al., 2025 ) . However, most S2V methods condition on a single reference image, which provides appearance information only for the visible surfaces and leaves other regions unconstrained. As the camera reveals previously unseen sides, generated videos may exhibit inconsistent geometry and appearance, which can lead to changes in silhouette, structure, and surface details. These ambiguities motivate the use of multiple reference views and explicit geometric information. The main difficulty is geometric rather than purely photometric since the model must infer 3D structure that is absent from the input.

Recent methods incorporate multiple reference views into S2V generation. MV-S2V ( Song et al., 2026 ) trains a feed-forward model using generated multi-view videos, whereas 3DreamBooth ( Ko et al., 2026 ) performs per-subject optimization using static multi-view images. These efforts motivate two complementary directions: training on geometrically consistent viewpoint changes and providing explicit 3D geometry during generation. For the latter, recent feed-forward reconstruction methods can recover 3D geometry from a few uncalibrated images ( Wang et al., 2024a ; Wang et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2026 ; Lin et al., 2026 ) , making explicit geometry available from the same sparse references used at inference.

We present OmniShowcase (Figure ), a framework that combines an automated data synthesis pipeline with geometry-aware conditioning. The pipeline renders multi-view videos from 3D assets and composites them into realistic scenes. For conditioning, we reconstruct geometry from the reference views and render a surface-normal sequence using the same procedure during training and inference. Our contributions are as follows:

- • We introduce an automated data synthesis pipeline that renders subject views from 3D assets and composites them into realistic scenes to construct training videos.

- • We present Showcase-4K , a dataset of 4,000 videos paired with diverse source meshes, camera trajectories, reference images, and surface-normal sequences, along with ShowcaseBench , a held-out benchmark with ground-truth geometry and camera parameters for evaluating subject consistency across viewpoints. We will publicly release Showcase-4K , ShowcaseBench , and the associated data-synthesis code.

- • We propose a geometry-aware in-context conditioning mechanism that uses reference images and a reconstructed surface-normal sequence to guide video generation without requiring frame-wise alignment between the normal and target sequences.

- • We evaluate our method against single- and multi-view S2V baselines on synthetic and real-world subjects, achieving state-of-the-art performance in subject identity preservation and 3D consistency while maintaining competitive video quality.


### 2 Related Work


#### 2.1 Video Generation Diffusion Models

Diffusion models ( Ho et al., 2020 ; Rombach et al., 2022 ) are the dominant approach to visual content synthesis, and the shift from convolutional U-Nets to transformer denoisers ( Peebles and Xie, 2023 ) with rectified-flow training ( Esser et al., 2024 ) improved their scalability and sample quality. These advances carried over to video. Early video models extended pretrained image backbones with temporal layers ( Singer et al., 2023 ; Blattmann et al., 2023 ; Guo et al., 2024 ) ; more recent systems use large diffusion transformers trained on web-scale corpora and generate high-resolution clips of steadily increasing length ( Yang et al., 2025 ; Kong et al., 2024 ; Wan Team, 2025 ; Brooks et al., 2024 ) . Image-to-video (I2V) variants add a starting frame as a condition, giving coarse control over appearance. Our framework builds on a pretrained I2V diffusion transformer and adapts it through low-rank tuning ( Hu et al., 2022 ) .


#### 2.2 3D Generation and Novel View Synthesis

Representing and rendering 3D content from images has been studied through neural fields ( Mildenhall et al., 2020 ) and point-based splatting ( Kerbl et al., 2023 ) . To synthesize unseen viewpoints without per-scene reconstruction, one line of work distills 2D diffusion priors into multi-view-consistent generators: image-conditioned models such as Liu et al. (2023) hallucinate novel views of an object, and multi-view diffusion models ( Shi et al., 2024 ; Liu et al., 2024b ; Voleti et al., 2024 ) jointly denoise several views to enforce cross-view coherence. These methods are mostly confined to isolated objects against clean backgrounds and do not model scene-level dynamics. In parallel, feed-forward geometry estimators build on monocular depth foundation models ( Yang et al., 2024 ) and recover dense 3D structure from sparse, uncalibrated images in a single pass ( Wang et al., 2024a ; Wang et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2026 ; Lin et al., 2026 ) . This makes it practical to derive explicit geometry from multi-view reference images. We use this capability to supplement coarse 3D information of unseen views as a geometry cue.

#### 2.3 Subject-Driven Video Generation

Personalizing generative models to a user-specified subject originates in the image domain, through identifier-based personalization ( Ruiz et al., 2023 ; Gal et al., 2023 ; Kumari et al., 2023 ) and later encoder- or adapter-based conditioning that injects reference features without test-time optimization ( Ye et al., 2023 ; Tan et al., 2025 ) . These ideas carried over to video, where subject-to-video (S2V) methods keep a reference identity stable throughout a generated clip. Optimization- and adapter-based approaches ( Jiang et al., 2024 ; Wei et al., 2024 ; He et al., 2024 ; Yuan et al., 2025 ) preserve identity over time, and reference-conditioned frameworks ( Liu et al., 2025 ; Deng et al., 2026 ; Jiang et al., 2025 ; Chen et al., 2025 ) extend this to multiple subjects and flexible prompts. These methods primarily encode the subject through 2D appearance features, leaving viewpoints not covered by the reference unconstrained.

Closest to our setting are recent attempts to move beyond single-view conditioning. MV-S2V ( Song et al., 2026 ) feeds multiple reference views into a video diffusion transformer and uses a shifted positional-encoding scheme to distinguish different views of the same subject. The references provide appearance information from several viewpoints, but do not explicitly encode the 3D surface structure underlying those views. Its training videos for object-centric generation are produced by a camera-controllable video generator, so viewpoint variation is not explicitly tied to a shared 3D asset. 3DreamBooth ( Ko et al., 2026 ) uses static multi-view images for subject-specific adaptation, preserving the pretrained model’s temporal behavior but requiring optimization for each new subject. In contrast, OmniShowcase reconstructs geometry from the input views at inference and uses it directly as a conditioning signal, without subject-specific optimization. ### 3 Method

Given a small set of reference views, we reconstruct the subject in 3D and render the reconstruction as a turntable of surface-normal maps. We provide these normal maps, together with the reference images, to a pretrained video diffusion model (Figure ).


#### 3.1 Preliminaries and Problem Setup


##### Problem setup.

A subject is given by a small set of reference views $\mathcal{R}=\{\mathbf{I}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}$ ( $N{=}4$ in our experiments), each depicting the object from a different angle. Given a text prompt, we seek to generate a video $\mathbf{V}=\{\mathbf{V}_{t}\}$ that follows the specified camera trajectory and scene while preserving the subject’s appearance and 3D structure across viewpoints. We further reconstruct an explicit 3D geometry from these views and provide it to the generator, reducing its reliance on the backbone’s priors for viewpoints that are not directly observed.


##### Backbone.

We build on a pretrained image-to-video (I2V) diffusion transformer ( Wan Team, 2025 ) and adapt it with low-rank tuning ( Hu et al., 2022 ) , keeping the base weights frozen. The model operates in the latent space of a causal video VAE and is trained with a rectified-flow objective. We denote the denoising transformer by $\mathcal{F}_{\theta}$ , where $\theta$ collects the trainable LoRA parameters.


#### 3.2 Automated 3D-Consistent Data Synthesis

> Figure 2: Automated 3D-consistent data synthesis pipeline. From a curated 3D asset ( Deitke et al., 2023 ) , Blender renders the target orbit and four reference views. (a) The target is composited into a prompt-driven scene by video inpainting ( Jiang et al., 2025 ) ; (b) the four views are reconstructed in 3D ( Li et al., 2026 ) and rendered as the surface-normal turntable used as the condition. Every stage runs without human intervention.

Our training data are designed to satisfy three requirements: subject identity preservation, geometrically consistent viewpoint changes, and explicit geometry for conditioning. A camera-controllable video generator does not guarantee these properties, because unseen surfaces are still synthesized by the generative prior. Real multi-view capture satisfies the first two but leaves the third to an estimator. Rendering from a 3D asset satisfies all three at once, because one mesh under known cameras produces every frame. However, direct rendering produces clean-background videos. We therefore add a separate scene-compositing stage to introduce realistic backgrounds corresponding to the camera motion. The pipeline used to construct Showcase-4K has two branches: a video-generation branch (Figure a) that produces the target video and a geometry-generation branch (Figure b) that produces the condition.

> Figure 3: Qualitative comparison of training data. Uni3C ( Cao et al., 2025 ) is an image-to-video model, so every surface the camera path uncovers is generated. Ours renders the subject from a 3D asset and generates only the background.

Figure makes the difference concrete. Given one view and a camera path, a camera-controllable generator resolves every surface the first frame does not show from a prior, and within a single clip the subject can change shape, leave the scene, or appear twice at once.


##### 3D-consistent reference and target views.

We source 3D meshes from Objaverse ( Deitke et al., 2023 ) and, for real-scanned subjects, from the Digital Twin Catalog ( Dong et al., 2025 ) and Google Scanned Objects ( Downs et al., 2022 ) . A heuristic pre-screen discards degenerate meshes, and a vision-language model inspects four rendered views of each remaining asset and keeps only those with valid geometry, plausible textures and category consistency (rubrics in Appendix ). We use four orbit-family motions (Orbit and Helix-Up/Down/In) with four rotation amounts each and eight additional motions—arc, truck, dolly, and zoom variants—with nine framing transitions each, yielding $88$ trajectory configurations in total (Appendix ). From the same mesh, Blender renders an $81$ -frame target video and $N{=}4$ reference views $\mathcal{R}$ at $90^{\circ}$ yaw intervals, so references and target share one underlying shape and appearance.


##### Subject-preserving compositing.

We composite the subject into a prompt-driven scene to close the domain gap from clean-background renders (Figure a). Subject masks from the render’s alpha channel drive a video inpainting model ( Jiang et al., 2025 ) that synthesizes a coherent background around the fixed subject, while keeping the rendered subject pixels unchanged. The scene prompt combines a location from a language-model-written scene bank with the sample’s camera motion and lighting, and a vision-language gate discards videos whose subject floats, deforms, or departs from the references. The target is trimmed to $80$ frames to meet the video VAE’s temporal constraint (below).


##### Geometry conditions from reference views.

Importantly, we reconstruct the geometry from the four reference views (Figure b) rather than using the source mesh. This ensures that the conditioning signal available during training matches the signal available at inference.

We mask the subject on its white background, predict aligned per-view pointmaps with a feed-forward multi-view estimator ( Lin et al., 2026 ) , and fuse the views into 3D Gaussians ( Kerbl et al., 2023 ) with a multi-view reconstruction module ( Li et al., 2026 ) . We render the Gaussians along a yaw orbit at $15^{\circ}$ elevation at $T_{c}{=}25$ uniformly spaced poses and convert each rendered depth map to surface normals. This yields a normal turntable $\{\mathbf{N}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T_{c}}$ that encodes the surface visible from each viewpoint. A vision-language gate discards reconstructions that depart from the reference or break apart.


##### Multimodal sample construction.

A video-language model (Tarsier2-7B) captions the normal turntable and target video separately. Each training sample comprises a $25$ -frame normal condition and an $80$ -frame target, together with four reference images. The normal and target segments are encoded separately before being concatenated along the temporal axis in latent space, while each reference image is encoded independently. Videos are sampled at $16$ fps from the renders. The two segments are paired with explicit [CONDITION] and [VIDEO] markers describing the normal turntable and target video, respectively.


#### 3.3 Geometry-Aware Conditioning

> Figure 4: Overview of OmniShowcase. From $N{=}4$ reference views, we reconstruct 3D geometry and render a surface-normal turntable. The reference images and normal condition are injected into the reference pathway of a LoRA-tuned image-to-video diffusion transformer. The model uses appearance information from the reference views and 3D structure from the normal condition to generate the target video without per-subject optimization.


##### Conditioning inputs.

The conditioning sequence holds the $N{=}4$ reference images followed by the $T_{c}{=}25$ -frame normal turntable, and the target segment follows as the frames to be produced. The target camera motion is specified by the text prompt, while the normal turntable provides a trajectory-independent geometry cue.

We encode the two conditions differently because they have different structures. Each reference image is encoded independently, while the normal maps form a temporal sequence and are encoded using the video VAE.


##### Geometry-aware in-context conditioning.

The sequence concatenates the reference, normal and target latents along the temporal axis and the transformer attends over it,

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{z}=\big[\,\mathbf{z}^{\mathcal{R}};\;\mathbf{z}^{\mathcal{N}};\;\mathbf{z}^{\mathcal{V}}\,\big]\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times C\times h\times w},\qquad L=N+T_{n}+T_{v}.$$ (1)

Encoding the $25$ -frame normal condition and the $80$ -frame target separately yields $T_{n}{=}7$ and $T_{v}{=}20$ latent frames, respectively. Together with the $N{=}4$ independently encoded reference images, the resulting sequence has length $L=N+T_{n}+T_{v}=31$ . A binary mask $\mathbf{m}\in\{0,1\}^{L}$ marks the first $N+T_{n}$ positions as given. In element-wise operations, $\mathbf{m}$ is broadcast over the channel and spatial dimensions. For channel-wise concatenation, we expand it into $\mathbf{M}\in\{0,1\}^{L\times 4\times h\times w}$ . The transformer input stacks three streams along the channel axis:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{\tau}$ $\displaystyle=\mathbf{m}\odot\mathbf{z}+(1-\mathbf{m})\odot\big(\tau\mathbf{z}+(1-\tau)\bm{\epsilon}\big),\quad\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}),$ (2) $\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{\tau}$ $\displaystyle=\operatorname{Concat}_{\mathrm{channel}}\!\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{\tau},\mathbf{M},\mathbf{m}\odot\mathbf{z}\right).$ (3)

Thus, the reference and normal latents remain clean, while only the target-video latents are interpolated with noise. The other two streams provide the mask and the clean conditioning latents zeroed over the target span. The mask states which positions were supplied, and the rectified-flow loss is applied where $m_{\ell}=0$ . The four channels of $\mathbf{M}$ follow the VAE temporal grouping. With $C=16$ latent channels, the three input streams therefore contribute $16+4+16=36$ channels.

No frame-wise correspondence between the normal and target sequences is imposed. This is why the turntable enters as its own span rather than as extra channels on the target latents. A channel-wise condition has to be read frame by frame, which would force an alignment between the $25$ normals and the $80$ target frames that does not exist, since the turntable is a fixed yaw sweep and the target follows whichever trajectory the prompt names.


#### 3.4 Training and Inference


##### Training.

We optimize only the LoRA parameters $\theta$ with the backbone’s rectified-flow objective. Let $\mathbf{c}$ denote the text condition and $\mathcal{F}_{\theta}^{\mathcal{V}}$ the target-video segment of the transformer output. Writing $\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\theta}^{\mathcal{V}}=\mathcal{F}_{\theta}^{\mathcal{V}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau},\tau\mid\mathbf{c})$ , the target velocity under the backbone’s flow convention is $\mathbf{z}^{\mathcal{V}}-\bm{\epsilon}^{\mathcal{V}}$ . We minimize

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{D_{v}}\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\theta}^{\mathcal{V}}-\big(\mathbf{z}^{\mathcal{V}}-\bm{\epsilon}^{\mathcal{V}}\big)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right],$$ (4)

where $D_{v}$ is the number of scalar elements in $\mathbf{z}^{\mathcal{V}}$ , and the expectation is over $\mathbf{z}$ , $\bm{\epsilon}$ , $\tau$ , and $\mathbf{c}$ . The squared error is averaged over target-video latent elements only; $\mathbf{z}^{\mathcal{R}}$ and $\mathbf{z}^{\mathcal{N}}$ remain clean context and do not contribute to the loss.


##### Inference.

At test time we run the same reconstruction and rendering steps on a new subject’s reference views (reconstruct the subject, render the yaw orbit, and derive the surface-normal turntable), then condition the generator on the references and normals to produce the target latents. The $7$ normal-condition latents and $20$ generated target latents are concatenated and jointly decoded. Under the causal VAE’s temporal compression factor of $4$ , the resulting $27$ -frame latent sequence decodes into $1+4(27-1)=105$ frames: $25$ normal-condition frames followed by $80$ target frames.


### 4 Experiment


#### 4.1 Experimental Setup

Datasets and baselines. We evaluate on ShowcaseBench , a held-out benchmark of $50$ subjects spanning four $360^{\circ}$ camera trajectories and sharing no 3D asset with Showcase-4K , and on NAVI ( Jampani et al., 2023 ) with $35$ subjects. Training and inference settings are given in Appendix . Phantom ( Liu et al., 2025 ) and MAGREF ( Deng et al., 2026 ) are single-view S2V models, and we run them in the multi-reference mode. We refer to these variants as Phantom-MV and MAGREF-MV, where the four reference images are treated as views of the same subject. Appendix also runs both in their original single-view mode, where the four-view extension is the stronger setting on almost every metric, so the multi-view numbers are the ones we report. We further compare against recent methods, MV-S2V ( Song et al., 2026 ) and 3DreamBooth ( Ko et al., 2026 ) . All methods in our main comparison use the same four reference images. For 3DreamBooth, these images are used for both subject-specific optimization and inference. Prompt details are provided in Appendices – .

Metrics. We sample $16$ frames per video and obtain subject masks for the reference images and sampled video frames using GroundingDINO ( Liu et al., 2024a ) and SAM ( Kirillov et al., 2023 ) . Every identity and 3D metric is computed on those masked subjects, so background content does not enter them, while text alignment and video quality use full frames.

Identity. DINO and CLIP similarity measure how well the generated orbit covers the supplied reference views. For each reference view we take the maximum similarity over generated frames and average across references. Features come from DINOv2-L ( Oquab et al., 2024 ) and CLIP ViT-L/14 ( Radford et al., 2021 ) .

3D consistency. MEt3R ( Asim et al., 2025 ) scores multi-view consistency between reference and generated views, taking the minimum dissimilarity per reference. We also lift the generated orbit to a point cloud with $\pi^{3}$ ( Wang et al., 2026 ) and compare it with the dataset’s ground-truth geometry. For ShowcaseBench , the ground-truth point cloud is back-projected from source-mesh depth renders; for NAVI, it is sampled from the 3D scan mesh. We report GT-CD as the symmetric mean nearest-neighbor Euclidean distance between the predicted and ground-truth point clouds, following independent centering, mean-radius normalization, and rigid alignment. We additionally report RotCoverage as an alignment-dependent diagnostic of viewpoint coverage, computed as the mean distance from each reference viewpoint to the nearest estimated generated viewpoint after pose alignment. Appendix gives the evaluation details.

Text alignment. We compute ViCLIP ( Wang et al., 2024b ) cosine similarity between the video and the prompt using eight sampled frames and the clause describing the target video.

Video quality. We report VBench ( Huang et al., 2024 ) imaging quality, aesthetic quality and dynamic degree for completeness.

On ShowcaseBench , Textured gives the lowest GT-CD among the three ablations, while Sparse gives the lowest auxiliary RotCoverage score. ### 5 Conclusion

We present OmniShowcase, which pairs an automated pipeline that renders omnidirectional videos from 3D assets with surface-normal turntable conditioning reconstructed from the reference views, requiring no temporal alignment with generated frames. OmniShowcase leads single- and multi-view baselines on subject identity and cross-view 3D consistency on both ShowcaseBench and NAVI. Two limitations remain: the background sometimes looks synthetic, and subjects that are not rigid across the four references are not handled (Figure , Appendix ). We leave both to future work. We will release the data, benchmark, and code.

### Appendix C Implementation Details

We fine-tune Wan2.1 I2V-14B-480P through its Diffusers implementation. Training applies LoRA to the DiT with rank $128$ and $\alpha=128$ , and every other weight stays frozen. Each training sample contains a $25$ -frame normal-condition segment and an $80$ -frame target segment at $480\times 832$ resolution. These segments are encoded separately into $7$ and $20$ latent frames, respectively, and concatenated along the temporal axis to form a $27$ -frame latent sequence. The four in-context reference images are encoded independently. We train for $33{,}000$ steps at a batch size of $2$ per device over four NVIDIA B200 GPUs with no gradient accumulation, which takes about $50$ hours. The optimizer is AdamW with $\beta=(0.9,\,0.95)$ and $\epsilon=10^{-8}$ , weight decay $10^{-4}$ and gradient clipping at $1.0$ . The learning rate is $2\times 10^{-5}$ , held constant after $100$ warmup steps. Training runs in bfloat16 under seed $42$ , and every variant reported in this paper uses the same $33{,}000$ -step checkpoint.

At inference, we take $50$ sampling steps with three-way classifier-free guidance, weighting the reference stream at $s_{\text{ref}}=5.0$ and the text stream at $s_{\text{text}}=5.0$ . The concatenated $27$ -frame latent sequence is jointly decoded into $1+4(27-1)=105$ frames, comprising $25$ normal-condition frames followed by $80$ target frames at $480\times 832$ resolution. Clips are written at $30$ fps.

For GT-CD, the ground-truth point cloud is back-projected from source-mesh depth renders for ShowcaseBench and sampled from the 3D scan mesh for NAVI. We independently center the predicted and ground-truth clouds at their respective centroids and normalize each by its mean Euclidean radius. We then align the normalized prediction to the ground truth using rigid ICP with scale fixed. The evaluation removes the outermost $3\%$ of points by radius and subsamples each cloud to at most $20{,}000$ points. For the resulting point clouds $P$ and $Q$ , we compute

[Tableau] $$d(P,Q)=\frac{1}{|P|}\sum_{p\in P}\min_{q\in Q}\lVert p-q\rVert_{2},\qquad\mathrm{GT\mbox{-}CD}=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(d(P,Q)+d(Q,P)\big).$$

The directed terms measure prediction-to-GT accuracy and GT-to-prediction completeness using Euclidean rather than squared distances.

RotCoverage is an auxiliary, alignment-dependent diagnostic of viewpoint coverage. After similarity alignment, the generated and reference camera poses are normalized by the RMS radius of the reference camera centers. Each pose is represented by five frustum vertices: the apex and four far-plane corners at depth $0.5$ and half-size $0.25$ . Pose distance is the mean Euclidean distance between corresponding frustum vertices. For each reference pose, we take the minimum distance over generated poses and average across references, yielding a dimensionless score.

### Appendix G Dataset


#### G.1 Curation and composition

Showcase-4K is the surviving remainder of a much larger candidate pool. Assets enter from Objaverse ( Deitke et al., 2023 ) , the Digital Twin Catalog ( Dong et al., 2025 ) and Google Scanned Objects ( Downs et al., 2022 ) and pass through four gates: G0 , a model-free heuristic on the mesh and a quick render; G1 , a vision-language check of four rendered views; G2 , the same check on the composited target video; and G3 , on the reconstructed turntable (rubrics in Appendix ). G0 rejected $17\%$ of downloaded candidates outright, and G1 kept $4{,}186$ of the $7{,}016$ assets it judged ( $60\%$ ). In the most recent production round, $44\%$ of $7{,}438$ composited clips passed G2 and $63\%$ of those passed G3, so $28\%$ of rendered clips survive; most G2 rejections are a changed object structure or physically implausible support. The binding constraint is G2, and we did not relax it. Over-generating and discarding is cheaper than letting a bad sample into training, because a subject that deforms in the target teaches exactly the failure the method is meant to remove. Over all rounds $4{,}829$ clips passed, and $4{,}000$ of them, covering $2{,}593$ assets in $620$ categories, form the released corpus.

Curation runs without a human in the loop: every semantic judgement is made by Gemini 2.5 Flash, which returns a binary score and a short rationale per criterion. Over all rounds the gates took about $29{,}000$ calls at about $120$ USD, roughly $25$ USD per thousand kept clips, so expanding the corpus scales with that cost rather than with annotator time.


#### G.2 Camera trajectories

Every prompt ends with one sentence describing the camera path. To keep the figure captions short we label each clip with a tag instead, and give here the sentence each tag stands for. A tag is written as a motion followed by either a rotation amount (Orbit and Helix) or a framing transition (everything else), for example Helix-Up 360° or Arc-Down L $\rightarrow$ R. The corpus covers every motion below, with the Orbit and Helix motions contributing the object-centric half and the arc, truck, dolly and zoom motions the extended-trajectory half.

The two suffix families do not mix. The four orbit-family motions (Orbit and Helix-Up/Down/In) each take one of four rotation amounts, which gives $16$ orbit-family trajectories, and the remaining eight motions each take one of nine framing transitions, which gives $72$ more, so the vocabulary defines $88$ distinct camera trajectories in total.

### Appendix H Failure Analysis

> Figure 13: Failure cases. Left two. Prompt adherence and the commanded rotation both hold, but the surroundings render with flat shading and little material detail, so the clip reads as slightly synthetic. Right two. Both receive four reference views of a subject caught in four different poses. The upper one is prompted in the usual static style and settles into an averaged pose that matches none of the references, while the lower one is asked to follow the motion in step with the orbit and tangles in 3D at the transitions between poses.


##### Analysis.

The two columns of Figure fail for unrelated reasons. The synthetic look of the background follows from how the training targets are built. Only the subject is physically rendered, and the scene around it is painted in afterwards by a video inpainting model conditioned on the prompt. The corpus therefore pairs a physically shaded subject with a generated background, and the light transport between the two was never consistent to begin with. The model reproduces that statistic, so the subject holds while the surroundings inherit the flat shading and thin material detail of their source.

The dynamic case is a property of the conditioning signal itself. The surface-normal turntable is obtained by lifting the reference views into a single static reconstruction and rendering that one shape from every viewpoint. When the four views catch the subject in four different poses, the reconstruction fuses them into one rigid body that corresponds to no pose the subject ever held, and the condition handed to the generator is a static representation of a subject that is not static. Under a static prompt the model follows that condition faithfully, which is exactly why the output settles into an averaged pose. Asking for motion in the prompt does not repair it. The text now requests a pose change that the geometry condition does not encode, so the two disagree, and the model has to interpolate between reference poses in image space with no 3D correspondence to guide it. The tangles appear at those interpolated transitions. Handling non-rigid subjects would require a conditioning representation that varies over the generated sequence, which we leave to future work.
