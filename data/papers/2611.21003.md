## K2-Horizon: A Frontier Fleet of Open Source Language Models

We present K2-Horizon, a fleet of six fully open language models, from a 0.9B dense model to a 375B-A23B MoE, built for general-purpose reasoning and agentic work across math and science, software engineering, deep research, office automation, and beyond. The models lead or are competitive with strong open models of their size on agentic and coding benchmarks. On the modeling side, to strengthen reasoning, we introduce Mixture-of-Value Attention (MoVA), applying MoE to attention value projections, and softplus attention gating, a more flexible, theoretically motivated gate. Our 36B-A4B model uses both and nearly matches a 32B dense model trained on the same data, with one-seventh the active parameters. We also introduce Uno, lossless diffusion adapters that speed up decoding by up to 2.2× without a separate draft model. Our release follows the LLM360 definition of open source for transparency and reproducibility: the full development history, including intermediate checkpoints from every training stage, pre-training data, training logs, and all training, evaluation, and infrastructure code, notably our production-scale xLLM framework for dense and MoE training and the RL360 reinforcement-learning stack. Beyond the data itself, we release and document in detail every pipeline that built it, the part of model development that open releases most rarely disclose. These pipelines produced roughly 10T synthetic pre-training tokens, showing that synthetic data can be a major ingredient of pre-training at scale. Four of the models share one 22T-token pre-training corpus and recipe, revealing consistent scaling behavior and enabling controlled studies of scale and sparsity. All artifacts are available at https://ifm.ai/k2/ .

### 1 Introduction

We present K2-Horizon , a fleet of six fully open language models designed for agentic tasks. While many capable open-weight models are available, the data, intermediate states, and decisions behind them are rarely released. K2-Horizon succeeds K2-V2 ( K2 Team, Institute of Foundation Models, 2025 ) in the LLM360 family. It extends that fully open approach from a single base model to the complete development lifecycle of six models: pre-training, mid-training, post-training, and deployment. The family ranges from a 0.9B dense model for on-device use to a 375B mixture-of-experts flagship that activates 23B parameters per token, and achieves competitive performance on general-purpose reasoning and agentic tasks, such as math and science, software engineering, deep research, file manipulation, customer service, and tool calling.

For all six models, we release the final weights, intermediate checkpoints from every training stage, quantized builds, training logs, the training data, and the code used throughout the training and evaluation pipeline. This code includes our training framework (xLLM), RL stack (RL360), and evaluation harness (Eval360). All released artifacts are documented in the Released Artifacts section. Four of the models (3.7B, 7B, 32B, and 36B-A4B) were pre-trained on the same 22T-token corpus with the same recipe, including two pairs trained on identical data batches. The release provides a complete, transparent recipe for reproducing frontier models at various sizes, allows controlled comparisons across scale and between dense and sparse architectures, and supports studies of how each ingredient influences the final model.

The family covers the main deployment settings (Table 1 ). The 0.9B targets phones and other memory-constrained devices. The 3.7B and 7B models are designed for local assistants and coding tools on consumer hardware, and serve as accessible platforms for fine-tuning and reinforcement-learning research. The 32B and 36B-A4B target self-hosted technical and agentic assistants, and the 375B-A23B targets multi-GPU server deployment.

When compared against four open-weight models released around the same time (GLM-5.2, MiniMax M3, Nemotron 3 Ultra, and Inkling), our 375B-A23B is smaller than most of them in both active and total parameters, yet ranks first or second on most agentic and coding benchmarks (see the overview figure on the first page). It leads all four on Toolathlon-Verified (65.3), where it also surpasses GPT-5.6 Terra and comes close to GPT-5.6 Luna. It also leads them on repository-level code understanding without internet access (SWE-Atlas-QnA, 48.4), and it exceeds both GPT-5.6 variants on $\tau^{3}$ -Banking. Its main gap is expert-level scientific reasoning (HLE and CritPt; § 7.2 ). The 36B-A4B matches or stays within a few points of our own dense 32B (seven times its active parameters), trained on the same data, on every benchmark (§ 7.3 ). Among small models, the 7B outperforms similarly sized open-weight models on most benchmarks, and both the 7B and 3.7B lead SWE-bench Verified by wide margins (§ 7.4 ).

Our main contributions are:

- • Architecture. Grouped RMSNorm across the family, and Mixture-of-Value Attention (MoVA) with a softplus attention gate in the 36B-A4B (§ 2 ).

- • Pre-training and mid-training. A production-scale test of the scaling-collapse approach of Bergsma et al. (2026) : four models that span dense and sparse architectures and nearly an order of magnitude in size, trained on 22T tokens with hyperparameters scaled together, follow nearly identical normalized loss curves. Pre-training, mid-training, and context extension all run on xLLM (§ 3.2.2 ), our open-source training infrastructure, which supports both dense and MoE models at production scale (§ 3 , § 3.3.2 ).

- • Data. About 10T synthetic pre-training tokens in a web-heavy mix chosen by benchmark scores predicted at production scale (§ 4 ), and agentic trajectories introduced from the start of context extension rather than only in post-training (§ 5 ).

- • Post-training. For the 375B-A23B, reinforcement learning of separate domain experts for search, knowledge work, tool use, scientific reasoning, and instruction following with abstention, merged into one model by weight merging and refined with targeted supervised fine-tuning (§ 6.3 , § 6.4 ); the other models follow adapted versions of this pipeline (§ 1.1 ).

- • Inference. Uno, lossless diffusion adapters distilled from each model that speed up decoding by up to 2.2 $\times$ without a separate draft model (§ 9 , § 10 ).

§ 1.1 walks through the development lifecycle and serves as a guide to the rest of the report.


#### 1.1 Model Development Lifecycle

Developing a large language model involves a sequence of training stages, each starting from the checkpoint produced by the previous one. We describe the sequence for the 375B flagship; the other models follow it with minor differences noted in later sections. Figure 1 shows the training timeline for all six models, including pre-training and mid-training for the 32B and 36B-A4B models.

Pre-training gives the model most of its knowledge and its general language ability: starting from initialization, the model is trained to predict the next token on 8k-token sequences of web text, code, mathematics, scientific literature, and multilingual data, about 10T tokens of which are synthetic (§ 4 ). This stage accounts for most of the compute, so its key decisions are made in advance through small-scale experiments. Learning rates follow a scaling rule fitted across model sizes (§ 3.1 ), and the data mix is chosen by its benchmark scores predicted at the production scale rather than measured in small ablations, which led us to a web-heavy mix (§ 4.9 ). The flagship is trained on 15.1T tokens in two phases, the second with an updated data mix, while the dense and MoVA models see 22T tokens. All runs except the 0.9B hold a constant learning rate to the end, and the decay is instead realized by averaging the final checkpoints before mid-training.

Mid-training continues the same objective on about 1.9T tokens of more carefully curated data in four stages, extending the context window from 32k tokens in stage 1 to 128k in stage 2 and 512k in stages 3 and 4, with the RoPE base raised alongside (§ 3.1.3 ). Most of the budget goes to stage 1, during which the learning rate also decays to 10% of its peak. The data shift toward the capabilities the final model needs: long documents, reasoning, coding, and instruction following, together with agent trajectories in our chat template. We introduce agentic data as soon as context extension begins rather than reserving it for post-training (§ 5 ).

Post-training sharpens the model on selected domains, especially long-horizon agentic tasks. This is where our recipes differ most across model sizes, as we adjust the recipe to fit the capability of each model. For our 375B flagship model, we choose the final mid-training checkpoint as the starting point. Based on it, a separate RL expert is trained for each of five domains: search, knowledge work, tool use, scientific reasoning, and instruction following with abstention. In each, the model acts in an environment such as a sandbox, a search engine, or a simulated user, a verifier scores the outcome, and the model is updated to favor successful behavior (§ 6.3 ). The experts are then merged into a single model by weight merging, which takes a few minutes and retains most of each expert’s gains (§ 6.4 ). Evaluating the merged model reveals the remaining gaps, such as unfamiliar agent harness formats, and a final round of supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on demonstrations targets them.

Evaluation runs throughout the lifecycle, both to track progress and to catch regressions, and its findings feed back into later data and training stages (Sections 3.4 and 7 ). Finally, the model is optimized for efficient inference (§ 10 ) and checked for safety (§ 11 ).

### 2 Architecture

The K2-Horizon series retains the established Transformer ( Vaswani et al., 2017 ) architecture, including GQA ( Ainslie et al., 2023 ) , rotary positional embedding (RoPE; Su et al. 2021 ), SwiGLU ( Shazeer, 2020 ) , and sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) ( Shazeer et al., 2017 ; Lepikhin et al., 2021 ; Fedus et al., 2022 ) .

It uses a 64,256-token vocabulary, compared with 250,624 tokens for the larger models. For the 3.7B, 7B, and 32B dense models, we adopt the depth, hidden width, and attention-head configurations of Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, and Qwen3-32B, respectively, as established architectural baselines ( Yang et al., 2025 ) .

#### 2.1 Grouped RMSNorm

To facilitate the description of the new techniques, we use $\bm{X}=\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$ and $\bm{Y}=\{\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{n}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$ to denote the input and output sequences with length $n$ , and assume the representations of the input and output sequences have the same dimension $d$ .

The root mean square layer normalization mechanism (RMSNorm) ( Zhang and Sennrich, 2019 ) simplifies the original layer normalization (LayerNorm) ( Ba et al., 2016 ) by dropping the re-centering (mean subtraction) and bias terms, and instead individually re-scaling each input vector $\mathbf{x}_{t},t\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$ with its root mean square statistic:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle v_{t}$ $\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}x_{t,i}^{2}+\epsilon}$ (1) $\displaystyle y_{t,j}$ $\displaystyle=\frac{x_{t,j}}{v_{t}},\forall j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}$ (2)

where we omit the commonly used scaling parameter for simplicity. RMSNorm achieves performance comparable to LayerNorm at a lower computational cost, and has been adopted as the default normalization mechanism in a range of frontier large language models ( Touvron et al., 2023 ; Meta AI, 2024 ; Qwen Team et al., 2025 ; DeepSeek-AI and others, 2024 ) . Theoretically, each output element $y_{t,j}$ lies in the range $(-\sqrt{d},\sqrt{d})$ . While a typical element has magnitude around $1$ , this bound grows with model width $d$ (e.g., $\sqrt{d}\approx 90$ for $d=8192$ ). As a result, individual outputs of RMSNorm can become disproportionately large in wide models, which may lead to training instability in extreme cases.

We propose Grouped RMSNorm and apply it to the 3.7B, 7B, 32B, and 36B-A4B models. Formally, Grouped RMSNorm first partitions the $d$ feature dimensions of each $\mathbf{x}_{t}$ into $k$ groups, with $d_{g}=d/k$ elements per group. The root mean square (RMS) statistic ( 3 ) is then computed independently within each group:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle v_{t,g}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{d_{g}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{g}}x_{t,i}^{2}+\epsilon},\quad g=1,\ldots,k.$ (3)

By controlling the group dimension $d_{g}$ through the number of groups $k$ , particularly by increasing $k$ as the model dimension grows, Grouped RMSNorm provides an explicit upper bound on the magnitude of each normalized output element, restricting it to the range $(-\sqrt{d_{g}},\sqrt{d_{g}})$ . Empirical results demonstrate that Grouped RMSNorm substantially improves training stability across a wide range of model scales in the K2-Horizon series.

#### 2.2 MoVA: Mixture-of-Value Attention

The standard self-attention mechanism is a function:

[Tableau] $$\bm{Y}=\mathrm{Attn}(\bm{X})=f\left(\frac{\bm{Q}\bm{K}^{T}}{\tau(\bm{X})}\right)\bm{V}$$ (4)

where $\mathrm{Attn}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times v}$ is the self-attention function. $\bm{Q}=\bm{X}W_{q},\,\,\bm{K}=\bm{X}W_{k},\,\,\mbox{and\penalty\ }\bm{V}=\bm{X}W_{v}$ are the sequences of queries, keys and values, with learnable parameters. $f(\cdot)$ is an attention function, e.g., the softmax function $f_{\mathrm{softmax}}(\cdot)$ ( Bahdanau et al., 2015 ) , the squared ReLU function $f_{\mathrm{relu^{2}}}(\cdot)$ ( Hua et al., 2022 ) or the Laplace function $f_{\mathrm{laplace}}(\cdot)$ ( Ma et al., 2023 ) . $\tau(\bm{X})$ is a scaling term, which is commonly set to $\tau(\bm{X})=\sqrt{d_{k}}$ for $f_{\mathrm{softmax}}(\cdot)$ , or $\tau(\bm{X})=n$ for $f_{\mathrm{relu^{2}}}(\cdot)$ and $f_{\mathrm{laplace}}(\cdot)$ . The commonly used multi-head variant of attention performs the attention function $h$ times in parallel.

MoE models extend standard transformer architectures by replacing the feed-forward network (FFN) with $E$ experts and a routing function that sparsely selects a subset for each token ( Shazeer et al., 2017 ; Fedus et al., 2022 ) . Given token representation $x$ , the router assigns probabilities over experts and activates only the top- $k$ :

[Tableau] $$\text{MoE}(x)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}(x)}p_{i}(x)\cdot E_{i}(x),$$

where $\mathcal{K}(x)\subset\{1,\ldots,E\}$ with $|\mathcal{K}(x)|=k$ denotes the selected expert indices and $p_{i}(x)$ are the normalized routing weights. Since $k\ll E$ in practice, per-token floating-point operations (FLOPs) scale with $k$ , enabling parameter count to grow quasi-independently of computation. In practice, each expert $E_{i}$ is instantiated as a two-layer MLP network with SwiGLU activation ( Shazeer, 2020 ) .

In K2-Horizon -MoVA-36B-A4B, we propose mixture-of-value attention (MoVA), which incorporates MoE-style sparsity into the computation of value vectors in multi-head attention. Formally, MoVA replaces the single value projection $\bm{v}_{t}=\bm{x}_{t}W_{v}$ in the standard attention mechanism with a routing function that sparsely selects a subset of $V$ value projections:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle V_{i}(\bm{x}_{t})$ $\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{t}W^{(i)}_{v}$ (5) $\displaystyle\text{V}(\bm{x}_{t})$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}(\bm{x}_{t})}p_{i}(\bm{x}_{t})\cdot\phi_{\textrm{silu}}\left(V_{i}(\bm{x}_{t})\right),$ (6)

where $\mathcal{V}(\bm{x}_{t})\subset\{1,\ldots,V\}$ with $|\mathcal{V}(\bm{x}_{t})|=k$ denotes the selected expert indices and $p_{i}(\bm{x}_{t})$ are the normalized routing weights. Unlike conventional MoE layers, where each expert $E_{i}$ typically consists of a two-layer MLP and thus introduces nonlinearity, an individual value projection in ( 5 ) is purely linear. To introduce nonlinearity into the resulting value vector $\text{V}(\bm{x}_{t})$ in ( 6 ), we therefore apply a $\mathrm{SiLU}$ activation function to the output of each value projection. Without this activation, the mixture reduces to $\bm{x}_{t}\big(\sum_{i}p_{i}(\bm{x}_{t})W_{v}^{(i)}\big)$ , an input-dependent linear map whose only nonlinearity comes from the routing weights.

MoVA opens a second axis for scaling sparsity in an LLM, beyond MoE in the FFN module. Importantly, MoVA enjoys the following advantages:

- • Compatibility with efficient attention. MoVA only modifies how each value vector is computed from its own token, leaving the attention computation itself unchanged. It is therefore directly compatible with FlashAttention, GQA, and sparse attention.

- • No additional KV cache. Each $\bm{v}_{t}$ is fully determined once token $t$ is processed, so only the mixed value vector is cached, with the same size as in standard GQA.

K2-Horizon -MoVA-36B-A4B combines MoVA with MoE FFN layers and activates about 4.6B parameters per token. Empirically, its pre-training loss closely tracks that of our 32B dense model, which was trained on the same data with identical settings, with the dense model maintaining a modest lead. Downstream evaluations reflect this: at 1.1M steps, MoVA trails the dense model by only 1.2 to 3.7 points on MMLU, GSM8K, HellaSwag, and HumanEval, despite activating roughly one-seventh as many parameters (see § 3.4 and full evaluation logs at W&B 1 1 https://forge.coreweave.com/wandb/llm360/K2-Horizon-36B , https://forge.coreweave.com/wandb/llm360/K2-Horizon-32B ).


#### 2.3 Softplus Activation in Attention Gate

In the K2-Horizon -MoVA-36B-A4B model, we use gated attention with a $\mathrm{softplus}$ gate activation. The gated attention mechanism has been extensively studied and widely used in frontier models ( Hua et al., 2022 ; Ma et al., 2024 ; Qiu et al., 2025 ; Ma et al., 2026 ; Ma et al., 2023 ) . Notably, Ma et al. (2023) show that gating substantially increases the expressiveness of attention: single-head gated attention is as expressive as multi-head attention.

Formally, the gated attention mechanism applies a gating vector to the attention output:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}$ $\displaystyle=\phi(\bm{X}W_{\gamma}+b_{\gamma})$ (7) $\displaystyle\bm{Y}$ $\displaystyle=\left(f\left(\frac{\bm{Q}\bm{K}^{T}}{\tau(\bm{X})}\right)\bm{V}\right)\odot\bm{\gamma},$ (8)

where $\odot$ denotes element-wise product, and $\phi(\cdot)$ is the activation function used to compute the attention gate. Two common choices for $\phi(\cdot)$ are $\mathrm{sigmoid}$ and $\mathrm{SiLU}$ , and Qiu et al. (2025) empirically show that $\mathrm{sigmoid}$ slightly outperforms $\mathrm{SiLU}$ . However, $\mathrm{sigmoid}$ is bounded in $(0,1)$ : at initialization it sits near $\mathrm{sigmoid}(0)=0.5$ , halving the attention output, and even at its maximum it can only recover the ungated output, never amplify it. This limits the expressiveness of the gate, since the result of Ma et al. (2023) requires the gate to represent the ratio between multi-head and single-head attention outputs, which can exceed $1$ . We therefore adopt the $\mathrm{softplus}$ function as the gate activation:

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{softplus}(x;\beta)=\frac{1}{\beta}\cdot\log\left(1+\exp(\beta\cdot x)\right),$$ (9)

where $\beta$ is a scaling coefficient. Like $\mathrm{sigmoid}$ , $\mathrm{softplus}$ is strictly positive, so the gate acts as a pure element-wise scaling factor; unlike $\mathrm{sigmoid}$ , it is unbounded above, allowing the gate to both attenuate and amplify. Moreover, setting $\beta=\log 2$ yields $\mathrm{softplus}(0;\log 2)=1$ , so that at initialization, when the gate pre-activations are close to zero, the gate is approximately the identity and gated attention behaves like standard attention.


### 3 Pre-Training and Mid-Training

This section describes the pre-training and mid-training of the K2-Horizon models, including the training recipe, infrastructure, and challenges encountered during training. § 3.1 presents the optimizer recipe, covering hyperparameter selection at small scale, the final pre-training settings for each model, and the mid-training and long-context schedule. § 3.2 describes the training infrastructure, including the cluster, tooling for maintaining long-running jobs, the training framework, and the parallelism configuration. § 3.3 details the flagship 375B model’s two pre-training phases, the loss spikes and associated rollbacks, and the subsequent mid-training run.


#### 3.1 Optimizer Recipe

Our hyperparameter-selection strategy builds on the dense-model approach used for K2-V2. We use small-scale experiments to guide learning-rate selection across model sizes and parameterize weight decay through the AdamW averaging timescale. We extend this approach to sparse models by investigating learning-rate transfer as the number of routed experts increases, while holding model width and the number of active experts fixed. These experiments inform the production recipes for our dense and sparse models.


##### 3.1.1 Pre-Training Hyperparameter Selection

Following Wang and Aitchison (2025) ; Bergsma et al. (2025) , we express the coupling between learning rate, weight decay, and training duration through the nominal AdamW timescale:

[Tableau] $$\tau_{\mathrm{epoch}}=\frac{B}{\eta\lambda D}=\frac{1}{\eta\lambda T},$$ (10)

where $B$ is the global batch size in tokens, $D$ is the training token budget, $T=D/B$ is the number of optimizer steps, $\eta$ is the peak learning rate, and $\lambda$ is the weight-decay coefficient. The timescale expresses how quickly weight decay discounts earlier parameter values, measured as a fraction of the training budget. Holding it fixed as learning rate or training length changes keeps the nominal accumulated decay comparable. Because the timescale is defined using the peak learning rate, it provides only a nominal measure; the accumulated decay also depends on the learning-rate schedule. We used small-scale experiments to inform timescale selection and the corresponding production weight-decay settings. For the 3.7B, 7B, 32B, and 36B-A4B, the production settings scale the timescale as $\tau_{\mathrm{epoch}}\propto 1/\sqrt{\mathrm{TPP}}$ , where TPP is the number of training tokens per active backbone parameter: $\tau_{\mathrm{epoch}}\sqrt{\mathrm{TPP}}$ lies between $2.6$ and $2.9$ for these four models.

We swept learning rates across dense models at a fixed tokens-per-parameter ratio, varying model size and attention-head count. The best observed learning rates decreased approximately as the inverse square root of the number of parameters:

[Tableau] $$\eta_{\mathrm{best}}\propto N^{-1/2},$$ (11)

where $N$ is the number of active backbone parameters. This is consistent with the width-dependent shift under standard parameterization shown in Yang et al. (2022, Figure 1, left) and the extrapolation strategy used for K2-V2 ( K2 Team, Institute of Foundation Models, 2025 ) . The learning rates of Table 2 follow the same rule from the 0.9B to the flagship 375B: $\eta\sqrt{N}$ lies between $6.8\times 10^{-4}$ and $8.6\times 10^{-4}$ for the active backbone counts of Table 1 . We used this relationship to guide learning-rate choices across scales.

We additionally swept learning rates for six-layer MoE models. We increased the number of routed experts from 4 to 128 while holding model width (256), expert width (512), and active-expert count (2) fixed. Batch sizes and training horizons varied across expert counts. The comparison therefore reflects the study’s training policy rather than holding all other training conditions constant.

Among completed runs, the best tested learning rates ranged from $3.16\times 10^{-3}$ to $10^{-2}$ , without a monotonic trend with expert count. We selected these rates using the final exponentially weighted moving average of training loss, with a 50-step half-life. The dense baseline and the sparsest tested configuration, with two of 128 routed experts active per token, both selected a learning rate of $7.5\times 10^{-3}$ . Although noisy, the results supported keeping a common starting learning rate as sparsity increases. Weak sensitivity to sparsity has also been observed under hypersphere optimization with HyperP ( Ren et al., 2026 ) , which uses a different optimization method.

We initially targeted a batch size, measured in tokens, close to that of DeepSeek-V3 ( DeepSeek-AI and others, 2024 ) , which was trained on a similar token budget ( $14.8$ T tokens versus our $15.1$ T): $8$ k sequences of $8$ k tokens ( $67$ M tokens; $\mathrm{k}=1{,}024$ throughout), compared with DeepSeek-V3’s $15{,}360$ sequences of $4$ k tokens ( $63$ M tokens). We tested this target on a small scale by scaling the batch down in proportion to the square root of the token budget, following the Power Lines framework ( Bergsma et al., 2025 ) , and compared that scaled-down batch with one half its size. For dense models with 61 layers and widths of 512, 640, and 768, trained at a fixed tokens-per-parameter ratio, the scaled-down batch sizes were $1.4$ M, $1.7$ M, and $2.1$ M tokens, respectively. We also evaluated batch sizes approximately half as large. For each model and batch-size setting, we selected the best-performing learning rate from nine candidates. Halving the batch size reduced the final training loss by $2.7\%$ , $5.1\%$ , and $3.4\%$ at widths of 512, 640, and 768, respectively. These losses were smoothed using an exponentially weighted moving average with a half-life of 50 steps. The validation gain was smaller, $1.1\%$ and $0.4\%$ at widths 512 and 768, and reversed at width 640, where the loss was $0.1\%$ higher. Based on these results, we halved the target for the 375B model to $4$ k sequences of $8$ k tokens ( $34$ M tokens; Table 2 ).

##### 3.1.3 Mid-Training and Long-Context Recipe

Mid-training has three jobs: to realize the learning-rate decay that pre-training postponed, to extend the context window from $8$ k to $512$ k tokens, and to shift the data toward long documents, reasoning, and agentic trajectories (§ 5 ). It reuses each model’s pre-training batch size, weight decay, and peak learning rate, so no new sweep is needed (Table 3 ).

Because pre-training ends at the peak learning rate (§ 3.1.2 ), mid-training starts from an average of the last few pre-training checkpoints rather than from a single one, which acts as an implicit decay ( Tian et al., 2026 ) . Stage 1 then re-warms the learning rate to the pre-training peak and decays it linearly to $10\%$ of the peak. Stage 1 carries most of the mid-training budget, about $1.1$ T of roughly $1.9$ T tokens, so this is where the explicit decay happens.

Context then grows in three steps, from $32$ k tokens in stage 1 to $128$ k in stage 2 and $512$ k in stages 3 and 4, and these longer-context stages use far fewer tokens than stage 1. As in K2-V2 ( K2 Team, Institute of Foundation Models, 2025 ) , the learning rate stays at the $10\%$ floor through stages 2–4 to keep optimization stable as the context grows, and the RoPE base rises with the context length, from $500{,}000$ in pre-training to $1$ million in stage 1 and $10$ million from stage 2 onward, so that rotary positions stay distinguishable over longer distances ( Xiong et al., 2023 ) .

The 0.9B differs because its pre-training already ended with a cosine decay, so it skips checkpoint averaging. After a brief context-extension phase of about $2{,}000$ steps on separate data, it runs two stages at $40$ k and $128$ k tokens of context, each with a short warmup and a cosine decay to $1\%$ of the stage’s peak. It stops at $128$ k to keep its memory footprint within the budget of the phones and other memory-constrained devices it targets. Its first stage uses $40$ k rather than $32$ k tokens because its smaller vocabulary turns the same text into more tokens, so data prepared for a $32$ k context would otherwise be truncated.


#### 3.2 Infrastructure

This section describes the system that ran the training. § 3.2.1 covers the cluster, its storage and checkpointing, the tooling that keeps jobs running through failures, and the loss monitor that restarts or rolls back a run when its loss misbehaves. § 3.2.2 covers the xLLM training framework and the parallel layout chosen for each model.


##### 3.2.1 Clusters, Storage, and Reliability

All models in the K2-Horizon family were trained on servers with the same node configuration as the K2-V2 cluster ( K2 Team, Institute of Foundation Models, 2025 , Sec. 3.4.1) . Each node hosts eight NVIDIA H200 GPUs (141 GB HBM, 700 W TDP) and two Intel Xeon Platinum 8468 CPUs with approximately 1.6 TB of system memory. For inter-node communication, each node is equipped with eight 400 Gb/s InfiniBand NDR host channel adapters, one per GPU, for an aggregate of 3.2 Tb/s of injection bandwidth per node. All nodes are connected through a fully non-blocking fabric. Jobs are scheduled with Slurm. The fleet is shared with other workloads, and training runs are submitted under dedicated accounts and quality-of-service (QoS) levels that govern priority and preemption.

Checkpoints are distributed and sharded: all ranks cooperatively save and reload model shards, while optimizer shards are written once per non-replicated shard by designated ranks. This enables parallel checkpoint I/O and resumption. We write a checkpoint about every 250 training steps to make potential rollbacks convenient. To bound storage use, older checkpoints are rotated out over time, while a subset is retained (milestone checkpoints, usually every 5000 steps) for evaluation. For durability beyond the cluster filesystem, a scheduled upload job copies completed checkpoints to Amazon S3.

Runs are managed through an in-house submitter that wraps Slurm and exposes three lifecycle operations. Submit launches a run, terminate stops it cleanly, and recover relaunches it from the most recent valid checkpoint. At the scale of our runs, hardware faults and transient job failures are expected. We therefore treat automatic restarts as routine operation and distinguish them from interventions , meaning deliberate human changes to a run’s configuration, data, or code. § 3.3.2 reports the spikes detected during the run and the rollbacks they led to.

Alongside the fault-driven restarts above, a separate monitor watches the training loss itself. Every 30 minutes it reads the most recent 35 minutes of per-step loss and applies two tests. The restart test flags a window of 10 consecutive steps in which every loss exceeds $0.97$ and the largest exceeds $1.2$ ; requiring the whole window to stay high ignores isolated noisy steps, while the second condition requires a large excursion. A flag triggers a rollback, and the run is relaunched through the submitter from the last checkpoint on the 250-step grid at or before the first flagged step. The alert test is looser (windows of 5 steps, with thresholds $0.95$ and $0.99$ ) and only sends a notification. Both sets of thresholds are absolute loss values, so they encode the loss range of the run and must be retuned if it shifts. The monitor reads the training loss only, and it has no guard against resuming repeatedly from the same checkpoint. § 3.3.2 counts restarts and rollbacks by how far the run was rewound, whichever mechanism triggered them. The design follows the monitor used for K2-V2 ( K2 Team, Institute of Foundation Models, 2025 ) .


##### 3.2.2 Training Infrastructure and Parallelism

All models are trained with xLLM , our in-house training framework, which we open source alongside this report. xLLM is designed to be an efficient, production-tested infrastructure that combines large-scale performance with research flexibility. Architectures, data mixtures, training stages, and objectives can be changed without rebuilding the surrounding stack. It covers the full model lifecycle: training, evaluation, checkpoint conversion, bridges to serving systems, and runtime kernels. xLLM was already the mid-training framework for K2-V2, though it was not named in that report ( K2 Team, Institute of Foundation Models, 2025 ) . For K2-Horizon we extended it to pre-training, so a single codebase now spans multiple training stages.

Each parallelism axis trades memory savings for communication cost. We summarize the axes we use, following K2 Team, Institute of Foundation Models (2025, Sec. 3.4.2) .

Data parallelism (DP) splits each global batch across workers, and each worker processes its own shard of the batch. In its plain form, every worker holds a full model replica, and gradients are synchronized with an all-reduce after each step ( Li et al., 2020 ) . This scales throughput almost linearly but saves no memory. xLLM instead implements DP with PyTorch FSDP in full-shard mode, which is equivalent to ZeRO-3 ( Rajbhandari et al., 2020 ; Zhao et al., 2023 ) . Parameters, gradients, and optimizer states are sharded across the DP group of each tensor-parallel rank. Parameters are all-gathered one layer at a time for compute and resharded after the forward pass. Gradients are reduce-scattered in fp32, not all-reduced. Each GPU therefore holds roughly a $1/(\mathrm{TP}\cdot\mathrm{DP})$ share of the model and optimizer state. The price is an extra parameter all-gather in both the forward and backward passes, and FSDP overlaps it with computation.

Tensor parallelism (TP) shards individual weight matrices by rows or columns, so each device computes a slice of every layer ( Shoeybi et al., 2019 ) . This cuts per-device parameter and activation memory, but it needs collectives inside every layer, in both the forward and backward passes. Only high-bandwidth intra-node links (NVLink) can absorb that traffic, so we restrict TP to 8-GPU groups within a node.

Expert parallelism (EP) , which K2-V2 did not need, distributes the experts of a mixture-of-experts layer across devices. Tokens are routed to their assigned experts through all-to-all communication ( Lepikhin et al., 2021 ; Fedus et al., 2022 ) . In xLLM, EP and TP are tied: experts are sharded across the same 8-GPU group used for tensor parallelism. Both axes therefore share one intra-node communication domain, and the all-to-all traffic never crosses the InfiniBand fabric.

Context parallelism (CP) partitions activations along the sequence dimension, and its benefit grows with context length: activation memory scales with sequence length while parameter memory does not. xLLM has no separate sequence-parallel (SP) axis ( Korthikanti et al., 2023 ) . Between layers, tensor parallelism already shards activations along the hidden dimension. Our CP uses the all-gather formulation ( Grattafiori et al., 2024 ) . Each CP rank holds a contiguous chunk of the sequence. For attention, it all-gathers keys and values from the other ranks and keeps only the causal prefix its queries need. CP ranks share parameters, so FSDP switches to hybrid sharding: parameters are sharded across DP and replicated across CP. We use no CP during 8k pre-training. CP is added in mid-training as context grows, and it reaches $\mathrm{CP}{=}4$ at 512k context for the 375B flagship.

Pipeline parallelism (PP) assigns contiguous groups of layers to different devices. xLLM has no pipeline axis. Pipelining introduces idle “bubbles” unless the per-device batch is large enough to fill the pipeline. K2-V2 showed that, at its operating point, the bubble overhead outweighed the memory benefit ( K2 Team, Institute of Foundation Models, 2025 , Sec. 3.4.2) , and the same reasoning applies at ours.

For the 375B-A23B, these heuristics yield a two-axis layout. Tied $\mathrm{TP}{\equiv}\mathrm{EP}{=}8$ runs within each node, and fully sharded (ZeRO-3) data parallelism spans all remaining devices. We use no context parallelism during 8k-context pre-training and defer it to the mid-training configuration. Master weights are kept in fp32 and sharded by FSDP. Forward and backward compute runs in bf16, and gradient reduction is performed in fp32. To save activation memory, we use selective recomputation: the gated-FFN intermediate activations are recomputed in the backward pass, while the output logits are kept. Attention uses FlashAttention. Smaller models reuse the same framework at smaller allocations (Table 4 ) and different recomputation controls to satisfy memory requirements.

##### 3.3.1 The Phase Transition

The flagship was pre-trained in two phases that share one architecture and one optimizer but differ in their data. ##### 3.3.2 Pre-Training Dynamics & Stability

This subsection describes how the pre-training runs behaved, with a focus on the 375B flagship: how the loss evolved across models, how we detect and count loss spikes, whether the gradient norm warns of them, and how restarts and rollbacks were used to handle them.

The 3.7B, 7B, 32B, and 36B-A4B models trained on the same $22$ T-token corpus, in two data orders: the 3.7B and 36B saw the same batches at every step, as did the 7B and 32B. Their normalized losses follow nearly the same trajectory (Figure 4 ). Each model’s loss is divided by its median over the final $1\%$ of steps and plotted against progress after warmup. The normalization aligns the endpoints by construction; it does not force the earlier part of the curves to agree. The agreement spans dense and sparse models and nearly an order of magnitude in parameters, and is consistent with Bergsma et al. (2026) , who find that loss curves collapse when hyperparameters are scaled together with the training budget, as ours are (§ 3.1.1 ).

> Figure 4 : Normalized pre-training loss of the four models trained on the same $22$ T-token corpus, on the accepted trajectory. Each model’s loss is divided by its median over the final $1\%$ of steps, and progress is counted after the $3{,}000$ -step warmup. Every $100$ th step is shown, without smoothing.

Loss spikes — sharp, transient jumps in the training loss that interrupt an otherwise smoothly decaying curve — are a recurrent instability of large language model pre-training ( Molybog et al., 2023 ; Takase et al., 2025 ) . They have been linked to aggressive learning-rate schedules and to a lagging second-moment estimate in Adam-style optimizers ( Shazeer and Stern, 2018 ; Wortsman et al., 2023 ) . Unlike K2-V2, whose learning rate followed a cosine decay from the start ( K2 Team, Institute of Foundation Models, 2025 ) , our pre-training runs held the peak learning rate for the whole run, except for the 0.9B, whose cosine schedule decays to $10\%$ of the peak. This difference may matter for when spikes occur (see below). To quantify spikes across the complete pre-training history of every K2-Horizon run on a common scale, we use the robust local z-score of K2 Team, Institute of Foundation Models (2025) , which measures how abnormal the current loss is relative to its recent history. For loss values $y_{t}$ over training steps $t$ , we compute a rolling median $m_{t}$ over a trailing window $W(t)$ spanning $1\%$ of the full training duration; to estimate the local variability of a curve whose level falls throughout training, we take, within each window, the median of the absolute deviations from the rolling median:

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{MAD}_{t}\;=\;\operatorname{median}_{s\in W(t)}\bigl|\,y_{s}-m_{s}\,\bigr|,\qquad z_{t}\;=\;\frac{\,y_{t}-m_{t}\,}{1.4826\;\mathrm{MAD}_{t}}.$$ (12)

We flag a loss spike wherever $z_{t}>5$ . One spike episode runs from the first step with $z_{t}>5$ until $z_{t}$ stays below $2$ for three consecutive steps, and a later excursion starts a new episode. Intuitively, $z_{t}$ reports how many typical fluctuations a given step lies above the local baseline, adapting to the local noise level as the loss falls. A run is often resumed from a checkpoint: from the latest one (a restart , rewinding at most the 250-step checkpoint interval) or from an earlier one (a rollback , rewinding further). A run that is resumed repeatedly leaves two histories: the accepted trajectory, the chain of checkpoint-resumed segments that led to the final model, and the branches abandoned along the way, whose rows on superseded segments cover step ranges that were later replayed. We apply the definition to both histories ( K2 Team, Institute of Foundation Models, 2025 ) , treating the spikes on abandoned branches as candidate failure events. Spikes on both histories are scored against the robust statistics $(m_{t},\mathrm{MAD}_{t})$ of the accepted trajectory alone, so an excursion has one consistent meaning across all runs. Table 5 lists, for each run, its length and its counts of spike episodes and rollbacks.

Table 5 applies the same detector to every pre-training run. Four runs trained stably: the 0.9B, 3.7B, 32B, and 36B models were rolled back at most $1.2$ times per $100{,}000$ steps. The 7B ( $3.9$ ) and the flagship ( $13.8$ in phase 1, $4.6$ in phase 2) were not. The flagship’s spikes are brief but heavy-tailed: half of its phase-1 episodes last four steps or fewer, yet the median phase-1 episode peaks at $z\approx 17$ and the largest at $z\approx 950$ (Figure 5 ). Spikes also persist late into training (Figure 7 b). In K2-V2, the vast majority of spikes fall in the first $40\%$ of training ( K2 Team, Institute of Foundation Models, 2025 ) , For our 32B model, the closest counterpart to K2-V2’s dense architecture, only 52% fall in the first 40% when counted the same way (including episodes on erased branches). We hypothesize that the long constant-learning-rate phase is the reason.

The gradient norm gives little warning of a loss spike. We modeled the per-step probability that a spike begins as a function of the gradient norm in the preceding steps, controlling for the time since the last spike, the number of recent spikes, and the time since the last restart. In both phases, the only window with a clear signal is the last ten steps: each standard deviation of elevation in the log gradient norm over those steps multiplies the odds of a spike by $1.9$ in phase 1 and $1.8$ in phase 2. Excursions $11$ to $100$ steps earlier add nothing measurable, and their confidence intervals span one in both phases. This rise is close enough to the onset that it may be part of the spike itself, so we do not read it as an independent warning. The reported norm is measured before clipping, which at a threshold of $1.0$ engaged on about $1.5\%$ of executed steps in phase 1 and $0.5\%$ in phase 2 (Figure 6 ).

The dashed line is the clipping threshold of $1.0$ , which engaged on $1.5\%$ of steps in phase 1 and $0.5\%$ in phase 2.

On the 375B flagship, a large share of the instability never reached the final model, because it occurred on branches that were later discarded when training resumed from a checkpoint; the share is larger in phase 1 than in phase 2. Counting spike episodes at every logged step, phase 1 has 83 episodes on the accepted trajectory and 184 on erased branches; phase 2 has 71 and 38, respectively. Erased branches thus account for $69\%$ of phase-1 episodes and $35\%$ of phase-2 episodes (Figure 7 a; Table 5 ).

Our standard response to a spike was to resume from the latest checkpoint. In 40 cases we instead rolled back to an earlier checkpoint: 29 times in phase 1 and 11 times in phase 2. Of these 40 rollbacks, 35 followed a spike episode, and the deepest rewound $4{,}217$ steps (Figure 7 a).

##### 3.3.3 The Mid-Training Run

Mid-training, which ran at a lower learning rate, was more stable than pre-training. It ran in four stages, each continuing from the end of the previous one: stage 1 at 32k context, stage 2 at 128k, and stages 3 and 4 at 512k. The learning-rate schedule and token budgets are given in § 3.1.3 , and the parallel layout, which changes only by adding context parallelism at 512k, in § 3.2.2 .

Interruptions were far milder than in pre-training. We call a resume from the latest checkpoint a restart and a resume from an earlier checkpoint a rollback. Over the four stages, the run restarted 30 times and was rolled back three times: twice in stage 1, rewinding at most $1{,}249$ steps, and once in stage 2, rewinding $628$ steps (Table 6 ). The deepest of these is less than a third of the flagship’s deepest pre-training rollback ( $4{,}217$ steps), and in stages 3 and 4 every interruption resumed from the latest checkpoint.

Spikes were rare and mostly marginal. Under the detector of § 3.3.2 , the accepted trajectory contains two spike episodes in stage 1, three in stage 2, seven in stage 3, and none in stage 4. The episodes in stages 2 and 3 are marginal ( $z$ between $5.0$ and $5.8$ ); only stage 1 has two larger excursions ( $z$ between $10$ and $20$ ). Within each stage the loss declines (Figure 9 ), for example from an average of $0.81$ over the first $100$ steps of stage 1 to $0.65$ over its last $100$ . Loss values are not comparable across stages, because the data mixture and context length can change from one stage to the next.

### 4 Pre-Training Data

Our 3.7B, 7B, and 32B dense models and the 36B-A4B model were pre-trained on 22T tokens, and the 375B MoE model on about 15T tokens in two phases (Table 2 ). The natural data portion builds on TxT360, the pre-training corpus of K2-V2 ( K2 Team, Institute of Foundation Models, 2025 ) , which we extend with two more web crawls, ClueWeb and HPLT (§ 4.1 ), with public datasets for web text, code, and math (§ 4.6 ), and with multilingual corpora covering 17 languages (§ 4.7 ). The bigger change from K2-V2 is synthetic data: about 10T of the tokens we train on are synthetic, and nearly 17% of the corpus consists of problem-solving trajectories with explicit reasoning (Sections 4.2 , 4.3 , and 4.4 ).

Our goal for synthetic data is to expand the model’s support beyond natural data. We synthesize data both to re-balance the training distribution toward target domains and to add content that is scarce or absent in natural data, such as explicit reasoning. This includes reasoning traces for 250M math problems (§ 4.2 ) and 259M synthesized programming tasks (§ 4.3 ), reasoning on instructions with verifiable constraints (§ 4.4 ), and texts that exhibit behaviors such as planning, weighing alternatives, or counterfactual reasoning (§ 4.5 ). Our hypothesis is that a base model with broader support is better prepared for post-training. This is motivated by our K2-V2 ( K2 Team, Institute of Foundation Models, 2025 ) work where we introduced reasoning in mid-training and by prior work on the relation between base-model support and RL ( Yue et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2025 ; Gandhi et al., 2025 ) .

Broad support requires diverse data. At trillions of tokens, synthetic data easily collapses into a few repetitive patterns, making diversity the main constraint in our data design. We address this with two techniques. First, we introduce “diversity knobs”: independent attributes of a prompt that are sampled and combined, so that the number of distinct prompts grows combinatorially with the number of knobs. This combinatorial explosion yields tens of billions of distinct prompts (e.g., $6.6\times 10^{10}$ concept–knob combinations for programming tasks; § 4.3 ). Second, we ground synthesis in documents retrieved by Search360 4 4 https://github.com/ifm-ai/search360 , our search index over 994M documents from our web corpus (§ 4.5 ). To verify that this works, we measure diversity by compression with WZIP, our own compressor: a less diverse corpus compresses better, and our final pipelines closely track the compression trend of natural web and code data (Figure 14 ).

Most of our synthetic data is not verified: math solutions are not checked against reference answers, and generated code is not executed. We view each document as a dataset of tokens, in which every token is a label for the context before it (Figure 12 ). A trajectory that ends with a wrong answer still contains thousands of tokens of valid steps, notation, and techniques, and its few mislabeled tokens are ordinary label noise. Strict verification-based filtering would discard all of these tokens and reduce data diversity. We do filter defects that corrupt many tokens at once, such as truncated generations, leaked special tokens, and degenerate loops.

> Figure 12 : A document is a dataset of tokens: each token is a label for the context before it. An error such as $2+2=5$ is one mislabeled token, and the tokens around it remain correct labels. This is why we do not discard synthetic trajectories that contain mistakes.

Which mix is best depends on scale. Math and SFT-style data help small models quickly but saturate, while web text pays off as model capacity grows. We therefore chose the mix by fitting scaling laws on small models and predicting benchmark scores at production scale (§ 4.9 ). The prediction reversed our decision: the mix that was best in 1.5B runs is predicted to be the worst of three at 32B (Table 17 ).


#### 4.1 Web data

Our web-data collection builds on the source mixture and curation methodology used for K2-V2 ( K2 Team, Institute of Foundation Models, 2025 ) , combining large-scale web crawls with curated domain-specific sources. For K2-Horizon, we extend this collection with ClueWeb ( Overwijk et al., 2022 ) and HPLT ( Burchell et al., 2025 ) .

The collection comprises the following sources, listed in descending order of cross-source deduplication priority: MegaMath, Wikipedia, HackerNews, StackExchange, PG-19, Ubuntu IRC, FreeLaw, Europarl, Europarl-Aligned, USPTO, arXiv, PhilPapers, S2ORC abstracts, S2ORC full text, PubMed abstracts, PubMed Central, Common Crawl, ClueWeb, and HPLT.

For sources shared with K2-V2, we follow the corresponding source-specific preprocessing and filtering procedures described in that report. The newly added ClueWeb ( Overwijk et al., 2022 ) contributes an additional large-scale web crawl, while HPLT ( Burchell et al., 2025 ) provides web text originating from Common Crawl and the Internet Archive. Both additions undergo the same processing and filtering pipeline used for Common Crawl in K2-V2, including language identification, URL filtering, line-level cleaning, document-level quality filtering, and removal of personally identifiable information. We apply global deduplication across the combined collection, retaining documents from higher-priority sources when duplicates are identified across sources.


##### 4.1.1 Web Data Annotation, Curation, and Shuffling

Annotation. We annotated 6.754 trillion tokens across 7.751 billion web documents, excluding the txt360-qa subset of TxT360 ( Tang et al., 2024 ) , whose QA-annotated passages duplicate web text already in the corpus. Following the ensemble bucketing approach of Nemotron-CC ( Su et al., 2024 ) , we combined FineWeb-Edu, fastText ELI5, and fastText PreSelect scores. Each score was mapped to one of 20 ordered buckets using fixed thresholds; the maximum bucket index determined the document’s quality tier (Table 10 ). This retains documents rated highly by any classifier. We additionally assigned topic and format labels with WebOrganizer’s TopicClassifier and FormatClassifier , which each distinguish 24 categories ( Wettig et al., 2025 ) .

Filtering and curation. We converted heterogeneous inputs, primarily JSONL, to typed Parquet for compact columnar storage and selective reads. The resulting annotations supported construction of the web-high and web-high-medium training subsets. Lazy queries in Polars enabled filtering by quality, topic, format, and document length, and aggregation of counts and scores to compare candidate data mixtures at scale. We used party for document inspection. We release party and other tools we developed for working with pre-training data.

A web document is a compressed record: it states its content but leaves implicit the many facts a careful reader could derive from it, such as which words recur, which entities it concerns, or how often a letter occurs. We make part of this latent content explicit by appending to each web-high document a few questions about the document itself, with answers computed programmatically rather than generated by a model. The augmentation adds no information that is not already in the document; it spells out the computation needed to extract it, so every answer is exact by construction and the augmentation costs no GPU time. Each document was analyzed with spaCy ( en_core_web_sm ) for tokenization, part-of-speech tags, sentence boundaries, and named entities, where a word is a lowercased alphabetic token. From this analysis we instantiated 11 question templates in four families:

- • Frequency : words that appear exactly three times, the longest word that appears more than twice, and the number of occurrences of a random letter.

- • Contextual filtering : the most frequent verbs excluding auxiliaries, words longer than six characters, and capitalized words that do not start a sentence.

- • Threshold and ranking : words that appear between five and ten times, and the three least common words that appear more than once.

- • Semantic grouping : the most frequent action verbs and state verbs (separated by a fixed list of state verbs), and the most frequent people, places, and organizations.

For each document we sampled between one and five of the applicable questions uniformly, shuffled their order, and appended them after a separator and a fixed instruction to answer using only the text above. Answers are short sentences and include counts where the question asks for a ranking. The pipeline was applied to all web-high documents longer than 300 words.

This data corresponds to TxT360-v2 in our release.


#### 4.2 Math reasoning

Our math reasoning data starts from about 250 million unique problems (after exact deduplication) from a variety of sources. These sources are drawn from permissively licensed mathematical datasets studied in the OpenThoughts project ( Guha et al., 2025 ) 5 5 https://www.open-thoughts.ai/blog/thinkagain . To reduce the risk of contamination, we use only training splits where applicable. The resulting collection spans a broad range of problem types and difficulty levels, including templated arithmetic and numerical tasks (e.g., determining whether a large number is prime), verification of mathematical statements, and other mathematical reasoning problems. We previously used this problem set to create mid-training data for K2-V2 ( K2 Team, Institute of Foundation Models, 2025 ) .

We generate solutions for each of the problems with Qwen3-32B with thinking ( Yang et al., 2025 ) and GPT-OSS-120B with high reasoning effort ( Agarwal et al., 2025 ) . Resulting pre-training documents are a simple concatenation of problem statement, reasoning trace, and answer after removing chat template special tokens. We filter out samples that did not finish within a 15.5k-token budget or contained unexpected special tokens (e.g., commentary or extra analysis channels for GPT-OSS). Resulting data consisted of 800B tokens for problems with Qwen3 generations and 424B for problems with GPT-OSS generations.

Solution traces show a single, uninterrupted line of reasoning. To also show how a solver responds to feedback (spotting an error, revising an attempt, confirming a result), we simulate tutoring dialogues between two LLM agents (Figure 13 ). Unlike the dialogue re-writes below, which recast an existing reasoning trace, these dialogues are generated interactively, and the student never sees the answer. We start from the hardest problems in our collection: those whose GPT-OSS-120B solution exceeded 8,192 tokens and that have a reference answer, 8.1M problems in total. More than half of them (4.5M) ask whether a large number is prime or composite (see the “Is it prime?” takeaway below), so we remove every problem that mentions either word, leaving 3.6M problems.

LLM reasoning traces often contain detailed analysis, derivations, and references to relevant mathematical results. However, the “style” of LLM reasoning lacks diversity and can often appear unnatural. To convert knowledge and mathematical techniques from the reasoning traces into more natural text, we developed two re-writing pipelines that we applied to problems with Qwen3 reasoning and answers:

- • Study guide re-write where we instruct GPT-OSS to “Transform the following problem-solving trace into educational content that teaches the concepts and methods used.” resulting in 566B tokens.

- • Dialogue re-write inspired by MIND ( Akter et al., 2025 ) . We adopt five styles: debate , interview , problem_solving , teacher_student , and two_students . For each, we create our own prompt and use GPT-OSS to re-write Qwen3 reasoning and answers into the corresponding dialogue style resulting in 1.5T tokens.

We release the full Math Reasoning data collection .


#### 4.3 Code reasoning

Unlike math, we did not have a large collection of programming problems to start from, so we had to synthesize the tasks ourselves. The key challenge is diversity: simply asking an LLM to write a programming problem over and over tends to produce similar ones. To address this, we seed every task with a random combination of a programming concept, a snippet of real-world code, and a set of diversity knobs.

As programming concepts, we choose 1,304 reference implementations of algorithms and data structures in 66 categories (e.g., graphs, dynamic programming, string processing) from the TheAlgorithms/Python 6 6 https://github.com/TheAlgorithms/Python and keon/algorithms 7 7 https://github.com/keon/algorithms repositories. We pair each concept with a random snippet of 20–30 lines of Python code from RefineCode ( Huang et al., 2025 ) , which serves as inspiration for the setting and style of the task. Diversity knobs then control how precisely the task is specified (from a vague user request to a detailed competitive programming statement), its difficulty, framing (algorithmic, practical, academic, debugging, or optimization), constraint style, application domain (one of 90), and twelve binary switches such as input/output examples, starter code, multi-part structure, type hints, or test-driven development. The knobs alone give 50.3M distinct configurations, or $6.6\times 10^{10}$ concept–knob combinations, before accounting for the code snippets. We made five passes over the 57M Python files in RefineCode, synthesizing one task per file with GPT-OSS-120B with high reasoning effort: three passes use a restricted set of knobs where every task is phrased as a vague user request, and two passes use all knobs. After filtering out generations that were truncated or exceeded 7.5k tokens, we obtained 259M tasks. Even tasks sharing the same concept rarely overlap, with only 1% of 5-grams in common on average.

We generate solutions with GPT-OSS-120B with high reasoning effort and a 32k token budget. Similarly to math, pre-training documents are a concatenation of the task, reasoning trace, and final answer. Code solutions tend to be long (median 8.6k tokens, and 11% exhaust the budget), so we split them by length: solutions within 15.5k tokens resulted in 100.7M documents (761B tokens), while longer ones (up to 31k tokens) resulted in 23.7M documents (543B tokens) that we reserved for mid-training. In both cases, we filter out solutions with stray channel markers. Tasks synthesized with all knobs tend to be more detailed and require longer solutions (median 10.5k vs. 7.7k tokens), so we release them as separate “hard” subsets. In addition, we release a version without reasoning (task and final answer only; 210M documents, 790B tokens), an earlier version of the reasoning data (82M documents), and the task synthesis traces from one of the all-knobs passes (49M documents, 286B tokens).

Finally, we reproduced the CodeI/O pipeline ( Li et al., 2025b ) , which turns code into reasoning about predicting a function’s inputs and outputs, resulting in 23B tokens.

We released the full Code Reasoning data collection .


#### 4.4 SFT reasoning


##### 4.4.1 Instruction-following (IF) reasoning

Real instructions often come with hard requirements on the form of the answer, such as a word limit, a keyword that must appear, or a fixed number of sections. Such requirements can be verified, which makes them a source of reasoning data whose answers are verified without a judge. We synthesized prompts with such constraints and used the responses, including the reasoning, in pre-training.

Each prompt pairs a writing task (e.g., an essay, a tutorial, a professional email, or a report on one of 15 topics) with one to five constraints drawn from 37 constraint types in 15 categories, following the taxonomy of verifiable instructions introduced by IFEval ( Zhou et al., 2023a ) . The types cover keyword frequencies and exclusions, letter counts, the number of paragraphs, sentences, words, bullet points, and highlighted sections, the first and last word of the response or of a paragraph, postscripts and placeholders, copying and repeating spans, the response language, and capitalization. Parameters such as keywords, counts, and positions are sampled for each prompt, and 13 incompatible pairs of types (e.g., two different requirements on the first word) are never combined. The number of constraints is skewed toward fewer constraints, from 41.5% of prompts with one to 1.9% with five. The writing tasks are our own templates, and no prompts are taken from evaluation sets.

We generated responses with GPT-OSS-120B ( Agarwal et al., 2025 ) at low, medium, and high reasoning effort. Every constraint type has a code-based verifier built on regular expressions and string operations, and we kept a response only if it satisfied all constraints. We then removed exact duplicates of the user prompt. Pre-training documents concatenate the prompt, the reasoning trace, and the response, and we removed GPT-OSS-specific artifacts as for our other GPT-OSS data.

The result is 8.6M documents with 44B tokens. The reasoning dominates: its median length is 3.6k tokens, compared with 465 tokens for the response. Deduplication also reshaped the mix: single-constraint prompts have few distinct instantiations, so their share fell from about half of the verified responses to 22%. This data is released as the instruction-following subset of SFT-Reasoning.


##### 4.4.2 SFT data as pre-training text

TxT360-3efforts , the SFT data we released with K2-V2 ( K2 Team, Institute of Foundation Models, 2025 ) , contains conversations in math, code, chat, STEM, instruction following, tool use and safety, with answers mostly generated by GPT-OSS-120B ( Agarwal et al., 2025 ) at low, medium and high reasoning effort. It is a 9.5M-conversation mix sampled from a larger pool in which most questions are answered at all three effort levels. We reuse this pool in pre-training by rendering each conversation, including the reasoning, as plain text, so the same problem appears with reasoning of different lengths.

Rendering tens of millions of conversations with a single template would make the template one of the most repeated patterns in the data. We therefore draw a format for every conversation. In 43% of conversations (68% of those with tools), the turns are marked with role labels drawn from 21 sets (e.g., User: / Assistant: , Human: / AI: , Markdown headers, bracketed or bold labels, and one-letter prefixes), combined with one of 7 turn separators and 7 wrappers around the conversation. The remaining conversations use one of eight other layouts: turns separated only by a delimiter, a document with sections (background, query, analysis, response), question–answer pairs, instruction and response blocks, named personas, an interview transcript, a screenplay, and a compact format with one line per turn. Conversations with tools use only the four layouts that render tool definitions, calls and results, and show the list of tools in one of four styles. In every layout the reasoning precedes the response, in some under its own heading.

The result is 32.2M documents with 79.5B tokens, released as the 3efforts-pretrain subset of SFT-Reasoning.


#### 4.5 Pre-training Behaviors

The question augmentation of web-high makes explicit what a single document leaves implicit. Knowledge about a topic, however, is spread across many documents, and web text rarely shows the behaviors we want a model to acquire, such as explaining a concept to a novice, planning under constraints, weighing alternatives, or reasoning from evidence to a conclusion. We decompress this knowledge across documents: for each question, we retrieve the relevant documents from our pre-training corpus and ask a model to write a text that exhibits a chosen behavior, grounded in what was retrieved. This data corresponds to Pretrain-Behaviors in our release.

We built Search360 8 8 https://github.com/ifm-ai/search360 , a dense retrieval service over the corpus itself. It indexes web-high together with the High-Quality and Medium-High-Quality partitions of Nemotron-CC-v2.1 ( NVIDIA, 2025a ) : 994M documents split into 4.0B passages of 128 tokens, where a final passage shorter than 64 tokens is merged into the one before it. Passages are embedded with Contriever-MSMARCO (768 dimensions) ( Izacard et al., 2021 ) and indexed with FAISS as an IVFPQ index with 4,096 centroids and 16 sub-quantizers of 8 bits, trained on 10M sampled embeddings and searched with 256 probes. The index stores no text. Arrays map each passage to its document and to a byte offset in the original JSONL shards, so a passage or a full document is read with a single seek. A FastAPI service returns either passages or full documents, ranked by the number of retrieved passages they contain, with optional re-ranking by jina-reranker-v3. Behind a round-robin load balancer with health checks, one 8-GPU node answered 1,000 queries in 53 s at 64–128 concurrent requests without re-ranking, or about 19 queries per second.

We started from Quora questions and Natural Questions and asked an LLM to write five deeper follow-up questions for each, which gave about 6M questions. For each question we retrieved the top eight documents in full.

Each prompt template specifies a behavior and receives the question and the retrieved documents, which the generated text must draw on. The templates fall into four families (Table 12 ). The general family has 58 genres, from research abstracts and legal memoranda to lesson plans, documentary scripts, and steelmanned critiques. The reasoning family has 47 modes of reasoning, such as counterfactual, first-principles, and game-theoretic reasoning. The planning family covers plans with risks and mitigations, timelines and dependencies, and fixed resource budgets, and the data science family covers machine learning system design, experimentation, and data analysis. We generated the texts with GPT-OSS-120B ( Agarwal et al., 2025 ) at high reasoning effort and kept only the final text, without the reasoning.

In a document-grounded variant, each prompt also received three attributes sampled from 22, including mood, education level, writing style, perspective, narrative structure, audience, and factuality level, each with 3 to 21 values. The attributes are appended as constraints that the text must satisfy without naming them, so the same document yields, for example, a speculative text for children or a formal, first-person text for experts. In the main run, diversity comes from crossing the templates with the questions and their retrieved documents.

When synthesizing data at trillion-token scale, it is easy to miss repetitive patterns that the model may then learn to reproduce. To quantify diversity, we developed a custom lossless compressor, WZIP. Its key distinction from Zlib/GZIP and Zstandard is the explicit coupling of match-length-dependent sliding windows, dedicated hash indexes, and length-conditioned distance coding . Short matches use compact local histories and specialized distance models, while larger histories use longer, more selective hash seeds to identify distant repetitions. This design controls short-match coding costs and limits unnecessary candidate scanning while retaining access to long-range repetitions. The decoded match length selects the distance model without an additional per-match selector. Together with bounded searches and Huffman coding, this organization targets high compression throughput on large datasets. Under consistent evaluation settings, we use the compression ratio (input bytes per compressed byte) as a quantitative proxy for textual redundancy to assess how our search and generation controls affect corpus diversity, complementing assessments of semantic coverage and data quality.

In Figure 14 , we present WZIP compression ratios for the reasoning pre-training behaviors, comparing them to an earlier version of this pipeline without search and diversity knobs, and to natural code and web data. Code and web data have different baseline compression ratios, but for both the ratio grows slowly with the number of documents due to natural data diversity. Our final pipeline nearly matches the compression ratio trend of natural data, while our earlier version lacks sufficient diversity. Compression ratio was among the key metrics guiding our synthetic data generation development.

#### 4.8 Benchmark decontamination

Benchmark contamination makes evaluation scores look better than the actual model performance. In a corpus of our size, benchmark items can come in through web pages that quote them, through synthetic data built from benchmark problems, or through public datasets that include evaluation splits. We therefore scan all our training data for overlap with the benchmarks and remove the documents that match. A benchmark item that was paraphrased or translated will not be found.

We use the AllenAI decon tool ( Allen Institute for AI, 2025 ) with a few changes. We added Python bindings and a stage that removes contaminated documents. We also use 8-token n-grams for questions, instead of the default 5, so that a match needs a longer overlap. Each benchmark item is split into a question, answer, and an optional passage. This way a document that copies only the question scores lower than one that copies the whole item. To find matches, we build an inverted index of hashed n-grams from the benchmark items and look for a matching 8-gram in each training document. When one is found, the match is extended token by token. The overlap is then scored so that rare n-grams count more than common ones, and short items have to match more closely than long ones. We remove documents that score 0.85 or higher.

We prepared evaluation data for the 208 benchmarks in our registry and scanned every data source against all of them. After looking at the results for each benchmark and each dataset, we removed contamination for the 18 benchmarks we report results on. These make up 27 entries in the registry. For ARC and GSM8K, we only count matches with the test split.

Each match records the file and line of the training document. Removal therefore only rewrites the files that have matches, and every removed line is logged. We remove the whole document and not just the matched text. Contamination is rare in all of our data. The highest rate, 0.026% of documents, is in the SFT reasoning data; the next highest, 0.017%, is in the pre-training behaviors data. In 35 of the 37 datasets with any match, the rate is 0.01% or lower.

Table 15 shows what we removed: 197,149 matches in 88,625 documents from 30 datasets. Only 23 of the 27 registry entries had any match. The table does not include the SFT reasoning data and the Arabic corpus, whose removal had not finished when we collected these numbers. They add another 12,616 matches in 8,462 documents. Most removed matches are near-exact copies, and 172k of them score 0.95 or higher. MMLU and MMLU-Pro account for 166k matches, which is 84.6% of the total. About two thirds of the removed documents come from four datasets: the data science behaviors, the public instruction-style data, and two web datasets.

#### 4.9 Data mix

The corpora above can be mixed in many ways, and the mix that looks best in a small ablation need not be the mix that is best at the scale we actually train. The two ends of our design space make this concrete. SFT-style and mathematical data are dense and immediately useful, but they are a small share of the available tokens and their benefit saturates quickly. Web text is broad and high-entropy, and we expect it to pay off as model capacity grows. We therefore chose the pre-training mix by comparing candidate mixes on their predicted benchmark scores at the target scale rather than on their measured scores at small scale.

We compared three candidates built from the same sources (Table 16 ): a web-heavy mix, a math-heavy mix, and an SFT-heavy mix. They share the code sources and the multilingual budget, and differ mainly in how they divide the remaining budget between web text on one side and mathematical and SFT-style data on the other. The web-heavy mix gives 36.8% of its tokens to web text and 4.1% to SFT-style data; the SFT-heavy mix gives 23.0% and 13.1%.

We predict a large model’s benchmark scores in two stages (Figure 15 ), each fitted only on runs that are cheap enough to repeat many times. Chaining the two stages yields a predicted score for every combination of mix, model size, and benchmark.

Stage 1: validation-loss scaling laws. For each candidate mix, we trained a grid of 33 small models spanning 36M to 1.5B parameters at 10 to 60 tokens per parameter, which gives 0.36B to 59B training tokens. We measured each checkpoint’s loss on a battery of held-out validation sets drawn from our data categories. Losses are measured in bits per byte, $\log_{2}(\mathrm{PPL})$ divided by the tokenizer’s bytes per token, so they are comparable across tokenizers. For each mix and validation set we fit a scaling law in parameters $P$ and training tokens $N$ ( Hoffmann et al., 2022 ; Li and others, 2025 ) . The fitted form is log-linear or additive in $\log P$ and $\log N$ , chosen per validation set. We then evaluated each law at the planned production configurations of 3.6B, 7.0B, and 31.2B parameters trained on 20T tokens. These targets lie about $20\times$ beyond the largest fitted model and about $340\times$ beyond the largest fitted token count.

#### 4.10 Tokenizer

The model uses a standard 256K byte-level BPE tokenizer (64K for 0.9B model) trained from scratch on a custom multilingual and code-focused corpus of approximately 25.2 GB. The training mixture contains about 9.9 GB of English, 5.9 GB of Arabic, 4.2 GB of code and structured text, and additional data spanning Hindi, French, Bengali, Chinese, German, Persian, Filipino, Indonesian, Italian, Japanese, Malayalam, Portuguese, Russian, Spanish, Turkish, and Urdu. The comparatively large Arabic allocation, roughly 24% of the on-disk corpus before including Persian and Urdu, was designed to provide substantially stronger Arabic coverage than general-purpose multilingual tokenizers. Code data includes general source code, JSON, Python, SQL, shell, HTML, and TeX. When tuning the tokenizer training mix, we measured fertility on our own code, TeX files from prior work, and synthesized examples of downstream use cases. This led us to up-weight JSON and Python data, where our first tokenizer was less efficient than other open-model tokenizers.

Text is NFC-normalized and processed with a Qwen-style Unicode-aware pre-tokenizer followed by byte-level BPE, including segmentation of long numerical strings into groups of up to three digits. The complete byte alphabet provides open-vocabulary coverage without an unknown token. The tokenizer includes BOS/EOS tokens plus markers for fill-in-the-middle coding, repository and notebook metadata, and tables. The 256K BPE model (64K for 0.9B model) is followed by 256 special-token IDs, comprising 50 functional markers and 206 reserved slots.


### 5 Mid- and Post-Training data

Our mid- and post-training data targets long context and agentic ability. Unlike common practice, we do not separate these stages explicitly: agentic data enters as soon as context extension begins (§ 3.1.3 ), mixed with long-context pre-training data, agentic data in both pre-training and chat formats, and additional instruction-following and reasoning data. We developed many task generation pipelines and collected public post-training datasets (§ 5.18 ). Together, they provide 106M unique documents and trajectories with 1.17T tokens, with 75% of the trajectories coming from our own pipelines. The data carries over the philosophy of our pre-training data (§ 4 ): diversity first, grounding in real data, and no verification in many pipelines.

Beyond Search360 (§ 4.5 ), we use the Keenable web search API 10 10 https://keenable.ai/ to find relevant sources such as repositories, office files, and papers, with about 5M requests supporting task synthesis. We also curate specialized collections of code and papers, which we query with simple keyword search when creating tasks. Our main curated source is PRism, a collection of GitHub pull requests (§ 5.1 ). Besides fueling four pipelines for agentic coding data (Figure 17 ), PRism turned out to be a rich source of office files: its repositories contain 1.3M PDF, Word, Excel, and PowerPoint files, which we use to synthesize knowledge-work and file manipulation tasks (§ 5.9.2 ).

Diversity remains our primary focus, but a strong teacher is also essential. In SFT (§ 6.6 ), we used the then newly released Kimi K3 ( Kimi Team, 2026b ) and DeepSeek-V4-Flash ( DeepSeek-AI, 2026 ) for both task synthesis and trajectory generation, which improved the performance of our model. A teacher is limited by its own abilities, but the training data we generate does not have to be. We developed generation techniques that produce trajectories beyond what the teacher can do in a single rollout (§ 5.16 ). Multi-turn PR chains replay the PRs of a repository as consecutive requests in one coding session: the teacher handles one PR at a time, while the training trajectory captures the entire session and reaches hundreds of thousands of tokens (§ 5.16.3 ). A privileged advisor uses the reference solution and hidden tests to find the solver’s mistakes and inserts targeted cues into its reasoning, without revealing the solution. Rerunning the tasks the solver failed with an advisor raises the share of tasks with a successful trajectory from 54% to 73% (§ 5.16.4 ). These techniques require extra compute for each trajectory, so we reserve them for the hardest tasks.

A particular focus of our data is AI for Science (§ 5.12 ): tasks and environments built from research papers, a simulated research community, Kaggle competitions, scientific simulators, and proofs from machine learning theory.

As in pre-training, many of these pipelines forgo verifying the final answer (Figure 12 ). Instead, every trajectory must pass strict programmatic checks of format and behavior (§ 5.17 ). Below, we describe our task generation pipelines organized by capability, followed by how we ensure the quality of the training trajectories.


#### 5.1 PRism Data Processing

We construct a corpus of GitHub pull requests (PRs) that combines development discussions with the corresponding code changes and repository context. The pipeline comprises three components: PRism-curator reconstructs and filters PR records, PRism-annotator adds structured semantic labels, and PRism-synthesis transforms the resulting data into synthetic reasoning documents, coding tasks, executable tasks for reinforcement learning, and multi-turn agent trajectories. This section describes curation and annotation; synthesis is covered separately. Figure 17 summarizes the data flow.

#### 5.2 PRism Data Curation

We process GitHub Archive events and retain information relevant to PRs, including PR updates, comments, reviews, pushes, and continuous integration events. Events are normalized and partitioned by repository identifier for parallel processing. We then group records by repository identifier and PR number, associating discussions and related events through PR references and commit identifiers. Each reconstructed record preserves the PR metadata, participants, available description revisions, and associated events.

We retain merged PRs targeting the main or master branch, with descriptions longer than ten characters after whitespace removal and changes to between one and fifteen files. PR descriptions may contain at most one distinct issue-style reference of the form #123 . A keyword filter excludes PRs whose titles or descriptions indicate dependency or version updates. We also require an identifiable landed commit, obtained from the recorded merge commit or inferred from push events. These criteria select changes with bounded scope and accompanying textual context.

For each retained PR, we retrieve the repository history and resolve the base and head revisions. Where available, the merge base defines the starting revision for extracting the change. We collect the combined diff, individual commit patches, changed file paths, repository file list, and pre-change contents of the affected files. Extraction is subject to configurable limits on file counts and content size; truncation and retrieval errors are recorded. The enriched records are stored in Parquet format, preserving the original PR fields and adding the extracted code context. This representation links the description and discussion of a change to its implementation and initial repository state.


#### 5.3 PRism Annotation

We use a language model with a fixed rubric to annotate each PR along three dimensions. The domain label identifies the principal technical area, such as data engineering, security, or compiler development. Change attributes provide multiple labels for properties such as bug fixes, feature additions, refactoring, and the inclusion of tests. The complexity label describes the predominant reasoning requirement: knowledge of frameworks or conventions, multi-step logical reasoning, or recognition of a subtle behavior or edge case. These categories describe the nature of the task rather than an ordinal difficulty level.

The annotation prompt includes PR metadata, discussion, repository information, and extracted code context. The rubric prioritizes code evidence, followed by PR text and labels, repository metadata, and discussion or continuous integration metadata. The model is instructed to score each candidate label against explicit criteria and return the selected labels, scores, and short rationales. Domain and complexity annotations allow a NONE label when evidence is insufficient, while change attributes may be empty. The prompt also specifies consistency rules for conflicting attributes.

Annotations are generated through vLLM with temperature zero and a JSON schema constraint. Long inputs are truncated to fit the available context, and the original and retained prompt lengths are recorded. PRs with code-retrieval errors remain in the dataset but are left unannotated. The output preserves all input columns and appends the annotations, parsing status, and prompt truncation metadata. The resulting corpus retains the source evidence alongside its semantic labels, supporting subsequent analysis and preparation of inputs for the synthesis pipelines.

#### 5.4 Developer thoughts synthesis

PR data records the detailed history of a repository but not the reasoning behind each change. To mitigate this gap, we turn human-written code changes into long-context agentic data in text format by asking an LLM to synthesize only the missing thoughts, while all the code comes from the PR itself.

We use about 21M curated PRs from 2020 to 2024 (§ 5.2 ). For every PR, we show its metadata, discussion, files before the change, and commit diffs to Qwen3.5-397B-A17B ( Qwen Team, 2026 ) and ask it to write three kinds of prose: a task description, the developer’s reasoning before each commit (or before each file within a commit), and a closing summary. We skip PRs with code-retrieval errors or whose prompt would exceed 32k tokens. We then splice the prose around the real repository tree, files before the change, and commit diffs. The result reads like an agentic coding session in text format: a task, the relevant files, and a sequence of reasoning steps, each followed by an actual code edit. All code in the document is real; only the thoughts are synthetic.

Applying a single prompt and a single document template to millions of PRs would produce highly repetitive data. We therefore draw knobs for every PR that control both what the LLM writes and how the final document is rendered. Prompt knobs set the style of the task description (GitHub issue, direct instruction, conversational, or specification), its level of detail, whether it includes a plan or names the files to change, and whether reasoning is written per commit or per file. Rendering knobs set the diff format (unified, search/replace, or full file), what is shown at the end (combined diff, files after the change, or both), line numbers and their style, how files are delimited, truncation of long files, whether files are shown upfront or right before the commit that touches them, whether and how the repository tree is shown, where the repository is mentioned, and the connector phrases between sections. Since rendering knobs are applied after generation, the same LLM output can be turned into many different documents.

Documents over 128k tokens are re-rendered more compactly (no files after the change, harder truncation of long files), and documents over 512k tokens are dropped. The resulting 14.6M documents (181B tokens) cover a wide range of lengths: 8.8M documents are under 8k tokens, while over 1M documents are between 32k and 512k tokens.


#### 5.5 PRism task synthesis

PRs are a natural source of realistic coding tasks for agents. We turn curated PRs into standalone tasks that can be solved on the repository before the change, wrap them into instructions for a coding agent, and collect agent trajectories solving them.

Starting from the curated PRs (§ 5.2 ), we give GPT-OSS-120B with high reasoning effort ( Agarwal et al., 2025 ) the PR metadata, commit subjects, combined diff, files before the change, and, optionally, the discussion comments, and ask it to write the task that the PR solves as an independent assignment, without referencing the PR or its diff. We skip PRs whose touched files exceed 5,000 lines in total, as well as prompts over 32k tokens. The task is then combined with general instructions for an agent working in the repository, including language-specific setup hints based on the primary programming language of the repository.

We do not verify the solutions produced by agents on these tasks, so controlling task difficulty is important: tasks that are too hard are likely to result in incorrect solutions, while tasks that are too easy teach little. The main knob here is the amount of hints, with five levels: bare (a one- to three-sentence request, as typed in a chat), concise (a title and a short description), standard (adds pointers to environment setup files and a high-level plan), guided (adds a step-by-step plan and testing hints), and detailed (adds pointers to the files, functions, and design decisions). We sample the level based on the size of the PR, measured by the number of commits, diff lines, and touched files. Small PRs mostly get few hints (bare or concise 65% of the time), while large PRs mostly get many (guided or detailed 65% of the time). Every level appears at every PR size, but overall the distribution is biased towards balancing difficulty: large changes become more approachable with more guidance, while small changes stay realistic and underspecified. The agent instructions follow the opposite pattern: the fewer hints a task contains, the more detailed the instructions and the stricter the requested verification.

Other knobs vary the style of the task (direct instruction, GitHub issue, or specification), the persona (direct request or a project maintainer guiding a contributor), the scope (the minimal targeted change or open to restructuring related code), whether the PR discussion is shown to the LLM, and how the task is rendered (Markdown, plain text, or conversational, with varied headers, separators, and section names). The agent instructions vary in verbosity, workflow (phases, checklist, or free-form), verification strictness, guidance on honestly reporting problems, and response format.

We run Qwen3.5-397B-A17B with thinking ( Qwen Team, 2026 ) in the OpenCode agent harness on the synthesized tasks, each in a sandbox with the repository at its state before the change. The agent explores the repository, sets up the environment, edits code, and runs tests using tools such as bash , read , and edit . The resulting 2.3M trajectories (70B tokens) are used in mid-training.


#### 5.6 PRism RL task synthesis

We use the same PR corpus as mentioned in § 5.2 to create agentic coding tasks for RL training. Each task needs an environment in which the agent’s solution can be run and verified. To build them, we filter the corpus, rewrite each remaining PR into a task, synthesize an environment for it, and filter the environments with a judge that inspects solver trajectories (Figure 19 ).

A task has four parts: the repository at the commit before the PR, an instruction that describes the change without revealing the solution, a sandbox in which the project’s tests run, and a verifier that decides whether the agent succeeded. The PR also gives us a reference solution (fix patch) which we use to check the verifier but never show to the agent. A good task needs a verifier that fails on the original code and passes with the reference solution. The task also has to be solvable, so that a failed attempt reflects its difficulty rather than a broken environment. The first property can be checked deterministically; the second needs an LLM judge and static checks.

Most PRs cannot be turned into a usable task, so we drop PRs annotated as documentation-only, version bumps, reformatting, code regeneration, or refactoring, unless they also contain a functional change. We also drop bot PRs, diffs over 200k characters, binary-only diffs, PRs against forks, PR bodies under 100 characters, and repositories with fewer than six stars or no package manifest. GPT-OSS-120B ( Agarwal et al., 2025 ) then scores the remaining PRs on eight dimensions, and the quality score weights content strength 0.60 and environment feasibility 0.40. Finally, we sample a task set balanced across 17 domains and label each PR as easy, medium, or hard relative to PRs with the same star bucket, domain, and language.

A merged PR already contains its answer, so we rewrite it into a specification of what to implement, with the level of detail set by the difficulty knobs. The reference patches come directly from the diff against the merge base which we split by file into a fix_patch and a test_patch . An LLM writes the title, description, acceptance criteria, and test commands, and a second pass compares the draft with the test patch to remove identifiers leaked from the fix and claims the tests do not check.

A task also needs an environment to run and a verifier we can trust. An agent using the OpenCode harness based on Qwen3.5-397B-A17B ( Qwen Team, 2026 ) builds both, delegating to sub-agents that explore the repository, set up the runtime, write and audit the evaluation script, and build the Dockerfile. It works in a blobless clone at the merge base with all git history and references removed. It installs dependencies with the project’s own package manager and checks with a dry run that the tests can be collected; it has at most 16 turns to finish. Hidden tests stay outside the working tree. An environment is accepted only if it is fail-to-pass : the evaluation script fails on the original repository and passes with the reference patch applied, both run inside the built image. Because the same agent writes the environment and its verifier, a test-analyst sub-agent and static checks audit the verifier before acceptance. Accepted environments are exported in Harbor format ( task.toml , instruction.md , environment/ , tests/ , and solution/ ) with a binary reward.

Passing fail-to-pass does not mean that a task is useful for training. We therefore run a solver on each accepted environment and judge its trajectory on five dimensions, including environment readiness, dependency completeness, and verifier integrity. The judge first identifies which of 18 failure patterns occur (e.g., a runtime that never starts, a missing package, tests that never run, or a solver that searches without editing), and lowers a dimension’s score only when it has identified a matching pattern. A deterministic rule then maps the scores to quality buckets like keep, downweight, or reject. When the judge flags a mismatch between the instruction and the verifier, we repair the task instead of discarding it: a faulty verifier is realigned with the instruction, and an unclear instruction is rewritten from the solver trajectory, with up to two candidates that are checked for leakage and accepted only if a new solver run scores higher. With this approach, we created roughly 64k environments that passed validation, of which 38k were rated high quality and used for RL training.


#### 5.7 Subagent delegation

Long coding tasks require an agent to spend context on repository exploration, file contents, command output, and test results. To study whether delegation can improve this process, we collect trajectories in which a parent agent may assign one bounded piece of work to a child agent. Each child runs in a fresh OpenCode session and returns a final report to the parent, which then checks the report and decides whether to use it. The complete parent and child sessions are retained separately.

We start from Harbor coding environments derived from merged pull requests. The original sandbox and verifier are left unchanged. Kimi K3 is used for task-instruction synthesis. Given a digest of the original change, it writes a short description of the required behavior, the shared interface between the workstreams, and several pieces of work that can be developed and checked independently. A fixed template then asks the solver to validate these workstreams and integrate the result before finishing. The instruction synthesizer produces prose only. We reject drafts that mention files from the reference patch, copy patch text, or explicitly instruct the solver to delegate or use subagents. This results in 908 tasks.

We run Kimi K3 with thinking enabled at maximum reasoning effort in the OpenCode harness ( Anomaly, 2026 ) , orchestrated by our rollout engine, with Harbor executing the sandbox and verifier. The parent agent has the usual repository tools and a task tool that can launch one of two specialists in a fresh context. The investigator traces repository behavior and answers a focused question; the implementer performs one bounded change. The parent prompt describes these roles and asks the parent to verify load-bearing details in a child’s report before relying on them. Delegation is optional: the task instruction does not require a child call, and recursive delegation is disabled. Parent and child tool calls are executed within the same Harbor task environment, while their OpenCode sessions and trajectories remain separate.

We retain valid four-hour rollouts together with eight additional valid trials from the two-hour run. Of the 908 tasks, 850 produced at least one valid trajectory, while 58 failed both attempts. Harbor evaluates each trial with the original task verifier.

Each parent and child session is serialized as a separate training trajectory. The parent trajectory contains the task call and the report returned by the child; the child document preserves the detailed work performed in the child session. A trial whose Harbor verifier returns reward 1 is labeled correct. Child labels are inherited from the parent trial and therefore are parent-level labels rather than independent child-verifier judgments.

#### 5.8 SWE Efficiency

Our SWE Efficiency pipeline, inspired by Ma et al. (2025) , transforms real-world, performance-oriented pull requests into reproducible optimization tasks and agentic engineering trajectories. Curation identifies suitable changes, prepares executable environments, and establishes correctness checks and representative performance workloads. During generation, coding agents investigate the repository, analyze behavioral invariants and compatibility requirements, develop optimizations, and iteratively validate their changes through tool execution and feedback. The emphasis is on evidence-grounded engineering: proposed improvements must preserve required behavior, and performance claims must be supported by measurements. We retain interaction histories and execution evidence to enable traceability, quality assessment, and subsequent auditing. Reviews consider functional correctness, measured performance, and consistency between the agent’s explanations and observed results, with accepted trajectories maintained separately from lower-confidence records. With this pipeline, we synthesized 700 SFT-format agentic traces.


#### 5.9 File Manipulation

We built two pipelines producing teacher trajectories for office work with files such as spreadsheets, documents, slide decks, PDFs, HTML pages and technical drawings. They are inspired by GDPval ( Patwardhan et al., 2025 ) . A task consists of optional input files and an instruction, usually an e-mail from a manager or colleague or an assignment brief, that asks for one or more files to be created or edited, or for a written answer drawn from the input files.

Both pipelines start each task from real files. They differ in where the files come from: the first collects public documents from the web (§ 5.9.1 ), and the second mines office files from the repositories of our PR dataset (§ 5.9.2 ).
