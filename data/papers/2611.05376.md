## Résumé

For a decade, progress in artificial intelligence has been measured by how much a machine can do on its own. Large foundation models now write code and run multistep tasks, and personal agents act on a person's behalf. Yet people still forget what they once knew, miss what they are not looking at, fail to find the right words, and face hard choices without the benefit of their own history, because the machine that could help stands outside the situation. As AI moves into the physical world, attention has settled on one kind of intelligent individual, the embodied robot; we argue that the era of Physical AI holds a second kind of equal importance, the person augmented by AI. Alongside artificial general intelligence, which asks how intelligent a machine can be, stands AI-augmented human intelligence (AHI), which asks how much intelligence a machine can enable in a particular person. Part I

The Paradigm


### 1 Introduction

For the past decade, progress in artificial intelligence has been scored on a single axis: how much a machine can accomplish on its own. Measured on that axis, the results are substantial. AI models now can write and review code, draft and summarize documents, search, plan, and carry out multistep tasks on a computer ( OpenAI, 2023 ; Yang et al., 2024 ; Anthropic, 2024a ) . Productivity at the desk, the part of work that takes place in front of a screen, has been transformed and in large part solved. An ordinary day, however, still contains the same losses it always did, and one person’s day is enough to show them. In the morning, working alone, a researcher has an idea that slips away before it is written down. In the afternoon, in a meeting, a colleague asks about a result she published three years ago, and she cannot recall the number. In the evening, facing a decision, she cannot remember how she reasoned the last time she faced one like it. Two further losses belong to the same family: a visitor in an unfamiliar country who does not know how to decline an invitation politely, and someone who has just failed at something, who needs one sentence from a person who knows their story and receives a paragraph of generic encouragement instead. The first three are failures of access, in which something the person already possesses fails to reach the moment in which it is needed, and the last two are gaps of knowledge and of support that the person cannot close alone at that moment; none of the five is a failure of intelligence. In each case an AI that holds the relevant knowledge exists, and in each case it is absent from the situation: it does not know who this person is, where they are, what they are trying to do, or what they are feeling, and it has no natural way to interact and communicate with them in the moment. Sections 9.2 , 8.6 , and 6.6 return to the three moments of this day.

Most readers already carry a picture of the system that would close these gaps. In the Iron Man films, Jarvis is always present, has known its owner for years, acts while he is speaking and moving, and never asks him to stop and explain. 1 1 J.A.R.V.I.S. appears in Iron Man (2008) through Avengers: Age of Ultron (2015). We use it only as a shared reference point, never as evidence. The reference is useful because it allows the subject of this perspective paper to be stated in one sentence: we are concerned with how every person could have such a system, and with how far beyond Jarvis such a system should go. The vignette Beyond Jarvis in Section 4.2 lists six directions, of which three matter most here, and each is developed later in the paper. Jarvis sees through a laboratory and a suit that its owner built, whereas a symbiont for everyone must see through a handful of affordable external devices, and it gains channels Jarvis never had, such as physiological signals, first-person video, and the owner’s own account, in their own words, of what they are thinking. Jarvis mostly executes and alerts, and rarely models its owner’s goals, emotions, or blind spots, whereas the center of gravity of a symbiont is exactly that layer of mind. And Jarvis gives almost no help with other people or other cultures, which is where a symbiont is most distinctive. Section 4.2 makes this comparison precise.


#### 1.1 The Other Half of the Endgame

AI is leaving the screen for the physical world, and with it comes the question of what kinds of intelligent individuals that world will contain. The answer that has drawn most of the field’s attention is the embodied robot, an AI with a body, senses, and goals of its own ( Gemini Robotics Team, 2025 ; NVIDIA, 2025 ) . We argue that the era of Physical AI will contain a second kind of individual of equal importance: a person whose own senses, memory, and judgment are joined to an AI that lives on their body (Section 2.2 ). This paper is a perspective on that second kind of individual. Foundation models, world models, and methods of self-improvement enter it as enabling technologies (Section 3 ) and as replaceable components (Section 7.4 ), while the proposal itself concerns the form in which an augmented person exists and is helped.

The second kind of individual also changes what progress in AI is measured against, and in this sense the endgame of AI has two halves. Artificial general intelligence (AGI) asks how intelligent the machine can be . The other half, which we call AI-augmented human intelligence (AHI) , asks how much intelligence a machine can enable in a particular person . The two are built on the same base: the same foundation model can act as an autonomous agent that completes tasks, or as a cognitive symbiont that augments one human being. What differs is the object of evaluation, the mode of existence, and the relationship with the person (Figure 2 ). This paper is entirely about the second half, and it makes two commitments to the first. A symbiont delegates errands to standard agents (Section 9.1 ), and it improves with every gain in the base model (Section 7.4 ).

> Figure 2 : The two halves of the endgame. AGI runs from chatbots to autonomous agents and is scored on what the AI completes. AHI runs from reactive assistants to cognitive symbionts and is scored on what the person can now do. Both share one base of models, agents, memory, and hardware, and both inherit every improvement to it. A system sits on one half or the other according to what it is evaluated on, so the same chat product is a chatbot when its answers are scored and a reactive assistant when what its user can then do is scored; today the first two stages of the AHI half are still scored on what the AI produces (Table 1 ). Arrows mark growth within a half, and the two halves are parallel, not successive stages.


#### 1.2 The Bottleneck Has Moved

A person’s cognitive bandwidth is fixed. Working memory holds a few items, attention goes one place at a time, and a day has twenty-four hours. The capability of AI on isolated tasks keeps rising, but the coupling between an AI and a given person has barely changed: how much the AI knows about the person, how fast it can step in, how much it interrupts when it does, and how far the person trusts it. The result is a widening gap between what a model could contribute and what a person actually receives (Figure 3 a). The next step for personal AI is tighter coupling, and tighter coupling is a different problem from a larger model. The claim requires qualification, since coupling is itself built on strong base models; Section 3 describes which technologies first made coupling possible, and the paper opposes only the assumption that a larger base is sufficient. Three observable symptoms mark insufficient coupling: every conversation starts by re-explaining the background, help arrives too late or too loudly, and people entrust nothing important to the system. They point, respectively, to Sections 6 , 8 , and 12 . Figure 3 b shows the mechanism by which the relationship nonetheless shifts: stronger models absorb more context, which lowers the cost of being understood, which moves interaction off the screen and onto the body, which produces a new kind of context, the record of a life, which makes those models more useful to that person in turn.

> Figure 3 : (a) AI capability on isolated tasks keeps rising while coupling with a particular person stays nearly flat; the shaded region is augmentation that is never realized. The curves are a conceptual sketch, not measurements. (b) The mechanism of the shift: a causal loop in which stronger base models lower the cost of being understood, move interaction onto the body, and generate a new kind of context that makes the models more useful to each person in turn, without being used to train them.


#### 1.3 Risk and Legitimacy

A system that makes a person more capable in front of other people raises questions that a system which only completes tasks does not. When a student can recall a textbook during an examination, or a negotiator is handed a line that reads the other side’s hesitation, the gain of one person is a cost to another, and the other party usually does not know. The same privacy that keeps the exchange between a person and their symbiont from intruding on others also hides the help from an examiner, an interviewer, or a friend. Products built on this capability have already been sold for cheating, as Cluely was in 2025 ( TechCrunch, 2025 ) , and chatbot providers have switched off features for the duration of China’s college entrance examination ( Bloomberg News, 2025 ) . Public attitudes toward cognitive enhancement are cautious, and the main objection is fairness ( Pew Research Center, 2022 ; Faber et al., 2016 ) . We treat these concerns as part of the proposal. A symbiont differs from earlier cognitive enhancements in a respect that matters for governance: it is external, removable, and inspectable, so it can follow the rules of the settings a person enters and show that it did. Part IV develops the risks and the governance that follows from this property.


#### 1.4 Thesis and Contribution

Our thesis is the following. Realizing the second half of the endgame requires an AI that exists in a new way: a cognitive symbiont that senses what the person senses, keeps their life, models their situation and their mind, takes part in their thinking at the right moment, carries out their errands with their context in hand, and keeps learning them while both of them change. The paper develops this thesis in four parts. Figure 1 lays them out. Part I (Sections 2 to 4 ) locates the paradigm historically and technically and gives it a conceptual core. Part II (Sections 5 to 12 ) follows one loop from sensing to trust, in which the output of each section is the input of the next. Part III (Sections 13 and 14 ) asks who gains and what a society of augmented individuals looks like. Part IV (Sections 15 to 18 ) examines what could go wrong, how augmentation can be governed, and how the claims of the paper can be measured.

The contribution of the paper is the paradigm itself, taken as a whole. We name a second kind of intelligent individual for the era of Physical AI, the person augmented by AI, and a second half of the endgame of AI, AI-augmented human intelligence, which differs from the first by its object of evaluation, its mode of existence, and its relationship with the person. We propose the cognitive symbiont as the form of system that realizes it, define it through the cognitive gap and six necessary properties, and organize its design space as a symbiosis loop whose stages are each given a mechanism, a set of research questions, and a way of being evaluated. We treat the risks of augmentation, to the person and through the person to others, as part of the paradigm, and propose that a symbiont follow the rules of the settings its owner enters and be able to show that it did. We change the unit of evaluation from the AI to the augmented individual and state the open problems of the direction as a research program. We propose no new algorithm; the claim of the paper is to the framing, to the organization of the design space, and to the standard of evidence it sets. Our hope is that the concept will orient a new paradigm of AI research, one in which the measure of progress is how much more capable each particular person has become.


### 2 Where We Stand

This section sets up the coordinate system used throughout the paper. The vertical axis is the relationship: four roles the AI has played with a person, in the order in which each became technically possible. The horizontal axis is existence: two forms an intelligent individual can take. We then place the paper in eighty years of work on the same question and state what has changed.


#### 2.1 Four Roles in Human–AI Relations

What the AI knows widens from the current message to a whole life. Where the AI sits moves from a chat window to a computer, to the cloud, and finally onto the body. And the value it offers shifts from giving the person information, to doing tasks for them, to doing those tasks their way, to making the person themselves more capable. The first three roles share an evaluation target, the AI, and the fourth changes it to the person.

#### 2.2 Two Forms of Intelligent Individuals

On the horizontal axis, we foresee, there are two ways for an intelligent individual to exist in the future world of Physical AI (Figure 4 ). An AI-augmented individual is a real person who gains a symbiont through wearables and other interfaces. The person remains the subject: the goals are theirs, the body is theirs, and the AI borrows their senses and their hands. An AI as individual is an embodied robot with its own body, perception, actions, and goals. The two forms matter equally for the future we describe, and this paper is concerned only with the first. Our central claim about the pair is that they share a cognitive core : long-term memory, a model of the situation and of other minds, timely intervention, and learning from experience. They differ in three places only: whose goals, whose body, and whose responsibility. The cognitive capabilities developed in Sections 6 to 10 therefore transfer to embodied robots, and this is the source of the paradigm’s generality; Section 14.2 returns to it. The third column of Figure 4 , AI-driven devices in the environment, appears in the paper only as the symbiont’s extended reach into the physical world (Section 14.2 ).

> Figure 4 : Two forms of intelligent individuals. Left: an AI-augmented individual, a person with a symbiont that senses and speaks through wearable devices. Middle: an AI as individual, an embodied robot. The two share one cognitive core, indicated by the common bar. Right, in lighter tone: devices in the environment that either form can reach into (Section 14.2 ). The paper’s scope is the left form. “AI as individual” is a functional description and carries no claim about consciousness or legal personhood.


#### 2.3 Eighty Years of the Same Question

Using machines to augment the human intellect is an old question, and Table 2 places the paper inside that lineage. Licklider (1960) imagined people and computers thinking together in real time; Engelbart (1962) made the unit of augmentation the person together with their tools and methods; Clark and Chalmers (1998) argued that cognition can extend into external tools; Weiser (1991) and the first wearable computing researchers ( Starner et al., 1997 ; Mann, 1997 ) put computing into daily life and onto the body; and lifelogging, from Bush ’s Memex ( 1945 ) through MyLifeBits ( Gemmell et al., 2006 ) and SenseCam ( Hodges et al., 2006 ) , recorded a life and showed that the record could support memory. More recent neighbors are egocentric AI ( Grauman et al., 2022 ; Yang et al., 2025 ) , mixed-initiative interaction ( Horvitz, 1999 ; Amershi et al., 2019 ) , just-in-time adaptive interventions in mobile health ( Nahum-Shani et al., 2018 ) , the thought-partner program of Collins et al. (2024) , and the lifecycle view of context engineering ( Hua et al., 2025 ) . For each line the table records what has been settled and what we ask next, and each of those questions is taken up in a later section. Our claim with respect to these lines is a narrow one: none of them has taken, as its target, the augmentation of a particular person across a life.

#### 2.4 Why Now

Five conditions turn an eighty-year-old question into a tractable research problem, and each is developed in the next section. Foundation models understand language, images, and sound and can reason over them, so that a symbiont has, for the first time, a brain (Section 3.1 ). Agent technology lets a model plan, use tools, operate a computer, and run for a long time, so that it has hands (Section 3.2 ). Long-term memory, retrieval, and context management let it keep an understanding of one person across months and years (Section 3.3 ). World models let it begin to predict what the environment and the person will do next (Section 3.4 ). And wearable devices have matured to the point where first-person audio and video can be captured all day and a reply can be delivered to the ear with low latency, so that the symbiont can have senses and be present (Section 3.5 ). What has not changed matters equally: the social infrastructure of privacy and trust, the observability of inner states, the stability of continual learning, and the cost of computing around the clock. These are the sources of Sections 12 and 17 .

#### 3.1 Foundation Models

The brain of a symbiont comes from four steps in the development of foundation models. Scale came first: the Transformer ( Vaswani et al., 2017 ) , in-context learning at the scale of GPT-3 ( Brown et al., 2020 ) , and the scaling laws that made the next steps predictable ( Kaplan et al., 2020 ; Hoffmann et al., 2022 ) . Alignment made the models usable by ordinary people in ordinary language ( Ouyang et al., 2022 ) , and GPT-4 ( OpenAI, 2023 ) together with openly released weights ( Touvron et al., 2023 ) spread the capability. Reasoning followed, from chain-of-thought prompting ( Wei et al., 2022 ) to models that think before they answer ( OpenAI, 2024b ; Guo et al., 2025 ) ; this is what makes the slow loop of Section 8.2 possible. The fourth step is the one that matters most here. Multimodal and then omni-modal models took images into the language model ( Radford et al., 2021 ; Alayrac et al., 2022 ; Liu et al., 2023 ) , then speech and video in real time ( OpenAI, 2024a ; Gemini Team, 2024 ) , then any-to-any input and output ( Wu et al., 2024 ; Xu et al., 2025a ) , and full-duplex speech models learned to listen and speak at the same time ( Défossez et al., 2024 ) . Context windows of a million tokens made the inclusion of an entire day in the context window expensive rather than impossible. For a symbiont, omni-modality is a requirement: its input is a first-person stream of sight and sound and its output is a whisper or a line of text, and a model that only reads and writes text never reaches the scene. What foundation models lack can be stated with equal precision. They have no persistence, since each conversation starts from nothing; no sense of time; no body; and they are trained on the average person and know nothing about the one in front of them. Sections 6 , 8 , 5 , and 7 answer these four gaps in turn.


#### 3.2 Agents and Harnesses

An agent is a model system that repeatedly observes, thinks, and acts toward a goal. A harness is everything outside the model that turns it into an agent: the loop itself, tool interfaces, memory, context management, planning and decomposition, permissions and sandboxing, verification of results, and reusable procedural knowledge, often called skills. The same model placed in different harnesses performs very differently, a fact that recurs across published results on coding agents, and it makes the harness a research object in its own right. The line of work runs from interleaving reasoning with action ( Yao et al., 2023 ) , learning to call tools ( Schick et al., 2023 ) , and reflecting on failure ( Shinn et al., 2023 ) , to agents that build a library of reusable skills ( Wang et al., 2024 ) , which is an early precedent for a harness that improves itself (Section 10.4 ). Generative Agents gave agents a memory stream, reflection, and planning ( Park et al., 2023 ) , which remains the closest academic prototype of an agent with a life, and CoALA organized these components in the language of cognitive architectures ( Sumers et al., 2024 ) . On the engineering side, Anthropic’s guide to building agents argued for simple, composable patterns and for the agent–computer interface as something to be designed ( Schluntz and Zhang, 2024 ) ; SWE-agent showed that an interface built for the agent changes its performance ( Yang et al., 2024 ) ; computer-use agents learned to operate any desktop or web page ( Anthropic, 2024a ; OpenAI, 2025b ) ; the Model Context Protocol standardized tool access ( Anthropic, 2024b ) ; and coding agents such as Claude Code and Codex became the first autonomous agents in large-scale daily use ( Anthropic, 2025 ; OpenAI, 2025a ) . In 2026 Muse and Dots pushed agents into personal life, and the Muse Gadget SDK for embedded platforms is a sign that agent platforms are reaching into devices ( Meta, 2026a ; Meta, 2026b ; OpenAI, 2026 ) . The benchmarks of the field all score task completion ( Zhou et al., 2024 ; Xie et al., 2024 ; Jimenez et al., 2024 ; Yao et al., 2025 ; Mialon et al., 2024 ) .

For a symbiont this line provides hands. Booking a trip, buying something, handling a desktop, writing code: today’s agents can already do these, and a symbiont delegates such work through its harness (Section 9.1 ). Beyond delegation, a symbiont is a new kind of harness, one organized around a life instead of a task (Section 11.3 ). Today’s harnesses are task-level: they have a beginning and an end, they are driven by requests, their tools are APIs, their memory is scoped to the task, and their score is completion. They have no body, no first-person senses, no model of the person, and no output that means “do nothing.” Sections 8 , 9 , and 11 take up these gaps.


#### 3.3 Memory, Context, and Personalization

Three kinds of work let a model remember a person. Memory systems grew from retrieval-augmented generation ( Lewis et al., 2020 ) to MemGPT, which brought the tiered storage of an operating system into the model ( Packer et al., 2023 ) ; to the memory stream and reflection of Generative Agents ( Park et al., 2023 ) and MemoryBank ( Zhong et al., 2024 ) ; to A-MEM, which lets memories link to one another and evolve ( Xu et al., 2025b ) ; to Mem0, a production-oriented long-term memory layer ( Chhikara et al., 2025 ) ; and to sleep-time compute, which organizes memory while the system is idle ( Lin et al., 2025 ) . LoCoMo and LongMemEval measure long-conversation memory ( Maharana et al., 2024 ; Wu et al., 2025 ) . Context management is the second kind: Context Engineering 2.0 describes a lifecycle of collecting, managing, and using context, and observes that the practice is shifting from prompting a model toward managing everything a long-running system knows ( Hua et al., 2025 ) ; long context, cache compression, hierarchical summaries, and on-demand loading are the engineering means. Personalization is the third: LaMP measures generation tailored to a user ( Salemi et al., 2024 ) ; parameter-efficient adaptation makes a small module per user feasible ( Hu et al., 2022 ) ; test-time training and its descendants change weights during use ( Sun et al., 2020 ; Sun et al., 2025 ; Behrouz et al., 2025 ) ; and the continual-learning literature deals with forgetting. These are the direct foundation of Section 6 and the toolbox of Section 10 , and this paper does not require a new memory mechanism. What the line lacks follows from what it remembers, which is dialogue rather than a life. There is no first-person stream of sight and sound, no physiology, no distinction between what the person said and what the system inferred (Section 7.2 ), no views that switch with the question (Section 6.4 ), no distinction between short-lived context and settled knowledge (Section 6.1 ), no consistent treatment of forgetting and deletion (Section 6.5 ), and the personalization it offers optimizes for sounding like the person, which is a different thing from understanding them (Section 10.1 ).


#### 3.4 World Models

A world model is an internal model that predicts what happens next. One thread comes from reinforcement learning, where World Models and the Dreamer family plan in imagination ( Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018 ; Hafner et al., 2020 ; Hafner et al., 2025 ) . A second is the predictive-representation route of JEPA and V-JEPA 2 ( LeCun, 2022 ; Assran et al., 2025 ) . A third is generative world simulation at the pixel level: Genie and Genie 3, Sora, and Cosmos ( Bruce et al., 2024 ; Parker-Holder and Fruchter, 2025 ; Brooks et al., 2024 ; NVIDIA, 2025 ) . A fourth is first-person data, which makes the world as seen through a person’s eyes a learnable object: Ego4D ( Grauman et al., 2022 ) , Project Aria and its second-generation research glasses ( Engel et al., 2023 ; Meta, 2025a ) , and EgoLife ( Yang et al., 2025 ) . A parallel thread models people. Large foundation models show real but brittle theory-of-mind ability ( Kosinski, 2024 ; Ullman, 2023 ; Strachan et al., 2024 ) . Park et al. (2024) built a generative agent for each of 1,052 real people from a two-hour interview and found that the agents reproduced their participants’ survey answers about as well as the participants reproduced their own answers two weeks later; it is the strongest evidence to date that a predictive model of a particular person can be built from a small amount of first-person narrative. Mental world modeling treats beliefs, intentions, and other mental variables as part of the world state itself ( Fei and Zhao, 2026 ) , which is the position this paper adopts for the mental side of the personal model (Section 7.5 ). For a symbiont, world models provide the ability to think ahead: to predict what is about to happen around the person (an external, physical world model) and how the person will think, feel, and react, including to a reminder (an internal, mental world model). Sections 7.4 and 7.5 show how both enter the personal model and why the system still stands, in a degraded form, without them. Today’s world models are third-person, short-horizon, and built once; the models of people are built from a single interview, never updated, and unable to tell that they have gone stale.


#### 3.5 Wearable and Embedded Hardware

In three years the hardware conditions for senses and presence have changed in four places. On the head, Ray-Ban Meta glasses showed that ordinary people will wear a camera and microphones all day ( Meta, 2023 ) ; the Orion prototype and Meta Ray-Ban Display added a display ( Meta, 2024 ; Meta, 2025b ) , lightweight display glasses such as the Even G1 appeared ( Even Realities, 2024 ) , and Project Aria serves as a research platform ( Engel et al., 2023 ) . On the body, pendants and recorders capture all-day audio ( Mehta, 2024 ; Silberling, 2025 ; Plaud, 2024 ) , rings and bands capture physiology, and Meta’s surface-EMG wristband, published in Nature , trains a generic model on data from thousands of people so that a new wearer can type and gesture with no calibration, and twenty minutes of personal data improve it further ( Kaifosh et al., 2025 ) . This is a ready-made example of a generic model with a small personal adaptation on the input side, and Section 10.3 returns to it. Closer to the body, AlterEgo reads the muscle signals of silent, deliberate speech ( Kapur et al., 2018 ) , and brain–computer interfaces, noninvasive ( Lévy et al., 2025 ) and implanted ( Willett et al., 2023 ; Card et al., 2024 ) , remain in research and early clinical stages. In compute, neural accelerators in phones and glasses, together with quantization and distillation, run billion-parameter models on the device ( Gunter et al., 2024 ) ; low-power always-on event detection is the premise of the duty cycle in Section 5.5 ; and full-duplex speech models make listening while speaking feasible in latency. The counterexamples are instructive as well: the Humane AI Pin, a dedicated assistant device launched in 2024, was discontinued within a year ( Zeff, 2025 ) , which suggests that a new device has to offer something the phone in the person’s pocket does not. The first generation of senses, private output, and local compute therefore exists, and Section 5 can discuss design. What is missing can be listed: battery and heat limit what “all day” means, display glasses are far from a shared canvas, there is no mature model from physiology to mental state, silent speech and neural interfaces are at an early stage, and above all no device has been designed as one sense of one symbiont, since each is a separate product.

### 4 Conceptual Foundations

This section gives the vision a theoretical shape. We first define the objects, then state the invariant that runs through the paper, then list the properties a symbiont must have and test them against the fictional reference everyone knows, and finally draw the map that organizes the research space. The pattern throughout is definition, formalization, example.


#### 4.1 Definitions

Five definitions turn the slogan “an AI that knows you better than you know yourself” into a claim that can be examined and disputed.

The cognitive gap is the invariant of the paper. It measures something other than intelligence: the price every person pays, every day, in what they forgot, failed to notice, could not think through, or could not put into words. A researcher who, writing a new paper, cannot recall an argument she made in a blog post three years ago is paying it, and so is a parent who snaps at a child because the sleepless night is not in working memory as an explanation. The job of a symbiont is to shrink $G_{t}$ : to return to the person, at the right moment, the part of their own context that is out of reach. Mind reading is not required; what is required is that the right slice of $C^{h}_{t}$ be placed into $c_{t}$ , or that the reasoning or the errand the person would have undertaken, given the time, be carried out on their behalf.

Condition (vi) is also where this paper draws the boundary that the extended mind ( Clark and Chalmers, 1998 ) leaves open for a tool that learns and takes initiative: the symbiont extends what the person can bring to a situation, and judgment and goals stay with the person.

The definitions settle the boundary with AGI in one line: AGI raises $U(\cdot;\,s)$ , and AHI raises $U(\cdot;\,h\oplus s)$ . Two cautions apply to the notation. $U$ is a situation-specific outcome and is never summed across situations, and $\oplus$ marks a coupling condition rather than an arithmetic operation. The formulas serve to remove ambiguity, and no theorem is derived from them.

#### 4.4 Scope and Non-Claims

The paper does not claim that an AI can read thoughts that were never expressed; that everyone should wear devices all day; that a complete context is a precondition for the system to work (Section 12.2 shows it must be useful under partial context); that a symbiont outperforms the person in every domain; that the four roles of Section 2.1 are inevitable historical stages; that any one foundation model, or any one way of organizing a system around it, is the only correct backbone (Section 7.4 ); that it proposes a form of foundation model or a route to general intelligence (Section 1.1 ); that a symbiont raises a trait such as intelligence or emotional skill (Section 15.3 ); that its use is legitimate in every setting (Section 16.2 ); or that any interface standard or protocol should be adopted. It claims that this is a research direction worth organizing on its own, with its own invariant, properties, design space, and standard of evidence.

### 5 Sensing and Interacting

The loop of Figure 6 opens with the question of what the symbiont senses the person with and what it speaks to them through. Hardware here is the symbiont’s borrowed body: borrowed senses on the way in, a borrowed voice and a borrowed canvas on the way out. The borrowed hands, devices in the environment, wait until Section 14.2 .


#### 5.1 Borrowed Senses, Borrowed Voice

An embodied robot comes with eyes, ears, and a mouth. A symbiont has none of its own and must borrow three things: the sensors on a person’s body, so that it sees what they see and hears what they hear; the person’s digital traces on screens, in documents, in code, and in messages; and a channel of output that only the person receives, a voice in the ear, a line in the glasses, a picture laid over the world. Its perception is therefore first-person, incomplete, and moving with the person, and its speech is close to the ear, short, and tied to the setting the person is in. The quality of the senses sets the ceiling on understanding and the quality of the voice sets the floor on help, and neither is the goal in itself. This is also the sense in which hardware and AI are the same project, which Section 5.6 develops.


#### 5.2 The Device Constellation

No single device can carry all of this, so a symbiont senses and speaks through a constellation (Figure 7 ): glasses with a first-person camera, microphones, eye tracking, and a one-line display; a pendant or pin that records audio and takes snapshots all day and stands in when glasses are not worn; earbuds or bone conduction for private voice output; a microphone close to the mouth so that the person can speak to it at the level of a breath without being overheard; a ring, watch, or band for heart rate, sleep, and activity, and increasingly for EMG gestures; a phone for compute and a screen on the move; and a desktop with authorized access to screen content, the IDE, documents, and meeting software. Each device carries a role on the way in, on the way out, or both, and a runtime (Section 11 ) manages them as one. Table 5 lists the signal sources and what each reveals about the physical and the mental side of a life.

Two further classes of device belong in the constellation even though they are not yet in daily use, and the first of them may become the most valuable wearable of all. Neural interfaces read the body’s own signals of intention and attention. At the surface of the body, EMG wristbands already decode the motor commands behind typing and gesture ( Kaifosh et al., 2025 ) , and electrodes in a headband or around the ear record the electrical activity of the brain, in many studies with results comparable to scalp recording ( Kaongoen et al., 2023 ) ; implanted interfaces, now in early clinical trials, decode attempted speech at far higher fidelity ( Willett et al., 2023 ; Card et al., 2024 ) . For a symbiont these interfaces matter for a specific reason. They are the channel through which a deliberate command could one day reach the symbiont without being spoken, typed, or acted out, at a lower cost to the person than any other channel. What they may read is held to the rule of every input channel (Section 5.4 ): commands the person directs to the symbiont, with states such as attention or fatigue read only under separate authorization. They are also the channel in which the privacy of thought (Section 12.4 ) is at its most literal, so the paper places them at the far end of the constellation, with the highest ceiling and the strictest conditions, and treats their maturation as part of the hardware problem of Section 17.4 . The second class is the environment itself: cameras and microphones in the home, the cabin sensors and head-up display of a car, the instruments of a laboratory, eye-tracking contact lenses, haptic garments, and medical implants such as continuous glucose monitors. These enter the constellation on the same terms as the wearables, each with a role on the way in or out, separate authorization, and management by one runtime, and they are where the symbiont’s senses turn into reach (Section 14.2 ). We therefore treat the full list of modalities in three tiers. Sight, sound, text, and digital traces are the bulk of what is usable today; physiology, motion, and gaze are collectable now and need research into their interpretation; and neural signals run from available, in surface EMG, through research, in non-invasive recording, to distant, in implanted interfaces, with the chemical senses furthest of all. “Omni” means that whatever signal arrives can be used; it does not require that every signal be collected.

#### 5.4 Private Channels

Privacy, as a property, has two parts that the design keeps apart: what passes between the person and the symbiont belongs to the person, and its delivery can be discreet, so that the person can speak to the symbiont and hear from it without disturbing anyone nearby. On the way in, this means a breath-level microphone, very short gestures (an EMG band removes the need to raise a hand), and typed text; silent speech from muscle signals and neural interfaces lie further out, and the boundary we keep is the one AlterEgo marks, since it reads what a person deliberately subvocalizes and nothing else ( Kapur et al., 2018 ) , a rule that binds a neural interface as much as a microphone. On the way out, it means in-ear voice, a line in the glasses, and haptic cues, with strength and content matched to the setting. Three requirements that sound alike must be kept apart. That the content of the exchange stays the person’s is what the paper requires. That its delivery is discreet is a capability whose use follows the setting: where a setting presumes unaided performance or the sincerity of what is said, as an examination, a negotiation, or an apology does, whether the person is being assisted becomes a declarable fact, and declared presence is the default there (Sections 16.1 and 15.5 ). That a device record other people without their knowledge is what the paper rejects, and Section 12.5 sets the rules.


#### 5.5 Always On

Companionship around the clock translates into a system requirement of availability: the symbiont can be reached and can pick up at any moment, which is a different thing from computing at full load at every moment. Battery life and latency are the two hard constraints, and the design that reconciles them is a cognitive duty cycle . Most of the time only lightweight event detection runs: someone says the person’s name, a meeting begins, their stress rises, they whisper. An event wakes heavier computation, and the quiet hours of the night are used for offline consolidation (Section 6.3 ). The structure mirrors human attention, which is also not continuous. It yields three research quantities: the recall and false-alarm rate of event detection, the latency budget from an event to a useful cue, and the daily energy and thermal budget. Low latency has to be written as an end-to-end budget: how many milliseconds each stage may take from sensor to model to ear, which stages can run on the device and which must go to the cloud, and which can be prepared in advance so that the fast loop of Section 8.2 finds its material ready. The duty cycle is also the first answer to the concern that round-the-clock AI depends on enormous compute; it turns the concern into an engineering quantity, and Section 11.4 completes the argument.


#### 5.6 Co-Evolution of Hardware and Models

The form of the senses will change with the capability of the models, and the training targets of the models will change with new senses. From today’s recorders and glasses to EMG bands, silent speech, and eventually neural interfaces, each step makes it easier for the AI to understand what a person has in mind, and each step raises the level of privacy at stake. Two judgments follow from the recent record. A dedicated device that adds little to the phone does not last, as the AI pin of 2024 did not ( Zeff, 2025 ) , and a cognitive core without the right hardware never reaches the scene. Hardware and the core are one design problem, and Section 10.4 treats a change of device as one of the ways the whole stack evolves: the person changes their glasses, and their symbiont is still theirs.


### 6 Personal Context and Lifelong Knowledge

The senses of Section 5 deliver streams. This section asks how streams become something a symbiont can use: the live context of what the person is doing now, and the knowledge that stays valid across years. The two words are not interchangeable. Knowledge is what has settled; context is what is current, from the project the person is working on this week to the scene the glasses see this second. Both must be kept, and the claim that organizes the section is that one entry point has to receive all of a person’s context, work and life together, for the picture of the person to be rich enough for cognitive-level help.


#### 6.1 Three Timescales of One Record

Context is information relevant to what the person is doing now. It is short-lived, high-volume, and mostly discarded once used: where this conversation has got to, which file is open in the editor and which error it stopped on, what the glasses can see and who is present, what is on today’s calendar. Knowledge is what has settled out of many contexts and stays relevant across them. It is long-lived, curated, and comparatively small: facts about the person and their world, relationships, preferences, commitments, their history, the reasons they gave for things. Between the two lies a third layer, working state , which lives for days or weeks: the project being pushed forward, the discussion that has not been closed, the decision due this week. The project tree in which events and projects organize one another belongs here. Two flows connect the three layers (Figure 8 ). Consolidation moves material upward, from context to working state to knowledge, through the filtering and merging of Section 6.3 . Recall moves material downward, placing the right slice of knowledge into the context of the moment, which is exactly the step from $C^{h}_{t}$ to $c_{t}$ in Definition 4.1 . When one word is needed for the whole we write the record .

The word context is also used for the input window of a language model, and in context engineering it refers to the information given to a model so that the model can complete a task ( Hua et al., 2025 ) . Personal context, as used here, is information about the person that is valid now, and its lifecycle serves a person’s life. The two meet at the recall step: injecting personal knowledge into a model’s context window is an act of context engineering. We borrow the lifecycle view and nothing else.

#### 6.3 From Streams to a Record

Three stages turn raw streams into knowledge. At the front, on the device and at the edge, filtering drops what is high-frequency, volatile, and not worth keeping and forwards the rest. At the back, storage absorbs frequent writes and updates and grows large. The third stage is periodic offline consolidation, which plays the role that sleep plays for human memory: in idle hours it merges the day’s fragments into events, files events into projects, promotes repeated patterns into stable knowledge, and demotes what has expired. Sleep-time compute and memory systems in which memories link and evolve are technical precedents ( Lin et al., 2025 ; Xu et al., 2025b ) , and the rhythm resolves the tension between filtering at the front and accumulation at the back. Scale forces a decision about fidelity. A day of first-person audio and video runs to gigabytes and a life to petabytes; keeping everything is impossible and unnecessary. Raw streams are kept briefly; event-level summaries and indices of when and where something of a given kind happened are kept for the long term; knowledge entries carry pointers back to their sources. The underlying difficulty is that what will matter later is unknown now, and the compromise is a low-fidelity index that allows the past to be revisited. Section 17.4 lists modeling a life’s record at this scale as the first open problem.


#### 6.4 One Life, Many Views

The organizing claim of the section is that the same record is projected into different views depending on the question, and that any view can be switched to at any time (Figure 9 , Table 8 ). A logic view arranges events and projects as a tree that grows into a graph; a domain view separates work, life, family, and health; a person view separates what concerns the person from what concerns others; a lifetime view separates stable knowledge from passing state, which aligns with the three timescales above; a chronological view orders by when something happened, when it was learned, and whether it still holds; and a physical/mental view separates what happened from how it felt. Views are projections of one store: the record underneath is one, and a view is generated by the question. The logic view is the main thread, and a life is never forced into a single tree. MyLifeBits offered multiple views for retrieval ( Gemmell et al., 2006 ) ; here views also serve understanding (Section 7 ) and the person’s own inspection and correction of what is held about them (Section 12 ). The paper specifies requirements on representation and retrieval and does not prescribe a graph or a vector store.

#### 6.5 Updating, Forgetting, and Repair

Frequent updating raises four problems: conflict, change, forgetting, and repair. Conflict between old and new is handled by keeping three times apart: when something happened, when the system learned it, and until when it holds. Change is the second, since preferences change, relationships change, and commitments are kept or cancelled, and the record has to follow them without losing the history. Forgetting, the third, comes in two kinds, decay that reduces noise and deletion that respects the person’s wishes, and deleting a raw record does not delete the summaries and judgments derived from it; the paper states the consistency requirement and leaves the mechanism open. Repair is the fourth. When the person says “you misunderstood me”, the system must locate the layer in which the misunderstanding lives, a fact, a reason, a current goal, or a long-term preference, and propagate the correction to the entries inferred from it and to the advice already given; changing its answer in the conversation is only the first step. This gives a measurable quantity: the half-life of an error in the record, and the number of inferences it reached before it was corrected. Section 10.4 returns to the related question of what survives when the base model is replaced.


#### 6.6 Remembering the Future, and the Reasons

A record holds more than the past. It holds commitments, plans, the question to ask someone at the next meeting, decisions still forming, and the longer view of who the person wants to become. Psychology calls this prospective memory; it is the kind of memory people fail at most often, and it is where a symbiont produces value most easily, by returning a future intention to the working context at the right moment. It also gives the proactive help of Section 8 its most natural trigger: the system acts because the person said they would, and does not have to guess that they might want to. The record also holds reasons and not only conclusions: why the person declined that offer, why they abandoned that plan, which two options they still have not chosen between, what new evidence would make them reconsider; the third moment of the day in Section 1 is a record that kept the conclusion and lost the reasons. Keeping reasons is the precondition for the decision support of Section 9.2 ; a record without reasons can only answer questions about history.


#### 6.7 The Others in Your Life

A person’s colleagues, family, and friends are part of their context, and a symbiont has to remember where a conversation with someone left off in order to support the person’s social life. Those people have not authorized being modeled. We distinguish relational facts, what has happened between the person and them, which may be kept, from inferred portraits, what kind of person they are, which must be held to stricter limits than anything about the person themselves. Section 12.5 sets the boundary in detail.


### 7 Understanding a Person

The record of Section 6 is evidence. This section asks how evidence becomes understanding of a person, and it is the technical center of the paper. It runs along one line: what must be understood, where the evidence comes from, how the understanding is represented, which backbone it runs on, what a world model adds to it, and where it stops. World models appear only at the fifth step, as the source of one capability, prediction.


#### 7.1 What Must Be Understood

Three objects must be understood. The first is the situation , the physical side: where the person is, whom they are with, what they are doing, what the world in front of them is about to do. The second is the mind , the mental side: the person’s intention at this moment, their cognitive load, their emotion, their uncertainty, and the more stable layer of goals, values, and blind spots. The third is change : the situation changes by the minute, the mind by the hour, and the person by the year, so that the difficulty of understanding someone lies less in drawing a likeness than in making the likeness move. The same experience has two faces, the event visible from outside and the person’s experience of it, and the record keeps both, so the model must represent both. Mental world modeling makes the same commitment at the level of world state, treating beliefs and intentions as state variables alongside physical ones ( Fei and Zhao, 2026 ) ; Section 7.5 builds on it. The third object, change, is the root of the staleness detection in that section and of the mutual growth in Section 10 .


#### 7.2 Two Roads to the Mind

There are two independent sources of evidence about a mind, and a symbiont needs both (Figure 10 ). The observation road infers from behavior, choices, expression, environment, and physiology; its strength is that it sees patterns the person cannot see themselves (they do not sleep after every meeting with a certain colleague), and its weakness is that one behavior has many possible causes. The self-report road takes what the person says: their account of a situation, a whispered sentence, a correction of a misreading; its strength is that only the person can say what they felt and why, and its weakness is that accounts shift with mood and flatter the teller. The two roads feed one layered model, and every entry carries its source and its uncertainty. Self-report has priority on feelings and reasons; observation has priority on patterns the person cannot see. When they disagree, the right response is to mark the disagreement, lower the weight placed on the inference, and ask when the moment is right, never to let the model overrule the person. The result of Park et al. (2024) , in which agents built from two-hour interviews reproduced their participants’ survey answers about as well as the participants reproduced their own, shows how dense the information on the self-report road is; those agents are never updated, and that is what the symbiont adds. Most existing systems travel only the observation road, inferring preferences from interaction history, and the dedicated low-friction channel of Section 5.4 is what gives a symbiont the second.

Both feed one layered model, with every entry tagged by source and uncertainty; disagreements are marked, down-weighted, and resolved by asking.

The table is therefore indexed by context. The differences are not contradictions to be averaged into one personality; the model’s task is to recognize which self is active, from language, setting, and company, and to act with that self’s manner, weights, and social norms. Cultural psychology documents the underlying phenomenon of cultural frame switching in bicultural people ( Hong et al., 2000 ; Ramírez-Esparza et al., 2006 ) , and the context-indexed model is what makes the cultural help of Section 9.4 possible.

#### 7.4 The Backbone

The personal model has to be computed by something, and the paper binds it to nothing in particular. The claim is precise. The personal model and the record are defined at the level of information : what must be known, what must be predicted, and how confidence is expressed. The backbone is the engine that turns evidence into that information, and the engine can be changed while the interface stays, so that what varies between engines is the quality of the estimates. Two independent axes describe the engine (Figure 11 ). The first axis is the foundation model itself, of which three kinds matter here. A language model understands what the person says and writes, holds a textual portrait in its context, and reaches memory through retrieval. An omni-modal model understands what the person sees and hears, reads situation and mood from tone and scene, and makes the first-person stream interpretable for the first time. A world model turns understanding into prediction: what is about to happen around the person, how they will think and react, and whether a given reminder will be welcome. The three are members of one family, each extending the evidence the model can take in and the inference it can draw from it, and later members contain the earlier ones. The second axis is orthogonal to the first. An agentic system belongs to this second axis rather than to the family: it is an organization of computation around a foundation model of any of the three kinds, the model together with the harness of Section 3.2 , and the model inside an agentic system can be exchanged for any member of the family. What the harness adds is that understanding ceases to be a single pass and becomes a process: the system can look something up, ask the person a question, check the record, update the model, and plan the next step, whichever model sits inside it. The grid of Figure 11 therefore has six cells, and the two axes can be traversed independently. The first usable systems will stand in the upper-left cell, a language model inside a harness, since that is what today’s agents are, and the design is headed toward the upper-right cell, a world model inside a harness that anticipates as well as perceives. The design must stand on the weakest cell and improve automatically along either axis. On a language model alone the symbiont is a text assistant with a memory; without a world model the intervention policy estimates reactions by heuristics; without a harness, understanding cannot check itself. The information interface is what makes the claim concrete: the mental-state layer of Table 9 asks every cell for the same output, an estimate of intent and an estimate of emotion, each with its confidence, which a language model produces from dialogue text, an omni-modal model from tone and scene as well, a world model from predictions of what comes next, and a harness from any of these together with the result of checking them against the record. This is also why Section 10.4 requires that a change of foundation model, or of harness, preserve the relationship.

Rows: how much system is organized around the model, from a single pass to a process inside an agentic harness. The two axes are independent, since a harness can be wrapped around any of the three models; the dashed path is the likely order in which systems will move through the grid, and the heavy line is the information interface of the personal model, which is the same in every cell.


#### 7.5 Two World Models

A world model, as a backbone, contributes to the personal model in two forms. An external physical world model takes the environment seen in first person as its object, scene, objects, people present, their dynamics, and predicts the next moment: the cup is about to tip, the bus is arriving, the slide is about to change. It serves help in the physical world, grounds mental cues in the situation (the person is tense because the deadline is on the screen), and supplies the extended reach of Section 14.2 ; its sources are the video world models and first-person data of Section 3.4 . An internal mental world model takes the person’s beliefs, attention, intentions, emotions, and goals as its object and predicts how they change after an event and after an action by the symbiont. It is the predictive version of the mental-state layer of Table 9 , and its sources are theory-of-mind research, individual-level simulation ( Park et al., 2024 ) , and mental world modeling, which places these variables inside the world state ( Fei and Zhao, 2026 ) . The two are coupled: physical events change mental states, and mental states drive actions in the physical world.

A competent world model has a state representation, temporal and relational reasoning, prediction of future states, prediction of the consequences of candidate actions, an expression of uncertainty, and detection of its own misspecification. Replace “the world” with “this person’s world” and every requirement stands, and the last one matters most. People change, so a model of a person will go wrong; the ability to detect that it has gone stale is what separates a symbiont from a static profile, and it is the same class of problem as misspecification detection in open-world modeling. In the smallest notation, with $z_{t}$ the joint latent state,

[Tableau] $$z_{t}=\big(z^{\mathrm{phys}}_{t},\,z^{\mathrm{mind}}_{t}\big),\qquad b_{t}(z)=P\!\left(z_{t}\mid O_{\leq t},\,R_{\leq t},\,K_{t}\right),\qquad\hat{z}_{t+1}\sim P\!\left(z_{t+1}\mid z_{t},\,a_{t},\,x_{t+1}\right),$$ (3)

where $z^{\mathrm{phys}}_{t}$ is the task-relevant state of the world around the person, $z^{\mathrm{mind}}_{t}$ the latent state of intention, emotion, and goals, $b_{t}$ the system’s belief over both, and the third expression says that the model must predict how both change after the system’s action $a_{t}$ and the new situation $x_{t+1}$ . The notation fixes what is predicted and conditioned on; it prescribes no Bayesian implementation. It underlies the intervention policy of Section 8.4 , which needs $b_{t}$ , and the learning rule of Section 10.2 , which needs $a_{t}$ . Without a world model the third expression degrades to heuristic rules, and the system still stands.


#### 7.6 Knowing When It Is Wrong

“It knows you better than you know yourself” has three defensible meanings and one that is not. It can hold the person’s whole self-context at once while they hold a small part (Definition 4.1 ); it can see patterns across years that the person is inside of and cannot see; and it remembers the reasons they gave and have since forgotten. The fourth meaning, reading thoughts that were never expressed, the paper gives up explicitly. Two kinds of limitation must also be kept apart. Familiarity with the person and competence on the problem in front of them are different things, and a system that knows someone very well may be poor at the specialist question they face this minute. The confidence of the personal model and the reliability of the backbone in that domain are estimated and reported separately; when the second is low the system narrows its advice or suggests asking a person, and it never covers its ignorance of the problem with its familiarity with the person. Understanding runs both ways, since the person also learns under which conditions the system is reliable, and mutual understanding has degrees and costs; the grounding framework of Clark and Brennan (1991) describes the process without the infinite regress of each knowing what the other knows. Finally, every judgment about the person carries a confidence, any of them can be overturned by a sentence from the person, the system learns to ask when it is unsure, and an overturned judgment propagates through the repair of Section 6.5 . Collins et al. (2024) argued that machines that think with people must model them; this section says what is modeled, on what evidence, on which backbone, and within which limits.


### 8 Living Alongside

Understanding has to become participation at the right moment. This section turns private, real-time, low-friction, embedded presence, and proactivity that is timely, into mechanisms. It is about how the symbiont is present, when it speaks, and in what form. What it says is the subject of Section 9 .


#### 8.1 Two Conversations at Once

Real-time voice assistants solved one problem: the person and the AI can speak and listen at the same time ( Défossez et al., 2024 ) . A symbiont solves a different one. The person is talking to another person; the symbiont is listening, thinking, and preparing at the same time, and through the private channel it hands the person a line, so that the person never has to stop for it. We call this cognitive full-duplex , the real-time form of the symbiosis Licklider (1960) imagined: two conversations at once, the person’s with the world and the person’s with the symbiont, with the second never interrupting the first. Figure 12 shows one instance. A colleague mentions a figure the person reported last quarter; the slow loop retrieves it and checks it against the record; a question is detected and a cue appears in the glasses within a second; the person answers with the figure; the topic moves on and the cue expires and is withdrawn. Four tracks run through the exchange, the external world, the person’s outward expression, the private channel, and the symbiont’s internal state, and the figure follows them along one timeline. This capability belongs in the first usable system, since without it the symbiont is confined to the moments when the person is alone.

#### 8.2 Fast Loop, Slow Loop

Real time has two timescales, and conflating them leads to the mistaken demand that everything be low-latency. The fast loop handles the immediate: a name, a number, “what he just said contradicts last week.” The slow loop handles the background: retrieval, reasoning, updating the model, preparing a summary for after the meeting. Between them sits a rolling current state: what this conversation is about, who wants what, what is still unresolved. Cues must update incrementally, an updated cue must replace its predecessor, and an expired cue must disappear. The latency budget has a shape. The fast loop’s ceiling is set by the pace of conversation, since a name is useless two seconds late, so it can use only small models on the device or at the edge together with material the slow loop has already prepared. The slow loop may be slow but must think ahead, preparing before the person opens their mouth what they are likely to need, which is where the prediction of Section 7.5 is put to use. Section 17.4 lists the end-to-end latency budget as an open problem.


#### 8.3 Implicit Intent

Much of what a person needs is never asked for. The evidence for implicit intent comes from the current activity, recent behavior, unfinished business (Section 6.6 ), the personal model (Section 7 ), and the half-sentence the person whispers. The purpose of inferring it is to have candidate help ready and to wait for a suitable moment, never to decide in the person’s place. When the inference is uncertain, the right move is one very short question, and the answer to that question is itself new evidence for the record; active clarification is a source of context in its own right.


#### 8.4 The Intervention Policy

Timeliness has to become a decision problem, and silence has to be one of the formal decisions. Table 10 separates the three judgments: what to help with, when, and how. The decision can be written as a constrained choice,

[Tableau] $$a^{*}_{t}\;=\;\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Delta U(a)\mid b_{t}\right]\;-\;\lambda_{t}\cdot\mathrm{Interrupt}(a,\,\mathrm{attention}_{t})\;-\;\rho\cdot\mathrm{Risk}(a),\qquad\text{s.t.}\ \ \mathrm{conf}(b_{t})\geq\theta(a),$$ (4)

where $\mathcal{A}_{t}$ always contains silence $\varnothing$ with zero interruption cost, and the confidence threshold $\theta(a)$ rises with the intrusiveness of the action: the louder the help, the surer the symbiont has to be , and acting on the person’s behalf carries the highest threshold of all. The three terms say that help must have expected value, that interruption has a cost which varies with the person’s current attentional state, and that risk is counted separately because a wrong reminder is worse than none. We add a notion of an attention budget : the amount of a person’s attention a symbiont may consume in a day is finite, and it should be managed the way battery charge is managed. The just-in-time adaptive intervention framework of mobile health has studied for a decade when an intervention of what strength is acceptable, with its notions of states of vulnerability and receptivity ( Nahum-Shani et al., 2018 ) , and those notions transfer directly. Earlier work on interruption and recovery shows how expensive an ill-timed intrusion is ( Iqbal and Horvitz, 2007 ) , and mixed-initiative principles already warned that initiative must be weighed against the cost of poorly timed action ( Horvitz, 1999 ) . The goal is therefore accuracy of intervention rather than a greater amount of it. The weights $\lambda$ , $\rho$ , and $\theta$ are given no numerical values in this paper and are research variables of Section 17 .

#### 8.5 Low Friction

Low friction means that the person never works for the help: no re-explaining the background, no reaching for a phone, no switching applications, no waiting. Friction has four sources, and each is removed elsewhere in the paper: explaining the background (the record, Section 6 ), switching devices (the runtime, Section 11 ), waiting for a reply (the two loops above), and tidying up afterwards (consolidation, Section 6.3 ). Some friction is worth preserving. Being asked one more question before an important decision, or being left to think for a moment, is friction that should survive optimization (Section 10.5 ); interfaces that make people examine a recommendation before accepting it reduce overreliance at the cost of being liked less ( Buçinca et al., 2021 ) , and that trade is the one a symbiont must learn to make per person and per moment.


#### 8.6 Beyond the Meeting Room

The meeting is the first proving ground: time pressure is high, information is dense, and timing decides everything, which is why a desktop copilot that analyzes a meeting as it happens and feeds cues back in real time is the natural starting point. The symbiont must, however, leave the room. Three settings of presence have different rhythms and channels. Sitting at a screen, in the desktop or the editor, the channel is wide and help can be expanded. In conversation, in a meeting or at dinner, only a line or a whispered word fits. On the move, in the street, in transit, in an unfamiliar city, the earpiece and the overlay carry what the moment needs. The second moment of the day in Section 1 resolves here: the result the researcher could not recall arrives as a line in the glasses while the conversation continues. What the symbiont does once present begins with doing things for the person, which is where the next section starts.


### 9 The Ladder of Assistance

Once present, what does a symbiont do? The answer is a ladder with three tiers (Figure 13 ). At the bottom it gets things done, which is what today’s autonomous and personal agents do, and a symbiont must do it as well as they do and better. In the middle it thinks and feels with the person: decisions, goal formation, learning, creative work, emotional steadiness, mentorship, and psychological support within clinical limits, the settings of highest cognitive density. At the top it lives among others with the person: real-time social cognition, hard conversations, culture and dialect, and the meeting of two symbionts. Three facts organize the ladder. Each tier rests on the one below, and a symbiont that cannot book a flight will never be worn. Each tier up moves the object of evaluation further from task completion and closer to how the person is changed. And all three tiers run on the same machinery of Sections 6 to 8 , differing only in how deep into the personal model they reach and how much intervention they are allowed (the threshold $\theta$ of Eq. ( 4 )). One function, patient care, runs across all three tiers and receives its own treatment in Section 9.5 .

#### 9.1 Getting Things Done

The floor of the ladder is general-purpose assistance: booking travel and hotels, shopping and comparing prices, keeping the calendar and the inbox, forms and payments, desktop work with files, slides, and spreadsheets, coding and technical work inside the editor, retrieving and organizing information, and multi-day tasks such as an application, a move, or an event. A symbiont does not rebuild these capabilities. Through its harness (Sections 3.2 and 11.3 ) it delegates them to standard agents and tools, computer-use agents, tool protocols, and coding agents, and keeps three jobs for itself: defining the task correctly, supplying the right personal defaults, and deciding when the person must be consulted. The reason the same task comes out better is the record and the personal model. An agent told “book my flight to the conference” has to ask where the person leaves from, what they will spend, when they prefer to travel, whether they want a window seat. A symbiont already knows that the person has a meeting on Monday morning that cannot move, that they dislike overnight flights, that they want to stay near the venue because they hope to meet a particular colleague, and that the last time they took a short connection they missed it. So it asks nothing, or it asks the one question that only the person can answer. One layer deeper, it knows why the person is making the trip, from the goals layer of Table 9 , so it frames the task correctly and books the meeting they were hoping for alongside the flight. #### 9.2 Thinking With You

The first half of the second tier is cognition itself, in six forms.


##### Decisions

A symbiont that remembers the person can help them reason with themselves. It returns what they forgot (why they declined last time, and whether the constraint still holds), lays out what they did not think of (a third option, a counterexample, a value they usually weigh and this time left out), and writes down what they concluded, with the reasons, for next time (Section 6.6 ). Its stance is to help the person see, never to choose for them, and it owes them a reasoned objection when a choice conflicts with a long-term goal they stated themselves, after which the decision returns to them. Reicherts et al. (2025) compared an AI that builds on the person’s own reasons with one that gives recommendations: the first integrated better with people’s thinking and produced slightly better outcomes, the second surfaced more novel insights at less effort, and the first is the form of help this paragraph describes. The third moment of the day in Section 1 resolves here.


##### Goal formation

Many important matters start without an executable goal: changing direction, taking or declining an opportunity, handling a relationship. The symbiont can unfold candidate goals, bring back the reasons that conflict, and ask which cost the person would find hardest to bear, so that a provisional choice forms in the conversation. It makes no claim to have discovered the person’s “true” goal.


##### Metacognition

This is help with knowing where one is uncertain, whether to check, and whether this is a moment to think alone, to think with the symbiont, or to ask a person. Such help arrives before an answer, and sometimes it takes the form “that is not the question you need.” A rise in confidence is never counted as a rise in understanding, and the cognitive-offloading literature ( Risko and Gilbert, 2016 ) is the background for when offloading is the right choice.


##### Learning and tutoring

A tutor who knows where the person does not understand, because it has seen every place they got stuck, can give a hint at the moment they need it instead of an answer, and can step back when they say they want to learn this themselves (Section 10.5 ). Language learning is the most natural case and connects to the dialect help of Section 9.4 .


##### Creative and long-horizon work

Two services belong here: catching the idea that slips away (the first moment of the day in Section 1 , the typical use of the whisper channel), and holding a thread across weeks, where this project got to, which hypothesis was dropped, which is still open, which is the working state of Section 6.1 . The failure to watch for is a symbiont that generates a great deal and drifts away from the question the person wanted to pursue.


##### Attention and memory

This includes guarding focus, by noticing that the person has been refreshing the same page for ten minutes, and the plainest memory support of all: a name, a detail, where something was put, what was said last time. These six forms draw on the goals and mental-state layers of Table 9 , use the expanded dialogue or the whisper channel, and span seconds to days, and it is here that the friction worth preserving (Section 8.5 ) matters most.


#### 9.3 Feeling With You

The second half of the tier runs from everyday emotion to the edge of clinical care, in four steps of increasing weight.


##### Everyday steadiness

The small disturbances of a day: a remark that stung, nerves before a talk, a piece of bad news. The symbiont’s help is to be present, to know what happened, and to say the one sentence that fits, or to say nothing and simply note it. Physiology gives early signs of stress and fatigue, and their interpretation has to remain conservative.


##### A mentor who knows your story

Useful comfort is never generic encouragement; it rests on a precise understanding of the person’s actual history, so that what is said touches the real point: this failure relates to those efforts and those hopes. Three levels follow. Being heard, when the symbiont restates the situation and the person thinks “yes, that is it.” Being understood, when it points out an attribution error the person could not see. Being moved, when it builds a next step from the way the person got through a similar time before. The preconditions, an established relationship of trust and a general competence that in many domains exceeds an ordinary person’s, are stated as conditions throughout.


##### Psychological support within clinical limits

This is where the section has to be most careful. Relative to any mental-health application, a symbiont has three advantages: it knows the person’s life and does not need it retold; it is present when the trigger happens, days before the next appointment; and it can see patterns, in sleep, in social withdrawal, in language. It is therefore suited to three things: being a daily practice partner for evidence-based methods such as the identification and reframing used in cognitive behavioral therapy; providing support and a record between professional sessions; and recognizing early signs of relapse. It is not suited to carrying treatment on its own. For depression and anxiety the paper says that this is a direction that deserves serious study, with randomized evidence for particular systems under particular conditions, from the early Woebot trial ( Fitzpatrick et al., 2017 ) to the recent trial of a generative chatbot ( Heinz et al., 2025 ) , and it does not say that these conditions can be treated by an AI. The symbiont’s first duty in such moments is to recognize when a human professional must be brought in, with a clear path for doing so when crisis signals appear, and in regulated jurisdictions this function is itself regulated, which the paper acknowledges.


##### Habits, behavior change, and company

Long-term behavior change, in sleep, exercise, or recovery, is the home ground of just-in-time adaptive interventions ( Nahum-Shani et al., 2018 ) , and a symbiont turns the phone notification into a sentence from someone present. As company it can be patient and non-judging, and the risk has to be stated directly: it must never substitute for human relationships, and it should steer the person back toward people. Studies of companion chatbots show both relief of loneliness and dependence, with effects that vary with how and how much they are used ( Maples et al., 2024 ; Fang et al., 2025 ) .


#### 9.4 Relating

The third tier is help with people and cultures, in four forms. Real-time social cognition. A meeting is one kind of social setting. In any of them the symbiont can recall the person opposite (where the conversation left off, what they care about), read the moment (the implication of what was just said, the tension between the person’s argument and the other’s position), and help the person say it (a better phrasing, the number they forgot). Reading the moment relies on the theory-of-mind ability of large foundation models, which has shown both promise and brittleness ( Kosinski, 2024 ; Ullman, 2023 ; Strachan et al., 2024 ) , and on inferences about other people that are used only for the interaction at hand and never accumulate into a portrait (Sections 6.7 and 12.5 ). Rehearsal and hard conversations. Practice beforehand for a salary negotiation, a breakup, or negative feedback to a colleague; a line during (the person’s pace is rising; the other is really asking something else); a review afterwards. A simulated counterpart in rehearsal is only a rehearsal and never a prediction of a real person. De-escalation belongs here too: the symbiont can see, in voice and heart rate, that the person is heating up before they notice, and a single vibration on the private channel is enough. Relationship maintenance. This goes deeper than a birthday reminder: the person has not spoken to their father in a month and he mentioned a medical test last time. It draws on the relations layer and on prospective memory (Section 6.6 ), and it is where the limits of Section 12.5 bite hardest. Language, culture, and the dialect self. Behind each language are different habits of thought and social rules. The help consists less in translation than in entering the frame: how people here greet, how they decline, what cannot be said directly. It needs the context-indexed model of Section 7.3 , which knows who the person is in that language, and the online learning of Section 10.3 , which learns the dialect of a small place. Those two sections share one example, used here and learned there, and we avoid claims of the form “language X makes people more open”: shifts of this kind have been reported in bicultural individuals ( Ramírez-Esparza et al., 2006 ) , and they do not license a rule about any particular person.


#### 9.5 Caring

The function the user of a symbiont may value most is one that is patient when they are not. Its core is consistency, patience, tirelessness, and noticing what people stop noticing, and it runs across all three tiers. Four settings follow; the populations themselves are treated in Section 13 . In parenting , a symbiont answers a child’s hundredth “why” without weariness, hands the parent a sentence when the parent’s own temper is rising, leaves the child to the parent, and notices developmental or emotional signs worth a professional’s attention; the symbiont belongs to the parent, and all-day sensing of children raises separate ethical questions (Sections 13.9 and 16.7 ). In aging and memory decline it is a memory prosthesis, who this is, whether the medication was taken, what was being looked for, with the SenseCam studies of memory-impaired patients as precedent ( Berry et al., 2007 ) ; it supports orientation and safety and connects the person to family. For caregivers , the people who care for others and receive the least support, it holds the detail of care so that they can rest. In rehabilitation and chronic self-management , where the task is long, repetitive, and easy to abandon, its patience is worth most, and the handoff to clinical systems must be explicit. The failure mode common to all four is the handing over of a person to a machine, and in each setting the symbiont’s last duty is to return the person to other people.


#### 9.6 Symbiont to Symbiont

The most imaginative scene in the vision is two people, each with a symbiont, in one room and not yet near each other, while their symbionts exchange cards, filter shared interests, decide whether a greeting is worth it, and if so bring the two together. It is at once fanciful and practical, and Figure 14 turns it into four research questions. Which information may represent the person in an exchange, in four rings of authorization: visible, discoverable, shareable, and committable, with the inner personal model never crossing the boundary. By what criterion symbionts match, and who sets it. Whether a greeting made on the person’s behalf counts as their commitment. And when two symbionts each optimize for their owner, whether the outcome is good for both. Section 15.7 asks what happens when this scales to a crowd. No protocol is proposed, and everything in Sections 5 to 8 stands even if symbionts never talk to each other.

### 10 Growing Together

After many rounds of participation, how do the system and the person each change? This section holds the paper’s most ambitious claims, the personal data flywheel, self-evolution, test-time training, learning a dialect from a few days of conversation, and it is written to be the strictest. The question “where does the symbiont learn” is broadened from memory and parameters to the whole stack: as the symbiont comes to know the person, the base model, the harness, and even the hardware have to evolve, and the one thing that must never be replaced is the relationship.


#### 10.1 Where Learning Happens

“Knowing you better” can happen in seven places, each with its own mechanism, risk, and reversibility (Table 13 ). The first three, the record, the personal model, and the intervention policy, are where most of what is called personalization lives, and they already create most of the value. The fourth and fifth, model parameters and the harness with its tools, are self-evolution in the strict sense. The sixth is hardware. The seventh is the person, which is the paper’s object of evaluation and which no other personalization research counts. #### 10.2 The Personal Data Flywheel

More authorized data gives a more accurate model, which gives more useful help, which earns more trust, which brings more data. The flywheel is the core technical bet of personal AI, and in a paper it is a research hypothesis with two brakes. The first is echo . The symbiont influences the person, then reads the influenced behavior as evidence that the person was always like this, and the model grows ever more certain of a portrait it painted itself. The remedy is to condition every update on what the system itself did:

[Tableau] $$M_{t+1}\;=\;\mathcal{U}\!\left(M_{t};\;o_{t+1},\,r_{t+1},\,a_{t}\right),$$ (5)

where the update of the model $M$ from new observations $o$ and self-reports $r$ must take the system’s own action $a_{t}$ as an argument; an update that ignores $a_{t}$ reads the result of an intervention as a trait of the person. Machine learning has a formal account of predictions that change what they predict ( Perdomo et al., 2020 ) . The second brake is validation : more data does not mean a truer understanding, and every round of updating has to survive the evaluation of Section 17 . Growth in the person can also be the intended result of help, so change is never treated as contamination by default; what is examined is whether the evidence for an update matches the conclusion drawn from it.


#### 10.3 Learning the Way a Person Learns

A symbiont has to learn new things the way a person does: in the course of life, from few experiences, incrementally, and without large offline training runs. The requirement is best stated through an example. A dialect spoken in a single county has no corpus and sometimes no written form; a person learns it by going there, talking to people, and being corrected. A symbiont needs the same ability, and it needs it under two constraints: without changing all of its parameters, through local and dynamic adjustment or no parameter change at all, and with generalization from little data. We write this as a ladder of research targets, each separately testable: remember the sentences it heard; compose expressions it has learned; adapt to a new speaker and accent; transfer to scenes it has not heard. Meta’s EMG wristband is a ready-made example on the input side: a generic model trained on thousands of people works for a new wearer with no calibration, and twenty minutes of personal data improve it further ( Kaifosh et al., 2025 ) . A generic model with a small personal adaptation is the learning curve this section argues for, with the object changed from gestures to a dialect and a mind. Test-time training ( Sun et al., 2020 ; Sun et al., 2025 ) and memory layers that learn at test time ( Behrouz et al., 2025 ) are the technical lineage, and several leading researchers have named continual, sample-efficient learning as the next frontier; the paper fixes requirements and validation levels and chooses no particular route.


#### 10.4 Evolving the Whole Stack

As understanding of the person deepens, the base model, the harness, and the hardware must all evolve, along three routes that share one set of constraints (Figure 16 ). Base model upgrades. The foundation model changes every few months (Section 3.1 ), and the symbiont cannot be reset with it. The requirement is the information-level interface of Section 7.4 : the record, the personal model, the intervention policy, and the harness configuration are defined outside the backbone, migrate when it changes, and are recalibrated during a transition period, since a new backbone may estimate the same evidence differently. This is the paper’s answer to whether a symbiont needs its own private model: what must belong to the person is the record and the model of them, and it is the relationship, and never the weights, that persists. The films give the failure case; when its owner moved from Jarvis to a successor system, the relationship started over. Self-improvement of the harness. A symbiont may rewrite the way it works: fix a routine for something the person does often, write a tool, accumulate skills in the manner of a skill library ( Wang et al., 2024 ) , rebalance the fast and slow loops, retune the parameters of the intervention policy. This is self-improvement in a bounded form. What changes is the harness, which is readable, auditable, and reversible, and every change leaves a record; the model’s own parameters change only by exception. Hardware generations. New glasses, a new band, or a new channel let the symbiont see and say a little more. The record and the model are bound to no device, and what has to be relearned after a change is only which channel works in which setting, never who the person is, which is the mechanism behind the co-evolution of Section 5.6 . The shared constraints are four: portability (change any layer and the relationship survives), auditability (what changed and why, visible to the person), reversibility (a bad change can be rolled back), and the echo brake at system level (what the system observes after changing itself cannot be read directly as the person). One more direction follows: the better the symbiont knows the person, the more specialized its procedures should become and the fewer generic defaults it should keep, and since specialization erodes general ability, a path back to the generic must be kept open, which is the system-side counterpart of the person’s right to do things themselves in the next subsection.

Dashed links show life events triggering system learning and system changes altering what is observed next. The heavy line is the record and personal model, the one thing never replaced. In the fiction, the counterpart of this growth is Jarvis’s cognitive core eventually gaining a body as the Vision (Section 14.2 ), and the counterexample is Ultron, a separate project whose goals diverged from its maker’s (Section 15.7 ).


#### 10.5 The Person Changes Too

The symbiont learns the person, and the person changes. Three changes are possible: the person learns something, which is a gain; they stop practising what they have handed over, which is a gain or a loss depending on whether they wanted to keep it; and their preferences are slowly shaped by the system, which has to be watched. The symbiont must support both “do this for me” and “I want to learn this myself”, and leave the choice with the person, which is the last layer of Table 9 . The evidence so far shows harm by default and protection only by design. Generative AI without guardrails harmed high-school mathematics learning while a guarded version did not ( Bastani et al., 2025 ) , and in a randomized study of developers learning a new library, AI assistance weakened conceptual understanding and debugging without a significant gain in speed, while three of six observed interaction patterns preserved learning ( Shen and Tamkin, 2026 ) . The old lesson of automation, that the operator who is relieved of a task loses the skill to take it back, still applies ( Bainbridge, 1983 ) . Section 17.1 counts the consequence under retained ability.


### 11 The Personal Runtime

Everything in the previous six sections has to run somewhere. This is the layer that, in the stack of Figure 5 , sits between the foundation models and the agentic harness, and that may in time grow into an AI-native operating system. We call it a runtime and do not propose to rewrite an operating system.


#### 11.1 Beyond an App

A symbiont needs persistent state across devices, a single permission model, scheduling of several senses and several output channels, management of the fast and slow loops, delegation to standard agents, and the one entry point for every context of Section 6.2 . These requirements exceed any single application and fall short of a new kernel. The personal runtime is the layer that meets them, and it can grow over time into the AI-native operating system of our vision, once enough devices and applications treat it as their entry point.


#### 11.2 Layers

Figure 17 expands the upper part of Figure 5 . From the bottom: the device constellation; base capabilities, a shared omni-modal model, a world model, and small on-device models, all replaceable; runtime services, identity, permissions, device abstraction, continuity, the attention budget, and entry-point aggregation; the harness, which plans, uses tools, delegates to standard agents, schedules the two loops, and holds the procedures and skills it has written for itself; and at the top the record and the personal model, which are bound to no backbone and are portable. #### 11.3 A Harness for a Life

Current harnesses are designed around a task (Section 3.2 ): it begins and ends, its input is a span of text, its tools are APIs. A symbiont’s harness faces a life: nothing begins or ends, the input is a continuous multimodal stream, the tools include a person’s senses and the devices around them, memory is a first-class component, and most of the time the correct output is nothing at all. Six requirements follow, and the paper states them without a specification: event-driven operation; the record and the personal model as native components; output channels matched to the setting; delegation to standard agents and reclamation of control from them (Section 9.1 ); a log of the system’s own actions, which the echo brake of Section 10.2 needs; and the ability to rewrite itself under audit, which Section 10.4 needs. This is the layer in which the current harness standard must evolve for the setting, and that evolution is one of the paper’s calls to the community.


#### 11.4 The Cost of Always-On Cognition

AI that computes around the clock depends heavily on compute and infrastructure, and the question of what happens when AI compute becomes cheap infrastructure deserves a direct answer. Cost has a structure that can be optimized. Most of the time a lightweight on-device model does nothing but detect events; a few events wake a heavy model, on the device or in the cloud; the night is used for consolidation. Three research quantities follow: the compute budget per person per day, the leverage of event detection on total cost, and the effect of the split between local and cloud on privacy and latency. Whether the paradigm is economical depends on the duty cycle and not on model size. If inference costs keep falling at the rate of recent years and on-device models keep improving, one always-on symbiont per person becomes affordable before it becomes trusted, and the bottleneck moves once more, to Section 12 .


#### 11.5 Continuity

Continuity is the runtime’s central duty, in three forms. Across devices: what the person started at the desk continues when they walk out the door. Across interruptions: when a battery dies, the person switches off collection, or the network drops, the system knows which stretch it missed and, on recovery, does not pretend to have been there. Across upgrades: when the base model or the device changes, the person’s symbiont is still theirs (Section 10.4 ). The second form is the most often forgotten and the most damaging to trust when it fails.


### 12 Trust

By what right does a system enter a person’s life? The judgment that organizes this section is that the largest obstacle to a symbiont, ahead of any technical one, is whether people will find it safe enough to open their whole context to it, the physical and the mental alike. Trust is therefore treated as part of the architecture. This section is about design; the risks that design does not remove, and their governance, are the subject of Part IV.


#### 12.1 Trust Is the Adoption Problem

A symbiont asks for authorization without precedent. It sees what the person sees, hears what they hear, keeps their life, infers their thoughts, and spends and speaks on their behalf. No existing product has asked for this much. The paper therefore reads “full open context” as a process and never as a precondition: the system has to be useful under very little authorization and earn more as trust accumulates.


#### 12.2 The Trust Curve

Figure 18 plots authorization against time together and value delivered. Data authorization climbs in steps, from desktop and text, to voice and calendar, to first-person sight and sound, to physiology, to the private channel of thought, and each step has something the system must prove first. Action permission climbs on a parallel curve, from look to suggest to draft to undoable execution to final execution (Section 9.1 ). The curve can fall: a breach sends authorization back a step, although it cannot undo an exfiltration, so the step back limits further exposure only, which is one more reason the most private data stays on the device. Three principles govern tiered access. The system must be useful at every level, so that “give me everything and then I can help” is never the offer. Data from a higher level is never used automatically for a task at a lower level. Processing stays local by preference, with the most private data handled closest to the body. This is also the answer to the question most often asked first about the paradigm, whether the system must watch everything to be worth anything: it must not, and the separate authorization of each source in Table 7 is the cross-section of the curve.

> Figure 18 : The trust curve. Data authorization (solid, read on the left axis) and action permission (dashed, read on the right axis) climb in steps as time together and delivered value accumulate. Each data level names what must be proved before the next is opened, each action level is earned a little after the data level it needs, and a breach sends authorization back one step. The system has to be useful at the lowest level already.


#### 12.3 The Symbiont’s Code

A personal AI should have values and a code of its own. We start from three rules, complete command of the owner’s information, responsibility to the owner, and protection of privacy and data security, and rewrite them as commitments that can be checked, with three additions.

- • Know only what you are permitted to know, and know it as accurately as possible. “Complete command” cannot stand ethically or legally; the standard is completeness within authorization.

- • Answer to the person, and to the interests the person endorses on reflection. Never to a passing impulse, and never to a third party: a platform, an advertiser, or an employer. Section 15.6 takes up the institutional conflict this creates.

- • Protect the person’s privacy, and the privacy of the people around them.

- • Do not manipulate. The system that knows a person best is the system best placed to steer them, and it must forgo that ability.

- • Be pausable, correctable, and leavable.

- • Follow the rules the person has accepted, even against a passing wish. A person who sits an examination, signs a contract, or joins a game has accepted its rules, and a symbiont that answers to the interests the person endorses on reflection does not help them break those rules (Section 16.2 ).

The aspiration that such a system be unbreakable is replaced by verifiability and tamper evidence: no system is unbreakable, and its behavior can be audited.


#### 12.4 The Privacy of Thought

A symbiont creates a category of data that has never been collected by any system: the thoughts a person whispers on the private channel, the mental states the system infers, and, as neural interfaces mature, signals of attention and intent read from the body itself. We call it thought data and argue that it deserves protection above every other kind of personal data: local by default, never used for training by default, deletable at any time, and never exchanged with anyone. The neuroethics literature’s discussion of mental privacy applies with little change ( Yuste et al., 2017 ) , and the term cognitive sovereignty names the right at stake.


#### 12.5 The People Around You

A symbiont hears other people speak and sees their faces. Three requirements follow. Collection must be perceptible to the people nearby. What has passed between the person and someone else may be kept in the person’s record, as a diary would keep it, while inferences about that person serve only the interaction at hand and never accumulate into a portrait (Section 6.7 ); a sensitive fact the person was told about someone else, such as their health, is kept only as told and is never analyzed or passed on. Raw audio and video of other people are kept no longer than the short window in which they serve the person (Section 6.3 ), and where someone asks not to be recorded the device stops. An opt-out is not enough where the law asks for more. Some jurisdictions require the consent of everyone recorded in a private conversation ( State of California, 2024 ) , biometric statutes require written consent before a template of a face is made ( Illinois General Assembly, 2008 ) , and data-protection law requires a lawful basis for processing a bystander’s data and treats biometric data as a special category ( European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2016 ) . A symbiont therefore follows the strictest rule of the place it is in, which in many places means that it records no conversation at all and serves the person from what they tell it. Contextual integrity explains why the same information is appropriate in one setting and wrong in another ( Nissenbaum, 2004 ) , and studies of bystanders of augmented-reality glasses show what people around a wearer actually want ( Denning et al., 2014 ) .


#### 12.6 A Symbiont Is a Target

Security takes a new shape. The symbiont holds the person’s whole life, so it is the most valuable thing to attack; it hears what the person hears, so a sentence someone says in front of them can be an injection; it acts and spends for the person, so a hijacked symbiont does real damage; and it rewrites its own harness, so an attack can consist of making it break itself. The paper offers no solution and names four classes of threat with their research directions: data exfiltration, injection through the physical world, manipulation of the intervention policy, and contamination of the self-improvement process.

Part III

Where This Leads


### 13 Whom It Serves

The first question of Part III is who stands to gain, and the answer is almost everyone, from a person with no special job or need to the most demanding expert. A claim of that breadth has no value as a list of wishes, so this section first gives a way of arguing it, then argues population by population, and ends by saying who should not come first.


#### 13.1 Universal Need, Uneven Gap

The invariant of the paper is what turns “everyone can benefit” into an argument. Everyone pays the cognitive gap of Eq. ( 1 ) every day. How much of it a symbiont can close differs by population, and four criteria say by how much: the size of the gap, how much a group loses daily to forgetting, not noticing, not thinking through, and not finding the words; its observability , whether those losses are visible to the symbiont’s senses, since some gaps happen entirely inside; its closability , whether a row of Table 12 matches it at a usable maturity; and the feasibility of channels and trust , whether the group is willing and able to wear the devices and how far it will authorize them. The natural first population is the one of highest cognitive density, professionals whose gaps are easiest to observe and who will pay to close them. The same symbiont may be worth more to three other groups: older adults whose memory is failing, for whom it is a memory prosthesis; people who are learning, for whom it knows what they do not; and people whose senses or language are limited, for whom it is a channel they lack. Figure 19 maps populations on two of these dimensions, the size and stakes of the gap and how much of it can be closed today (observability times closability), with bubble size for population and color for the maturity of the evidence; feasibility is argued in the text, and the rest of the section argues each bubble.

#### 13.2 Everyday Life

The gaps of an ordinary day are modest in size and relentless in frequency: a bill, a parcel, a promise to a child; the signs one misses, one’s own fatigue, a partner’s mood; the decisions one cannot think through, whether to change jobs or move; the words one cannot find for a parent or a manager; and the errands of the whole first tier. Most of these are observable, since they happen in calendars, messages, and conversations. Closability is high: the first tier is available now and the everyday emotional and decision functions need research. The obstacle is feasibility, price and trust, and it is the largest obstacle any group faces. The value takes the form of ease: a little less forgotten, a little less wrong, a little more of one’s own time. An ordinary person has no need of Jarvis; a helper that remembers, arrives in time, and does not interrupt is enough. This is also the positive form of the fairness question in Section 15.7 : a paradigm that reaches only those who can pay forgoes its largest total value.


#### 13.3 Knowledge Work

Researchers, engineers, managers, clinicians, lawyers, founders, negotiators, and diplomats share high decision density, large information loads, short windows, and a high price for a wrong sentence. Their gaps lie in three places: real-time recall in meetings and conversations (Section 9.4 ), threads that span weeks and months (Section 9.2 ), and blindness to their own state under load (Section 9.3 ). The gaps are large, nearly all observable, since the day is lived in screens and meetings, closable with current technology for the meeting case (Section 8 ), and most feasible of all, since this group pays for productivity and will wear the devices. They come first because they are the easiest group on which to verify the paradigm, whatever the size of their need. A researcher gains a record in which every branch of a years-long question is kept, with metacognitive and creative support; a clinician gains real-time recall in consultation and an organized account of what the patient said, with diagnostic decisions explicitly outside the boundary; a manager or negotiator draws most on the relations layer, the shared history with each counterpart, while real-time reading of the other side stays within the limits of Section 15.5 and the law of the workplace. The section treats this group as the most easily verified proving ground.


#### 13.4 Learners and Teachers

Learning is where the value of a symbiont is clearest and where restraint matters most. A student gains a tutor that has seen every place they got stuck and can give a hint at the right moment instead of an answer; a language learner gains a hand in real conversation; an adult learner changing fields gains a bridge from what they know to what is new. A teacher gains real-time social cognition in the classroom, which student has fallen behind, and afterwards notes on each student’s progress, kept within the rules the school sets for student data. The gap is large and long; the process is largely observable; closability is moderate, with the effect of one-to-one tutoring as the backdrop ( Bloom, 1984 ) and the evidence of Section 10.5 as the warning; feasibility for minors is a separate question (Section 13.9 ). The default in learning has to be the hint, with “I want to learn this myself” as a first-class option.


#### 13.5 Families Across the Life Course

Three generations have three gaps. Parents are short of sleep, overloaded, and carried along by their children’s emotions; the symbiont serves the parent, helping them stay steady, remember, and notice, and any sensing of a child passes through the parent’s authorization and is limited to safety and development. Children do not, in this paper, have all-day symbionts of their own; their gaps are served indirectly through parents and teachers, for reasons given in Sections 13.9 and 16.7 . Older adults sit in the upper right of Figure 19 . The gap of memory decline is the most literal instance of Definition 4.1 ; it is highly observable, since the forgotten thing is right there; it has precedent, in the SenseCam studies of memory-impaired patients ( Berry et al., 2007 ) ; and its obstacles are wearing and learning cost, so the symbiont of an older adult must be the simplest of all. The value is independence for a few more years and a few more degrees of connection to family, and the caregivers of Section 9.5 belong to the same picture. The ethical weight here is second only to Section 13.7 : for people whose capacity to consent is declining, authorization itself is the question, and the paper requires proxy authorization with exit available at all times.


#### 13.6 Sensory, Motor, Cognitive, and Language Barriers

For people whose abilities are limited, a symbiont is a channel before it is an augmentation, and the evidence here is the most mature in the paper. For blind and low-vision users, first-person description and navigation already exist ( Be My Eyes, 2023 ; Microsoft, 2017 ) , and what the symbiont adds is that it knows the person’s surroundings and their people. For deaf and hard-of-hearing users, live captions and speaker identification are what a line in the glasses was made for. For users with motor impairments, EMG and silent-speech input, neural interfaces as they mature, and the errands of the first tier have the highest value of any group. For people with cognitive differences, there is executive-function support for starting and switching tasks, and real-time support for social cues, where the design must support and never correct, with the person defining what help they want. For migrants, second-language speakers, and dialect communities, the cultural frame switching of Section 9.4 is daily survival. All four criteria score high, and this group already wears assistive devices. The accessibility tradition of “nothing about us without us” applies: these users should define their own symbionts.


#### 13.7 Mental Health and Wellbeing

Three groups belong here: people under long-term pressure approaching burnout, people with mild to moderate anxiety or depressive symptoms, and people in professional treatment. The gap is the largest in the paper, since emotional distress consumes cognitive bandwidth directly and shrinks the $c_{t}$ of Definition 4.1 . Observability is moderate and rising, since sleep, social withdrawal, and changes in language are all perceptible, and the interpretation of each is fragile. Closability is split: a first randomized trial of a generative chatbot for treatment reported reductions in symptoms ( Heinz et al., 2025 ) , while evidence on unsupervised use over long periods remains thin. Feasibility is paradoxical: this group needs the private channel most and fears for its data most. The position of the paper is to enter through support and early recognition and never through treatment, to make the handoff to professionals part of the function, and to place people in serious distress in the upper left of Figure 19 : a large gap that cannot be closed first.


#### 13.8 Public-Facing and High-Stakes Roles

These are people whose work is real-time interaction with others under a high cost of error: frontline service and customer-facing staff (recalling this person, reading this moment), teachers (Section 13.4 ), emergency and safety personnel (an external working memory and checklist under pressure), interpreters and diplomats (all of Section 9.4 ). Their gap is concentrated in real-time social cognition, highly observable, closable to the degree the fast loop’s latency and accuracy allow (Section 8.2 ), and feasible since the workplace already permits worn devices. The ethical question is the employer: whom the symbiont answers to (the commitment of Section 12.3 to answer to the person) is sharpest inside an employment relation, and compelled wearing is one of the cases of the next subsection.


#### 13.9 Who Should Not Come First

Three groups should not be first, and saying so is part of the argument. Children with all-day sensing of their own: consent, development, and the permanence of a lifelong record are unresolved, and children are served through the symbionts of the adults around them. People in acute psychological crisis with a symbiont as their only support: not until handoff paths are mature. Anyone in a compelled setting, an employer’s requirement, an institution’s rule, a correctional facility: the premise of a symbiont is the person’s own authorization, and a compelled system is a monitor, which is one of the futures of Section 15.7 . The resulting order follows. The first adopters are knowledge workers, because they are the easiest to verify. The largest gain per person is for older adults and for people with barriers, because their gaps are the most direct. The largest total gain is in everyday life, because the population is the largest.
