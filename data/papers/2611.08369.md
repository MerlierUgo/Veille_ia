## NPCBench: A Clinical Apprenticeship Benchmark for Multimodal Care-Pathway Reasoning in Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma

Current medical benchmarks typically break down clinical competence into isolated factual questions, single-image tasks, or static case vignettes. These tests score individual answers, but reveal less about whether a model can integrate subspecialty knowledge with multimodal evidence and track a patient’s condition throughout a course of care. To close this gap, we introduce NPCBench , a clinical apprenticeship benchmark for nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC). NPCBench mirrors specialist training, from guideline and foundational knowledge to case planning, longitudinal review, CarePath integration, and open-ended consultation. It contains 4,777 component and consultation items and a 100-patient CarePath cohort with 417 scored diagnostic and care tasks. The episode-level evaluation tests whether diagnostic and care answers are jointly correct within a care pathway. By linking stepwise tasks to patient-level care trajectories, NPCBench evaluates whether models ground decisions in visual evidence, interpret changes in patient state, and meet clinical requirements in consultation. Across 30 evaluated models, we observe a composition gap: strong performance on individual tasks does not ensure jointly correct diagnostic and care answers for the same patient. Among 21 multimodal models, strict CarePath success reaches at most 23%. Paired controls and physician calibration further examine evidence grounding, longitudinal revision, and consultation judging. Overall, NPCBench broadens the evaluation of medical artificial intelligence beyond static question-answering paradigms to an apprenticeship-oriented assessment of clinical workflows, thereby offering a testbed for evidence-based and temporally coherent reasoning in oncology care.


### 1 Introduction

Medical large language models (LLMs) and multimodal LLMs (MLLMs) now achieve strong results in medical examinations, visual question answering, and broad benchmark suites [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 ] . However, exam-style accuracy offers limited insight into subspecialty care, where clinicians must synthesize disease-specific guidelines, multimodal evidence, and evolving patient states over time. During clinical apprenticeship, physicians do not learn these skills in isolation. Recalling a clinical guideline is only the first step; specialists must also ground staging in diagnostic images, tailor treatment plans to patient constraints, and revise decisions as new responses or toxicities emerge. Evaluating medical AI therefore requires following this same progression from foundational knowledge to longitudinal management. While isolated tasks can pinpoint weaknesses in individual skills, only linked patient episodes can reveal whether these skills succeed together for the same patient [ 7 , 8 , 9 ] .

Evaluating the progression from individual competencies to integrated clinical care requires a disease setting in which multimodal evidence and sequential decisions are closely connected. Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), prevalent in Southern China and Southeast Asia [ 10 , 11 , 12 ] , is managed according to staging systems and clinical guidelines [ 13 , 14 , 15 ] . Diagnosis, staging, and risk assessment require integrating histopathology, multi-sequence MRI, and plasma EBV DNA [ 16 , 11 ] . Clinical evidence and management objectives vary across stages of care, with subsequent decisions shaped by both the current patient state and preceding clinical events. During treatment and follow-up, tumor response, treatment-related toxicities, and recurrence or progression may influence management strategies [ 14 , 15 , 12 ] . NPC therefore provides a suitable setting for evaluating both individual specialist competencies and their integration across successive stages of care.

To systematically assess these capabilities, we introduce NPCBench , a clinical apprenticeship benchmark for NPC modeled on the progressive training of specialist physicians. As shown in Fig. 1 , it organizes the evaluation into six progressive levels and an open-ended consultation suite. L1 and L2 test guideline recall and application. L3 tests pathology and MRI evidence grounding. L4 tests case planning from a single patient state, L5 longitudinal decision revision, and L6 linked CarePath episodes. O1 evaluates rubric-based specialist consultation. By pairing component tasks with patient trajectories, NPCBench asks a central measurement question: Are diagnostic and care answers jointly correct for the same patient when component scores are high? Models with similar average accuracy can exhibit different patient-level error patterns, or even select the right treatment while misinterpreting the underlying MRI evidence. NPCBench therefore pairs task-level scores with strict patient-level correctness, while holding patients and questions fixed in matched controls to examine how visual evidence, clinical chronology, and answer formats shape model decisions.

Using NPCBench, we investigate four research questions (RQ1–RQ4) across 30 medical and general-purpose models. RQ1 examines how well models translate guideline knowledge into case-level planning: guideline recall exceeds 92%, whereas the highest case-planning accuracy is 71.9%. RQ2 probes whether staging predictions are grounded in accurate visual interpretation: complete invasion correctness reaches at most 5.0% on the 100-patient anatomical panel, showing that correct stage labels often lack complete anatomical support. RQ3 examines decision revision and patient-level success: history can correct answers and introduce new errors. In CarePath, Gemini-3.1 achieves 89.9% Care accuracy but 11.0% Strict success; Claude-4.6 has the highest Strict success at 23.0%. RQ4 assesses consultation safety and automated judge reliability: GPT-5.1, the highest-scoring consultation model, triggers major-error criteria in 14.6% of responses under the primary judge, while rubric judges show sensitivity–specificity imbalances against physician annotations.

We summarize the main contributions of this work as follows.

- ① A clinical apprenticeship evaluation framework. We shift the evaluation focus from static answer correctness to pathway correctness. NPCBench assesses whether models make consistent clinical decisions across patient-state transitions, integrating guideline constraints, multimodal evidence grounding, and longitudinal disease evolution.

- ② A specialist-curated multimodal and longitudinal dataset. We release 4,777 component and consultation items alongside 100 CarePath episodes with 417 scored diagnostic and care tasks, unifying guideline rules, pathology slides, multi-sequence MRI scans, longitudinal EHR trajectories, and consultation rubrics within a single oncology pathway.

- ③ Diagnostic dissection of the clinical composition gap. Across 30 models, we combine capability profiling with controlled interventions and physician-calibrated judging to identify where visual grounding separates from treatment decisions, how patient history changes decisions, and where high local scores coexist with patient-level errors.

### 2 Related Work

From exam QA to clinically structured benchmarks. Early evaluations of medical LLMs relied on static, examination-style question–answering benchmarks, including MedQA [ 17 ] , MedMCQA [ 18 ] , PubMedQA [ 19 ] , the medical subsets of MMLU [ 20 ] , MultiMedQA [ 2 ] , MedBench [ 21 ] , CMB [ 22 ] , and CMExam [ 23 ] . These benchmarks capture medical knowledge and local reasoning, but they do not test whether a model can accumulate patient evidence, review decisions, and preserve coherence across the care pathway. Recent broad suites such as MedHELM [ 8 ] , MedXpertQA [ 24 ] , and AnesSuite’s AnesBench [ 25 ] move the evaluation toward clinically meaningful capabilities and specialty-aware reasoning. NPCBench follows this direction and makes the clinical apprenticeship ladder explicit: it measures component skills alongside longitudinal decisions and episode-level care correctness.

Multimodal, longitudinal, and rubric-based clinical evaluation. Medical MLLM benchmarks have expanded from image QA datasets such as VQA-RAD [ 26 ] , SLAKE [ 27 ] , and PathVQA [ 28 ] to broader evaluations of multimodality, trustworthiness, endoscopy, clinical reasoning, and sequence grounding [ 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 ] . In parallel, HealthBench [ 34 ] , LiveMedBench [ 35 ] , LLMEval-Med [ 36 ] , MedDialogRubrics [ 37 ] , and ClinConsensus [ 38 ] use expert-validated rubrics or temporally grounded cases to evaluate open-ended clinical responses. Workflow-oriented resources such as MedJourney [ 39 ] , MedAgentBench [ 40 ] , MedAgentBoard [ 41 ] , and MTBBench [ 42 ] further shift evaluation from static snapshots toward state transitions. NPCBench brings these directions into a single disease setting: pathology and MRI evidence grounding, patient-level longitudinal revision, strict CarePath episodes, and criterion-level consultation rubrics. Appendix B gives a fuller comparison with recent medical benchmark families.


### 3 NPCBench: A Clinical Apprenticeship Benchmark


#### 3.1 Overview

NPCBench organizes the clinical apprenticeship framework into a progressive capability ladder (L1–L6) and cross-level consultation rubrics (O1). The ladder spans guideline memory, atomic application, multimodal evidence grounding, case-level planning, longitudinal decision revision, and linked care episodes. Lower levels (L1–L4) assess individual capability components; L5–L6 examine longitudinal decisions and joint diagnostic–care correctness within patients. O1 complements these tasks by assessing free-text consultation quality. These tasks assess whether specialist decisions follow guidelines, use multimodal evidence, and remain consistent with a patient’s clinical course.

Four design principles guide task construction and clinical review: (i) Competence isolation requires each level in the progressive capability ladder (L1–L6) to measure a distinct, clinically meaningful component of specialist competence. (ii) Evidence-boundary control fixes the available evidence at the intended clinical decision point, removing future treatment, outcomes, and other answer-leaking cues. (iii) Clinical determinacy requires reference decisions supported by guidelines or expert consensus, given the provided clinical evidence. (iv) Auditable open-ended scoring uses case-specific consultation rubrics (O1) to assess clinical justification, evidence grounding, conditional reasoning, and safety. All patient materials are de-identified, and NPC oncologists verified component validity, decision determinacy, and the fidelity of patient-state transitions. Figure 2 shows the composition of each subset, and Fig. 3 illustrates the pipeline for constructing L1–L5 items.


#### 3.2 Construction and Validation

Expert-curated clinical materials are converted into standardized evaluation items and reference answers. L1–L2 were derived from CSCO and NCCN guidelines; L3 from pathology and multi-sequence MRI images; and L4–L6 and O1 from de-identified real-world patient records, covering case-level planning, longitudinal decision-making, full CarePath evaluation, and open-ended consultations with scoring rubrics.

Multidisciplinary reviewers in oncology, pathology, and radiology assessed clinical validity, evidence sufficiency, and reference standards. For L1–L2, experts verified guideline consistency and answer determinacy; for L3, they validated pathology labels, MRI sequence and anatomical structure identification, invasion status, and T/N staging against imaging evidence. For L4, they assessed case sufficiency and appropriateness of reference decisions given patient-specific clinical states; for L5–L6, they verified evidence from decision-points, reference actions, and transitions from clinical state; and for O1, they reviewed core clinical requirements, supporting evidence, conditional recommendations, and major/minor error criteria to ensure clinically valid and auditable scoring.

#### 3.3 Evaluation Levels and Scoring Units

L1.G-Mem: Guideline memory. The 250 expert-reviewed questions assess NPC guideline knowledge: 153 have both CSCO and NCCN support, 80 CSCO support only, and 17 NCCN support only. They probe treatment indications, staging implications, recommendation boundaries, surveillance, and contraindications. Item accuracy is the primary endpoint.

L2.A-App: Atomic guideline application. The 185 short-vignette questions cover initial definitive management, recurrence, metastatic/systemic management, and toxicity or contraindications. The main score averages accuracy over these four capability groups, giving each group equal weight.

L3.MM-EG: Multimodal evidence grounding. Pathology contains 69 overview cases (39 NPC-positive and 30 negative). MRI comprises 200 sequence-recognition questions, 400 T-stage cases, and 400 N-stage cases, with patient-disjoint T and N cohorts. Within the 400 T-stage cases, a 100-patient anatomical panel provides 2,700 structure cards covering 18 base structures, evaluating both structure identification and tumor invasion assessment. Overall, we report pathology, MRI sequence, structure-identification, T-stage, and N-stage accuracy separately, with additional metrics for invasion accuracy, joint structure–invasion correctness, and evidence-grounded T-stage success.

L4.C-Plan: Case-level planning. The 165 questions cover 84 patient groups and provide clinical snapshots of stage, treatment history, response, organ function, and other relevant constraints. Plausible alternative plans test whether a model identifies the next action appropriate to that specific state. Patient-macro accuracy weights patients equally.

L5.L-Rev: Longitudinal revision. The 305 questions cover 100 trajectories with one to five scored checkpoints at treatment, reassessment, toxicity, recurrence, or follow-up. Each checkpoint requires interpreting new findings in the context of earlier treatment and response. We report turn-micro accuracy and all-turn patient success. The primary task supplies full history in one prompt; matched-history and serial-interaction controls are evaluated separately.

L6.CP-Epi: CarePath episodes. The 100-patient CarePath cohort comprises 417 tasks: 100 pathology assessments, 100 MRI T-stage assessments, and 217 care decisions. Clinical records remain patient-linked; care-decision selection uses model-informed difficulty enrichment to prioritize clinical decisions that challenge models (Appendix C.3.5 ). Strict requires correct pathology, MRI T-stage, and every care answer; structure predictions provide auxiliary diagnostics. Each care question is answered independently with clinical context, incorporating the model’s pathology/T predictions and reference N/M/overall stages where required.

O1.C-Rub: ConsultRubrics. The 103 consultation items span 42 patients and 1,726 rubric criteria: 1,069 positive requirements and 657 negative criteria. Satisfied positive requirements add weighted credit; triggered major and minor errors deduct two and one points, respectively. The sum is floored at zero after item-level aggregation, normalized to 0–100, and averaged over all 103 item scores. The primary judge and physician calibration are described below.

Patient-level Metrics. On CarePath episodes, we report both decision-level accuracy ( $\mathrm{Care}$ ) and strict patient-level success ( $\mathrm{Strict}$ ), which requires correct pathology, MRI T stage, and all selected care answers for the same patient. For a given model, let $p=1,\ldots,P$ index patients and $\mathcal{S}_{p}$ denote the nonempty set of scored care decisions for patient $p$ , with $n_{p}=|\mathcal{S}_{p}|$ and $N=\sum_{p=1}^{P}n_{p}$ . Let $c_{pj}$ , $d_{p}$ and $t_{p}\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether the care response $j$ , the pathological label, and the T-stage MRI label are correct, respectively. With $P$ patients and $N$ total decisions, the percentage endpoints are

#### 4.2 Guideline Knowledge and Case Planning

Table 1 summarizes model performance across all benchmark tasks. Addressing RQ1 , we examine how foundational guideline competence translates into case-level planning. Although Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview leads the guideline recall with 94.0% and the atomic application with 92.5%, its precision drops to 71.9% in L4, followed by Claude-Sonnet-4.6 and Gemini-2.5-Pro. In addition, models with nearly identical L1 and L2 scores—such as GPT-5.1 and DeepSeek-V3.1—diverge sharply on L4 planning (63.5 vs. 51.8%). Although these subsets differ in item composition and weighting, this divergence shows that multi-factor case planning must be evaluated directly rather than inferred from guideline accuracy. Furthermore, a paired comparison on 71 physician-audited L1 items reveals that even high guideline scores partly rely on multiple-choice cues: removing answer options while holding the target knowledge fixed lowers accuracy by 7.0 percentage points (pp) for Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview, 12.7 pp for GPT-4.1, and 15.5 pp for GPT-5.1 shown as in Figure. 7 i.


#### 4.3 Evidence Grounding and Longitudinal Revision

Stage accuracy and anatomical grounding reveal different weaknesses. For RQ2 , we first assess whether models can assign report-derived T and N categories across separate 400-patient cohorts. The highest T-stage accuracy is 43.5% across 400 patients, compared to a baseline of 37.5% in the majority-class. N-stage accuracy reaches 42.8% across a separate 400 patients, whose N1 majority baseline is 40.0%. However, overall accuracy masks differences in performance across different stages: Qwen3.6-27B achieves the highest overall N accuracy of 42.8% but only 31.9% balanced accuracy, whereas Gemini-3.1 maintains consistent performance across stages. Thus the total stage accuracy alone can therefore obscure class-imbalance shortcuts, a pattern that persists across T-staging image formats (Table A11 ) and on an external 53-patient cohort from a second center (Table A12 ).

A 100-case anatomical panel tests whether stage predictions rest on valid visual evidence. Direct T-stage accuracy reaches 43.0%, yet complete 18-structure invasion and evidence-grounded T-stage success (requiring correct labels and final stage) both fall below 5.0% (Table A15 ; Fig. 7 a). Correct stage labels thus frequently coexist with flawed anatomical assessments. To isolate this breakdown, we test a fixed staging rule across three evidence conditions (Fig. 7 f): reference findings ( Ref. ), predicted invasion with known anatomy ( Known ), and fully predicted evidence ( Pred. ). With reference findings, both a deterministic rule and a fixed GPT-OSS-120B stager reach 99.0% accuracy (Tables A17 and A18 ), confirming that downstream staging logic is sound. However, the accuracy of Gemini-3.1 drops to 49.0% ( Known ) and 36.0% ( Pred. ). Even with correct anatomical localization, flawed invasion recognition remains the primary bottleneck. A separate 25-case static vs. cine MRI comparison yields an inconclusive T-stage difference ( section C.4 ).

Longitudinal decision accuracy does not ensure patient-level success. For RQ3 , we first examine L5 performance across 305 decisions from 100 patients. Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview achieves the highest turn accuracy (87.9%) and all-turn patient success (71.0%), followed by Claude-Sonnet-4.6 (85.2% and 63.0%, respectively; Fig. 4 b). While turn accuracy measures individual decisions in isolation, all-turn success is substantially lower across models because it depends on how errors distribute across trajectories—any single misstep invalidates an entire patient episode.

In paired controlled runs across the same 305 questions and 100 patients, we compare full chronological history against a snapshot that strips time markers and regroups the same records by content (Table A25 ). Full history yields higher turn-accuracy point estimates for 14 of 20 models, yet the patient-bootstrap 95% confidence intervals span zero for 19 models; DeepSeek-V3.1 gains 4.9 pp and Gemini-3.1 gains 3.3 pp. Net gains can hide offsetting errors: GPT-5.1 is correct on 22 questions only with full history and 19 only with the snapshot. Thus a 1.0 pp turn-accuracy gain coexists with a 1.0 pp decline in all-turn success. Three of 20 models show opposite directions for these endpoints (Fig. 7 b,g). Correcting one turn may leave other errors, whereas a new error can invalidate an otherwise correct episode.

To separate record order from temporal information, an order-only control shuffles complete lines while retaining all time markers. Nineteen of 20 models have confidence intervals spanning zero, with Gemini-3.1 as the sole exception (+3.0 pp; 95% CI: 0.6–5.7 pp). Tables A25 and A26 report the paired results. A separate conversation-retention comparison involving 87 patients also yields an interval spanning zero.

For RQ3 , all-care correctness is $C_{p}=\prod_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{p}}c_{pj}$ and joint pathology–MRI T-stage correctness is $F_{p}=d_{p}t_{p}$ (Eq. ( 1 )). In the CarePath cohort, Gemini-3.1 achieves the highest Care accuracy (195/217 decisions, 89.9%) and All-care success (83.0%), yet its Strict success is only 11.0%. In contrast, Claude-4.6 achieves lower Care (80.6%) and All-care (64.0%) but the highest Strict success (23.0%; exact 95% CI: 15.2–32.5%; Fig. 4 c–e). Thus care accuracy alone does not identify which models most often answer all diagnostic and care questions correctly for a patient.

Joint correctness identifies the patients behind this gap. For Gemini-3.1, 72 of 100 patients have all care decisions correct despite at least one incorrect pathology or MRI T-stage label; the corresponding number is 41 for Claude-4.6. Conversely, Hulu-Med-32B has 38 patients with both diagnostic labels correct but at least one incorrect care decision (Fig. 6 ; Table A20 ). Care answers and diagnostic labels can therefore disagree in correctness.

To further examine whether diagnostic evidence affects care decisions, we compare predicted and reference pathology, MRI T-stage, and invasion findings in 25 CarePath patients. Fourteen local models answer the same 146 care questions under both conditions, with other clinical context fixed. Patient-averaged care accuracy, excluding diagnostic scores, changes by $-3.2$ to $+4.1$ pp, with 12 of 14 intervals including zero (Fig. 7 d; Table A23 ). These mixed changes show no consistent care benefit from reference evidence under the supplied clinical context.

Care and All-care outcomes also differ across episode groups. Nine of 21 models have lower decision-weighted Care but higher patient-weighted All-care in the 75 assigned-pathology episodes than in the 25 patient-matched episodes. Claude-4.6 scores 76.5% versus 84.3% for Care, but 68.0% versus 52.0% for All-care (Table A21 ). The groups contribute 102 and 115 decisions, respectively. Figure 7 c,h further examines performance by decision count: 1–2/3/4–5 for L5 and 1/2/3–6 for CarePath, as patient-level success requires every scored decision to be correct.

Class imbalance also matters: constant T3 predictions score 58% in CarePath, above the best model’s 52%. The highest balanced T accuracy is 30.5%, versus 25% for constant predictions (Table A19 ; Fig. A4 ). L3 therefore complements CarePath’s joint endpoint with stage-discrimination and grounding tests.

### 5 Conclusion

NPCBench links foundational guideline knowledge and multimodal image interpretation to longitudinal decision-making, joint patient-level correctness, and specialist consultation in NPC care. Evaluating 30 models reveals that local accuracy rarely guarantees coherent patient management: stage predictions frequently lack complete anatomical support, longitudinal history corrects prior mistakes while introducing new errors, and high-scoring consultations still trigger critical safety violations. On CarePath episodes, accurate treatment choices routinely coexist with flawed image interpretations, capping strict patient-level success at just 23.0% despite substantially higher decision-level accuracy. These findings establish evidence–decision consistency as a central benchmark target: meaningful clinical progress requires models to ground interpretations in valid evidence and sustain accurate decisions across the entire course of care for the same patient.

Limitations. Our evaluation is constrained by primarily single-center records, assigned pathology in 75 model-selected CarePath episodes, score sensitivity to class balance and episode length, and the six-case Expanded Audit. Future more multi-center cohorts with fully patient-matched modalities and prospective studies are therefore needed to establish whether benchmark gains improve clinical care.

### Appendix A Data Curation and Statistics

The following sections describe the clinical sources, question construction, patient coverage, and reference annotations.

The retrospective use of clinical data was approved under IRB protocol B2026-465-01. Patient materials follow the hospital’s de-identification procedure. Direct identifiers and unnecessary location details are removed, and dates are expressed relative to the patient’s clinical course where appropriate. L4 retains clinical decision dates needed to identify the applicable guideline edition. Age and sex are retained when needed for reasoning. NPC oncology specialists review clinical validity, reference answers, supporting evidence, and transitions between care decisions.

L1 and L2 translate guideline knowledge and decision thresholds into questions with adjudicated answers. L4 combines diagnosis, stage, treatment history, response, toxicity, and patient-specific constraints into fixed-state planning cases. L5 organizes longitudinal records around clinical decision checkpoints, retaining the cumulative evidence available at each point and removing subsequent orders or outcomes that reveal the target decision. L6 links pathology, MRI, and selected care decisions within patient episodes. O1 uses consultation scenarios with separate criteria for core conclusions, supporting evidence, conditional reasoning, and clinically important errors. L1 retains item-level guideline references and supporting evidence. All 350 L2/L4 items include their construction sources and reviewed answer rationales. Source records distinguish the guideline edition stated in a question from references supporting its answer: the former is available to the model, whereas the latter explains the reference decision. Recorded edition constraints occur in two L2 and four L4 questions and are included in the model inputs; edition and section references are available for a subset of items.

The evaluated sets contain 250 L1, 185 L2, and 165 L4 questions. Reviewers checked the evidence available at the decision point, the applicable guideline, and whether the options supported a determinate answer. L2 includes one explicit multiple-select item and L4 includes two; the other items are single choice. Multiple-select answers must match the complete reference set. The question specifies this requirement, and selecting only part of the set receives no credit. Reviewed rationales accompany all 350 L2/L4 items.

### Appendix B Detailed Related Work

Static medical QA and broad medical suites. Examination-style benchmarks such as MedQA, MedMCQA, PubMedQA, the medical subsets of MMLU, CMB, and CMExam have provided a practical basis for assessing medical knowledge and localized reasoning skills [ 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 22 , 23 ] . MultiMedQA and MedBench extended this approach by combining multiple medical QA datasets and testing more general medical reasoning across them [ 2 , 21 ] . More recent benchmarks—including MedHELM, MedXpertQA, and AnesSuite’s AnesBench—emphasize construct validity, specialty-specific reasoning, and clinical abilities alongside exam-style performance [ 8 , 24 , 25 ] . NPCBench focuses on NPC and reports component performance and joint patient correctness separately.

Multimodal medical evidence grounding. Medical MLLM evaluation has progressed beyond image-question datasets like VQA-RAD, SLAKE, and PathVQA toward benchmarks with broader modality coverage and greater clinical specialization [ 26 , 27 , 28 ] . Frameworks such as OmniMedVQA, GMAI-MMBench, BenchX, CARES, and BrainMD assess dimensions including the breadth of medical imaging modalities, model reliability, and cross-modality generalization [ 29 , 48 , 49 , 33 , 50 ] . More domain-specific benchmarks—EndoBench, DrVD-Bench, MedSG-Bench, MMOral, M3CoT-Bench, and MedThinkVQA—target fine-grained image understanding, temporal or sequence-level grounding, and reasoning across multiple images [ 30 , 32 , 31 , 51 , 52 , 53 ] . NPCBench examines these components in NPC, where pathology underpins diagnosis, MRI anatomy delineates local spread, sequence recognition shapes image interpretation, and assessment of local invasion guides staging and treatment intensity.

Rubric-based and real-world clinical response evaluation. Open-ended clinical evaluation is increasingly grounded in explicit scoring rubrics and physician-validated criteria. HealthBench assesses multi-turn health-related dialogues using item-specific rubrics; LiveMedBench focuses on temporally separated clinical cases; LLMEval-Med incorporates physician adjudication combined with checklist-guided evaluation; MedDialogRubrics and ClinConsensus extend rubric-based assessment frameworks to consultation and longitudinal care scenarios [ 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 ] . O1 ConsultRubrics uses the same criterion-level approach for NPC consultation, in which positive requirements capture key clinical conclusions, supporting patient evidence, and conditions that change the recommendation, whereas negative criteria differentiate between major and minor safety-critical errors.

Workflow, longitudinal, and agentic clinical benchmarks. Workflow evaluations test information gathering, interaction, and decisions across multiple steps. MedJourney structures evaluation around the patient journey, whereas MedAgents, MDAgents, MedAgentsBench, and MedAgentBoard focus on collaborative problem-solving, tool integration, and multi-step clinical task execution [ 39 , 54 , 55 , 56 , 41 ] . In oncology, MTBBench emulates multidisciplinary tumor-board decision-making, and recent work on autonomous oncology agents investigates specialized workflows for treatment decision support [ 42 , 57 ] . AgentClinic evaluates tool use and information gathering in simulated clinical encounters, while MedAgentBench tests tasks in a FHIR-compatible electronic health record [ 58 , 40 ] . Clinical-environment simulators additionally expose models to explicit state transitions [ 59 ] . NPCBench evaluates related questions within NPC: L5 tests decisions under different representations of longitudinal records, and L6 checks image labels and selected care answers for the same patient. Its primary care evaluation supplies clinical context without feeding previous care answers back, whereas separate serial controls test conversation retention. This distinction separates joint patient correctness from autonomous interaction with a clinical environment.

##### C.2.2 Classification Diagnostics

For a target class $k$ , $\mathrm{TP}_{k}$ counts correctly predicted members of that class, $\mathrm{FN}_{k}$ counts missed members, and $\mathrm{FP}_{k}$ counts other examples predicted as class $k$ . Class recall $R_{k}$ , precision $Q_{k}$ , and secondary $F1_{k}$ are proportions:

[Tableau] $$R_{k}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}_{k}}{\mathrm{TP}_{k}+\mathrm{FN}_{k}},\qquad Q_{k}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}_{k}}{\mathrm{TP}_{k}+\mathrm{FP}_{k}},\qquad F1_{k}=\frac{2\mathrm{TP}_{k}}{2\mathrm{TP}_{k}+\mathrm{FP}_{k}+\mathrm{FN}_{k}}.$$ (A4)

These ratios require nonzero denominators; undefined diagnostic ratios are unavailable. For $K$ evaluated classes with reference support, balanced accuracy is $100K^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K}R_{k}$ . T staging uses $K=4$ (T1–T4), so a majority-class predictor has 25% balanced accuracy. Per-class recalls shown as percentages equal $100R_{k}$ . L3 structure-card accuracy and these class-balanced measures have different aggregation units; the main structure column uses card-micro accuracy.


##### C.2.3 Paired Effects and Uncertainty

For the binary endpoint in Eq. ( A6 ), index the $M$ matched units by $i$ . Let $a_{i}^{(0)}$ and $a_{i}^{(1)}$ be correctness under baseline and intervention, respectively. The counts are $u=\sum_{i}\mathbf{1}\{a_{i}^{(0)}=0,a_{i}^{(1)}=1\}$ and $v=\sum_{i}\mathbf{1}\{a_{i}^{(0)}=1,a_{i}^{(1)}=0\}$ . The remaining $M-u-v$ units retain the same correctness. Consequently, $\Delta\in[-100,100]$ is a percentage-point difference and $\Phi\in[0,100]$ is a percentage of correctness flips; changes between two incorrect answers do not contribute to either count.

The image-label comparison instead compares within-patient care accuracies. For condition $k\in\{0,1\}$ , define the proportion $A_{p}^{(k)}=n_{p}^{-1}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{p}}c_{pj}^{(k)}$ . With $k=0$ for predicted image labels and $k=1$ for reference image labels, its reported effect is

[Tableau] $$\Delta_{\mathrm{patient}}=\frac{100}{P}\sum_{p=1}^{P}\left(A_{p}^{(1)}-A_{p}^{(0)}\right).$$ (A5)

Here $P=25$ and $\sum_{p}n_{p}=146$ . The better , worse , and same columns in Table A23 count patients according to the sign of $A_{p}^{(1)}-A_{p}^{(0)}$ . The patient-macro effect also incorporates each patient’s change in accuracy.

Care accuracy and paired patient comparisons use a patient-cluster bootstrap. Each draw resamples patients with replacement, retaining all of a sampled patient’s scored units and both conditions of a pair. Each draw recomputes the relevant estimator: a ratio of turn totals for micro accuracy or a mean of patient accuracies for patient macro. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles form the reported 95% interval. L1 controls resample paired items; physician-calibration intervals resample consultation-response clusters. CarePath cohort strict-success intervals are exact binomial (Clopper–Pearson) intervals over 100 patients. Multi-model intervals are descriptive and unadjusted.

##### C.3.3 L3 Multimodal Evidence-Grounding Diagnostics

The L3 diagnostics examine three linked questions: whether the marked anatomy is identified, whether its invasion status is correct, and whether the resulting evidence supports the T category. They use the 100-case anatomical panel, with 18 invasion labels and one T category per patient. Structure identification is scored over 2,700 cards covering 18 base structures; nasopharynx cards are excluded from this primary MRI scoring set. The text-stager experiment uses structured findings as input; the CarePath and cine inputs are described in sections C.3.5 and C.4 .

Patient-level measures then distinguish partial evidence from complete evidence. Pt. Inv. averages invasion correctness over all 18 structures within each patient and then across patients. Complete Inv. requires all 18 labels to be correct; EG T additionally requires the correct final T stage. A model can therefore obtain a correct direct T label while failing complete evidence or EG T. ##### C.3.5 CarePath Evaluation and Cohort Composition

The CarePath cohort contains 100 patients, 811 clinical context entries, and 217 scored care decisions. Each model provides 100 pathology predictions, 100 MRI predictions, and 217 care answers. Table A19 reports component performance and patient-level outcomes; Table A20 gives their intersections. Figure A5 shows how requiring pathology, T stage, and care answers to be correct together narrows the set of successful patients.

HealthGPT-Pro-8B, Qwen3-VL-8B, Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B, and Qwen3.5-27B participated in cohort and decision selection. They are marked in the results tables, and the other 17 models were not used in cohort construction. The 25 episodes with matched pathology contribute 115 decisions; the 75 with assigned specimens contribute 102. MRI and clinical records are patient-linked throughout. All assigned specimens are labelled NPC-positive; the corresponding episode records identify their specimen source.

Selection used the four construction models’ care answers with gold pathology and MRI evidence and reference history. Twenty-five patients with matched multimodal data were included. Among the other 86 reviewed patients, those with at least one construction-model error were ranked first by total errors across models and checkpoints, then by the number of checkpoints missed by at least two models, checkpoints missed by any model, maximum errors per checkpoint, and mean errors per checkpoint; a fixed source identifier broke remaining ties. The first 75 patients completed the CarePath cohort. Within these 100 patients, checkpoints answered correctly by all four models were removed from scoring. This procedure yields 217 scored decisions, including a 93-decision subset missed by at least two construction models. Selection therefore targets clinically relevant decisions that these models found difficult.

Six treatment questions are excluded from scoring because the source records do not establish a unique treatment choice at the specified checkpoint. The identified issues include combining ordered and administered medicines and incomplete evidence of radiotherapy. Their exclusion reasons accompany the data, and all 100 patients remain in the scored cohort. The scored set contains 52/23/3/7/8/7 patients with 1/2/3/4/5/6 care decisions, respectively. The 75 assigned-specimen episodes contain no scored initial-treatment-selection question; those questions occur in the matched-pathology group.

Care prompts incorporate predicted pathology and T-stage labels where specified: 207 reference predicted pathology, and 133 retain supplied N/M/overall-stage information. Prior care answers are not supplied in the primary protocol. Strict success requires correct pathology, correct MRI T stage, and every selected care answer for a patient. Care accuracy weights the 217 decisions equally; all-care correctness and strict success weight the 100 patients equally. MRI structure predictions and the 811 clinical context entries support the evaluation but are not additional strict-success criteria.

#### C.5 Consultation Scoring and Results

O1 evaluates 103 consultation responses from each model against 1,726 clinician-defined criteria. Positive requirements measure what an answer should contain; error criteria identify claims or recommendations that warrant deductions.


##### C.5.1 Scoring Protocol

Table reports the primary O1 ConsultRubrics results. Each model first generates one free-text consultation response for each of the 103 O1 items. Each response is assessed against its item-specific criteria, yielding 1,726 binary criterion judgments per model. For item $i$ , let $\mathcal{A}_{i}$ , $\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\mathrm{maj}}$ , and $\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\mathrm{min}}$ be the disjoint sets of positive requirements, major-error criteria, and minor-error criteria. Criterion $k$ has a binary judgment $z_{ik}\in\{0,1\}$ , corresponding to criteria_met . A positive-criterion judgment of one means the requirement is satisfied; a negative-criterion judgment of one means the error is present. Each positive anchor has clinician-assigned weight $w_{ik}\geq 0$ in points. The available positive score is $B_{i}=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{i}}w_{ik}=10$ points for every item.

Each triggered major error deducts two points, and each minor error deducts one point. The raw score $r_{i}$ , normalized score $R_{i}$ , and primary model score are

[Tableau] $\displaystyle r_{i}$ $\displaystyle=\max\left\{0,\ \sum_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{i}}w_{ik}z_{ik}-2\sum_{k\in\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\mathrm{maj}}}z_{ik}-\sum_{k\in\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\mathrm{min}}}z_{ik}\right\},$ (A7) $\displaystyle R_{i}$ $\displaystyle=100\,\frac{r_{i}}{B_{i}},\qquad\mathrm{O1}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}R_{i}.$

Here $\mathcal{V}$ contains items with valid judgments for every criterion. Thus $r_{i}\in[0,10]$ is measured in points, while $R_{i}$ and O1 lie in $[0,100]$ . Deductions accumulate across criteria before the item score is floored at zero. The primary mean weights items equally; a clinical-tag score restricts $\mathcal{V}$ to items with that tag. All 30 generators have valid judgments for all 103 items, so $|\mathcal{V}|=103$ .

Error rates have a different unit from score deductions. For severity $s\in\{\mathrm{maj},\mathrm{min}\}$ , let $H_{i}^{s}=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{E}_{i}^{s}}z_{ik}$ count triggered criteria. The item-level error rate is

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{ErrorRate}_{s}=\frac{100}{|\mathcal{V}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{1}\{H_{i}^{s}>0\}.$$ (A8)

An item with several errors of the same severity contributes once to this percentage, although each error contributes its deduction in Eq. ( A7 ). Figure 5 d instead uses criteria as the unit: positive-section rates are the percentage of criteria satisfied, and error-avoidance rates are the percentage of error criteria not triggered. Each criterion counts once in that panel; clinician-assigned weights enter the consultation score in Eq. ( A7 ). In panel (a), $r$ denotes the Pearson correlation across models between major-error rate and O1 score.

The primary judge is GPT-OSS-120B with greedy decoding and medium reasoning effort. Its criterion-specific prompt is shown in appendix E . All 30 generators use this same judge and scoring rule. Physician calibration separately assesses four judges on shared response–criterion pairs, as described in Appendix D .

### Appendix D Clinical Review and Judge Calibration

#### D.1 Clinical Annotation and Adjudication

In routine care, pathology and radiology examinations were initially interpreted by a physician with 1–3 years of experience and finalized by a senior subspecialist with more than five years of experience. Pathology reference labels were taken from finalized reports. For benchmark MRI annotation, another physician independently reviewed the examinations, reports, and staging criteria to assign structure-invasion and T-category labels. Three senior NPC radiation oncologists resolved disagreements by consensus. The independent benchmark review followed routine clinical reporting and preceded consensus adjudication.

For L2, reviewers checked whether the vignette supplied the facts needed for one guideline decision and whether the answer options supported a determinate choice. Missing variables, leading wording, and inconsistent guideline editions prompted revision. For L4, they assessed whether diagnosis, stage, treatment history, response, and contraindications supported the proposed management plan. The evaluated questions distinguish single-choice and explicit multiple-select items ( appendix A ).

For L5, review preserved the order of clinical events after date normalization and restricted each question to evidence available at that checkpoint. Distractors differed in evidence use, treatment timing, toxicity management, or follow-up requirements. For O1, specialists separated essential conclusions, supporting case evidence, and conditional recommendations, then assigned major and minor error penalties according to clinical severity.

#### D.3 Agreement Measures and Sensitivity Analyses

Within either calibration set, let $h_{\ell},\hat{h}_{\ell}\in\{0,1\}$ be the physician and judge labels for criterion $\ell$ , and let $n$ be the number of valid paired labels. Define $\mathrm{TP}$ , $\mathrm{FP}$ , $\mathrm{TN}$ , and $\mathrm{FN}$ as the counts of $(h_{\ell},\hat{h}_{\ell})=(1,1),(0,1),(0,0),(1,0)$ , respectively; they sum to $n$ . A physician-positive label means the criterion is true. For a negative error criterion, this means an error is present, not that the response is clinically correct.

Observed agreement $p_{o}$ , physician-positive rate $q_{H}$ , judge-positive rate $q_{J}$ , chance agreement $p_{e}$ , and Cohen’s $\kappa$ are

[Tableau] $$\begin{gathered}p_{o}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{TN}}{n},\qquad q_{H}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}}{n},\qquad q_{J}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}}{n},\\ p_{e}=q_{H}q_{J}+(1-q_{H})(1-q_{J}),\qquad\kappa=\frac{p_{o}-p_{e}}{1-p_{e}}.\end{gathered}$$ (A9)

Sensitivity is $\mathrm{TP}/(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN})$ , specificity is $\mathrm{TN}/(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP})$ , and balanced agreement is their arithmetic mean. The first conditions on physician-positive labels and the second on physician-negative labels. These rates and $p_{o}$ are proportions; percentage agreement is $100p_{o}$ , as in Table 2 .
