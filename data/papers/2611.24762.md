## A 3 Bench: Compositional Reasoning over Aggressive Interactions in Videos

Recent Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have achieved strong performance on general video understanding benchmarks, yet their ability to reason about participant roles in aggressive interactions remains largely unexplored. Existing violence and crime datasets primarily evaluate whether aggression occurs, without testing whether models can identify who performed the action, who received it, and how participants are related within the scene. To address this gap, we introduce A 3 Bench , a benchmark to study aggressive action and association in videos. The benchmark evaluates whether models can jointly recognize aggressive actions, distinguish aggressors from victims and bystanders, and correctly bind actions to participants under challenging distractor settings. We benchmark 17 multimodal models and find that they struggle substantially on fine-grained aggressive-scene understanding. Performance degrades consistently as reasoning complexity increases, with models frequently confusing aggressors with victims or bystanders, revealing a systematic failure in role-action association rather than coarse aggression recognition alone. We further observe that increasing model scale or generic reasoning models experience limited improvement. Finally, we explore a simple role-graph prompting strategy that encourages structured reasoning over participant interactions, leading to improvements in compositional reasoning. Our findings highlight that reliable aggressive-scene understanding remains a major challenge for current MLLMs and motivate future research on role-aware video reasoning beyond coarse violence detection. Code and dataset: https://github.com/austincbaker/A3Bench .

### 1 Introduction

Recent Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have demonstrated strong performance on video understanding tasks, including action recognition Carreira and Zisserman (2017) ; Goyal et al. (2017) ; Soomro et al. (2012) ; Kuehne et al. (2011) ; Heilbron et al. (2015) , temporal event localization, and video question answering Li et al. (2024) ; Liu et al. (2024b) ; Yue et al. (2024) . Existing action datasets have been highly effective in evaluating models across a wide range of everyday activities, sports actions Li et al. (2021) ; Shao et al. (2020) , human-object interactions Sigurdsson et al. (2016) ; Ji et al. (2020) , and complex temporal events Monfort et al. (2020) ; Yeung et al. (2018) . While these tasks are important for general video understanding, reliable real-world systems must also recognize safety-critical behaviors, including physical altercations or threatening interactions. However, understanding aggression requires more than detecting that a violent or harmful action has occurred. In practical settings such as security, public safety monitoring, school safety, sports analysis, and online video moderation, it is often not enough to recognize what is happening; it is also necessary to understand how each person in the scene is involved. Without role-aware reasoning, a system may correctly classify a scene as aggressive while still producing an incorrect or harmful interpretation.

Prior work has studied aggressive actions through crime detection Sultani et al. (2018) ; An et al. (2025) , violence detection Wu et al. (2020) ; Ali et al. (2025c) ; Nievas et al. (2011) ; Hassner et al. (2012) ; Cheng et al. (2021) ; Kollias et al. (2025) ; Mahi et al. (2024) , and incident understanding datasets Ali et al. (2025b) ; Luo et al. (2017) . Some provide multiple aggressive action categories, while others formulate the task as binary classification, such as normal vs. anomalous or violent vs. non-violent. These resources have advanced the study of abnormal and dangerous events, but they typically evaluate only whether aggression is present. They often do not require reasoning about the individuals involved in the scene, including who initiates the action, or who is affected by it. In particular, existing evaluations rarely test whether an aggressive action can be bound to the correct actor, whether aggressors can be distinguished from victims and bystanders, or whether multi-person interactions can be resolved. This limitation can reward models that rely on contextual shortcuts, scene priors, or coarse motion cues, rather than learning the semantic relationships between people, actions, and roles. This necessitates a more fine-grained capability: aggressive action and association , where the goal is not only to recognize an aggressive behavior, but also to associate it with the correct participant roles.

To address this, we introduce A 3 Bench, a benchmark for evaluating aggressive action and association (A 3 ) in MLLMs. A 3 Bench contains 2,683 video clips and 15,004 question-answer pairs that move beyond coarse aggression recognition to evaluate role-aware reasoning in aggressive scenes. Rather than asking only whether aggression occurs, the benchmark probes models on aggressive actions and participant-role associations, including aggressors, victims, and bystanders. These questions require models to distinguish individuals, interpret interactions, and correctly bind actions to participants. By focusing on fine-grained role-action reasoning, A 3 Bench provides a challenging testbed for assessing whether current MLLMs can reliably understand aggressive scenes.

Our experiments show that recent MLLMs struggle on this benchmark, with performance remaining below 50%. Accuracy consistently drops as questions require more compositional reasoning, from identifying a single attribute to jointly reasoning about actions and participant roles (Figure 1 , right). Error analysis further reveals a strong bias toward visually salient individuals, with models frequently misclassifying victims or bystanders as aggressors. These findings suggest that current MLLMs fail to reliably capture the interaction structure underlying aggressive scenes. Finally, we explore a simple role-graph prompting strategy that encourages structured reasoning over participant interactions, leading to improved performance on compositional reasoning tasks.

We make the following contributions: 1) We introduce A 3 Bench, a benchmark for evaluating aggressive action and association in videos designed to assess fine-grained role-aware reasoning beyond coarse violence recognition, 2) We conduct a comprehensive evaluation of recent multimodal large language models in a zero-shot setting, showing that current systems struggle to reliably associate aggressive actions with the correct participants, with performance degrading significantly as reasoning complexity increases, and 3) Through detailed analysis, we identify systematic failures in role-action association, and show that a simple role-graph prompting strategy can improve structured reasoning in aggressive-scene understanding.


### 2 Related Work

Action recognition: The advancement of action recognition can be noted across video Tong et al. (2022) ; Wang et al. (2025a) , skeleton Wu et al. (2025) ; Zhu et al. (2025a) ; Wang et al. (2026) , and multimodal modalities Rasheed et al. (2023) ; Wang et al. (2025b) ; Ni et al. (2022) ; Huang et al. (2024) ; Ahmad et al. (2025) ; Cheng et al. (2024) . Certain methods, such as temporal localization Shi et al. (2023) ; Liu et al. (2024a) ; Liu and Liu (2025) allow more precise event boundary detection. Fine-grained recognition has advanced through unified keypoint-based frameworks Hachiuma et al. (2023) and atomic action decomposition Peng et al. (2024) . Action detection methods further localize and identify what each person is doing in space and time, but they typically focus on individual actions rather than the relationships between interacting participants. These methods still treat actions as a classification problem over participant-agnostic categories, without modeling the relationships between those involved.

Crime and anomaly detection: Anomaly detection and behavior analysis in security videos have been widely studied for identifying abnormal, unsafe, or criminal activities Zhao et al. (2023a) ; Liu et al. (2025) . In parallel, dedicated violence and aggression datasets have advanced the evaluation of models across diverse harmful scenarios, including fight detection, crowd violence, real-world security violence, intruder activity, child aggression, and crime/anomaly recognition Nievas et al. (2011) ; Kollias et al. (2025) ; Soliman et al. (2019) ; Hassner et al. (2012) ; Cheng et al. (2021) ; Mahi et al. (2024) ; Ali et al. (2025b) ; Wu et al. (2020) ; Ali et al. (2025c) ; An et al. (2025) ; Sultani et al. (2018) . Beyond visual understanding, text-based aggression detection has also examined aggressive behavior in language, such as harmful or hostile social-media comments Akter et al. (2022) , showing that aggression analysis spans multiple modalities. Most existing video benchmarks focus on violence, aggression, or anomaly detection with coarse event-level labels. In contrast, A 3 Bench evaluates fine-grained aggressive action association by requiring models to identify the actor, target, and role of each visible participant, exposing failures that prior benchmarks do not measure.

Compositional video reasoning: Existing benchmarks have explored compositional video understanding through temporal and causal reasoning Yi* et al. (2020) , spatio-temporal question answering Grunde-McLaughlin et al. (2021) , and situated reasoning over human interactions Wu et al. (2021) . More recent work evaluates fine-grained associations between agents, actions, and events using video-language entailment and challenging negative captions Saravanan et al. (2025) . While these assess general compositional reasoning, A 3 Bench focuses on aggressive interactions, requiring models to distinguish aggressors, victims, and bystanders and correctly associate actions with participant roles and interaction direction.

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs): Recent multimodal models have significantly expanded the scope of visual understanding by linking visual evidence with language-based semantic reasoning Radford et al. (2021) . Building on large-scale cross-modal pretraining, such models have enabled zero-shot and open-vocabulary recognition of human actions and activities Ali et al. (2025a) ; Wang et al. (2025b) ; Ni et al. (2022) ; Rasheed et al. (2023) ; Huang et al. (2024) ; Ahmad et al. (2025) ; Wang et al. (2024) . Recent video-centric MLLMs Bai et al. (2025a) ; Zhu et al. (2025b) ; Lu et al. (2025) ; Google DeepMind (2026) , further extend these capabilities to temporal and multi-frame reasoning over complex events Maaz et al. (2024) ; Yu et al. (2023) ; Zhao et al. (2023b) . These advances motivate the use of MLLMs for aggressive action and association, where models must not only identify the observed action but also reason about the relationships between people and their roles.


### 3 Aggressive Action and Association Benchmark (A 3 Bench)

A 3 Bench is designed to evaluate fine-grained aggressive action and association in real-world videos. The benchmark construction begins with video curation and structured annotation of aggressive interactions, including actions, participants, roles, and scene context. These annotations are then converted into three levels of diagnostic VQA questions, ranging from basic attribute recognition to compound association and detailed aggressor-action-victim reasoning. The resulting benchmark enables controlled evaluation of MLLMs under a unified multiple-choice protocol.

#### 3.1 Construction

Video curation and annotation: We curate a benchmark of 2,683 video clips collected from diverse sources, including publicly available social-media videos, UCF-Crime Sultani et al. (2018) , and RWF-2000 Cheng et al. (2021) . Source videos are trimmed into shorter clips centered on individual interactions, resulting in an average clip duration of 5.75 seconds (standard deviation of 1.69 seconds), with most clips approximately 4 to 8 seconds long. The videos span a wide range of recording conditions, camera viewpoints, resolutions, scene layouts, and levels of visual clutter, reflecting the heterogeneity of real-world scenarios. The final corpus contains 2,234 clips with aggressive behavior and 449 non-aggressive clips.

A group of annotators labeled each video with structured scene information. The annotations include: (1) the aggressive action being performed, selected from 17 aggressive action categories and a control “none” category for non-aggressive clips; a short appearance description of the (2) aggressor; (3) victim; (4) bystanders and (5) the environment or setting. As seen in Figure 2 , person descriptions are primarily based on clothing and coarse visual appearance, such as “person in a dark jacket,” while avoiding names, faces, demographic attributes, or other identifying information. This design reduces the risk of relying on sensitive attributes and focuses the benchmark on visual role and action understanding. If a certain role does not exist in the video clip, or the appearance is too ambiguous, that is left out in the annotations.

Dataset distribution. A 3 Bench intentionally contains more aggressive than non-aggressive clips, as its primary objective is to evaluate fine-grained action and participant-role reasoning within aggressive interactions. Non-aggressive clips are included to evaluate whether models can recognize the absence of aggression. As shown in Figure 3 , A 3 Bench also exhibits a male-skewed participant distribution, reflecting the composition of the collected real-world source videos. This observation is consistent with broader crime statistics reporting higher male representation in several offense categories. According to the FBI’s 2019 arrest statistics, males accounted for 72.5% of all arrests, including 84.2% of robbery arrests, and 76.5% of aggravated-assault arrests Federal Bureau of Investigation (2020) . UNODC Global Study on Homicide 2023 also reports that approximately 91% of suspects brought to police for intentional homicide (across 50 countries) between 2018 and 2021 were male United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (2023) .

Annotation quality assurance: We employ a structured, consensus-driven annotation protocol involving four annotators to ensure visually grounded and reliable annotations of aggressive interactions. Each annotator independently reviews assigned videos and labels the aggressive-action category, appearance descriptions of aggressors, victims, and bystanders, and scene environment. Participants are identified using clothing and other coarse, non-identifying visual cues (e.g., “person in a red shirt”), while avoiding race, gender, age, and other sensitive attributes. Demographic statistics in Figure 3 are reported only at the aggregate corpus level and are not used in questions, answer options, or role labels.

Annotators flag videos with ambiguous actions, interaction direction, participant visibility, or appearance descriptions, particularly in crowded, occluded, blurry, black-and-white, or low-resolution scenes. Flagged annotations are withheld from finalization pending group review. After each annotation batch, another team member independently re-reviews a randomly selected subset. Aggressor-victim direction conflicts occur in fewer than 5% of cross-checked cases, while appearance-description disagreements occur in approximately 20%, primarily because annotators select different clothing or coarse visual cues to describe the same participant rather than identifying different participants. All flagged cases and disagreements identified during cross-checking are collectively reviewed. Role-direction disagreements are resolved by determining who initiated and who received the aggressive action, while appearance-description disagreements are resolved by selecting the clearest non-identifying description. When reliable consensus cannot be reached, the corresponding attribute is omitted rather than guessed. Periodic spot checks and targeted re-review of difficult cases continue throughout benchmark development, refining participant descriptions and removing ambiguous attributes while retaining only visually supported annotations.

Question generation and distractor design: From the annotation corpus, we generate multiple-choice video question-answer pairs that function as controlled diagnostic probes for aggressive interaction understanding. Rather than only evaluating whether a model can recognize an action label, A 3 Bench is designed to expose distinct failure modes in video-language models, including weak role grounding, poor action-participant binding, confusion between visible and relevant people, failure to model interaction directionality, and reliance on answer-choice priors. The questions are organized into three tiers of increasing complexity: basic questions , compound questions , and detailed questions . Figure 1 illustrates representative examples from these three categories, and Figure 5 reports their subcategories and overall distribution across the benchmark.

Basic questions test whether a model can recover a single atomic attribute of the scene. These include primary action identification, where the model must recognize the aggressive action taking place; aggressor or victim identification, where the model must identify who performs the aggression or who is targeted; and role identification, where the model is given a person description and must assign the correct role, such as aggressor, victim, or bystander. These questions diagnose whether the model can localize individual actions and participant roles before requiring relational reasoning.

Compound questions test whether a model can bind two scene attributes correctly. These include aggressor-victim, action-aggressor, and action-victim associations. Since each answer choice is only correct when all components are jointly correct, these questions diagnose whether models merely recognize visible actions or participants independently, or whether they can associate the correct action with the correct person and role.

Detailed questions test whether a model can recover the full event structure of an aggressive interaction. These include aggressor-action-victim questions, which ask who did what to whom, and sequence verification questions, which require selecting the correct structured description of the interaction. These questions specifically diagnose failures in directionality, role distinction, and holistic event understanding, since a model must distinguish the aggressor from the victim and construct a coherent representation of the interaction rather than predict a participant-agnostic action category.

Importantly, Basic, Compound, and Detailed questions are generated from the same underlying video pool, with each video contributing approximately seven questions across multiple complexity levels.

> Figure 5 : Distribution of question types in A 3 Bench. The benchmark contains three levels of questions with increasing association complexity: Basic questions focus on single attributes; Compound questions require associating two scene components; and Detailed questions require full interaction understanding.

We design distractor choices as targeted diagnostic stress tests: they are visually plausible, semantically close to the correct answer, and tied to specific failure modes. Cross-video distractors are sampled from real annotations of other clips, so incorrect options still contain natural actions, roles, and participant descriptions rather than synthetic or obviously invalid answers. Same-video distractors use visible non-target participants from the queried clip, such as the victim or a bystander when asking for the aggressor, preventing models from relying only on visibility, saliency, or person presence. Role reversals and bystander substitutions preserve much of the event content while swapping aggressor and victim roles or assigning bystanders to active roles, directly testing interaction directionality and the distinction between active participation and mere presence. Negation questions include answers such as “no aggressive action is taking place” to test whether models verify visual evidence rather than assume aggression is present. Frequency-inverted distractors are constructed so that each key distractor property, such as an action, role, or participant description, appears across multiple answer options, making marginal answer frequency uninformative. This forces models to reason over the complete aggressor-action-victim structure rather than selecting the option with the most common or familiar textual components.


#### 3.2 Benchmarked Models

We evaluate 17 representative model configurations on A 3 Bench to assess their ability to perform aggressive action and association. All of our chosen models are trained on very large datasets with high-level video understanding.

General-Purpose Models. The evaluated models include two CLIP-based video-language models, ViCLIP Wang et al. (2024) and X-CLIP Ni et al. (2022) , alongside several recent open-weight MLLM families, including Gemma Google DeepMind (2026) , InternVideo Wang et al. (2025d) , InternVL Chen et al. (2024) ; Zhu et al. (2025b) ; Wang et al. (2025c) , LLaVA-Video Zhang et al. (2025b) , Ovis Lu et al. (2025) , Qwen-VL Bai et al. (2025b) ; Bai et al. (2025a) , and VideoLLaMA Zhang et al. (2025a) . Among closed-source, we include GPT 5.1 OpenAI (2025) . These families differ in visual encoder design, video modeling strategy, instruction tuning, model scale, and reasoning behavior.

Reasoning and Prompted Variants. To investigate whether explicit reasoning improves aggressive action and association, we additionally evaluate reasoning-oriented variant of Qwen3-VL-8B Bai et al. (2025a) . We also evaluate InternVL3.5-8B Wang et al. (2025c) under two reasoning-style prompting settings: a detailed chain-of-thought (CoT) prompt and a Dream-of-Thoughts (inspired from Grover et al. (2024) ) prompt. These variants encourage intermediate reasoning before producing a final answer, allowing us to test whether step-by-step inference helps models distinguish aggressors, victims, bystanders, actions, and scene context.


#### 3.3 Implementation Details, Evaluation Protocol, and Metrics

All experiments are zero-shot. Open-source models are evaluated using their official implementations, hyperparameters, and released weights on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 (48 GB) or A100 (80 GB) GPU. Qwen2.5-VL-72B-Instruct Bai et al. (2025b) was run on the quantized version to fit in memory. We use a multiple-choice video question answering (VQA) protocol across all models: for each video-question pair, the model is prompted to select the most appropriate answer from the provided options. Depending on the question type and the size of the valid answer space, each question contains four to eight answer choices, including one correct answer and a set of diagnostic distractors. The position of the correct answer is uniformly shuffled across all questions to mitigate positional bias. We report accuracy as the primary evaluation metric.

#### 4.1 Analysis

Performance degrades as aggression understanding becomes more compositional. Models achieve an average accuracy of 44.11% on Basic questions, but performance drops to 35.51% on Compound questions and further to 27.29% on Detailed questions. This monotonic decline shows that current MLLMs do not simply fail at rare aggression labels; they fail progressively as the task requires role-action binding. Models can sometimes identify isolated visual cues, such as an action category, a salient participant, or a single role, but struggle once these cues must be composed into a structured interaction. Compound questions expose failures in binding two attributes, such as linking the correct action to the correct aggressor or victim. Detailed questions further require the full aggressor-action-victim structure, or who did what to whom. The sharp drop on these questions indicates failures in directionality, role assignment, and person-action binding.

Model-level trends show that the compositional drop is not uniform across systems. VideoLLaMA3-7B exhibits the steepest collapse, suggesting strong reliance on single-attribute cues. Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct shows a small Basic-to-Compound drop but a much larger Compound-to-Detailed drop, indicating that pairwise association does not transfer to full aggressor-action-victim reasoning. Ovis2.5-9B shows the reverse pattern, with larger early drops and smaller later drops, suggesting that role-action binding is their main bottleneck. Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct achieves the strongest Compound performance but still degrades on Detailed questions, while ViCLIP and LLaVA-Video-7B-Qwen2 remain weak across all tiers. The InternVL3.5-8B DoT Wang et al. (2025c) ; Grover et al. (2024) variant and X-CLIP are notable exceptions, with Detailed accuracy slightly exceeding Compound accuracy.

Reasoning helps, but not uniformly. Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking underperforms its instruction-tuned counterpart across all categories, dropping from 46.96% to 39.37% overall, suggesting that generic thinking mode does not necessarily improve role-action association. In contrast, reasoning prompts improve InternVL3.5-8B: CoT and DoT increases overall accuracy. The gains are most pronounced on Detailed questions, where InternVL3.5-8B rises from 28.75% to 33.13% with CoT and 40.90% with DoT. This suggests that structured reasoning is more useful when the task requires full aggressor-action-victim binding.

MLLMs struggle not only to recognize aggressive actions, but also to assign the correct roles to the persons involved.

Models often identify participants without recognizing the correct aggressive action. To understand whether errors arise from recognizing the aggressive behavior or identifying the relevant participants, we analyze videos that include both a Basic primary action question and at least one aggressor or victim identification question. Figure 6 (left) reports results averaged across models. Models identify the correct aggressor or victim while failing to recognize the correct action in 26% of cases, whereas they recognize the correct action while failing to identify the relevant role in only 17% of cases. Another 16% of cases are correct on both, while 41% are incorrect on both. This pattern indicates that models are more likely to localize socially salient participants than to distinguish the fine-grained aggressive action being performed. Thus, even when participant grounding partially succeeds, current MLLMs often still lack reliable aggression understanding.

Models are biased toward assigning the aggressor role. Figures 6 (center, right) show a consistent role-grounding failure: models tend to over-assign the aggressor role when identifying participants in aggressive interactions. In Figure 6 (center), for videos containing both Basic victim and aggressor identification questions, only 22% of cases correctly identify the aggressor and the victim simultaneously, while 31% fail on both roles. Among partial successes, models more often identify the aggressor while missing the victim (28%) than the other way (19%), suggesting that victim grounding is unreliable. Figure 6 (right) shows the same bias in role classification: many ground-truth victims (37%) and bystanders (32%) are predicted as aggressors. These errors suggest that models often treat visual involvement, proximity, or saliency as evidence of aggression, rather than reasoning about who initiates the action and who is targeted.

Distractor analysis reveals different failure modes across question complexity. Distractor analysis shows that models are especially vulnerable when incorrect options preserve visually plausible participants or partially match the interaction structure. For Basic questions, role-reversal distractors are consistently more challenging than cross-video distractors for most models, indicating that models often recognize relevant people but confuse their roles. For Compound questions, role-reversal and wrong-action distractors dominate, showing that models struggle to jointly bind the correct action with the correct aggressor or victim. In Detailed questions, frequency-inverted distractors dominate showing that models may rely on answer-choice priors rather than visual grounding, selecting options with familiar or frequently co-occurring action-role-person components instead of recovering the full aggressor-action-victim structure.

Model-level patterns reveal distinct vulnerabilities across question tiers. In Basic questions, Ovis2.5-9B is vulnerable to role-reversal distractors, while LLaVA-Video-7B spreads errors across cross-video, wrong-role, and bystander-substitution options, suggesting weaker visual grounding. InternVL3.5-8B-DoT, ViCLIP, and X-CLIP also show high bystander-substitution errors, suggesting over-attention to visible non-participants. In Compound questions, ViCLIP, X-CLIP, and LLaVA-Video-7B show more wrong-aggressor and wrong-victim errors than most models, indicating weaker participant grounding. In Detailed questions, frequency-inverted distractors dominate for most models, while GPT-5.1 shows more errors involving role reversals.

Models over-predict aggression even in non-aggressive clips. We conduct a free-form social appropriateness evaluation on four relatively strong models Lu et al. (2025) ; Chen et al. (2024) ; Zhu et al. (2025b) ; Bai et al. (2025a) . Each model is asked to characterize the social appropriateness of the action shown in a video. Models are generally able to identify aggressive or socially inappropriate behavior in aggressive clips, with most models reaching 78.2%-89.8% accuracy. However, on non-aggressive clips, they frequently still attribute inappropriate or aggressive behavior to the videos, with most models near or below 60% accuracy. This indicates that current models are sensitive to aggressive cues but also exhibit a strong aggression prior, over-predicting aggression even when no aggressive behavior is present. InternVL2.5-8B is more balanced across aggressive and non-aggressive clips, but both scores are close to 60%; given the 50% binary chance level, this suggests that it does not reliably solve either side of the task.

Scene context adds another layer of grounding difficulty. We further evaluate whether models can associate aggressive actions with their surrounding scene context using a subset of videos with clearly identifiable environments. Models are asked to identify both the action and the environment in which it occurs. Adding this scene-grounding requirement makes the task harder for most models: 12 evaluated models show lower accuracy on action-environment association than on primary action recognition, with their average accuracy dropping from 38.56% to 32.34%. This indicates that models struggle not only to recognize aggressive actions, but also to ground them in the correct environmental context. Interestingly, InternVL3.5-8B and its reasoning variants are relatively robust, showing little degradation. Overall, however, the drop for most models suggests that reliable action-environment association requires joint reasoning over local behavior and broader scene context.

#### 4.2 Role-graph prompting

We observe that models often over-assign the aggressor role, confusing victims or bystanders with active participants. To encourage more explicit role-action reasoning, we explore a role-graph prompting strategy as a diagnostic structured-reasoning intervention. As shown in Figure 7 , the graph represents aggressive-scene understanding through interaction direction, participant roles, and aggressive actions. We prepend this graph-based instruction template to each multiple-choice VQA query. The linguistic prompt serves as an interface that directs the model’s attention toward interaction direction and participant roles, without altering the underlying visual evidence or evaluation protocol.

Table 3 compares baseline accuracy with role-graph prompting (RG) across six models. Role-graph prompting improves performance on five of the six models, with an average accuracy increase from 42.50% to 45.26%. Ovis2.5-9B shows the largest improvement, increasing from 43.16% to 50.28%. Smaller improvements are observed for InternVL2.5-8B and both Qwen2.5-VL models, while GPT-5.1 shows a slight decrease. These results suggest that explicitly encouraging models to consider participant roles and interaction direction can benefit aggressive-scene understanding, although the effectiveness varies across models. We view this as an exploratory approach to structured role-aware prompting rather than a comprehensive solution to compositional role-action reasoning. ### 5 Conclusion

We propose A 3 Bench, a benchmark for evaluating whether MLLMs can reason about aggressive actions and participant associations in videos. Unlike prior evaluations focused on aggression detection, A 3 Bench measures whether models can identify who initiates an action, who is affected, and how participants interact. Our evaluation across 17 models shows that current systems struggle with this task, with performance declining as questions require more compositional reasoning over actions and roles. We further observe an aggressor bias, where victims and bystanders are frequently misclassified as aggressors, suggesting that models fail to reliably capture interaction structure. Finally, we explore a role-graph prompting strategy that encourages structured reasoning over participant interactions and improves performance on most evaluated models. We hope A 3 Bench serves as a diagnostic benchmark for studying fine-grained role-aware video reasoning.

#### A.1 Limitations and Broader Impact

A 3 Bench focuses specifically on visible physical aggression in short video clips and does not cover broader forms of harmful behavior such as verbal abuse, long-term conflict, or subtle social interactions. Furthermore, A 3 Bench is designed to evaluate compositional reasoning over aggressive interactions rather than spatial or temporal action detection, and therefore does not provide participant bounding boxes or temporal tracks. Extending the benchmark with such annotations represents an important direction for future work. While A 3 Bench includes diverse videos, question types, and model families under a controlled evaluation protocol, cross-dataset generalization remains unexplored. Evaluating role-action reasoning across additional datasets and domains is an important direction for future research. This work aims to advance safer and more reliable video understanding systems for applications such as public safety, content moderation, and assistive monitoring. At the same time, aggressive-scene understanding systems may inherit societal biases or produce harmful misclassifications, particularly in high-stakes settings involving surveillance or security. We therefore position A 3 Bench as a diagnostic evaluation framework rather than a deployment-ready solution.

#### A.5 Role-Graph Prompting

We translate the proposed role graph into a structured natural-language prompt for aggressive-scene understanding. The prompt defines each graph component, specifies the directed dependencies between components, and provides illustrative examples for aggressive scenes, bystander cases, and non-aggressive scenes. During evaluation, this role-graph prompt is prepended to each multiple-choice VQA query, and the model is instructed to output only the selected option number. The exact prompt used in our experiments is provided below.

Your primary task is to act as a causal reasoning engine for understanding aggressive scenes in videos. You will observe the persons present and use a causal graph to identify each person’s role and the nature of the aggressive interaction.

### Component Definitions

First, understand the meaning of each component (node) in our causal graph. These are the building blocks we use to analyze any aggressive scene.

- • P (Persons): All individuals visible in the video. For example, ‘a man in a black coat’, ‘a woman in a red shirt’, ‘three people in the background’. Each person must be assigned exactly one role.

- • I (Interaction): Whether a given person is engaged in a direct physical interaction with another person. This is a binary branch point: – YES — the person is physically engaged with at least one other person, e.g., striking, grabbing, restraining, being struck. – NO — the person has no physical contact with any other person in the scene.

- • D (Direction): For persons where I = YES, the direction of the aggressive interaction: – INITIATING — the person is the one delivering the aggressive act, e.g., throwing a punch, grabbing, pushing. – RECEIVING — the person is the one on the receiving end of the aggressive act, e.g., being punched, being grabbed, being pushed.

- • R (Role): The role assigned to each person, inferred from I and D: – Aggressor — a person where I = YES and D = INITIATING. – Victim — a person where I = YES and D = RECEIVING. – Bystander — a person where I = NO, not physically involved.

- • A (Aggressive Action): The specific type of aggressive behavior being performed, inferred from the Aggressor’s motions and the Victim’s reactions. For example, ‘punch’, ‘shove’, ‘kick’, ‘choke’, ‘hair grab’. This is determined jointly by the Aggressor and Victim roles.

### The Causal Graph: How Components are Related

The components are connected in a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG). Each arrow ( $\rightarrow$ ) represents a direction of inference — follow the graph in order to assign roles correctly.

The directed edges of the graph are:

$P\rightarrow I$

$I(\text{YES})\rightarrow D$

$I(\text{NO})\rightarrow R(\text{Bystander})$

$D(\text{INITIATING})\rightarrow R(\text{Aggressor})$

$D(\text{RECEIVING})\rightarrow R(\text{Victim})$

$R(\text{Aggressor})+R(\text{Victim})\rightarrow A$

Here are the causal relationships explained in detail:

- • $P\rightarrow I$ (Does this person interact?): For every person identified in P, ask whether they are physically interacting with another person. This is the first branch point. A person standing and watching is I = NO. A person throwing a punch is I = YES.

- • $I(\text{YES})\rightarrow D$ (What is the direction of their interaction?): For persons with I = YES, determine whether they are initiating or receiving the physical contact. Watch carefully — the aggressor moves toward the victim with force; the victim reacts to the aggressor’s action.

- • $I(\text{NO})\rightarrow R(\text{Bystander})$ : Any person with no physical involvement is immediately classified as a Bystander. No further reasoning is needed for them.

- • $D(\text{INITIATING})\rightarrow R(\text{Aggressor})$ : A person who is initiating the aggressive physical contact is the Aggressor.

- • $D(\text{RECEIVING})\rightarrow R(\text{Victim})$ : A person who is on the receiving end of the aggressive physical contact is the Victim.

- • $R(\text{Aggressor})+R(\text{Victim})\rightarrow A$ (What is the Aggressive Action?): Only after both the Aggressor and Victim are identified can the Aggressive Action be determined. Observe the Aggressor’s body motion, e.g., arm swinging forward, and the Victim’s reaction, e.g., head snapping back, together to name the action precisely.

### Illustrative Examples

Example 1: A clear two-person aggressive scene

A video shows two people. One person in a black jacket swings their fist at a person in a white shirt, who stumbles backward.

- • Causal Analysis (internal, do not output): 1. P: Two persons — ‘person in a black jacket’, ‘person in a white shirt’. 2. I for ‘person in a black jacket’: YES (physical contact with fist). I for ‘person in a white shirt’: YES (receiving the fist). 3. D for ‘person in a black jacket’: INITIATING (swinging the fist). D for ‘person in a white shirt’: RECEIVING (stumbling from the impact). 4. R: ‘person in a black jacket’ $\rightarrow$ Aggressor. ‘person in a white shirt’ $\rightarrow$ Victim. 5. A: The Aggressor’s motion (arm swinging forward, fist connecting) and Victim’s reaction (head snapping, stumbling) $\rightarrow$ Aggressive Action = ‘punch’.

Example 2: A scene with a bystander

A video shows three people. One person in a red hoodie grabs another person in a grey jacket by the collar. A third person in a blue shirt stands nearby watching.

- • Causal Analysis (internal, do not output): 1. P: Three persons — ‘person in a red hoodie’, ‘person in a grey jacket’, ‘person in a blue shirt’. 2. I for ‘person in a red hoodie’: YES. I for ‘person in a grey jacket’: YES. I for ‘person in a blue shirt’: NO. 3. D for ‘person in a red hoodie’: INITIATING (grabbing). D for ‘person in a grey jacket’: RECEIVING (being grabbed). 4. R: ‘person in a red hoodie’ $\rightarrow$ Aggressor. ‘person in a grey jacket’ $\rightarrow$ Victim. ‘person in a blue shirt’ $\rightarrow$ Bystander (I = NO, skip D). 5. A: The Aggressor grabs the Victim’s collar and pulls — Aggressive Action = ‘clothing grab’.

Example 3: No aggressive action

A video shows two people standing and talking to each other without any physical contact.

- • Causal Analysis (internal, do not output): 1. P: Two persons. 2. I for both persons: NO (no physical contact with any other person). 3. Since I = NO for all persons, no one reaches the D step. 4. R: Both persons $\rightarrow$ Bystander (no aggressor, no victim). 5. A: No Aggressor and no Victim exist $\rightarrow$ No aggressive action is taking place.

### Your Task

You will now be given a new video. Apply the causal graph above to reason about every person you see.

Reason through these steps internally. Do not output your reasoning.

- • P (Persons): Identify all individuals in the video. For each person, carefully describe their appearance — focus first on clothing (color and type, e.g., ‘black coat’, ‘white t-shirt’, ‘blue jeans’), then hair color/style, then any other distinguishing feature. These descriptions will serve as each person’s identifier throughout the rest of your reasoning, and must match the appearance-based labels used in the answer options.

- • I (Interaction): For each person, determine YES or NO — are they physically interacting with another person?

- • D (Direction): For each person with I = YES, determine INITIATING or RECEIVING.

- • R (Role): Assign each person a role — Aggressor, Victim, or Bystander — following the graph edges.

- • A (Aggressive Action): Using the identified Aggressor and Victim, determine the specific aggressive action. If no Aggressor and Victim exist, the answer is that no aggressive action is taking place.

From the list of options below, select the answer that most accurately reflects the question asked about the video. There might also a choice if none of the other options apply. Review the video and options carefully. Only one choice is correct.

Output format: reply with the choice number only.
