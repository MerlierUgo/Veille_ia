## Sage-Med: Scaling Medical Multimodal Intelligence

Establishing effective scaling laws for medical multimodal large language models (MLLMs) remains a persistent and fundamental challenge. Our systematic evaluation shows that existing medical MLLMs tend to underperform frontier general-purpose MLLMs across a range of medical tasks, while scaling model size primarily improves text capabilities rather than multimodal perception, and enabling reasoning modes fails to close this visual gap. To investigate how medical multimodal intelligence scales in practice, we introduce Sage-Med-Preview, a 35B-parameter medical multimodal model with 3B activated parameters, built by combining stage-specific curation over 322.7 million candidate records with a progressive training curriculum spanning medical pretraining, staged supervised fine-tuning, and adaptive reinforcement learning. Across this curriculum, progressive supervised refinement and adaptive reinforcement learning yield consistent stage-wise gains, substantially advancing both specialized visual understanding and medical language capabilities. Evaluated against 77 competing models across 238 clinical tasks spanning 61 public and 13 private multi-center benchmarks in more than 40 modalities, Sage-Med-Preview achieves an average score of 58.26% on the 31 public multimodal benchmarks. It outperforms the state-of-the-art open-source medical model Hulu-Med-235B-A22B at 50.99% and the general scientific model Intern-S2-Preview at 52.24%, and approaches the advanced proprietary model GPT-6.2-Sol across multiple tasks such as comprehensive multimodal understanding, report generation, and visual grounding. Although substantial challenges remain in complex medical reasoning and multi-step clinical workflows, this report provides empirical evidence, actionable data and training recipes, and a rigorous benchmark toward scalable clinical multimodal intelligence.

### 1 Introduction

Medical image interpretation is a rate-limiting step in diagnostic care. Diagnostic capacity is distributed far less evenly than disease burden: shortages of radiologists, pathologists and other diagnostic specialists are projected to widen through 2050, and are most acute in the low- and middle-income regions that already carry the heaviest burden [ 1 ] , so that the standard of interpretation a patient receives varies with where, and by whom, they are seen. Closing that gap requires more than assigning a label to an image: a clinically useful system must reach a judgement and express it in terms a colleague can act upon, placing perception and language within one model [ 2 , 3 ] . Where the input is text, this has largely been achieved: large language models encode clinical knowledge at expert level [ 4 , 5 ] and, once optimized for diagnostic dialogue, produce differential diagnoses more accurate than those of unassisted clinicians [ 6 , 7 ] . On images, capability has instead accumulated one specialty at a time, in retinal imaging [ 8 ] , dermatology [ 9 ] and computational pathology [ 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 ] , each model trained for a single specialty and adapted task by task rather than instructed in language across them. Medical multimodal large language models are intended to unite the two, and report broad gains across image and video tasks [ 15 , 16 , 17 ] . Efforts to organize their assessment around clinician-validated task taxonomies, however, have so far addressed text alone [ 18 , 19 ] .

Progress toward this unification has been rapid. Instruction tuning on image-caption pairs from the biomedical literature first allowed models to answer questions about medical images in natural language [ 20 , 21 ] . A single generalist model then reached competitive performance across radiography, pathology and dermatology [ 22 ] , and openly released medical model families showed this breadth to rest at least as much on the composition and curation of training data as on architecture [ 23 , 24 ] . Inputs have since widened from single two-dimensional images to volumetric scans, whole-slide images, procedural videos and serial examinations [ 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 ] ; outputs, from answers to localized findings and diagnostic reports, are judged by blinded radiologists rather than by benchmark score alone [ 29 , 28 ] . A smaller body of work places interpretation and synthesis in one model, over a narrow range of synthesis tasks, and finds the two mutually reinforcing [ 30 , 31 ] . These gains are uneven across modalities, tasks and settings, and rest on curated datasets under controlled conditions; where image and text are presented together, accuracy tracks the informativeness of the text rather than of the image [ 32 ] , so how much of a reported capability is read from the image at all is seldom established. No such system is yet in routine diagnostic use, and what these models can be relied upon to do across a diagnostic pathway, and where that reliability ends, remains undrawn.

Establishing effective scaling principles for medical MLLMs presents three challenges: (1) limited data (high-quality medical image-text supervision remains scarce and unevenly distributed across specialties, modalities and conditions, while expert curation is costly; expanding training corpora therefore requires maintaining clinical accuracy and diversity as data volume increases [ 23 ] ); (2) effective adaptation (general-purpose vision-language models provide a strong foundation, but translating heterogeneous medical data into specialist capabilities requires careful coordination of visual-language alignment, clinical knowledge acquisition and instruction learning; how to compose and sequence these training signals while preserving general capabilities remains an open design problem [ 23 , 33 ] ); and (3) rigorous evaluation (widely used benchmarks often assess isolated image-question pairs or examination-style cases, incompletely representing clinical workflows that require gathering information, integrating imaging with patient context and making sequential decisions [ 34 , 35 , 19 ] ; documented quality defects include erroneous reference answers and questions answerable without inspecting the image [ 36 ] , while overlap between training corpora and publicly available test data introduces a risk of contamination [ 36 , 33 ] ). Addressing these challenges requires scalable data curation, training strategies that integrate complementary sources of medical supervision, and rigorous evaluation grounded in clinical practice.

Here we investigate how medical multimodal large language models scale in practice and how well the resulting capabilities generalize across specialties and clinical settings. We introduce Sage-Med-Preview, a 35B-parameter medical multimodal model with 3B activated parameters initialized from Intern-S2-Preview, built by combining stage-specific data curation with a progressive training curriculum spanning medical pretraining, staged supervised fine-tuning, and adaptive reinforcement learning. We evaluate Sage-Med-Preview systematically across public benchmarks and multi-center clinical datasets. Our main contributions are as follows:

- • Scalable medical data curation. We assemble 322.7 million stage-indexed candidate records from over 200 public datasets, over 120 million biomedical literature records derived from 25 million source documents (including PDF documents, books and web pages), and more than 100 private resources. The resulting pool comprises 233.8 million multimodal and 88.9 million text-only records. Through source-aware evidence organization and three stage-specific six-step curation pathways, we construct filtered pools of approximately 93.8 million records for pretraining, 69.6 million for supervised fine-tuning, and 0.3 million for reinforcement learning, spanning 51 fine-grained modality and 31 anatomical categories. We document the composition of each candidate pool, stage-wise retention after filtering, and the data mixtures used for training.

- • Progressive medical multimodal alignment. We develop a curriculum combining continual pretraining on 83.8 million records and task-oriented mid-training on 28.1 million records, three-stage progressive supervised fine-tuning, and adaptive reinforcement learning with policy-conditioned difficulty sampling and independent reference replay, while retaining general-purpose supervision throughout specialization. Medical pretraining builds medical knowledge and visual–semantic alignment. The three fine-tuning stages then establish instruction alignment, broaden task coverage and refine reasoning-intensive tasks. Adaptive reinforcement learning finally focuses on tasks of informative difficulty and anchors updates to reference answers.

- • Comprehensive evaluation. We evaluate Sage-Med-Preview alongside 77 general-purpose and medical models, comprising 68 open-source and nine proprietary models, on 238 clinical tasks from 61 public benchmarks and 13 private multi-center benchmarks contributed by 14 hospitals and institutions, covering more than 40 modalities under unified test splits and standardized scoring protocols. On the 31 public multimodal benchmarks, this evaluation shows that existing medical MLLMs still trail general-purpose MLLMs on 29 of them, which indicates that domain-specific training alone has not yet closed the gap to general foundation models. Sage-Med-Preview narrows this gap: it leads all open-source models on the same 31 benchmarks with an average of 58.26%, and it outperforms the proprietary GPT-6.2-Sol in comprehensive multimodal understanding and report generation while performing comparably in visual grounding. This advantage also holds beyond public data. On the private multi-center benchmarks, Sage-Med-Preview is the strongest of all evaluated models, averaging 57.72% against 51.24% for GPT-6.2-Sol.


### 2 Sage-Med-Preview


#### 2.1 Scaling Medical Training Data to over 300 Million Candidate Records

Recent medical vision–language foundation models, including MedGemma, Lingshu, Hulu-Med and ClinFusion, have demonstrated the value of combining medical images, biomedical knowledge and task-oriented supervision [ 24 , 23 , 27 , 28 ] . Scaling these resources requires preserving evidence across heterogeneous source formats and matching supervision to distinct training objectives, as data composition and quality shape the utility of additional training data [ 37 , 38 ] . We therefore combined source-aware evidence organization with stage-specific curation to assemble approximately 322.7 million stage-indexed candidate records: 232.8 million for the pretraining (PT) family, 87.4 million for supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and 2.5 million for reinforcement learning (RL) (Fig. 3 b). The inventory contains approximately 233.8 million multimodal and 88.9 million text-only records. These counts describe candidates before stage-specific filtering, rather than the records used in individual training stages. Record definitions and inventory accounting are detailed in Supplementary Section S1.1 .


##### 2.1.1 Data Collection and Source Organization

We integrated more than 200 public medical datasets, over 120 million biomedical literature records derived from 25 million source documents (including PDF documents, books and web pages), and more than 100 private data resources, spanning image–text pairs, task collections and specialty imaging resources. As summarized in Fig. 3 a, public resources and private corpora were processed through document extraction or dataset integration according to their source structure. For PDF documents and web page files, MinerU [ 39 ] extracted text and figures. Existing multimodal collections were standardized by checking media references and image–text pairing structure, normalizing metadata, and preserving available annotations and source context. Text-only and task-oriented resources were organized while retaining available references, source links and supervision targets. Modality, anatomical and medical-concept metadata were retained where available, supporting subsequent record selection and coverage analysis.

The two processing routes converged on source-linked evidence: extracted figures were associated with their original legends, in-text references and relevant surrounding passages, whereas standardized dataset records retained their accompanying text, annotations and available source context (Fig. 3 a). This evidence supported three curation operations: retaining usable supervision, refining deficient descriptions or responses, and synthesizing additional tasks supported by the source material. The resulting supervision was organized into four groups: image descriptions, question answering and reasoning, report generation, and visual grounding. Report supervision preserved available section structure, such as clinical history, findings and impression, while grounding required appropriate localization references or annotations. Source links were preserved throughout processing, allowing revised and synthesized targets to be checked against the available evidence during stage-specific filtering. Further details on evidence organization and supervision construction are provided in Supplementary Section S1.2 .


##### 2.1.2 Stage-specific Data Filtering

The organized records and their source evidence supported three distinct six-stage curation pathways (Fig. 3 c). PT emphasized medically relevant content and reliable image–text associations, SFT required accurate instruction–response demonstrations, and RL required reliable evaluation targets and informative model outcomes. Each pathway combined exclusion with the applicable revision, verification and routing operations. Intermediate and retained-pool counts in the figure are illustrative estimates; stage-wise accounting is provided in Supplementary Table S2 .

The PT pathway processed 232.8 million candidate records. First, visual and textual usability checks assessed media accessibility, content readability and record completeness to exclude unusable inputs. Second, medical-content selection retained material relevant to medical or biomedical learning, removing accessible but out-of-domain content. Third, textual and visual deduplication identified repeated and near-identical records to limit redundant supervision, using MinHash-based text retrieval and image difference hashing. Fourth, image–text consistency assessment checked whether paired descriptions agreed with the associated images and available source context, identifying mismatches and unsupported claims. Fifth, caption rewriting and rechecking addressed descriptions that were incomplete, repetitive or weakly aligned but could be improved using the image, original legend and linked passages. Adequate descriptions were retained unchanged, and revised targets were verified against the source evidence. Sixth, image-quality assessment examined clarity, exposure, contrast, noise and artifacts using modality-specific criteria to exclude images whose degradation compromised interpretation. Text-only records underwent the applicable textual checks and bypassed image-dependent assessments. The pathway is summarized by an estimated retained pool of 93.8 million records. Selection criteria, duplicate detection and caption refinement are detailed in Supplementary Section S1.3 .

The SFT pathway processed 87.4 million candidate instruction–response records. First, rule-based screening checked required fields, conversation structure, media references and non-empty targets to establish valid training instances. Second, deduplication removed repeated instruction instances to limit redundant demonstrations. Third, language-model-assisted quality scoring assessed medical accuracy, instruction relevance, completeness, logical consistency and evidential support to screen the overall quality of each demonstration. The main scoring branch required a score of at least 3 on a five-point scale, with source-specific acceptance rules applied in other branches. Fourth, length and format checks assessed whether responses met the task’s length and output requirements, including the required answer representation and structure. Fifth, task and reasoning verification assessed substantive correctness: direct answers were compared with reference targets, structured outputs with required fields, and image-grounded claims with visual evidence or annotations. Explicit reasoning was checked for internal consistency and support for intermediate claims. Sixth, retained-pool consolidation assembled accepted records into a common instruction pool for subsequent curriculum selection. The pathway is summarized by an estimated retained pool of 69.6 million records. Scoring criteria and task-verification procedures are provided in Supplementary Section S1.4 .

The RL pathway processed 2.5 million candidate records. First, prompt and schema checks established valid task specifications and input fields so that candidates could be presented and evaluated consistently. Second, task typing assigned the appropriate task family and evaluator, accounting for differences between closed-answer, open-ended and spatial outputs. Third, reference validation checked evaluation targets against original annotations or supporting source evidence, including numerical units, clinically important findings and spatial references where applicable. This step established the evaluation basis before model performance was used to estimate difficulty. Fourth, blind rollouts and task evaluation measured model performance. The answering model received task inputs without reference targets, which were available only to the evaluator. In the closed-answer branch, eight responses per prompt provided an initial estimate of model-relative difficulty. Fifth, difficulty-based routing prioritized prompts with two to six correct responses, favouring tasks on which the model could succeed but remained inconsistent. Other success-rate groups were routed to further selection, review or capability retention. Sixth, semantic checks and replay routing assessed agreement with reference conclusions and supporting evidence, complementing the earlier task evaluation. Grounding retained task-specific spatial verification, and eligible records were assigned to RL task families or supervised replay. These offline assessments established the initial candidate selection and routing before RL optimization. The figure summarizes the pathway with an estimated retained pool of approximately 0.3 million records. Reference validation, task-specific evaluators and routing rules are detailed in Supplementary Table S7 and Supplementary Section S1.5 .

After filtering, the annotated image-reference subset of the curated multimodal data spans 15 modality groups (Fig. 3 d) and 10 anatomical categories (Fig. 3 f). Coverage is broad but uneven, with pathology, MRI and CT the most prevalent modality groups, and head and neck, thorax and abdomen/pelvis the most frequent anatomical categories. Complementary t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) projections visualize local structure in sampled image representations, coloured by modality (Fig. 3 e) and anatomical category (Fig. 3 g). Category mappings and analysis procedures are detailed in Supplementary Section S1.6 . The retained pools provide candidates for subsequent training-stage allocation, with selected record counts reported separately in Supplementary Section S1.7 and Supplementary Table S11 .


#### 2.2 Model Architecture and Training

Sage-Med was initialized from Intern-S2-Preview, a 35-billion-parameter scientific multimodal foundation model [ 40 ] . We organized training into medical adaptation, progressive supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and adaptive reinforcement learning (RL), progressively shifting the emphasis from medical knowledge and visual–semantic alignment to instruction following and reward-guided policy refinement (Fig. 2 c). Medical adaptation combined continual pretraining with task-oriented mid-training. Three successive SFT stages then expanded medical instruction coverage and selectively introduced more demanding supervision, while retaining examples from earlier stages. Finally, adaptive RL allocated rollouts according to the evolving policy, alongside independent reference supervision on medical and general-purpose tasks.


##### 2.2.1 Medical Multimodal Knowledge Pretraining

Continual pretraining used 83.8 million records comprising medical image–text pairs, document-derived evidence, textual medical knowledge and supplementary scientific content. These records were assembled through the source integration and stage-specific filtering procedures described in Section 2.1.2 . Image-associated text provided supervision linking visual content to medical descriptions and concepts, whereas text-only records extended coverage of medical terminology and knowledge. This stage provided broad medical-domain exposure before the introduction of more task-oriented training mixtures.

Mid-training used 28.1 million records to introduce task structure alongside continued medical and scientific supervision. The mixture combined medical task data and knowledge-and-reasoning examples with sampled pretraining records and general scientific examples. Question–response formats increased the emphasis on using knowledge to address explicit requests, while the retained pretraining material maintained broader content coverage. The transition from continual pretraining to mid-training therefore shifted the data mixture towards task-oriented learning before the subsequent SFT curriculum.


##### 2.2.2 Progressive Supervised Fine-tuning

Progressive SFT organized medical instruction learning around three successive priorities: instruction alignment, task diversification and selective refinement (Fig. 2 c). The curriculum established coverage of medical requests and response formats, expanded image-conditioned tasks, and then introduced targeted supervision for difficult examples and explicit reasoning. Medical and general-purpose supervision were retained across stages, with selected earlier-stage examples incorporated into later mixtures. The reported stage sizes describe complete training mixtures, including replay, and are not mutually exclusive sample counts.

Stage 1 used 6.1 million records to align medical responses with explicit instructions across text-only, single-image and multi-image inputs. Medical question answering, report generation, visual grounding and selected reasoning tasks linked medical knowledge and visual evidence to different response requirements. The length-stratified medical subset contained approximately 25% short (up to 64 characters), 50% medium-length (65–512 characters) and 25% long (more than 512 characters) reference responses, providing supervision across different levels of response detail. Focused questions were paired with concise answers, whereas explanatory tasks supplied more extended responses; conversational examples additionally supervised the use of context across turns. General-purpose examples complemented these medical tasks with broader instruction-following supervision. Together, these components established initial coverage of task-appropriate answers, explanations and interactions for the subsequent expansion of multimodal tasks.

Stage 2 used 9.6 million records to broaden image-conditioned instruction learning through additional medical visual tasks and task collections. The mixture comprised approximately 60% multimodal and 40% text-only records, combining expanded visual-task supervision with continued text-based instruction learning. Visual tasks required image content to be used according to the request, with reference outputs ranging from focused answers and localization outputs to longer medical descriptions. Medical text and general-purpose examples complemented this supervision with knowledge-based responses and broader instructions. A dedicated Stage 1 replay component accounted for approximately 1.6% of the complete mixture, retaining supervision on selected earlier tasks and response formats as additional task collections were introduced. This stage thus broadened medical visual-task coverage while continuing to train on textual instructions and previously introduced requests.

Stage 3 used 6.2 million records to combine targeted supervision for difficult tasks and explicit reasoning with broader medical instruction learning. The mixture comprised approximately 53% multimodal and 47% text-only records, retaining substantial coverage of both image-conditioned and textual tasks. Dedicated difficult-example and chain-of-thought pools accounted for approximately 8.5% and 13% of the complete mixture, respectively. The former concentrated supervision on selected challenging questions, whereas the latter provided intermediate reasoning steps alongside final answers. A separate replay component from Stages 1 and 2 contributed approximately 7%, maintaining supervision on earlier tasks alongside broader medical and general-purpose examples. Direct-answer examples remained part of the mixture, so explicit reasoning supervision was accompanied by continued training on concise responses. The final stage therefore introduced targeted refinement across input modalities while retaining a wider range of tasks and response requirements.


##### 2.2.3 Adaptive Reinforcement Learning

RL extended reference-based instruction learning through task-specific reward feedback, using group relative policy optimization (GRPO) [ 41 ] . The framework combined policy-dependent difficulty assessment, task-specific rewards and adaptive sampling with independent reference replay (Fig. 2 c). The RL and replay pools each comprised 80% multimodal and 20% text-only examples. Within RL, the task-design quotas were 85% closed-form and 15% open-ended tasks. Initial policy-based assessment classified 14.98% of the assessed prompts as difficult, 73.37% as intermediate or inconsistently solved, and 11.65% as relatively easy.

Because prompt difficulty changes as the policy learns, we initialized difficulty estimates from responses generated by the SFT checkpoint and updated them using fresh rollouts during RL. Evaluation followed the supervision available for each task. For closed-form tasks, task-specific verifiers compared responses with reference targets, providing correctness observations for estimating prompt-level success rates. For open-ended tasks, including report generation and explanatory responses, a fixed judge applied task-specific rubrics using reference answers and available supporting evidence. These rubrics assessed medical content and evidence agreement, including clinically consequential errors where applicable. Verifier outcomes and rubric scores provided task-specific reward feedback and informed subsequent sampling updates. For difficulty tracking, these assessments were kept separate from auxiliary reward components, so that formatting improvements alone did not indicate improved medical correctness.

Sampling priorities reflected the training signal available under the current policy. For closed-form tasks, smoothed success estimates were used to estimate the probability of obtaining both correct and incorrect responses within a rollout group. For open-ended tasks, priorities reflected within-group variation in rubric scores after accounting for judge variability, with uniform sampling retained when reliable calibration was unavailable. Priorities were updated within the four categories defined by multimodal/text-only inputs and closed-form/open-ended tasks, subject to cumulative coverage quotas. Exposure-based downweighting reduced repeated concentration on individual prompts, while a uniform exploration component maintained a non-zero sampling probability for each prompt. Alongside this adaptive RL stream, independent reference replay supplied teacher-forced supervision on reference responses sampled from a separate distribution. Replay remained outside the adaptive RL sampler and did not inherit its sampling weights. The replay loss was combined with the GRPO loss to update the same policy, maintaining direct reference-based supervision as RL sampling priorities evolved.


### 3 Results


#### 3.1 Evaluation Settings


##### 3.1.1 Benchmarking on 70+ LLMs and MLLMs

The core evaluation compares Sage-Med-Preview with 77 LLMs and MLLMs. These models cover the general and medical domains and include 68 open-source and nine proprietary models. By model type, they span autoregressive LLMs and MLLMs, diffusion large language models, and unified understanding–generation models. SDAR-Chat, Lumina-DiMOO, and MMaDA-Base are diffusion large language models, and all other open-source models are autoregressive models. Model sizes range from 0.182B parameters for BiomedGPT to 1T total parameters for Intern-S1-Pro. In the general domain, the open-source LLMs are SDAR-Chat [ 42 ] and DeepSeek-V3.2 [ 43 ] . The general MLLMs include Qwen3-VL in its Instruct and Thinking variants [ 44 ] , Qwen3.5 at 4B, 9B, and 35B-A3B [ 45 ] , Qwen3.8 [ 46 ] , InternVL3.5 [ 47 ] , LLaVA-OneVision [ 48 ] , LLaVA-OneVision-1.5 [ 49 ] , MiniCPM-V 2.6 [ 50 , 51 ] , Llama 3.2-Vision [ 52 ] , and Gemma-4-IT [ 53 ] . We also evaluate the scientific multimodal models Intern-S1 [ 54 , 55 , 56 ] and Intern-S2-Preview [ 40 , 40 ] . The general unified understanding–generation models are Show-o2 [ 57 ] , BAGEL [ 58 ] , Janus-Pro [ 59 ] , Lumina-DiMOO [ 60 ] , InternVL-U [ 61 ] , MMaDA-Base [ 62 , 63 ] , SenseNova-U1 [ 64 ] , and Lance [ 65 ] . In the medical domain, the open-source LLMs are MMed-Llama-3 [ 66 ] , Baichuan-M3 [ 67 ] , and AntAngelMed [ 68 ] . The medical MLLMs include BiomedGPT [ 69 ] , LLaVA-Med [ 20 ] , MedDr [ 70 ] , HuatuoGPT-Vision [ 71 ] , BiMediX2 [ 72 ] , GMAI-VL [ 73 ] , MedGemma [ 74 ] , MedGemma 1.5 [ 75 ] , Lingshu [ 23 ] , Hulu-Med [ 27 ] , and ClinFusion [ 28 ] . They also include MedVLM-R1 [ 76 ] and QoQ-Med [ 77 ] , which employ reinforcement learning to enhance medical reasoning capabilities. The medical unified understanding–generation models are HealthGPT [ 30 ] and UniMedVL [ 31 ] . The proprietary models are GPT-4o [ 78 ] , GPT-5.4 [ 79 ] , Gemini-2.5-Flash [ 80 ] , Claude-Sonnet-4 [ 81 ] , and Claude-Opus-4.6 [ 82 ] , together with GPT-6.2-Sol and GPT-6.2-Astra [ 83 ] , Gemini-4-Argon [ 84 ] , and Claude-Opus-5.5 [ 85 ] , which are evaluated on a subset of the benchmarks.


##### 3.1.2 Comprehensive Evaluation across Clinical Task Types

The evaluation covers 238 clinical tasks from 74 benchmarks: 222 tasks from 61 public benchmarks and 16 tasks from 13 private benchmarks. Table S12 in Appendix S2.1 gives an overview. These benchmarks together cover more than 40 modalities across medical imaging and clinical text. The 45 core public benchmarks span eleven categories. Five comprehensive multimodal understanding benchmarks, including OmniMedVQA [ 86 ] and GMAI-MMBench [ 87 ] , assess cross-modality image understanding. Two knowledge-intensive understanding benchmarks, MMMU-Med [ 88 ] and MMMU Pro-Med [ 89 ] , test college-level knowledge on medical figures. The multi-frame understanding benchmark MedFrameQA [ 90 ] requires reasoning over multiple images, and the medical visual grounding benchmark MedSG-Bench [ 91 ] requires localizing targets with bounding boxes. Fifteen specialized multimodal understanding benchmarks, including PathVQA [ 92 ] , each target a single modality or anatomical region. Seven medical reasoning benchmarks, including MedXpertQA [ 35 ] , test expert-level image and text reasoning. Five comprehensive text understanding benchmarks, including MedQA [ 93 ] , assess clinical text comprehension. Two medical examination benchmarks, MedMCQA [ 94 ] and MMLU-Med [ 95 ] , test knowledge through multiple-choice questions. Three real-world clinical workflow benchmarks, including MedR-Bench [ 96 ] , test multi-step clinical decisions. The multilingual understanding benchmark MMedBench [ 66 ] evaluates text in multiple languages, and three medical report generation benchmarks, including MIMIC-CXR [ 97 ] , evaluate chest X-ray reports. The other 16 public benchmarks are ClinHallu [ 98 ] for clinical hallucination identification and 15 additional specialized-modality benchmarks described in Appendix S2.3 . The private benchmarks, detailed in Appendix S2.2 , span ten imaging modalities from 14 hospitals and institutions, covering disease classification, cancer staging, outcome prediction, lesion localization, and report generation.

We apply the same inference hyperparameters in Table S15 to all open-source autoregressive models and to proprietary models accessed through their official APIs. Diffusion large language models instead keep the default inference hyperparameters of each model. During inference, we prompt non-reasoning models to answer directly and reasoning models to produce a thinking process before the final answer. Appendix S2.6 provides the prompt templates for both model types. After inference, we score model responses on closed-ended benchmarks by accuracy with rule-based matching. On open-ended benchmarks, we follow their official metrics and judge models when these are provided. Otherwise, model performance on open-ended benchmarks is measured by accuracy with GPT-6-Luna-High as the judge model. For report generation, BLEU [ 99 ] , METEOR [ 100 ] , and ROUGE [ 101 ] measure the semantic similarity between model-generated reports and reference reports, while RaTEScore [ 102 ] assesses the clinical correctness of key medical entities in the generated reports. Table S12 in Appendix S2.1 details the metric and judge model of each benchmark.


#### 3.2 Comprehensive Evaluation across Understanding and Reasoning Tasks


##### 3.2.1 Performance Patterns across Models and Tasks

Domain-specific training has not yet lifted medical MLLMs above general-purpose foundation models, and the gap widens against frontier models. We compare 22 medical MLLMs with 29 general MLLMs, including GPT-6.2-Sol, among 68 multimodal models evaluated on the 45 core public benchmarks. As Fig. Sage-Med: Scaling Medical Multimodal Intelligence a shows, the medical group scores lower in all 11 core public categories and on 43 of 45 core public benchmarks, except PathVQA and TemMed-Bench. The largest gaps appear on knowledge and text tasks. Medical MLLMs average 42.71% on knowledge-intensive understanding and 65.00% on comprehensive text understanding, whereas general MLLMs reach 57.12% and 78.14%. Even medical report generation, the category with the smallest gap, shows 49.22% against 52.19%. The gap is larger still for the strongest models. Hulu-Med-235B-A22B averages 59.84% over the 45 core public benchmarks, above every other medical MLLM, whereas GPT-6.2-Sol averages 71.49% and scores lower on only seven of them. GPT-6.2-Sol also exceeds every medical MLLM in nine of 11 core public categories. It scores 86.18% against at most 57.16% on knowledge-intensive understanding and 64.25% against at most 5.65% on medical visual grounding. Open-source general models with far fewer activated parameters already match the strongest medical MLLMs. Intern-S2-Preview-35B-A3B activates 3B parameters against 22B for Hulu-Med-235B-A22B, yet averages 59.90%. Hulu-Med-235B-A22B wins 17 of their 45 benchmarks. Hulu-Med-30B-A3B has the same 3B activated parameters as Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, yet averages 56.17% against 58.18% and wins 12 of the 45 benchmarks. Among proprietary models outside Fig. Sage-Med: Scaling Medical Multimodal Intelligence , GPT-5.4 averages 61.47% against 60.02% for Hulu-Med-235B-A22B on their 44 shared core benchmarks. Medical MLLMs hold a clear lead only over the previous Qwen3-VL generation. ClinFusion-32B averages 57.34% against 53.85% for Qwen3-VL-Instruct-32B and wins 31 of their 43 shared benchmarks. Domain training thus brings smaller gains than one generation update of general foundation models.

Beyond the gap between model groups, all models vary widely across task types. As Fig. Sage-Med: Scaling Medical Multimodal Intelligence b shows, models score lower on multimodal benchmarks than on text-only benchmarks. The 68 multimodal models average 68.93% on the eight text-only understanding benchmarks but 44.59% on the 24 multimodal understanding benchmarks. Within the multimodal benchmarks, performance declines sharply from comprehensive to specialized categories. The models average 63.02% on five comprehensive multimodal understanding benchmarks, 50.26% on the multi-frame understanding benchmark, and 48.93% on two knowledge-intensive understanding benchmarks. On the 15 specialized multimodal understanding benchmarks, they average 40.09%. Medical visual grounding is the weakest, with an all-model mean of 5.56% on MedSG-Bench. Models score even lower on medical reasoning than on multimodal understanding. The models average 36.36% on the seven medical reasoning benchmarks, below the 44.59% on multimodal understanding and the 68.93% on text-only understanding. On the real-world clinical workflow benchmarks, which require multi-step decisions over whole clinical cases, the average drops to 31.97%. These results reveal two gaps in current training data. First, public medical data still lack high-quality, modality-balanced paired multimodal data [ 23 ] , leaving models with persistent weaknesses on multimodal understanding tasks. Second, single-turn, complete-information questions and short answers dominate training data, providing little supervision for seeking information or handling uncertainty across turns [ 6 ] .

Models from the same family, and families from the same team, show consistent strengths and weaknesses across task types. ### 4 Training Analysis

Prioritizing informative content and modality balance yielded greater downstream medical performance gains than pretraining on the full data pool. Standard continual pretraining (CPT) used the full pretraining dataset, whereas refined CPT used a selected 30-million-record subset with a more balanced modality distribution. Both recipes were followed by the same SFT1 supervision, allowing their downstream utility to be compared after a common instruction-alignment stage. All evaluations in Fig. 9 a–d use the same 20 benchmarks, comprising 14 multimodal and six text-only benchmarks, with performance summarized by the macro-average across benchmarks. Standard CPT followed by SFT1 increased the macro-average from 56.33 for the base model to 61.26, while refined CPT reached 62.82, exceeding the full-data recipe by 1.56 percentage points (Fig. 9 a). The stronger performance of the smaller mixture highlights the value of increasing information density and balancing complementary sources of medical supervision. For heterogeneous medical corpora, these findings favor improving content selection and modality composition before further expanding data volume.

Targeted supervised refinement and subsequent RL contributed most of the improvement achieved after initial instruction alignment. Following CPT and SFT1, the macro-average increased from 61.26 to 61.45 after SFT2 and to 62.02 after SFT3, corresponding to successive gains of 0.19 and 0.57 percentage points. RL then raised performance to 63.03, adding a further 1.01 points and bringing the cumulative improvement over SFT1 to 1.77 points (Fig. 9 b). The larger increments during targeted refinement and reward optimization indicate that substantial scope for improvement remained after broad instruction learning and task expansion. In particular, the additional gain from RL demonstrates the continued utility of task-specific reward feedback after three supervised stages. These results motivate a progressive curriculum in which successive stages develop task coverage, refine performance through focused supervision and further improve responses through reward-based optimization. The training-loss trajectory of Sage-Med-Preview-35B-A3B provides a complementary view of this process across pretraining, mid-training and SFT (Fig. 9 e). Loss generally decreased within individual phases, with changes at stage boundaries followed by further reduction under the next training recipe.

The performance advantage of Intern-S2-Preview persisted across all three SFT stages, showing that initialization continued to shape the level of medical capability reached after adaptation. Intern-S2-Preview initialization exceeded Qwen3.5-35B-A3B initialization by 4.31, 2.54 and 2.72 percentage points after SFT1, SFT2 and SFT3, respectively (Fig. 9 c). Across these stages, the Intern-initialized model improved from 61.26 to 62.02, while the Qwen-initialized model improved from 56.95 to 59.30. The Qwen-initialized model gained 2.35 points between SFT1 and SFT3, compared with 0.76 points for the Intern-initialized model, narrowing the gap while retaining a lower final macro-average. This trajectory distinguishes the capacity to improve during adaptation from the capability ultimately achieved. Evaluating initialization choices across successive checkpoints therefore captures both responsiveness to further training and the performance attainable under the medical curriculum. The consistently stronger adapted performance of Intern-S2-Preview supports its selection as the base model for Sage-Med.

Greater text coverage within a multimodal-majority mixture and an intermediate short-answer share improved medical instruction alignment in the respective pilot experiments. For Qwen3-VL-8B, increasing the text share from 29.4% to 38%, with the multimodal share changing from 70.6% to 62%, raised the 20-benchmark macro-average from 48.70 to 49.79, a gain of 1.09 percentage points (Fig. 9 d, left). The improvement shows that allocating more supervision to text can strengthen aggregate medical performance while preserving a predominantly multimodal training mixture. Response-length composition exhibited an intermediate optimum among the tested settings. In the Qwen3-VL-4B pilot, increasing the short-answer share from 15% to 25% improved the same macro-average by 1.87 percentage points, whereas increasing it to 30% yielded a smaller gain of 1.44 points relative to the 15% setting (Fig. 9 d, right). The strongest result at 25% favors a length-diverse mixture that combines concise response targets with substantial supervision from contextual explanations and extended reasoning. Guided by this comparison, the final length-stratified medical subset uses 25% short, 50% medium-length and 25% long references. These pilots identify modality allocation and response-length distribution as actionable components of training-data design, with their value assessed through downstream medical performance.


### 5 Limitations

Although Sage-Med-Preview provides empirical reference points and actionable recipes for scaling medical multimodal intelligence by combining stage-specific data curation, progressive supervision, and adaptive reinforcement learning, formally characterizing quantitative scaling laws across wider parameter, data, and compute regimes warrants systematic multi-scale investigation. In particular, experiments spanning multiple model scales and compute budgets are needed to predict capability emergence and to vary corpus size and image-grounded supervision density independently, thereby distinguishing the returns of broader clinical case coverage from those of richer annotations. Moreover, because modality proportions shifted across post-training stages (approximately 60% and 53% multimodal in the second and third supervised fine-tuning stages, respectively, and 80% in the reinforcement-learning pool) alongside changes in task composition and difficulty, isolating the independent effects of modality balance remains challenging. Controlled mixture experiments, intermediate checkpoint evaluations, and targeted ablations will help clarify how distinct supervisory signals, such as adaptive difficulty sampling and independent reference replay, govern the acquisition and retention of specific capabilities. Furthermore, under a fixed visual token budget, systematic comparisons across tasks and imaging modalities are needed to establish when higher spatial resolution versus broader volumetric slice or temporal frame coverage yields greater diagnostic gains. Finally, owing to API and computational cost constraints, our evaluation did not cover all latest frontier general-purpose models across the full benchmark suite, with several recent proprietary releases assessed only on selected subsets. Nevertheless, it is evident that these frontier models already exhibit remarkably strong medical multimodal capabilities even under standalone inference without external tools or task-specific harnesses. Beyond scaling native model capabilities alone, integrating multimodal foundation models into agentic frameworks equipped with specialized clinical tools, iterative verification, and structured harnesses represents a promising pathway toward general-purpose medical multimodal intelligence.


### 6 Conclusion

Effective scaling of medical multimodal intelligence depends not merely on expanding parameter counts or raw corpus volume, but on aligning supervision quality, modality balance, and training curricula with the diverse capabilities required in clinical practice. Through a systematic evaluation of 77 models across 238 clinical tasks spanning 61 public and 13 private multi-center benchmarks, this study reveals a structural bottleneck in current scaling paradigms: enlarging model size or enabling text-centric reasoning disproportionately advances language performance while failing to close the gap in medical visual perception. To overcome this limitation, Sage-Med-Preview establishes a scalable paradigm that pairs stage-specific curation over 322.7 million candidate records with medical pretraining, progressive supervised fine-tuning, and adaptive reinforcement learning, advancing visual perception and medical language understanding in tandem. This recipe raises average performance across 31 public multimodal benchmarks from 52.24% for the base model Intern-S2-Preview to 58.26%, outperforming all open-source medical and general comparators while generalizing reliably to unseen multi-center clinical cohorts (57.72% versus 51.24% for GPT-6.2-Sol). Complementing these results, our training analyses distill actionable scaling principles, showing that a compact, modality-balanced pretraining mixture outperforms unfiltered full-data scaling, and that targeted supervised refinement followed by adaptive reinforcement learning unlocks consistent post-alignment gains. Taken together, this work provides empirical scaling evidence, practical data and training recipes, and a rigorous clinical evaluation foundation toward scalable generalist medical AI, laying the groundwork for future systems that tightly integrate fine-grained visual evidence with complex reasoning across end-to-end clinical workflows.

### S1 Medical Training Data Construction and Curation


#### S1.1 Data Sources, Inventory and Accounting

We assembled medical training records from more than 200 public datasets, over 120 million biomedical literature records and more than 100 private resources. These resources included image–text collections, question-answering datasets, textbooks, journal articles, medical documents and specialty imaging collections. MedTrinity-25M and PubMedQA illustrate the public multimodal and question-answering resources included in the collection [ 103 , 104 ] . Source counts describe the underlying collections and documents. For resources redistributed through aggregate datasets, the upstream source and the derived record were distinguished when organizing provenance.

A candidate record was a training item indexed by its intended stage family: pretraining (PT), supervised fine-tuning (SFT) or reinforcement learning (RL). A record could contain one or more images, text alone, or a task with a reference target. One source item could support several tasks or be considered for more than one stage. The candidate collection comprised approximately 322.7 million records, including 233.8 million multimodal and 88.9 million text-only records (Fig. 3 b; Table S1 ). The public/private provenance shares shown in Fig. 3 b were 91.2%/8.8% for PT and 55.7%/44.3% for SFT. Provenance and multimodal/text-only composition describe different attributes of the same stage-family inventories.

#### S1.2 Source-aware Extraction and Evidence Organization

Processing followed two routes according to source structure: document extraction and dataset integration (Fig. 3 a). For PDF documents, MinerU recovered text and figures [ 39 ] . Recovered figures were linked to their original legends, available in-text figure references and relevant surrounding passages. For compound figures, panel-specific associations were retained when panel identifiers and contextual references could be recovered. Existing datasets instead underwent media and schema normalization while preserving their original supervision and available source context. The retained evidence depended on the source type, as summarized in Table S3 .

Source-linked evidence supported three operations: retaining usable supervision, refining deficient descriptions or responses, and synthesizing additional tasks supported by the source material. Original evidence and revised targets served different roles: the source legend, annotation or passage supported verification, whereas a generated description or answer remained a candidate training target. Refinement and synthesis therefore preserved the link to the underlying evidence rather than treating model-generated text as an independent reference.

The resulting supervision comprised four groups: image descriptions, question answering and reasoning, report generation, and visual grounding. Descriptions summarized supported visual content; question-answering and reasoning tasks required a reference answer grounded in the available evidence. Reports preserved available section structure, including clinical history, findings and impression, without requiring every source to contain all sections. Grounding required an applicable spatial annotation or localization reference; the mention of an organ in a caption alone was insufficient to define a bounding box or mask. Modality, anatomical and medical-concept metadata were retained where available for subsequent selection and coverage analysis.

Text duplicate detection combined MinHash and locality-sensitive hashing for candidate retrieval with Jaccard similarity of the corresponding token or shingle sets [ 105 , 106 ] . Image difference hashes provided a complementary signal through Hamming distance between binary image signatures. These signals identified candidate duplicates rather than defining supervision equivalence on their own. For example, repeated question wording paired with different images did not establish a duplicate, and the same image could support distinct valid targets. Duplicate assessment therefore considered the associated evidence and task content, preventing repeated source material from being confused with every legitimate reuse of that material.

Caption refinement used Qwen-VL-2.5-235B with the image, original legend and available linked passages as context. The revision sought a self-contained description while preserving the distinction between visible observations and interpretations supported by the source text. Contextual information could resolve an anatomical reference or explain a panel, but did not by itself justify an unsupported visible finding. The five-point review considered linguistic completeness, repetition, medical plausibility, image–text agreement and evidential support. Short descriptions were revised when they lacked necessary information, rather than solely because they were short. For multi-panel figures, panel-specific statements were retained only when the visual input and source context supported the association. Rechecking evaluated the revised target against the original evidence; a more fluent caption was not sufficient for acceptance if its content remained unsupported.


#### S1.4 Supervised Fine-tuning Data Curation

SFT curation required valid instruction–response examples with accurate, relevant and adequately supported targets. The pathway processed 87.4 million candidates and produced an estimated retained pool of 69.6 million records. Its six stages separated record validity, redundancy, overall demonstration quality, output requirements and substantive correctness (Table S5 ). The consolidated pool supported subsequent curriculum selection rather than defining the composition of any individual SFT stage.

Quality assessment examined whether the response answered the instruction correctly and whether its claims were supported by the available image, text or reference. The rubric covered medically incorrect statements, contradictions, unsupported claims, incomplete answers, off-topic responses and defective multimodal supervision. A score of 1 denoted unusable or medically incorrect supervision, a score of 3 denoted usable supervision with minor defects, and a score of 5 denoted strong and complete supervision. Passing the scoring threshold did not replace the subsequent format and task-verification checks. Missing or unparsable scores did not constitute acceptance. Task difficulty, expertise level and the presence of reasoning were treated separately from correctness, so a difficult or detailed response was not considered high quality solely on those grounds.

Length and format checks reflected the expected output: an option label or organ name could be a complete answer, whereas a report or explanation required sufficient content for its task. Structured responses were checked for the required fields and answer representation before their contents were compared with reference targets. For image-grounded responses, verification concerned the agreement of stated findings with the available visual evidence or annotation. For explicit reasoning, a correct conclusion did not compensate for fabricated intermediate observations or contradictory supporting statements. Records entered the consolidated pool only after the applicable checks had been satisfied; later curriculum allocation determined which accepted examples were used in each SFT stage.
