## Benchmarking Physical Agency

AI systems can now operate Blender, follow instructions in complex video games, and manipulate objects with robot arms. What range of physical goals can today’s AI systems achieve autonomously? Answering this question requires testing systems on tasks that demand different combinations of perception, memory, planning, and action. We introduce WorldLeague , a benchmark that compares AI systems on a common set of 60 simulated robot tasks by measuring progress toward each task’s physical goal from the robot’s trajectory. Robots explore scenes to gather evidence in WorldSeek , rearrange objects in WorldShape , and adapt to changes in WorldShift . Tying these goals to a single control interface limits which AI systems can be compared. In WorldLeague, vision-language models (VLMs) choose targets and motions, while vision-language-action models (VLAs) and world-action models (WAMs) use their native controls. For each of six capability dimensions, we measure how much progress a system makes toward its goals (Interactive Score) and how often it achieves them (physical success rate). Together, these measures form the system’s Physical Agency Profile . Among 13 representative AI systems, Claude Fable 5.2 achieves 13 of the 60 goals and earns the highest mean Interactive Score (28.58/100). Fable leads in four dimensions, while GPT-6.1-Sol and GPT-6-Astra tie for first on rule-following tasks. Even when VLMs answer every final question correctly, they receive an Interactive Score of zero in 71.8% of those episodes. WorldLeague gives Embodied AI, Physical AI, and Agentic AI a shared basis for comparing what AI systems achieve autonomously, from partial progress toward a physical goal to full goal completion.

### 1 Introduction

Foundation models have recently become a promising direction for building general-purpose robots [ 51 ] . In digital environments, agents built on frontier models can follow instructions in open-world video games [ 4 ] and turn visual references into editable 3D scenes in Blender [ 12 ] . The range of tasks that physical robots can perform has also grown [ 14 , 31 ] . Robots have performed long-horizon household tasks, such as cleaning a kitchen or bedroom, in homes they had never seen during training [ 19 ] .

To clean an unfamiliar kitchen, a robot must use what it sees to decide what to do next. Embodied AI systems connect perception to action in different ways [ 16 ] . A vision-language model (VLM) can identify an object from a spatial description [ 7 ] and estimate its distance from other objects [ 5 ] . With a robot interface, it can use such judgments to choose where to move or grasp [ 9 ] . Vision-language-action (VLA) models produce robot actions directly from instructions and visual observations [ 20 ] . For example, Gemini Robotics 1.5 interleaves reasoning and action during multi-step manipulation [ 50 ] . World-action models (WAMs) jointly predict future observations and robot actions [ 23 , 44 , 57 , 26 ] . This approach has enabled closed-loop manipulation on new tasks and in new environments [ 63 ] .

Across these approaches, the ambition is the same. A person specifies a physical goal and leaves the system to decide how to achieve it [ 30 , 9 , 27 , 25 , 39 , 42 ] . We call the system making those decisions the agent and its capacity to choose and execute actions through a body its physical agency . The decisions an agent must make depend on the goal. To find a hidden object, it must decide where to look and what to move out of the way [ 11 , 10 , 17 ] . To put that object through a narrow opening, it must also choose an orientation that fits. An agent may therefore find the right object and still fail to put it away. This leads us to the central question of our study. What range of physical goals can today’s AI systems achieve autonomously?

Organizing robot benchmarks around household activities [ 21 , 37 ] obscures what success on each activity establishes about an agent. Consider a robot asked to deliver a cup. If the user names the cup and it is in plain sight, the robot can select it from its current view. If the user asks for the cup that has moved since the robot last visited the room, the robot must use an earlier observation to decide what to pick up. Both tasks count as cup delivery. Yet success on the first leaves the decisive requirement of the second entirely untested. To establish the range of an agent’s physical abilities, we must vary what the agent has to work out for itself and verify that its actions bring about the requested outcome.

We introduce WorldLeague , a benchmark of 60 simulated robot tasks organized by the physical demands that determine success. We evaluate VLMs, VLAs, and WAMs against shared goals and constraints. For each task, the robot’s trajectory shows which goal requirements it satisfies and whether it completes the task. The tasks span three tracks, each defined by what the robot must do to achieve its goal. WorldSeek tests navigation and goal grounding as systems explore and gather evidence. WorldShape tests geometric reconfiguration and physical reasoning as systems change the scene. WorldShift tests temporal memory and constraint management as systems track a changing world and recover from disturbances. The three tracks span six capability dimensions (Sec. 3.1 ). We group each task by its primary challenge, and a single task can draw on capabilities from several dimensions.

To compare systems across these tasks, we instantiate each goal in a scene with specified objects, initial conditions, and constraints. Physical predicates check conditions such as stable placement or recovery after a disturbance. We inspect all 60 task–scene pairs, verify that reference runs meet the goals and constraints, and check the scoring conditions against the task specifications. Each system pursues its goal through physical interaction. For VLMs, our decision-preserving action abstraction draws on how sensorimotor mechanisms coordinate fine-grained human movement [ 53 , 59 , 13 ] . The interface executes motions, and the agent chooses the targets, action parameters, sequences, and responses to feedback that shape the outcome. We adapt a motion interface [ 9 ] to implement this abstraction on R1 [ 21 ] . VLA and WAM policies use their native interfaces under shared task goals, constraints, and physical scoring rules.

We score the resulting trajectory for progress toward individual requirements and for physical success, which requires satisfying the full goal and all task constraints. A Physical Agency Profile reports Interactive Score for progress and physical success rate for complete goal achievement in each capability dimension. For VLMs, terminal question answering (QA) provides an auxiliary measure of scene understanding, outcome recognition, and memory within the same episodes.

Among the 13 evaluated embodied AI systems, Claude Fable 5.2 earns the highest overall Interactive Score, 28.58 out of 100, and achieves physical success on 13 of its 60 tasks. Fable leads in navigation, goal grounding, geometric reconfiguration, and temporal memory, while GPT-6.1-Sol and GPT-6-Astra lead in constraint management. The trajectory analysis also identifies goal requirements that remain unmet after systems make physical progress. Of 347 VLM episodes with full terminal QA credit, 249 (71.8%) receive an Interactive Score of zero. These results show that goal-relevant understanding and successful intermediate actions can coexist with unmet physical goals.

Turning demonstrated capabilities into desired physical outcomes is central to Embodied AI, Physical AI, and Agentic AI, which share the ambition of building systems that can pursue human goals through their own actions in the world. WorldLeague turns this ambition into a common experiment. A person specifies the desired outcome, an agent determines how to pursue it through a body, and the evaluation verifies what it achieves in the physical environment. Evaluating systems against common goals and physical success criteria allows advances in perception, reasoning, and control to be assessed in terms of what they enable these systems to accomplish [ 56 ] . Our broader aim is to make autonomous goal achievement a shared measure of progress for AI systems that act in the world.

We make three contributions to the evaluation of physical agency in embodied AI systems.

- • We test physical agency by goal achievement on tasks with different capability combinations.

- • We build and validate WorldLeague, a suite of 60 tasks that measures progress and physical success across six capability dimensions using shared goals and scoring rules.

- • We compare the capability profiles of 13 systems and identify goal requirements that remain unmet in episodes with correct answers or partial physical progress.


### 2 Related Work


#### 2.1 The Demands of Physical Goals

BEHAVIOR-1K [ 21 ] takes everyday household activities as the unit of evaluation. Within a single activity, what a robot must decide can change as it acts [ 32 ] . In RoboQuest [ 41 ] , a robot uses trial impressions to decide how to stamp the requested pattern. MeMoREval [ 54 ] makes a later request depend on what the system encountered earlier, while RobotAgentBench [ 34 ] introduces disturbances that require an agent to revise an ongoing manipulation. WorldLeague organizes goals by the primary challenge they pose. Finding a specified object and fitting it through a narrow opening both require identifying the object, but the second goal also requires reasoning about orientation and clearance. Grouping tasks this way lets us trace how success varies across exploration, physical transformation, and adaptation.


#### 2.2 Action Abstraction for Embodied AI Systems

An action interface determines which decisions the agent makes [ 62 ] . SayCan [ 1 ] uses a skill’s feasibility to guide selection, and Code as Policies [ 28 ] lets a model compose robot actions in a program. Inner Monologue [ 18 ] feeds execution outcomes back into planning. Show-Harness [ 9 ] translates agent-selected commands through an embodiment-specific interpreter, and HumanCLAW [ 47 ] uses the separation between action selection and motor execution to study how VLMs direct a body. WorldHarness adapts Show-Harness to preserve the VLM’s decisions throughout a task. To place an object, the VLM chooses where to grasp, how far to move, and when to release. The controller executes the motion and returns feedback that the VLM can use to correct its next move. VLA and world-action policies pursue the same goals through their native interfaces.


#### 2.3 Measuring Physical Goal Achievement

ESI-Bench [ 15 ] lets systems move and manipulate objects to gather evidence for a spatial answer. ALFRED [ 45 ] scores both task success and the fraction of goal conditions met. IMBench [ 35 ] examines understanding, planning, and execution in separate stages. WorldLeague builds on selected ESI-Bench scene records to pair what a VLM can report with what it physically achieves in the same episode. Once the physical outcome is fixed, terminal QA probes the VLM’s understanding of the scene and its actions. Across all three model families, Physical Agency Profiles show how partial progress and full goal achievement vary across the six dimensions.

RoboQuest and IMBench couple active perception to manipulation by asking agents to uncover information they need to change a physical configuration. MeMoREval tests whether an earlier observation guides a later choice, and RobotAgentBench tests recovery under constraints. WorldLeague evaluates all six dimensions through physical goal achievement, so the same system’s ability to find and manipulate a target can be compared with its ability to track that target as the scene changes.

### 3 WorldLeague Benchmark


#### 3.1 Physical Goals and Task Demands

A human specifies a desired outcome and leaves the agent to decide how to bring it about. To measure the range of goals an embodied AI system can achieve this way, WorldLeague varies what the physical world demands of it. Across 60 tasks, systems explore to find the evidence a goal requires, change the physical configuration of a scene, and adapt as events alter what they must do next. These challenges define the tracks.

WorldSeek. Where the robot goes determines what it can reach and what it can see. An ordered pickup tests spatial navigation . The robot chooses its route, visits six rooms in the prescribed order, and collects the requested items on its tray. A search for a hidden object tests goal grounding . The robot must decide where to look or what to move to expose the candidates, then use what it sees to identify and retrieve the requested object.

WorldShape. WorldShape asks the system to rearrange objects in furnished rooms. The room layout determines which objects are within reach, which paths have enough clearance, and which surfaces can support the final arrangement. In a geometric reconfiguration task, the robot must orient a cutting board so it clears the sides of a narrow kitchen storage slot. In a physical reasoning task, it collects a board and two boxes from separate utility-room counters and chooses their stacking order. The tower must remain stable on the designated surface after release.

WorldShift. A change in the scene can determine which object to deliver or force the robot to revisit a completed step. In a temporal memory task, the robot must compare its current view with an earlier one, identify the object that changed, and leave it at the destination specified for that change. A kitchen delivery tests constraint management . The robot must clear the work area before delivering an apple, then inspect the area again. If the obstruction has returned, the robot must put it back in storage and leave the area clear.

We assign each task to the dimension that best describes its primary challenge (Table 2 ). Individual tasks draw on several capabilities. Grouping them by their primary demand lets us compare, for instance, a system’s success in finding a target with its success in physically reconfiguring one.

#### 3.2 Task Construction and Validation

For each goal, we write the public instruction, specify the constraints, and define physical predicates for each goal requirement. We bind object roles to scene entities, set their poses and the robot’s starting pose, and configure the task events. The evaluator uses the same object bindings to check the private predicates. In the six-room pickup task, the required visit order becomes a sequence of room-entry events, while collection requires the designated objects to remain stable on the tray after release (Fig. 2 ).

> Figure 2: From goal to executable task. (A) The instruction specifies the visit order, pickups, and tray constraint. (B) The scene fixes object placements and the robot’s starting configuration. (C) The evaluator checks visits and pickups in order, tray contents, stability, and release.

We use BEHAVIOR-1K layouts and assets [ 21 ] to instantiate the 60 goals, adapting ESI-Bench scene records [ 15 ] for 47 and building scene configurations for the other 13. InteractiveWorld [ 55 ] informed the design of nine challenges. We manually inspected all 60 task–scene pairs, verified that reference runs satisfied their goals and constraints, and checked scoring thresholds against the task specifications (Appendices B.1 and D.7 ). These fixed instances form the evaluation suite. The generator also instantiates the same goals in new configurations by varying objects, layouts, robot starts, and task events while preserving the scoring predicates (Appendix F ).


#### 3.3 Action Interfaces and Decision Responsibility

To evaluate how a VLM pursues a goal, we let it choose a target, a motion, and a response when the outcome changes its plan. Human motor control offers a model for this division of responsibility. Sensorimotor feedback coordinates the fine movements needed to carry out a chosen action [ 53 , 59 ] . Show-Harness [ 9 ] and HumanCLAW [ 47 ] show how an embodied interface can separate action selection from motor execution. In our decision-preserving action abstraction , the VLM chooses where to grasp, how far to move, when to release, and what to try next if a motion stops early.

WorldHarness implements this abstraction on R1 by adapting Show-Harness (Fig. 3 ). Given the public goal, camera observations, and retained history, the VLM selects a base, arm, trunk, or gripper command. The controller executes the motion and returns feedback, which the VLM can use to revise its next command. The evaluator records the resulting physical states and task events.

VLA and WAM policies use their native interfaces and trained embodiments to produce control commands or action chunks. All three model families share the task goals, constraints, and physical scoring rules. WorldLeague can therefore compare different policies, harnesses, and robots by the goals each embodied AI system achieves through its own perception, reasoning, and control.

#### 3.4 Measuring Physical Goal Achievement

A task can require an object to occupy a region at termination, remain stable over an interval, or reach a destination after a specified event. We evaluate these requirements on the episode’s timestamped trajectory $\tau_{i}$ of states, controls, and task events. For each of the $m_{i}$ goal predicates $G_{ij}$ , the evaluator assigns progress $p_{ij}(\tau_{i})\in[0,1]$ . Each item earns fractional credit where the task defines it and binary credit otherwise. We compute the Interactive Score as the mean credit across items, scaled to 100,

[Tableau] $$I_{i}(\tau_{i})=\frac{100}{m_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}p_{ij}(\tau_{i}).$$ (2)

A task with an indivisible goal has one item. A required completion declaration is itself a goal predicate. The binary physical success indicator checks all goal predicates and constraints together,

[Tableau] $$S_{i}(\tau_{i})=\prod_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\mathbf{1}[G_{ij}(\tau_{i})]\,\prod_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}\mathbf{1}[C(\tau_{i})].$$ (3)

In T38, the VLM surveys the scene before learning which previously observed objects to deliver. Four completion items record whether it finished the survey, reached the delayed request, selected the requested original objects, and delivered all of them. Completing the first two items alone yields $I_{i}=50$ and $S_{i}=0$ . These items locate the remaining work at the transition from observing the scene to acting on the remembered identities.

Physical Agency Profiles. For each primary dimension $d$ , we report mean Interactive Score and physical success rate over its evaluated tasks $\mathcal{T}_{d}$ . Let $\bar{I}_{i}$ and $\bar{S}_{i}$ be the episode means for task $i$ . The pair for $d$ is

[Tableau] $$(I_{d},S_{d})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{d}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_{d}}(\bar{I}_{i},\bar{S}_{i}).$$ (4)

These six pairs form the system’s Physical Agency Profile . Track and overall scores also weight tasks equally. The profile shows where the system makes progress and how often it reaches the full goal.

Terminal question answering. For VLMs, we pair the physical outcome with what the model can report about the same episode. The questions appear in the public instruction. After interaction ends, we freeze the physical result and ask the VLM to answer using the initial overview, terminal observations, and retained public memory. The 73 questions cover the initial scene (32), final state (27), and interaction history or changes across phases (14). We match answers to the task’s reference values under its answer schema, allowing unordered responses where specified. The percentage correct per task, averaged across tasks, gives a separate measure of scene understanding, outcome recognition, and memory.


### 4 Experiments


#### 4.1 Evaluation Protocol

We give 13 embodied AI systems the same physical goals and score what each achieves through its own perception and control. Eight VLMs direct R1 through WorldHarness. Three VLA and two WAM policies act through their native controllers on PandaOmron or R1Pro. Each VLM runs all 60 tasks. Each continuous-control policy runs 59 tasks, excluding T52, whose containment condition already holds at initialization. With one episode per system and task, this gives 775 episodes at seed 0. We also repeat the full suite with GPT-6.1-Sol at seed 1.

Within each interface family, systems have the same task-specific interaction budgets, ranging from 10 to 342 VLM decisions or 1,000 to 40,000 control steps. Six VLMs select parameterized motions and can revisit earlier images and save notes. Gemini and Jev select a command and a repetition count from current views and public interaction records (Appendix C.2 ). We report mean Interactive Score ( $I$ , out of 100) and physical success rate ( $S$ , in percent), weighting tasks equally. Terminal QA probes the VLM’s account of the same episode after its physical outcome is fixed.

#### 4.4 Decisions, Feedback, and Termination

WorldHarness records 32,455 decisions across 480 VLM episodes. For Qwen, 3,127 of 4,440 decisions yield no valid command. Jev completes 95.26% of the motions it initiates, yet succeeds only on T52, where the goal already holds at the start. Sol completes 70.74% of its motion commands and achieves nine goals.

Which decisions does the interface leave to the agent? With object-level grasp and placement skills, Sol must still bring the target within reach. Of its 52 named grasp attempts, 30 target objects outside the workspace. On T24, Sol completes the task with motion commands and scores zero with the skills available.

Even after a system resumes moving, the original obstacle can persist. Across 6,241 three-decision windows following a stopped command, 88.75% contain a completed command and 56.55% include a recurrence of the original stop status. Both occur in 47.54% of windows (Appendix D.3 ). In T21, Sol encounters an arm joint limit after placing the first box. It goes on to place the second box and release both hands, leaving an upright, stationary tower. In the kitchen delivery, Astra clears the work area, delivers the apple, then sees that the obstruction has returned. It puts the obstruction back in storage before declaring completion (Fig. 4 and Appendix D.4 ).

On T22, Sol completes placement with full execution feedback and declares completion at decision 41. With status, failure reasons, and recovery hints hidden, placement is still unverified at the 52-decision limit. Both runs answer the terminal question correctly.

The agent also chooses when it has done enough. Of 279 episodes that end with a final DONE , 41 satisfy the physical goal, 42 earn credit with a goal or constraint unmet, and 196 earn zero. Of the 201 episodes that exhaust the budget, 31 earn partial credit and 170 earn zero (Appendix D.5 ). ### 5 Conclusion

WorldLeague evaluates physical agency across 60 tasks in exploration, physical transformation, and adaptation. Across 13 models, the highest Interactive Score is 28.58 out of 100. Every VLM scores higher on auxiliary QA than on physical operation, and completed commands often leave physical goals unmet. Of the 347 VLM episodes that receive full credit for terminal QA, 249 (71.8%) receive zero operation credit for the physical task. WorldHarness traces these outcomes to the agent’s choice of targets and motion parameters and to its subsequent actions. The same predicates that determine the capability profile show which requirements remain unmet in each trajectory, from stable placement after release to finding and delivering the right object after a scene change. The world continues to evolve while a model reasons, making inference latency relevant to the evaluation of physical interaction.

Future Work. We could adapt JEPA-based planners such as V-JEPA 2-AC [ 2 ] to WorldLeague to test whether action-conditioned latent predictions help with geometric reconfiguration and recovery from disturbances. For these experiments, we would use the generator in Appendix F to sample a template $i$ with fixed probability $w_{i}$ and then draw an instance $z$ from $p_{i}$ , yielding $p(z,i)=w_{i}p_{i}(z)$ . We would group instances by scene, object-role assignment, layout, and events before assigning groups to splits, retaining the 60 fixed evaluation instances as the test set. The same generator could supply a screened pool for online reinforcement learning. At each reset, the environment would sample an instance from the pool, initialize the objects and robot pose, clear the episode history, and install the task events. The policy would receive the public goal, observations, and feedback through the interaction interface (Sec. 3.3 ), and physical progress and terminal success could serve as reward signals.

#### A.3 Reading the Six Capability Dimensions

Table 1 marks a dimension as fully covered when the benchmark scores its demands together. Partial coverage means that an explicit test measures one component. The Execution column records who controls the motions. Feedback-guided robot motion earns a full mark, delegated skills, symbolic commands, and exploration for QA earn a partial mark, and passive prediction has no execution mark.

WorldSeek. Navigation links route or region choices to physical arrival. Arrival checks, symbolic traversal, and route questions each test a component. Goal grounding links identification of the intended target to action that reveals decisive evidence [ 17 ] . Selecting a visible referent tests the identification component.

WorldShape. Geometric reconfiguration couples choices about shape, clearance, or orientation to an achieved arrangement. Relational placement and insertion alignment test individual components. Physical reasoning links a prediction about an action’s consequences to an intervention, such as stabilizing a support or choosing a launch. Contact predictions and material-outcome checks each test a component.

WorldShift. Temporal memory requires the agent to use earlier physical evidence when a changed scene calls for a decision. Dialogue recall and reproduction of a demonstrated sequence each test memory. Constraint management couples task rules or resource limits with recovery under disturbance. Enforcing a rule or testing recovery in isolation covers one component.


#### A.4 Tasks Behind the Comparison

Exploration and memory. AgentBench [ 30 ] tests symbolic household traversal and target selection in ALFWorld [ 46 ] , and decisions under enforced rules in card games. ESI-Bench [ 15 ] asks agents to uncover occluded targets and answer questions about routes, arrangements, physical outcomes, and earlier states. LoCoMo [ 33 ] tests temporal and multi-hop recall over conversations. LongMemEval [ 60 ] tests recall across sessions, temporal reasoning, and knowledge updates. MeMoREval [ 54 ] carries earlier evidence into a physical request. In spatial_shelf_vacate , a figurine’s former shelf location identifies the book to retrieve, and the agent uses complete skills to execute its plan.

Prediction and action. Physion [ 3 ] tests whether a model can predict object contact from the opening frames. CoPhyBench tests physical reasoning from an observed video segment [ 58 ] . Video world models can use spatial memory to predict future frames more efficiently [ 43 ] . World-action models use predictions of future states to guide action selection [ 44 ] . Under visual perturbations, a model can predict a plausible outcome yet execute an action that fails [ 26 ] . DreamZero [ 63 ] executes action chunks on AgiBot and DROID and observes the result before acting again. Its tasks include object matching, orientation, and shape-matched insertion, with partial credit on DROID.

Household activities. ALFRED [ 45 ] combines egocentric navigation and target masks with simulator actions for pickup, placement, and object-state changes. BEHAVIOR-1K [ 21 ] scores spatial, cloth, and material states through BDDL goals [ 48 ] . RL-VMC issues joint commands and RL-Prim selects skills. In LIBERO [ 29 ] , spatial descriptions identify the target and On / In predicates score placement. RoboCasa365 [ 37 ] checks base position and heading in navigation, size in nesting, and pan tilt and final containment in TiltPan .

Manipulation guided by evidence. RoboDojo [ 8 ] scores target identity, insertion depth and alignment, transferred fluid volume, and reproduction of a sorting sequence. In RoboQuest [ 41 ] , Stamps scores the pattern produced by contact and WobblyStand requires selecting shims that support a ball. Hidden targets reward inspection and protected objects constrain manipulation. BEHAVIOR-Spatial [ 7 ] checks arrival near a target, referent selection, and spatial relations such as the side of a knife or the height of a shelf placement.

Dynamics and recovery. IMBench [ 35 ] pairs occluder removal, insertion, and balancing with forced-slip and stack-collapse tests. Its Stage-3 VLM uses MoveEEF and gripper commands with updated observations. RobotAgentBench [ 34 ] scores navigation, contextual target selection, placement, and cloth coverage under contact budgets and interference. Its C/C/C agent composes motion functions and checks their outcomes. The C/L/– planner delegates subtasks to a learned executor. Embodied Playground [ 6 ] checks puzzle configurations, target impacts, legal moves, ammunition, and target order in manipulation and launch tasks.

### Appendix F Scene Generation

A new layout changes where the robot must go and what it can reach. To preserve the task, the generator holds its goal, constraints, and physical predicates fixed and varies the scene, objects, starting pose, or events. For example, a narrower slot may require a new approach, but the reoriented board must still fit.


#### F.1 Which Instances Preserve the Goal?

For a task template $i$ in dimension $d$ , let $z=(e,o,x,r,\eta)$ contain the scene, selected objects, object poses, robot starting pose, and task-event parameters. The generator draws proposals from $q_{i}$ . Initialization conditions $V_{i}(z)$ , dimension-specific challenge conditions $K_{d}(z)$ , and manual review $H_{i}(z)$ define which proposals are accepted. The resulting conditional distribution is

[Tableau] $$p_{i}(z)=\frac{q_{i}(z)\,V_{i}(z)\,K_{d}(z)\,H_{i}(z)}{Z_{i}},$$ (5)

where the three acceptance functions take values in $\{0,1\}$ and $Z_{i}>0$ is the normalizing constant. The interface exposes scene choice, object sizes, layout extent, robot-start regions, event parameters, and seed. Sampling takes a target instance count and a finite candidate budget.

The initialization conditions cover role-compatible objects, object dimensions, overlap, support placement, and navigation and manipulation access. The dimension conditions preserve each template’s principal challenge. Manual review checks instruction clarity, feasible interactions, the assigned capability, and unintended shortcuts. The review outcome is $H_{i}(z)$ .


#### F.2 WorldSeek Routes and Evidence

Spatial navigation. Represent traversable regions by a graph $G_{z}=(V_{z},E_{z})$ , with edges for robot-accessible passages. Sample a starting region, goal regions, visit order, and object placements within those regions. Layout variation changes obstacle positions and route lengths while retaining a path satisfying the required visit sequence. For a route $\gamma$ , let $L(\gamma)$ denote the ordered regions visited and let $\sigma_{i}$ be the required sequence. A candidate satisfies the route condition when

[Tableau] $$\exists\gamma\subseteq G_{z}:\quad\sigma_{i}\preceq L(\gamma),$$ (6)

where $\preceq$ denotes an ordered subsequence. A connectivity template preserves the adjacency relation queried by its goal. A collection template additionally preserves access to every required object and destination. The template varies route length, branching, and goal-region separation. Manual screening checks that the passage geometry admits the required route and that the task requires the intended region or route reasoning.

Goal grounding. Sample a target, distractors, task cues, and their spatial arrangement. Occluders, inspection surfaces, or reflectors are placed according to the template. Let $C(h)$ be the set of target candidates consistent with the task cues observed in history $h$ . For templates requiring disambiguation, construct an initial history $h_{0}$ and an accessible observation sequence $a_{1:k}$ such that

[Tableau] $$|C(h_{0})|>1,\qquad C(h_{0},a_{1:k},o_{1:k})=\{o^{\star}\},$$ (7)

where $o^{\star}$ is the intended target. For direct grounding templates, the condition instead requires an observable cue that uniquely identifies the target. Sample distractor similarity, occlusion, and cue-viewpoint separation within these conditions. Manual screening checks cue visibility, the correspondence between the cue and the real object, and access to the required observation and retrieval actions.


#### F.3 WorldShape Geometry and Physical Outcomes

Geometric reconfiguration. Sample object dimensions and poses jointly with apertures, receiving regions, or support geometry. Let $O$ denote an object’s occupied volume, $R$ its orientation, and $b(O,R)$ its projected extents. For an orientation-sensitive opening with usable dimensions $a$ , choose an initial orientation $R_{0}$ and a feasible orientation $R^{\star}$ satisfying

[Tableau] $$b(O,R^{\star})+m\mathbf{1}\leq a,\qquad b(O,R_{0})+m\mathbf{1}\nleq a,$$ (8)

with clearance margin $m>0$ . The instance conditions also require a collision-free approach and insertion path. Arrangement and sorting templates sample objects whose dimensions or relative poses determine distinct goal configurations. Sampling varies clearances, orientation changes, and competing placements. Review checks access, graspability, and the required reconfiguration. The T52 control template preserves its initially satisfied containment goal.

Physical reasoning. Sample object shapes, support arrangements, and the physical parameters permitted by the template. Represent a candidate action sequence by $u$ and its predicted outcome under the template’s physical model by $Y_{z}(u)$ . Construct a feasible sequence $u^{+}$ and a competing sequence $u^{-}$ with

[Tableau] $$G_{i}(Y_{z}(u^{+}))=1,\qquad G_{i}(Y_{z}(u^{-}))=0,$$ (9)

where $G_{i}$ denotes satisfaction of the template’s physical goal. In stacking tasks, contact geometry and the projected center of mass relative to the support region describe the stability challenge. Ramp-trial templates vary candidate shapes and release conditions while holding the comparison conditions fixed. Review uses simulator trajectories to examine contact, release, settling, and the physical outcomes of different actions.


#### F.4 WorldShift Memory and Recovery

Temporal memory. Sample object identities, an observation phase, intervening events, and a later instruction that depends on the observed history. Events can move, exchange, remove, or replace objects within the template’s rules. For a history-dependent identity task, construct two possible histories $h$ and $h^{\prime}$ with the same current task-relevant observation and different required targets, so that

[Tableau] $$o_{t}(h)=o_{t}(h^{\prime}),\qquad y_{i}(h)\neq y_{i}(h^{\prime}),$$ (10)

where $y_{i}$ denotes the correct target or response. Event-tracking templates preserve the required order of observations and changes. Sampling varies the number of relevant objects, intervening changes, and delay. Manual screening checks that earlier observations reveal the needed information, object identity remains coherent through the event sequence, and the later goal requires the intended historical information.

Constraint management. Sample resource quantities, compatible replacements, protected objects, delivery regions, and disturbances. For state $s$ , define the set of feasible plans

[Tableau] $$\Pi_{i}(s)=\{\pi:G_{i}(\tau_{s,\pi})=1,\ C_{i}(\tau_{s,\pi})=1\},$$ (11)

where $G_{i}$ and $C_{i}$ aggregate the goal and constraint predicates and $\tau_{s,\pi}$ is the resulting trajectory. A recovery template samples a disturbance $\delta$ such that $\Pi_{i}(\delta(s))$ is nonempty and the previous plan requires a new continuation. Resource-allocation templates preserve at least one feasible assignment while making resource or compatibility constraints consequential. Manual screening checks disturbance observability, access to recovery actions, and compliance with the declared constraints.
