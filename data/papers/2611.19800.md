### 1. Introduction

Scaling Transformer [ 1 ] language models involves deciding both how much parameter capacity to allocate and how to organize it [ 2 , 3 ] . Depth and width are two conventional architectural choices: stacking more blocks in sequence or enlarging the transformations within each block [ 4 , 5 , 6 ] . Recent advances in LLM architectures have substantially expanded the design space within this template through innovations in attention mechanisms [ 7 , 8 ] , sparse expert computation [ 9 ] , and residual connections [ 10 , 11 , 12 ] , while the sequential composition of full blocks remains a common foundation. In this work, we explore branching , the parallel composition of full Transformer blocks, as a complementary way to organize and scale model parameters, and ask:

Can Transformers scale more effectively and efficiently through parallel compositions of full blocks?

To this end, we introduce the Multi-Branch Transformer ( MBT ), which enables parallel computation at the granularity of full Transformer blocks (Figure 1 (a)). An MBT processes the input through shared initial layers (the prelude 1 1 We use the terminology prelude and coda following Geiping et al. [13] . ) , splits computation across $K$ independently parameterized branches that execute concurrently, fuses their outputs, and passes the result through shared final layers (the coda ). The key idea is to preserve sequential composition within each branch while allowing subsequent shared layers to build on the results of multiple transformation paths. In comparison, related work on parallel Transformer architectures explores different design axes (Figure 2 ): concurrency within individual blocks [ 14 ] , allocation of width and depth [ 4 , 5 , 6 ] , or alternative parallel computation paths [ 15 ] . Systems approaches such as tensor and pipeline parallelism [ 16 ] are orthogonal to and compatible with this architectural design.

Relative to a standard Transformer, an MBT can be defined in three ways at comparable compute: Wide-Shallow MBTs preserve the baseline’s block width and distribute its blocks across branches; Narrow-Deep MBTs preserve the baseline’s depth along each branch using narrower blocks; and Balanced MBTs interpolate between these choices by jointly adjusting branch width and depth.

To further exploit the parallel branch structure of MBTs, we propose Joint-KV , which constructs a shared key-value (KV) cache from all branch representations at each depth while retaining separate queries for each branch (Figure 4 ). For $K$ branches, this reduces cache storage in the branch region to $1/K$ of Independent-KV at fixed branch depth and KV dimensions. We combine Joint-KV with Balanced MBTs as our final model recipes (Figure 1 (b)). To translate our architectural design into practical inference gains, we develop an inference engine based on nano-vLLM [ 17 , 18 ] that exploits the parallel branches and shared KV caches through branch grouping and head folding, reducing sequential execution steps and redundant KV reads during decoding.

Extensive experiments validate the effectiveness and efficiency of MBT. First, branching improves language modeling performance . Across a scaling ladder of mixture-of-experts models (0.8B-2.1B total parameters), our scaling laws first indicate that the best 2-branch Wide-Shallow MBT matches a standard Transformer trained with 1.23 $\times$ as much compute, while allocating approximately 79% of blocks to parallel branches yields performance on par with the standard Transformer. Balanced MBTs achieve the strongest performance among the studied allocations and maintain comparable quality as the number of branches increases. Second, Joint-KV MBTs, together with our inference engine, substantially improve inference speed while retaining strong modeling performance. In particular, 2-, 3-, and 4-branch Joint-KV MBTs match standard Transformers trained with 1.16–1.26 $\times$ as much training body compute, while reducing KV cache size by 19–34% . On a single H200 GPU, our models achieve up to 51.4% higher decoding throughput than the standard Transformer with 64K-token inputs and 8 concurrent requests.

Extensive ablation studies spanning five scaling sweeps further demonstrate that the benefits of branching extend beyond conventional adjustments to model width and depth. At matched KV-cache capacity, sharing KV across branches (Joint-KV) achieves better language modeling performance than keeping a private KV cache in each branch, highlighting the benefit of exploiting MBT’s branch structure for improving long-context inference efficiency. We also find that MBTs are robust to the choice of fusion function and perform best when branches are placed in the middle layers. Together, these findings highlight the organization of full Transformer blocks as a promising architectural dimension for improving both language modeling quality and inference efficiency.

### 2. Multi-Branch Transformer

An MBT replaces part of a serial Transformer stack with independently parameterized branches (Figure 1 (a)). Shared blocks precede the branches and follow their fusion; we call these stacks the prelude and coda , respectively.


#### 2.1 Architecture

Let $x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{T})$ be an input token sequence and $H\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d}$ its hidden states at a block’s input, where $d$ is the residual width. Each block $\mathcal{T}_{\ell}$ applies causal attention followed by a mixture-of-experts (MoE) feed-forward module [ 19 ] :

[Tableau] $$H^{\prime}=H+\operatorname{Attn}_{\ell}(H),\qquad\mathcal{T}_{\ell}(H)=H^{\prime}+\operatorname{MoE}_{\ell}(H^{\prime}).$$ (1)

Here $H^{\prime}$ is the hidden state after the attention residual update; the blocks’ pre-normalization is omitted for clarity. A standard Transformer embeds the tokens and composes $L$ blocks before decoding:

[Tableau] $$X=\operatorname{Emb}(x),\qquad\widehat{Y}=\operatorname{Dec}\bigl((\mathcal{T}_{L}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{T}_{1})(X)\bigr).$$ (2)

The decoder $\operatorname{Dec}$ applies final normalization, the LM head, and softmax; $\widehat{Y}_{t}$ is the predicted distribution of $x_{t+1}$ .

An MBT replaces the serial middle stack with $K\geq 2$ independently parameterized branch stacks $\mathcal{G}_{i}$ . The prelude $\mathcal{P}$ , fusion $\mathcal{F}$ , and coda $\mathcal{C}$ connect them:

[Tableau] $$H=\mathcal{P}(X),\quad Z_{i}=\mathcal{G}_{i}(H),\quad\widehat{Y}=\operatorname{Dec}\bigl(\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{F}(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{K}))\bigr).$$ (3)

Here $H$ is the shared prelude output, and $\mathcal{G}_{i}$ is a stack of $L_{i}$ blocks ( $i=1,\ldots,K$ ). When widths differ, $\mathcal{P}$ includes a shared input projection to the branch width.

Fusion. At each token position, we normalize the branch outputs, concatenate them along the hidden dimension, and project the result back to the shared width $d$ :

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{F}(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{K})=\operatorname{Concat}\bigl(\mathcal{N}_{1}(Z_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{N}_{K}(Z_{K})\bigr)W_{F}.$$ (4)

Each $\mathcal{N}_{i}$ is an RMSNorm, and $W_{F}$ is a bias-free linear map. The projected output goes directly to the coda , with no separate branch residual. We explore more expressive fusion in Section 4.3 , but find Eq. 4 to be sufficient.

#### 2.2 Width–Depth Allocation

Let $P$ and $C$ denote the numbers of prelude and coda blocks ( $L_{\rm in}$ and $L_{\rm out}$ in Figure 1 (a)), and $L_{i}$ denote the number of blocks in branch $i$ . The total block count $L_{\mathrm{physical}}$ and depth $D$ of an MBT are

[Tableau] $$L_{\mathrm{physical}}=P+\sum_{i=1}^{K}L_{i}+C,\qquad D=P+\max_{i=1,\ldots,K}L_{i}+C.$$ (5)

With the shared prelude and coda fixed, we compare three branch width–depth allocations under matched training compute:

- • Wide–Shallow: preserve the original block width and redistribute the middle blocks across parallel branches.

- • Narrow–Deep: preserve the original sequential depth along each branch while reducing block width.

- • Balanced: use intermediate branch width and depth between these two endpoints.

For Narrow–Deep and Balanced, we adjust the residual width and MoE dimensions within each branch to match the training FLOPs of the Standard reference. All other configurations ( e.g., total expert, top- $k$ ) remain unchanged.

Figure 3 illustrates these variants using a six-block Transformer, $A\to B\to C\to D\to E\to F$ . Here, $A$ and $F$ serve as the shared prelude and coda , respectively, while the middle stack is reorganized into two parallel branches. Wide–Shallow places two original-width blocks in each branch; Narrow–Deep uses four narrower blocks per branch; and Balanced uses three blocks per branch with an intermediate width.

#### 2.3 KV-Cache Sharing and Infrastructure Design

In the default configuration, which we call Independent-KV, each block in each branch constructs keys and values from its own hidden states and maintains a separate KV cache, so more branches result in more caches. To improve the long-context efficiency of MBTs, we introduce Joint-KV, which shares the KV cache across branches per depth, and an inference engine that turns the parallel branches and the shared caches into faster decoding. Joint-KV changes the model and is used in training. The engine changes only how the model is executed.

Joint-KV. In Joint-KV, all branches share one KV cache at each depth, constructed jointly from their pre-attention inputs, while queries remain local to each branch. Formally, let $\bar{S}_{i,\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d_{b}}$ denote the RMS-normalized input to branch $i$ at depth $\ell$ . The two KV-cache constructions are

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{KV}^{\rm ind}_{i,\ell}=\bar{S}_{i,\ell}W^{\rm ind}_{i,\ell},\qquad\mathrm{KV}^{\rm shared}_{\ell}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}[\bar{S}_{1,\ell};\ldots;\bar{S}_{K,\ell}]W^{\rm shared}_{\ell}.$$ (6)

Let $d_{b}$ denote the branch width, $h_{\mathrm{KV}}$ the number of KV heads, and $d_{h}$ the head dimension. At each depth, Independent-KV uses $K$ learned projection matrices $W^{\rm ind}_{i,\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{b}\times(2h_{\mathrm{KV}}d_{h})}$ , whereas Joint-KV uses a single $W^{\rm shared}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{(Kd_{b})\times(2h_{\mathrm{KV}}d_{h})}$ . The factor $2$ accounts for keys and values. Both constructions therefore have the same KV-projection parameter count and matrix-multiplication FLOPs. $[\cdot;\cdot]$ concatenates along the hidden dimension. Subsequent key normalization and RoPE are omitted for clarity. Projection weights and caches remain depth-specific: Joint-KV shares only across branches at the same depth, requiring synchronization before attention.

Cache Size. At fixed branch depth and KV dimensions, the Joint-KV cache in the branch region is $1/K$ that of Independent-KV, so the savings increase with the branch count.

Serving. Our inference engine, built on nano-vLLM [ 18 ] , uses the same underlying kernel backend and cache manager for MBTs and Standard. A decode step processes the prelude , branch region, and coda in order. We use two additional execution mechanisms, branch grouping and head folding, to run the branches together and reduce repeated KV reads (Figure 4 ).

Branch Grouping. Our engine groups blocks at the same depth across branches into a batched execution stage. With Joint-KV, the engine first constructs the shared KV cache from the branch input representations, then computes the branch-specific attention and MoE outputs together. Compared with executing every block sequentially, branch grouping reduces the number of sequential block stages from $L_{\mathrm{physical}}$ to $D=P+\max_{i}L_{i}+C$ (Eq. 5 ). For example, a 4-branch W3 Balanced MBT contains 24 physical blocks but requires only 12 sequential block stages with branch grouping, compared with 16 for Standard.

Head Folding. Sharing KV caches reduces storage, but separate attention calls for different branches can still reload the same KV data. Head folding folds the branch dimension into the query-head dimension, grouping queries by their shared KV head so that all branches use a single grouped-query attention (GQA) call [ 20 ] . This allows the attention kernel to reuse shared KV data across branches, reducing redundant memory traffic. Each query head still computes its own softmax over the same keys and values, preserving the attention function itself.

Distributed Execution. MBTs can be distributed through tensor parallelism or branch parallelism. With Joint-KV, these layouts differ in shared KV-cache construction. With tensor parallelism, devices replicate branch hidden states while sharding projection weights and attention heads. Each device constructs its assigned shared KV heads locally, without an additional collective. Head folding also remains local when the corresponding query heads from all branches are placed on the same device. Alternatively, branches can run on different devices. Each device computes its branch’s contribution to the shared KV projection, and an all-reduce combines these contributions before attention at each depth. The all-reduce operates on a tensor of width $2h_{\mathrm{KV}}d_{h}$ , avoiding an all-gather of branch representations of total width $Kd_{b}$ . At fixed KV dimensions, its per-token tensor size is independent of the branch count, although communication cost depends on the group size and topology. This layout distributes branch weights and computation across devices but requires synchronization at every branch depth. This layout may be useful when branch sharding is needed to fit the model in memory and fast interconnects are available.


### 3. Experiments


#### 3.1 Scaling Ladder Setup

We pretrain standard Transformers and MBTs at four scales, W1–W4, with standard models ranging from 0.80B to 2.07B total parameters (139M–394M active-body parameters). All models use FineWeb-Edu [ 21 ] , the Llama 3 tokenizer [ 22 ] , and a context length of 4,096 tokens. Our scaling ladder is comparable in scale to that of Attention Residuals [ 12 ] , a recent industrial technical report. Our training recipes, experimental protocols, and backbone components also draw on recent technical reports [ 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 ] . At each scale, Standard and MBT models share a training-token budget of approximately 200 tokens per parameter (TPP) [ 23 ] , using active-body parameter count and excluding embeddings and the LM head. A brief overview of our ladder is provided in Table 1 .

Our backbone uses pre-RMSNorm [ 27 ] blocks with full causal GQA [ 20 ] , a 2:1 query-to-KV head ratio, and a head dimension of 64. We apply headwise Q/K normalization, RoPE [ 28 ] , and sigmoid gating of attention outputs [ 29 ] . Each block contains a latent MoE [ 25 ] with 64 SwiGLU experts and top-8 routing. Quantile Balancing [ 26 ] balances expert loads without an auxiliary balancing loss.

We train in BF16 using AdamW [ 30 ] , with $\beta=(0.9,0.95)$ , weight decay of 0.1, and gradient clipping at 1.0. The learning rate warms up linearly over the first 1% of training, then follows a cosine schedule to one tenth of its peak. Our primary peak-learning-rate candidates are $\{1,3,5,7,10,30\}\times 10^{-4}$ . We extensively tune the learning rate for the standard Transformer at each scale to establish competitive baselines. For MBTs, we tune the learning rate on a single configuration and reuse the selected value across the remaining settings. Global batches contain 128 sequences for W1/W2 and 256 for W3/W4. Table 7 in Appendix A.1 provides the full experimental details.

#### 3.2 Scaling Transformers with Parallel Branches

We first study how allocating blocks, width, and depth across branches affects language-model quality. In this subsection, the experiments use independent-KV caches in each branch.


##### 3.2.1 Scaling Laws of 2-branch MBTs

Research Question. What fraction of a Transformer’s blocks can be placed in two parallel branches while preserving or improving model quality?

Setup. Using Wide–Shallow MBTs (where total training FLOPs and parameters remain almost identical to the standard reference model), we vary the fraction of physical blocks in two equal-depth branches, $\rho=(\sum_{i=1}^{2}L_{i})/L$ , from zero (Standard) to one (fully branched). Within each scale, we fix the backbone width, total physical block count, and training tokens, and split the remaining blocks equally between the prelude and coda ( $P=C$ ). Branch outputs are fused by normalized concatenation followed by a linear projection. We compare final validation loss across allocations (Figure 5 ), then select the lowest-loss allocation at each scale to study body-compute scaling and zero-shot downstream performance (Figure 6 ). Table 1 lists these selected allocations.

Result and Interpretation. All four scales exhibit a highly consistent U-shaped relationship between parallel block ratio and validation loss. The best tested allocations cluster around $1/3$ of the blocks (28.6–37.5%), reducing loss by 0.025–0.031 relative to Standard. Larger parallel regions also retain Standard-level quality: the largest measured ratios no worse than Standard are 66.7%, 71.4%, 75.0%, and 77.8% for W1–W4.

The sweep adds one block to each branch at a time, giving a discrete ratio increment of $2/L$ ; exact equality with Standard need not fall on a sampled allocation. We therefore linearly interpolate between neighboring observations to estimate the equality points. All four crossings fall within 78–80%. The consistent curve shape, minima near $1/3$ , and tightly clustered crossings suggest a stable block-allocation pattern across model scales.

The fitted scaling curves indicate an approximately 1.23 $\times$ body-compute advantage for the selected two-branch MBTs over Standard at matched validation loss (Figure 6 (a)). The downstream gains are also consistent across tasks. Averaged over W1–W4, MBT outperforms Standard on all nine benchmarks, raising overall mean zero-shot accuracy from 54.23% to 56.03% (Figure 6 (b)).

##### 3.2.2 Scaling Laws of multi-branch MBTs

Research Question. Do the language modeling gains persist beyond two branches, and how do they depend on the allocation of width and depth?

Setup. We compare MBTs with 2, 3, and 4 branches using the Wide–Shallow and Balanced variants defined in Figure 3 , matching training compute at each scale. 2 2 Our 2-branch Narrow–Deep results suggest benefits from retaining depth as models scale (Section 4.1 ). With 3 or 4 branches, however, preserving full depth at fixed compute requires much smaller widths and is accompanied by poorer quality, especially with 4 branches (Appendix A.4.1 ). We therefore study Balanced MBTs, trading some depth for greater width within the same compute budget. Relative to the standard model at each scale, we retain half of the blocks in the shared prelude and coda , keeping their block configurations unchanged, and reorganize the middle region into parallel branches. Wide–Shallow models place half of the original blocks in this region, preserving their configurations and distributing them as evenly as possible across branches. Branch depths may differ when this block count is not divisible by the number of branches. Balanced models instead adjust branch widths and depths to match the reference training body compute; their total physical block count and fraction of blocks in branches can therefore differ. Figure 9 shows the results. Averaged over the four scales, the 4-branch Joint-KV MBT decodes faster than Standard in every setting. Its speedup ranges from $1.12\times$ with a single 4K-token request to $1.50\times$ with 16 concurrent 128K-token requests. It is at least $1.08\times$ faster at every scale and setting. Averaged over scales, two branches range from $1.01\times$ to $1.32\times$ . Four branches outperform two at every scale and in every setting, since they need fewer serial steps and read fewer KV bytes. These speedups come without a quality cost: at every scale, both models reach lower validation loss than Standard (Table 11 ).

Sources of the Speedup. The time of a decode step has two parts. The first is a fixed cost for executing the serial blocks. It depends on neither the number of requests nor the input length. Branch grouping runs the blocks of all branches at the same depth as one step, so an MBT pays this cost for fewer blocks. The depth of Standard divided by that of the MBT is the depth ratio $r_{\mathrm{d}}$ (Table 15 ). The second part is the time to load data from memory and compute on it, which grows with the concurrency and the context length. For the models we test, this part is bound by memory traffic. With Joint-KV and head folding, each depth of the branch region reads its shared KV cache once. An MBT therefore reads only $1-s$ of the KV bytes of Standard, where $s$ is the KV saving of Table 11 . We denote by $F$ the fixed cost and by $M$ the KV-read time of Standard. The speedup is then approximately

[Tableau] $$S\approx\frac{F+M}{F/r_{\mathrm{d}}+(1-s)\,M}.$$ (7)

Our settings span 4K to 128K tokens and 1 to 64 requests. With short inputs and low concurrency, $M$ is small compared with $F$ , and the speedup comes mainly from the depth ratio. As the context length grows, KV reads take most of each step, and the speedup approaches the bound $1/(1-s)$ . Appendix B.3 gives the profiles and a more detailed analysis. Appendix B.4 measures the contribution of each engine mechanism.

#### 4.1 Width and Depth Ablations

Ablation Goal. We test whether the gains of branching can be explained by a shallower, wider network, and whether they persist when Standard’s depth is preserved.

Setup. We retain Standard and 2-branch Wide–Shallow MBT as fixed references at each scale, with roughly half the blocks in parallel branches in the latter. We add three controls. ### 5. Related Work

Parallelism in large language models. Architectural work on language models explores concurrency within individual blocks, as in PaLM [ 14 ] , width–depth trade-offs in sequential stacks [ 4 , 5 , 6 ] , and parallel passes through a shared backbone with learned prefixes, as in ParScale [ 15 ] . From a systems perspective, parallelism supports efficient execution across devices: tensor and pipeline parallelism distribute model computation [ 16 ] , and track parallelism uses narrower stacks with periodic synchronization to reduce communication overhead [ 37 ] . Our approach explores an architectural design axis orthogonal to those studied in prior work on parallel language models, while remaining compatible with existing systems approaches. More broadly, multi-branch architectures have a long history in computer vision, with prominent examples including Inception [ 38 ] , ResNeXt [ 39 ] , FractalNet [ 40 ] , and HRNet [ 41 ] . Earlier NLP work (Pre-LLM era) explored parallel branches within attention modules [ 42 , 43 ] and encoder backbones [ 44 ] . However, parallel composition of full Transformer blocks remains underexplored as a means of scaling modern autoregressive language models.

Information flow and KV-cache sharing. Recent work improves information flow through expanded residual streams and selective aggregation of representations across depth [ 10 , 11 , 12 , 45 , 46 ] . These approaches enrich interactions within a sequential block stack, although maintaining and mixing additional representations can introduce extra memory traffic. MBT takes a different approach by reorganizing full Transformer blocks into parallel branches and fusing their outputs before subsequent shared layers. Prior work shares keys and values across attention heads [ 47 , 20 ] or across decoder layers, as in Cross-Layer Attention and YOCO [ 48 , 49 ] . Joint-KV shares along a different axis that exists only in multi-branch models: across parallel branches at the same depth, while retaining branch-specific queries. This axis is orthogonal to head and layer sharing (our models already combine it with GQA) and enables head folding to reduce redundant KV reads during long-context decoding. Our results show that the resulting cache savings and inference gains coexist with stronger language modeling performance than standard Transformers.


### 6. Conclusion

We introduced MBTs, which organize full Transformer blocks into parallel branches between shared initial and final layers. Across four MoE model scales, branching improves language-model quality and fitted scaling at comparable training compute. Balanced branch allocations, Joint-KV, and an inference engine that exploits branch structure together enable better modeling quality and faster decoding. These results establish branching as a practical architectural choice alongside width and depth. These findings open several directions for future work:

- • Joint Scaling Laws with Branching. Existing scaling studies guide model and training configurations under a given compute budget [ 3 , 50 ] . Extending this framework to jointly account for branch count, branch depth, and hidden dimensions could identify how best to allocate parameters within that budget. Incorporating inference cost would further guide configurations that balance modeling quality and serving efficiency.

- • MBT + YOCO + Cross-Layer Attention. YOCO enables aggressive cache reduction and early-exit prefill [ 49 ] , while Cross-Layer Attention shares KV across neighboring layers [ 48 ] . Recent work such as HySparse2 combines selective sharing across layers with sparse attention [ 51 ] . Combining these approaches with MBT would enable joint design of KV sharing across branches and layers, potentially improving the trade-off between modeling quality, prefill efficiency, and decoding speed.

- • MBTs for Multimodality. Multimodal Transformers such as MM-DiT already use distinct parameters for different modalities [ 52 ] . MBTs could extend this idea to branches that serve as text, image, or audio backbones, with widths, depths, and block types tailored to each modality. Studying where these branches should interact and fuse could improve the balance between modality specialization and cross-modal information exchange.

#### A.8 Fusion implementation

This subsection gives the implementation details for the fusion ablation in Section 4.3 and Table 6 .

Parameterization. $h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ are normalized branch outputs, with $d=1280$ and auxiliary width $r=128$ . The weights have shapes $W_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ , $A_{i},B_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}$ , and $P\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$ . Both gates take $x=[W_{1}h_{1};W_{2}h_{2}]$ :

[Tableau] $$g=\sigma\!\left(G_{o}\operatorname{SiLU}(G_{i}x)\right),\qquad\beta=\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(G_{o}\operatorname{SiLU}(G_{i}x)\right).$$

Here $G_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times 2d}$ ; $G_{o}$ has $d$ rows for channel competition and 2 rows for token softmax. Softmax operates over branches at each token, without uniform-weight smoothing. All projections are bias-free.

Initialization and computation. The learned branch RMSNorms start with unit gains and use $\epsilon=10^{-5}$ . The concat projection uses normal standard deviation $0.02/\sqrt{32}$ ; auxiliary inner matrices use 0.02. Zero-initialized $P$ and $G_{o}$ make corrections vanish and gates equal, recovering concat for the learned correction and gating variants. We retain the fused concat projection and add corrections to preserve the identical initial BF16 output. Gates and correction accumulation use FP32; inner projections use BF16. Auxiliary work adds 0.96–1.09% body compute. Other settings follow the W3 training recipe.

Projection-free control. The normalized sum uses $z=(h_{1}+h_{2})/\sqrt{2}$ without a fusion matrix. It retains the two learned RMSNorm gains ( $2d=2{,}560$ parameters), so only the aggregation, not the whole module, is parameter-free. Removing the concat projection saves $2d^{2}=3{,}276{,}800$ parameters.


### Appendix B Inference

This appendix describes the inference engine of Section 2.3 , the workloads and measurement protocol of Section 3.3 , the decode speedups of Joint-KV MBTs at all four scales, and an ablation of the engine mechanisms.


#### B.1 Engine implementation

Shared infrastructure. The engine extends nano-vLLM [ 18 ] . Standard and MBTs share the same infrastructure: a paged KV cache [ 17 ] , FlashAttention-3 [ 53 ] for attention, and FlashInfer [ 54 ] for sampling. Each decode step is captured once as a CUDA graph and replayed.

Branch grouping. At each depth of the branch region, the engine stacks the weights of the $K$ branches into one tensor. It also stacks their hidden states into a $K\times T\times d_{b}$ tensor, where $T$ is the number of tokens in the step and $d_{b}$ is the branch width. Each operator of a block then runs once for all branches, including a single attention call (see head folding below). In the MoE layer, the expert indices selected by branch $i$ are offset into a concatenated expert table of all branches. A single MoE kernel call thus serves the tokens of every branch.

Head folding. All models use two query heads per KV head. Head folding reshapes the queries of the $K$ branches from $[KT,2h_{\mathrm{KV}},d_{h}]$ to $[T,2Kh_{\mathrm{KV}},d_{h}]$ . The $2K$ query heads that share one KV head then form one group, so one attention call reads the shared bank once for all branches.

Figure 12 shows what each mechanism changes for the 4-branch W3 Balanced MBT. Branch grouping cuts the serial steps per token from 24 to 12, against 16 for Standard. Joint-KV stores $0.675\times$ the KV cache of Standard, against $1.2\times$ for Independent-KV. Head folding cuts the KV read per decode step from $1.2\times$ to $0.675\times$ of Standard.

#### B.2 Benchmark workloads and protocol

Prompts. All prompts are token sequences under the Llama 3 tokenizer [ 22 ] used in training. They are stored in fixed prompt files, so every model receives identical token ids. The 4K-token prompts are the first 256 consecutive 4096-token windows of the tokenized validation split of FineWeb-Edu [ 21 ] , the corpus used in training. The prompts of 16K, 64K, and 128K tokens come from the official RULER generator [ 31 ] . We use seven of its tasks: five needle-in-a-haystack tasks ( niah_single_2 , niah_single_3 , niah_multikey_1 , niah_multivalue , and niah_multiquery ) and two question-answering tasks ( qa_1 and qa_2 ). Each task has 40 samples per length for 16K and 64K and 20 for 128K, i.e. , 280 and 140 prompts per length. Each sample is truncated from the left to exactly the target length, which keeps the question at the end.

Requests. Requests run in rounds of $N$ . In each round, $N$ requests start together. Each request is prefilled and then decodes exactly 1024 tokens, because an end-of-sequence token does not stop generation. The next round starts when all $N$ requests have finished. The rounds cycle through the first $4N$ prompts of the prompt file, with $N$ prompts per round. Sampling uses no top- $k$ truncation and a fixed seed.

Measurement. Timing starts after the model is loaded, the CUDA graphs are captured, and two warm-up rounds have run. Throughput is then measured in three windows, each lasting at least 60 s and ending at the next round boundary. CUDA events split each round into prefill, which includes the first generated token, and decode. Decode throughput is the number of tokens generated in the decode phase over all $N$ requests, divided by the decode time. We report its mean over the three windows. Over the three windows, the decode throughput of every Joint-KV run in Figure 13 varies by at most 0.33% (coefficient of variation). For 4K-, 16K-, and 64K-token inputs, all models of a scale run on one GPU in the order Standard, Joint-KV with $K{=}4$ , $3$ , and $2$ , and Standard again. For 128K-token inputs, each Joint-KV model runs on its own GPU between two Standard runs.

Per-kernel profiles of decode steps on one H200, with the same engine, explain these results. The profiled speedups agree with Table 16 within 2.5%.

Short inputs at low concurrency. With a single 4K-token request, every MBT reaches 0.84 to 0.91 of the depth ratio (Table 15 ). Most of the step scales with depth: excluding the operations that only MBTs perform, a four-branch MBT takes the depth-scaled step time of Standard within 4%. The rest does not shrink with depth. The embedding, LM head, and sampling take 10 to 13% of a Standard step. The MBT-specific operations, namely the branch input map, the fusion, the bank construction, and head folding, add 100 to 160 $\mu$ s per step with four branches and 130 to 190 $\mu$ s with two. Branch counts with equal depth, such as three and four branches at W4, reach similar speedups. At W1, two branches remove only one block. This saves less than the MBT-specific operations cost, so they are slower than Standard.

Short inputs at higher concurrency. Each layer routes a token to 8 of its 64 experts. More requests therefore make a step read more distinct experts, which accounts for most of the time added from $N{=}1$ to $N{=}8$ at 4K. This cost scales with the total expert parameters, which Balanced MBTs keep close to Standard. With four branches, the ratio is 1.02 at W1, 0.89 at W3, and 1.00 at W4. The measured growth of expert time matches these ratios (1.05, 0.89, and 1.04). The speedup thus dips at W1 and W4. At $N{=}64$ , nearly all experts are read, KV reads grow, and the speedup rises again.

Long inputs at high concurrency. KV reads dominate these steps, so the speedup approaches the bound $1/(1-s)$ . For the four-branch W3 MBT, this bound is $1.48$ . With 32 concurrent 64K-token requests, the MBT spends $0.668\times$ the attention time of Standard and reaches $1.46\times$ . With 16 concurrent 128K-token requests, each step reads the same number of KV tokens as with 32 concurrent 64K-token requests. The MBT then reaches $1.58\times$ , which exceeds the bound. This happens because attention in the full-width layers runs slower at 128K and takes 27% longer than at 64K. Attention over the shared banks does not slow down. All of the KV cache of Standard sits in full-width layers, but only 74% of that of the MBT does. The MBT therefore reads KV at a higher average bandwidth (3.05 against 2.81 TB/s), so its time saving exceeds its byte saving. This comparison is fair. Both models use the same engine, GPU, paged KV cache, and attention kernel and settings. The full-width layers of the MBT slow down as much as those of Standard. The extra gain therefore comes from head folding.

#### B.4 Engine ablation

Setup. We measure how much each engine mechanism of Section 2.3 contributes to the decode speedup. We fix the 4-branch W3 Joint-KV MBT and compare three engines on one GPU, with 4K-, 16K-, and 64K-token inputs. The naive engine runs the branch blocks one at a time, each with its own attention call. The second engine adds branch grouping. The third engine also adds head folding and is the full engine.

> Figure 14: Contribution of each engine mechanism. Decode speedup over Standard of the 4-branch W3 Joint-KV MBT under three engines on one H200 GPU, for $N$ concurrent requests that each generate 1K tokens.

Results. Figure 14 shows the results. The naive engine is slower than Standard in every setting, because it runs more serial blocks and reads the shared KV cache once per branch. Branch grouping shortens the serial path, so it helps most with short inputs and low concurrency. Head folding removes the repeated KV reads, so its gain grows with the input length and concurrency. Only the two mechanisms together make the MBT faster than Standard in every setting. Each mechanism reduces one of the two costs in Eq. 7 .
