### 1 Introduction

Architecture forms the foundation of a large language model (LLM), shaping its performance, scaling behavior, and efficiency during training and inference. When teams such as DeepSeek, Qwen, and Kimi release new open models, they also introduce architectural changes and innovations. Validating these designs involves costly training experiments. However, the experiments and trade-offs behind most models remain inaccessible, leaving the community with little account of which ideas succeeded, which were abandoned, and why. An open and complete record of validation would therefore be as valuable to subsequent research as a new idea itself.

The value of such records also depends on whether researchers discover the documented ideas and build on them. However, much of the attention to new architectures is concentrated on research from industry: our survey 1 1 See Appendix F for survey details. shows that academic work accounts for about a third of the papers but receives less than 10% of the citations (Figure 1 ). Academic ideas need greater visibility and proper validation so that the community can discuss these ideas and draw inspiration from them, and even explore new architectural designs on these bases.

We therefore introduce ArchSpace, a public platform for Recording, Reviewing, and Referencing Architecture Research . Each proposal is recorded with its experiments and reasoning, reviewed by the community to check whether the results support the conclusion, and referenced by later work. To evaluate the architectural changes described in each proposal, the validation framework uses scaling laws [ 13 , 9 ] to compare models across multiple compute budgets, a systematic and standard practice widely used in industry [ 20 , 6 , 5 , 16 , 11 , 17 ] .

As initial references, we provide Qwen3.5 and OLMo3 baselines. Validated architecture innovations can in turn become new baselines for later work. ArchSpace aims to build a community around these contributions, where the research process is openly documented and a wider range of ideas can gain visibility and recognition.


### 2 An Open Community for Architecture Research

ArchSpace aims to build an open community for Recording, Reviewing, and Referencing Architecture Research . Each proposal is recorded with its experiments and reasoning, reviewed by the community to check whether the results support the conclusion, and referenced by later work.

Contributing to ArchSpace begins with an Architecture Proposal issue, which states the proposed change, its rationale, the baseline, and the validation plan (Figure 2 ). Community discussion helps contributors refine the architectural idea and plan a fair comparison with the baseline. Contributors then implement and evaluate the proposed architecture under the validation framework (Section 3 ) and submit their work for public review through a pull request (PR) linked to the issue. The PR links to the implementation, configurations, checkpoints, training logs, evaluation results, and reproduction instructions. It also records failed trials and search choices so readers can trace how the conclusion was reached. Community reviewers then use this record to check whether the implementation matches the proposal, the comparison is reproducible, and the experiments support the conclusion. The verified PR is then merged into ArchSpace for future reference, and the proposed architecture can serve as a new baseline.

Our broader aim is for reviewed PRs to have the same standing as traditional research papers and become the primary way to communicate and cite contributions in ArchSpace. A PR combines a complete account of the contribution with direct links to the resources needed to reproduce and extend the work. Follow-up PRs can link to earlier contributions, so researchers can trace how architectural ideas develop across studies. By bringing these contributions together, ArchSpace aims to provide a shared foundation for subsequent architecture research.


### 3 The ArchSpace Validation Framework

ArchSpace standardizes architecture comparisons through a two-phase validation framework (Figure 2 ). Phase I tests whether the proposed architecture improves the compute–loss trend across the five budgets of the scaling ladder. Phase II trains 1B models on 150B+10B tokens to test whether the gain persists through longer training and to evaluate downstream capabilities directly.

To isolate the effect of the architectural change, the proposed architecture and its baseline use the same training data, tokenizer, and validation data in both phases. The ArchSpace training dataset provides official OLMo 3 mixtures pre-tokenized with the OLMo 3 tokenizer [ 19 ] . 2 2 Tokenizer identifier: allenai/dolma2-tokenizer . In our training dataset, the data for pretraining in both phases come from the Dolma 3 Pool 150B sample , and the data for Phase II long-context mid-training come from the Dolma 3 Longmino Mix (50B) .


#### 3.1 Phase I: Scaling across compute budgets

Architectural gains can vary with the compute budget, so Phase I validates the proposed architecture against its baseline across multiple budgets using fitted scaling laws that estimate each architecture’s lowest achievable loss [ 13 , 9 ] . The proposed architecture is considered an improvement if its compute-optimal loss curve lies on the Pareto frontier. The validation loss $L$ used for fitting is computed as the equal-weight average over the eleven domains of the original OLMo 3 validation set [ 19 ] (Appendix C ). Throughout the report, loss refers to this validation loss.

Let $M$ denote a model’s scale in training FLOPs per token at its training context length, 3 3 Computing $M$ across architectures requires a consistent convention for counting forward and backward FLOPs. We use Megatron-LM as a reference. $D$ the number of training tokens, and $C=MD$ the total training FLOPs. At a fixed budget $C$ , increasing $M$ leaves fewer tokens for training, with $D=C/M$ . To fit a scaling law for each architecture, ArchSpace sweeps the five compute budgets $C_{j}$ of its scaling ladder : $1\times 10^{18}$ , $3\times 10^{18}$ , $1\times 10^{19}$ , $3\times 10^{19}$ , and $1\times 10^{20}$ FLOPs. The largest-budget runs dominate the training cost, while runs at smaller budgets add modest compute. Training models across the scaling ladder therefore enables systematic comparisons between architectures at little additional computational cost.

We provide three settings that differ in the scope of hyperparameter search. Broader searches require more training runs and therefore a larger total FLOPs budget. When the proposed architecture is similar to its baseline, the optimal learning rate (LR) and batch size (BS) can also be similar for the two architectures. The baseline’s search results can then be reused to reduce the cost of validation. The choice of setting therefore depends on the extent of the architectural change and the available compute.

At each budget of the scaling ladder, Setting 1 reuses the baseline’s compute-optimal settings, including model size, training token count, LR, and BS. Setting 2 performs an IsoFLOP sweep over model size and training token count while reusing the baseline’s LR and BS. Setting 3 additionally tunes LR and BS during the IsoFLOP sweep (Figure 2 ).


##### Setting 1: baseline-optimal settings.

Setting 1 trains one model per budget. The baseline’s scaling law gives the target model scale $M^{*}(C_{j})$ (Section 4.2 ). A realizable model of the proposed architecture rarely has exactly this scale, so the model with training FLOPs per token closest to the target should be selected. The selected model is trained either for the token count given by the baseline’s compute-optimal curve, allowing the budget to shift slightly, or at the fixed budget $C_{j}$ , accepting a small departure from the optimal model size and training token count (Appendix C.4 ). LR and BS should be taken from the best measured baseline recipe at the same budget and closest model scale (Appendix B ). The five measured losses are then used to fit a power law in compute, following Kaplan et al. [13] , with the irreducible loss term used in the Chinchilla law [ 9 ] :

[Tableau] $$\widehat{L}(C)=L_{\infty}+\alpha_{C}C^{-\gamma_{C}}.$$ (1)

The fitting parameters are the irreducible loss $L_{\infty}$ , amplitude $\alpha_{C}$ , and exponent $\gamma_{C}$ .


##### Setting 2: IsoFLOP sweep over model size and training data.

An IsoFLOP sweep compares models of different sizes trained with the same total compute budget, adjusting the training token count for each size [ 9 ] . This comparison identifies the combination of model size and training tokens that gives the lowest loss at that budget. Setting 2 searches four to six model sizes at each budget of the scaling ladder. The selected sizes should bracket the expected optimum for the proposed architecture. LR and BS should be reused from the best measured recipe of the nearest baseline model–budget group, as in Setting 1. The losses measured across these IsoFLOP sweeps are used to fit $\widehat{L}(M,D)$ using the coupled form in Equation 3 . This law describes how loss varies with model scale and training token count.


##### Setting 3: LR- and BS-tuned IsoFLOP.

At each model–budget configuration in the size sweep, the LR–BS search starts from the baseline’s predicted optima (Appendix C.4 ) or, at an imposed BS, from the optimal LR at that BS (Equation 12 ). The grid can be adjusted to the proposed architecture and the available compute; we use a $4\times 4$ grid as an example. The losses from the sweeps over model size, LR, and BS are used to fit the full scaling law $\widehat{L}(M,D,\eta,B)$ in Equation 2 , where $\eta$ and $B$ denote LR and BS.


#### 3.2 Phase II: Training 1B models on 150B+10B tokens

Phase II tests whether gains measured on the scaling ladder persist through longer training and context extension, and evaluates downstream capabilities. The framework adopts 1B parameters as a reasonable model size for this evaluation, with pretraining on about 150B tokens at 8K context followed by long-context mid-training on 10B tokens at 64K. 4 4 Context length and BS use binary multiples ( $1\mathrm{K}=2^{10}$ ); token and parameter counts use decimal multiples ( $1\mathrm{B}=10^{9}$ ).

For pretraining, the LR and BS predicted by the Phase I scaling laws provide a good starting point for hyperparameter search. In practice, the predicted optima differ from the best measured settings, so we conduct a further search over LR and BS. We then use the selected pretrained checkpoint for long-context mid-training and tune LR and BS with the pretraining settings as a reference (Section 5 ). As in Phase I, researchers can directly reuse the tuned settings, or run their own search when additional compute is available.

Runs are compared at the final checkpoint of each training stage, after its LR schedule completes, because intermediate rankings can reverse during LR decay (Appendix E.3 ). Downstream capabilities are evaluated with OLMES [ 7 ] 5 5 https://github.com/allenai/olmes using the evaluation suites introduced in OLMo 3 [ 19 ] . After pretraining, the Base Main suite tests general capabilities, involving mathematical reasoning and question answering. After long-context mid-training, evaluation focuses on the Base Long-Context suite, with RULER [ 10 ] as the primary development signal and HELMET [ 29 ] for complementary evaluation. The Base Main suite is also rerun to check whether the capabilities developed during pretraining are retained. The proposed architecture should use the same evaluation configuration as the baseline, so differences in their scores reflect only architectural differences.


### 4 Phase I: Baseline Ladders and Scaling Laws

Hybrid architectures have become a mainstream design choice for LLMs. We therefore select Qwen3.5 and OLMo3 as baselines. Qwen3.5 interleaves three Gated DeltaNet layers [ 28 ] with one full-attention layer [ 24 ] , whereas OLMo3 interleaves three sliding-window attention layers with one full-attention layer [ 19 ] .

For each architecture, we provide a baseline ladder : a series of models trained at the five compute budgets of the scaling ladder, with LR and BS swept within each model–budget group. We fit a scaling law to each ladder’s measured losses to obtain the compute-optimal model size and training token count, together with predicted optima. We then test the loss predictions on extrapolation runs at larger budgets to validate the effectiveness of the fitted scaling laws.


#### 4.1 Experimental setup

The models in both ladders are pretrained on the Dolma 3 Pool 150B sample using the shared OLMo 3 tokenizer (Section 3 ) and 8K-token sequences. Training combines Muon [ 12 , 15 ] with AdamW (Appendix B.1 ) under a warmup–stable–decay (WSD) schedule [ 11 ] , with a square-root cooldown over the final 20% of updates [ 8 ] .

Following Phase I Setting 3 (Section 3.1 ), we conduct IsoFLOP experiments by sweeping an outer grid of model sizes and compute budgets (Figure 3 ). Within each model–budget group, we further sweep an inner grid of LR and BS to find the best hyperparameters (Figures 4 and 9 ). Appendices A and B specify the model sizes and LR–BS grids for each ladder.


#### 4.2 Analysis method


##### Fitting the scaling law in two stages.

We fit a scaling law in two stages to describe how loss varies with model scale $M$ (training FLOPs per token), training token count $D$ , LR $\eta$ , and BS $B$ :

[Tableau] $$\widehat{L}(M,D,\eta,B)=L^{*}(M,D)+\Delta L(M,D,\eta,B),\qquad\Delta L\geq 0.$$ (2)

Here the base surface $L^{*}(M,D)$ gives the minimum loss achievable under the given model scale and training token count. The LR–BS term $\Delta L$ gives the additional loss when LR or BS departs from its optimal value. At the predicted optimal LR and BS, $\Delta L=0$ and $\widehat{L}=L^{*}$ .

In the first stage, we fit $L^{*}(M,D)$ to the lowest measured loss from each LR–BS inner grid, following OpenEuroLLM [ 1 ] . In the second stage, we fit $\Delta L$ using the losses of all swept runs across both outer and inner grids. We hold the fitted $L^{*}$ fixed during this stage to preserve the IsoFLOP curves fitted to the group minima (Appendix D.2 ).

Both stages minimize the sum of Huber losses on the differences between predicted and measured log loss. The Huber loss grows quadratically for small differences and linearly once their magnitude exceeds a threshold. Appendix C.2 gives the exact definition, threshold, and fitting objectives.


##### Modeling the minimum loss across model and data scales.

For the base surface, we extend the Chinchilla law [ 9 ] 6 6 Chinchilla uses parameter count $N$ as model scale, whereas DeepSeek LLM uses training FLOPs per token $M$ [ 5 ] . $N$ and $M$ are approximately interchangeable when fitting scaling laws because $M\approx 6N$ . We use $M$ to represent model scale because it reflects training compute more accurately. with a coupling exponent $\gamma_{MD}$ , following Skaling [ 27 ] and OpenEuroLLM [ 1 ] :

[Tableau] $$L^{*}(M,D)=L_{\infty}+\left(\alpha_{M}M^{-\gamma_{M}}+\alpha_{D}D^{-\gamma_{D}}\right)^{\gamma_{MD}},$$ (3)

Here $L_{\infty}$ is the irreducible loss. The amplitudes $\alpha_{M}$ and $\alpha_{D}$ set the magnitudes of the model and data terms. The exponents $\gamma_{M}$ and $\gamma_{D}$ determine how these terms decrease as $M$ and $D$ increase, while $\gamma_{MD}$ controls their interaction. The amplitudes and exponents are all positive.

When $\gamma_{MD}=1$ , the two power-law terms add directly, recovering the Chinchilla form with $M$ as model scale. We call this the additive form and fit it as an ablation to the standard coupled form (Appendix D.1 ). Table 1 gives the fitted coefficients of the coupled base surface for each baseline ladder.

##### Modeling loss away from the optimal LR and BS.

We first model the optimal LR $\eta^{*}$ and BS $B^{*}$ as power laws in $M$ and $D$ , a form used in StepLaw [ 14 ] , OpenEuroLLM [ 1 ] , and Microsoft Law [ 3 ] :

[Tableau] $$\eta^{*}=\alpha_{\eta}M^{\gamma_{\eta,M}}D^{\gamma_{\eta,D}},\qquad B^{*}=\alpha_{B}M^{\gamma_{B,M}}D^{\gamma_{B,D}},\qquad h=\alpha_{h}M^{\gamma_{h,M}}D^{\gamma_{h,D}}.$$ (4)

We also model the curvature scale $h>0$ in the same way, which will be used in the LR–BS term $\Delta L$ (Equation 5 ). The fitting parameters are $\alpha_{\square}>0$ and $\gamma_{\square,\triangle}$ , with $\square\in\{\eta,B,h\}$ and $\triangle\in\{M,D\}$ .

To guide the form of $\Delta L$ , we use the optimization proxy for Muon-type methods [ 25 , 26 ] . Starting from this proxy, we derive two hyperbolic cosine ( $\cosh$ ) terms that describe its joint dependence on LR and BS in log space (Appendix C.1 ). We therefore model the loss increase away from $\eta^{*}$ and $B^{*}$ with two cosh terms. With $\widetilde{\eta}=\ln(\eta/\eta^{*})$ and $\widetilde{B}=\ln(B/B^{*})$ denoting the deviations of LR and BS from their optima in log space, the LR–BS term is

[Tableau] $$\Delta L(M,D,\eta,B)=2h\left[\cosh(\widetilde{\eta}-\gamma_{\eta,B}\widetilde{B})-1\right]+2h\rho\left(\cosh\widetilde{B}-1\right)$$ (5)

This decomposition lets us obtain the optimal LR for any chosen BS. At that BS, the second term in Equation 5 gives the minimum loss increase achievable by optimizing LR, while the first term gives the additional increase from using a different LR. The optimal LR therefore makes the first term zero. This minimum occurs at $\eta=\eta^{*}(B/B^{*})^{\gamma_{\eta,B}}$ , where the curvature in log LR is $2h$ . Thus, $\gamma_{\eta,B}$ determines how the optimal LR changes with BS, while $h$ controls how sharply loss rises around that LR.

With LR optimized at each BS, the second term describes how the minimum loss varies with BS. Its curvature in log BS at $B^{*}$ is $2h\rho$ , so $\rho>0$ is the ratio of this curvature to the curvature $2h$ in log LR. We fit one $\rho$ per baseline ladder. Appendix C.1 derives the optimal LR at a fixed BS and the curvature expressions.

#### 4.3 Loss prediction and hyperparameter selection

We evaluate loss predictions across the scaling ladder and at larger compute budgets, then examine how the predicted optima can guide LR–BS searches.


##### Fit quality across the scaling ladder.

We compare the base-surface predictions with each model–budget group’s lowest measured loss (Figure 3 ) and the full-law predictions with the losses of all swept runs. We report mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) at the group minima (Best-point) and over all swept runs (All-point), as defined in Appendix C.3 .

We first compare the coupled surface with the additive ablation, which fixes $\gamma_{MD}=1$ and is fitted to the same group minima. For the fitted range $0<\gamma_{MD}<1$ , the coupled form makes the loss reductions from model scale and training data reinforce each other; the additive form keeps them independent (Appendix D.1 ). Both forms have fitted IsoFLOP minima within the swept range of model sizes, but the additive form deviates most from the measured losses at the smallest and largest budgets (Figure 3 , bottom row). Its best-point MAPE is 0.48% for Qwen3.5 and 0.62% for OLMo3, compared with 0.13% and 0.11% for the coupled surface (Table 20 ). The coupled surface therefore fits the measured IsoFLOP minima more closely across the scaling ladder.

Finally, we compare the predicted optimal LR and BS with the best measured configuration in each model–budget group (Table 22 ). The predicted optimal LR, however, differs from the best measured LR with a MAPE of 35.1% for Qwen3.5 and 23.1% for OLMo3. #### 4.5 Implications for the validation framework

The settings of Section 3.1 reuse the baseline ladders in two ways. ### 5 Phase II: 1B Baselines and Training Recipe

To provide reusable baselines for the longer training and downstream evaluation in Phase II (Section 3.2 ), we train 1B Qwen3.5 and OLMo3 models on 150B tokens at 8K context, followed by 10B tokens of long-context mid-training at 64K. ### 6 Related Work


##### Open research resources.

Shared baselines and documented evaluations support reproducible model comparisons. OLMo 3 [ 19 ] releases training data, recipes, and intermediate checkpoints, while OLMES [ 7 ] standardizes evaluation choices that affect comparisons between models. Using OLMo 3 data and OLMES evaluation, ArchSpace connects reusable architecture baselines to a continuing public contribution process: proposals, implementations, experimental records, reasoning, and reviews remain linked for later citation and reuse (Section 2 ).


##### Scaling laws for architecture comparison.

Kaplan et al. [13] established power-law relationships between language-model loss, model size, data, and compute. Chinchilla [ 9 ] used IsoFLOP profiles and an additive loss surface to determine compute-optimal model sizes and training token counts. DeepSeek LLM [ 5 ] represented model scale by non-embedding FLOPs per token, while Porian et al. [23] showed how output-layer compute, warmup, and optimizer tuning explain discrepancies in compute-optimal scaling. These studies provide the basis for Phase I’s IsoFLOP comparisons with consistent accounting of training FLOPs (Section 3.1 ).

The form of the loss surface determines how predictions vary across model sizes and training token counts. Skaling [ 27 ] couples the model and data terms through an outer exponent, reducing systematic residuals of the additive Chinchilla form. OpenEuroLLM [ 1 ] fitted this coupled surface to the lowest measured loss of each model–budget group. We adopt the coupled form and this fitting target for the base surface $L^{*}(M,D)$ , using training FLOPs per token $M$ as model scale, and add an LR–BS term to describe loss away from the predicted optima (Section 4.2 ).


##### Hyperparameter scaling and loss modeling.

Hyperparameter scaling laws range from relationships along a prescribed training path to functions of model size and token count. DeepSeek LLM [ 5 ] fits optimal LR and BS along the compute-optimal path, whereas the Porian Law [ 23 ] describes a path at about 20 TPP. Microsoft Law [ 3 ] models optimal LR as a product of powers of model size and token count at a fixed BS. StepLaw [ 14 ] uses this product form for LR and a token-count-only power law for BS. OpenEuroLLM [ 1 ] first estimates optima from a quadratic LR–BS surface within each model–budget group, then fits product power laws for both LR and BS. We fit power laws for the optima through the losses of all swept runs and compare prior laws along the paths on which they are defined (Sections 4.2 and 4.4 ).

Optimization theory provides a complementary basis for modeling the effect of LR and BS on loss. Shulgin et al. [25] derive a proxy for Muon-type optimization, and Su [26] analyzes generalized power-law proxies by minimizing first over LR and then over BS. Starting from the fixed-momentum Muon-type proxy, we derive its cosh representation in log space. This representation and the proxy’s square-root scaling of optimal LR with BS motivate our fixed-slope LR–BS term, which we combine with fitted power laws for the optima and curvature (Section 4.2 ; Appendix C.1 ).


##### Architecture evaluation after context extension.

OlmPool [ 2 ] directly studies how architectural choices affect long-context extension, finding that short-context metrics poorly predict the resulting long-context capabilities. It trains 7B models on about 140B pretraining tokens followed by 10B long-context tokens. Phase II adopts the same sequence of pretraining and long-context mid-training, using 1B models trained on 150B+10B tokens to evaluate architectural gains beyond the compute–loss comparisons of Phase I (Section 3.2 ). RULER [ 10 ] tests retrieval, multi-hop tracing, aggregation, and question answering with synthetic tasks; HELMET [ 29 ] covers a broader range of downstream applications. Phase II uses these benchmarks in the Base Long-Context suite and evaluates general capabilities with the Base Main suite (Section 5.3 ).


### 7 Conclusion

ArchSpace records, reviews, and references architecture experiments under a standardized validation framework with shared data and evaluation. In Phase I, loss is compared across the compute budgets of the scaling ladder. Phase II then evaluates downstream capabilities in 1B models after pretraining on 150B tokens and after long-context mid-training on 10B tokens. We provide Qwen3.5 and OLMo3 baseline ladders, fitted scaling laws, measured recipes, and Phase II baselines as initial references for this workflow.

The scaling laws combine a coupled base surface with a fixed-slope cosh LR–BS term and fit the measured losses from both baseline ladders with a MAPE of 0.25%–0.27%. The coupled base surface fits the group minima more accurately than its additive ablation, and the fitted coupling exponents and data exponents of optimal BS are close to those in prior work. The frozen laws predict extrapolation losses at up to ten times the largest swept budget with a MAPE of 0.32%. These laws also give compute-optimal model sizes and training token counts. Locating optimal LR, however, is harder than predicting loss, so we use the predicted optima to initialize direct recipe searches.

In Phase II, Qwen3.5’s selected pretraining LR is about 3.6 times the predicted optimum at the same BS. Loss predictions nevertheless remain close to the measurements for both baselines. Long-context mid-training then raises the Base Main scores and establishes 64K long-context capability for both baselines. The recipe tuned for Qwen3.5 also yields large gains for OLMo3 when reused without tuning.

With these baselines, ArchSpace offers the community an ongoing cycle of architecture research. Contributors introduce an idea through an Architecture Proposal, evaluate it under the validation framework, and submit the resulting record for public review. A Verified contribution can then become a new baseline for the next proposal. The reviewed records let later studies reproduce comparisons and reuse implementations. This cycle addresses the gaps identified at the start of this report: incomplete public records of the experiments and reasoning behind successful and unsuccessful architectural decisions, and research attention concentrated around industrial releases. ArchSpace aims to broaden the range of ideas that receive public review, citation, and recognition, making today’s baselines the starting points for tomorrow’s architecture research.

### Appendix B Training Recipe and Best Measured Recipes


#### B.1 Optimizer and parameter groups

AdamW updates the input embeddings, the output head, and the scalar, vector, and convolution parameters. Muon optimizes all other two-dimensional matrix parameters, including projection and feed-forward matrices (Table 13 ). AdamW uses betas $(0.9,0.95)$ and gradient-norm clipping at 1.0; clipping is disabled for the Muon parameters.

> Table 13: Optimizer assignments and weight decay for Qwen3.5 and OLMo3. Gated DeltaNet (GDN) rows apply only to Qwen3.5.

[Tableau] Parameters Optimizer Weight decay Input embedding matrix AdamW 0 Output head matrix AdamW 0.1 Normalization weights AdamW 0 GDN decay and time-step parameters AdamW 0 GDN depthwise convolution weights AdamW 0.1 All other 2D matrix parameters Muon 0.1

Muon approximately orthogonalizes gradient momentum, preserving its singular-vector directions while bringing the singular values toward one [ 12 ] . We use momentum 0.9 and approximate the orthogonalization with five Newton–Schulz iterations using a fifth-degree polynomial, avoiding an explicit singular-value decomposition. Following the Muon update rule [ 15 ] , the optimization part of the matrix update is

[Tableau] $$\Delta W_{t}=-\eta_{t}\,0.2\sqrt{\max(d_{\mathrm{out}},d_{\mathrm{in}})}\,O_{t},$$ (6)

where $O_{t}$ is the approximately orthogonalized momentum, $d_{\mathrm{out}}$ and $d_{\mathrm{in}}$ are the dimensions of the matrix being orthogonalized, and $\eta_{t}$ is the scheduled LR. The root mean square of $O_{t}$ varies with the matrix dimensions. The square-root factor compensates for this variation, and the factor 0.2 scales the update to a magnitude near that of AdamW. These factors let the two optimizers share $\eta_{t}$ ; the reported LR specifies the peak of this shared schedule.


#### B.2 Initialization

All baseline series use a scaled truncated-normal initialization adapted from a Megatron-based training recipe. The base initialization scale depends on model width as in Nguyen and Salazar [ 18 ] ; residual output projections are additionally scaled with depth. For model width $d$ and block count $n_{\mathrm{layers}}$ , the scales are

[Tableau] $$\sigma_{\mathrm{base}}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{5d}},\qquad\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}=\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{base}}}{\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{layers}}}}.$$ (7)

Embeddings, input projections, convolution weights, and the output head use a zero-mean normal distribution with scale $\sigma_{\mathrm{base}}$ ; residual output projections in the Gated DeltaNet and attention layers and in the feed-forward networks use $\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}$ . Each normal distribution is truncated at three standard deviations on either side of zero. Finally, normalization weights are initialized to one.

Gated DeltaNet represents its decay rate with a logarithmic parameter so that the rate remains positive [ 28 ] . We initialize this parameter as $\log A$ , with $A$ sampled uniformly between $1\times 10^{-2}$ and $16$ . The time-step bias is initialized by applying the inverse softplus to a value sampled log-uniformly between $1\times 10^{-3}$ and $1\times 10^{-1}$ .


#### B.3 Schedule and search grids

The shared WSD schedule [ 11 ] warms up the LR over 33.6M ( $2^{25}$ ) tokens, holds it at the peak, and decays it over the final 20% of updates to 10% of the peak with a square-root profile [ 8 ] . The stable phase is independent of the total training length. We therefore reuse this phase across runs that differ only in budget, then apply a separate LR decay for each budget, reducing the cost of the sweeps. The Phase II schedules are specified in Appendix E.1 .

We choose LR values around the expected optimum, with lower values for deeper models (Table 14 ). This choice follows the decrease in optimal LR with model size reported by the Porian Law [ 23 ] and StepLaw [ 14 ] . The tested BS values range from 128K to 2M tokens; for each model–budget pair, they give 500–200,000 updates and keep warmup below 20% of the training tokens.

### Appendix C Scaling-Law Fitting and Validation

The scaling laws are fitted to validation losses measured at the final checkpoints after WSD decay. Validation loss is averaged with equal weights over eleven domains, as specified in Section 3.1 : C4 English, Dolma books, Dolma common crawl, Dolma pes2o, Dolma Reddit, Dolma Stack, Dolma Wiki, ICE, M2D2 S2ORC, Pile, and WikiText-103 [ 19 ] .


#### C.1 LR–BS term


##### Origin of the cosh form.

To guide the form of $\Delta L$ , we use convergence analysis to describe how LR and BS affect optimization. Our baseline ladders use Muon, and Pethick et al. [22] show that an idealized form of Muon belongs to the class of methods based on a linear minimization oracle. For these methods, Shulgin et al. [25] construct an optimization proxy from convergence bounds. With fixed momentum and many training steps, the proxy simplifies to three terms:

[Tableau] $$Q(\eta,B;D)=\frac{a_{1}B}{D\eta}+\frac{a_{2}}{\sqrt{B}}+a_{3}\eta,\qquad a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}>0.$$ (8)

The first term describes incomplete optimization within the training budget and decreases with the number of training steps $D/B$ and with LR. The second represents gradient noise and decreases with BS. The third comes from the smoothness bound and grows with the size of each update.

At a fixed BS, differentiating with respect to LR gives the minimum at $\eta_{Q}^{*}(B)=\sqrt{a_{1}B/(a_{3}D)}$ , where the first and third terms are equal. To derive the cosh form, we express LR relative to this optimum as $r=\eta/\eta_{Q}^{*}(B)$ . The sum of the first and third terms is then proportional to $r+r^{-1}$ . Using $\cosh x=(e^{x}+e^{-x})/2$ , we obtain the increase above this minimum:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle Q(\eta,B;D)-\min_{\eta}Q(\eta,B;D)$ $\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{a_{1}a_{3}B}{D}}\,(r+r^{-1}-2)$ (9) $\displaystyle=2\sqrt{\frac{a_{1}a_{3}B}{D}}\left[\cosh\!\left(\ln\frac{\eta}{\eta_{Q}^{*}(B)}\right)-1\right].$

Thus, cosh expresses the balance between the inverse-LR and proportional-LR terms in log coordinates. Multiplying or dividing the optimal LR by the same factor gives the same increase in the proxy.

Substituting the optimal LR at each BS leaves

[Tableau] $$\min_{\eta}Q(\eta,B;D)=2\sqrt{\frac{a_{1}a_{3}}{D}}\sqrt{B}+\frac{a_{2}}{\sqrt{B}}.$$ (10)

The first term increases with BS, while the second decreases. Minimizing their sum gives the optimal BS $B_{Q}^{*}=a_{2}\sqrt{D}/(2\sqrt{a_{1}a_{3}})$ and the corresponding optimal LR $\eta_{Q}^{*}=\eta_{Q}^{*}(B_{Q}^{*})\propto D^{-1/4}$ . This order of minimization follows Shulgin et al. [25] and the general-exponent treatment of Su [26] . With $h_{Q}=a_{3}\eta_{Q}^{*}$ and $Q^{*}=Q(\eta_{Q}^{*},B_{Q}^{*};D)=4h_{Q}$ , we then rewrite the full proxy as

[Tableau] $\displaystyle Q-Q^{*}={}$ $\displaystyle 4h_{Q}\left[\cosh\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{B}{B_{Q}^{*}}\right)-1\right]$ (11) $\displaystyle+2h_{Q}\sqrt{\frac{B}{B_{Q}^{*}}}\left[\cosh\!\left(\ln\frac{\eta}{\eta_{Q}^{*}(B)}\right)-1\right].$

The first term is the increase after optimizing LR at the chosen BS; the second is the additional increase from using another LR at that BS. This decomposition motivates the same two parts in the validation-loss model.


##### Loss at a specified BS.

To model validation loss, we adopt the LR–BS term in Equation 5 . It retains the proxy’s square-root relation between BS and its optimal LR through $\gamma_{\eta,B}=1/2$ . At fixed model size, $h_{Q}=a_{3}\eta_{Q}^{*}$ gives $h_{Q}\propto D^{-1/4}$ . We extend this dependence by modeling $h$ with the same power-law form as the optima in Equation 4 , fitting its exponents in $M$ and $D$ . We also fit one $\rho$ per baseline ladder.

We use the peak LR of the WSD runs in place of the proxy’s constant LR; these runs use approximate Muon updates and auxiliary AdamW (Appendix B.1 ). Equation 5 uses a curvature scale $h$ that is shared across BS values at fixed $M,D$ . It also uses $\cosh\widetilde{B}$ for the BS dependence, with $\rho$ fitted to determine its size relative to the LR response. Equation 11 instead has an LR amplitude proportional to $\sqrt{B/B_{Q}^{*}}$ and a BS dependence through $\cosh[\tfrac{1}{2}\ln(B/B_{Q}^{*})]$ . Appendix D.3 compares the adopted form with alternative shapes and slopes, including a fitted form that retains the proxy shape.

For a specified BS, the derivative $\partial\Delta L/\partial\widetilde{\eta}=2h\sinh(\widetilde{\eta}-\gamma_{\eta,B}\widetilde{B})$ vanishes at $\widetilde{\eta}=\gamma_{\eta,B}\widetilde{B}$ . The optimal LR at that BS is therefore

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\eta^{*}(M,D\mid B)$ $\displaystyle\equiv\arg\min_{\eta}\widehat{L}(M,D,\eta,B)$ (12) $\displaystyle=\eta^{*}(M,D)\left[\frac{B}{B^{*}(M,D)}\right]^{\gamma_{\eta,B}}.$

Thus $\gamma_{\eta,B}$ is both the power-law exponent of optimal LR in BS and the slope $\partial\ln\eta^{*}(M,D\mid B)/\partial\ln B$ . It determines how the best LR changes when a different BS is chosen.

Using $\widetilde{\eta}-\gamma_{\eta,B}\widetilde{B}=\ln[\eta/\eta^{*}(M,D\mid B)]$ , the full scaling law becomes

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\widehat{L}(M,D,\eta,B)={}$ $\displaystyle L^{*}(M,D)+2h\rho(\cosh\widetilde{B}-1)$ (13) $\displaystyle+2h\left[\cosh\!\left(\ln\frac{\eta}{\eta^{*}(M,D\mid B)}\right)-1\right].$

The first line is the minimum predicted loss at the chosen BS, attained by optimizing LR. The second line is the additional loss from choosing a different LR at that BS. Consequently, $\rho$ controls how quickly the minimum predicted loss rises as BS moves away from $B^{*}$ after LR has been adjusted. If LR is held fixed while BS changes, the optimal LR also shifts, and both terms contribute. The coefficients of the LR–BS term are fitted together in the second stage (Appendix C.2 ). After fitting, Equation 12 gives the optimal LR for each specified BS, as in the fixed-BS analysis of OpenEuroLLM [ 1 ] .


##### Curvature and the role of $\rho$ .

The expansion $2(\cosh x-1)=x^{2}+x^{4}/12+O(x^{6})$ gives

[Tableau] $$\Delta L=h\left[(\widetilde{\eta}-\gamma_{\eta,B}\widetilde{B})^{2}+\rho\widetilde{B}^{2}\right]+O\!\left((|\widetilde{\eta}|+|\widetilde{B}|)^{4}\right).$$ (14)

For $h,\rho>0$ , this quadratic is positive definite and has elliptical contours around the predicted optimum. It extends the fixed-BS log-LR analysis of Bjorck et al. [3] to two variables. The positive higher-order cosh terms make loss rise faster farther from the optimum. The Hessian $\mathbf{H}$ is the matrix of second derivatives with respect to $(\widetilde{\eta},\widetilde{B})$ , evaluated at this optimum:

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{H}=2h\begin{pmatrix}1&-\gamma_{\eta,B}\\ -\gamma_{\eta,B}&\gamma_{\eta,B}^{2}+\rho\end{pmatrix}.$$ (15)

The curvature scale $h$ equals $H_{\eta\eta}/2$ , half the curvature with respect to LR in log space. The off-diagonal entry describes the LR–BS coupling, with $\gamma_{\eta,B}=-H_{\eta B}/H_{\eta\eta}$ . Optimizing LR at each BS leaves $\min_{\widetilde{\eta}}\Delta L=2h\rho(\cosh\widetilde{B}-1)$ , whose curvature at $B^{*}$ is $2h\rho$ . Hence

[Tableau] $$\rho=\frac{\left.\dfrac{d^{2}}{d\widetilde{B}^{2}}\min_{\widetilde{\eta}}\Delta L\right|_{\widetilde{B}=0}}{H_{\eta\eta}}=\frac{H_{BB}-H_{\eta B}^{2}/H_{\eta\eta}}{H_{\eta\eta}}.$$ (16)

The numerator is the curvature in log BS after optimizing LR; the denominator is the curvature in log LR at the optimum. The numerator is the Schur complement of $H_{\eta\eta}$ [ 4 ] .

This ratio has a direct interpretation when comparing BS values near $B^{*}$ . If LR is optimized at every BS, the leading loss increase is $h\rho\widetilde{B}^{2}$ . If LR is instead held at $\eta^{*}$ , the leading increase is $h(\gamma_{\eta,B}^{2}+\rho)\widetilde{B}^{2}$ . The extra $\gamma_{\eta,B}^{2}$ arises because the fixed LR moves away from the optimal LR as BS changes. Fitting $\rho$ therefore allows the remaining sensitivity to BS after optimizing LR to be estimated separately from the slope $\gamma_{\eta,B}$ . For the proxy, the corresponding expansion of Equation 11 gives a slope and curvature ratio of $1/2$ . The fitted loss model retains this slope and estimates the ratio from the ladder runs.


#### C.2 Fitting procedure

The coefficients are fitted in two stages, one per term of the law. Let $g$ index the model–budget groups, $L_{g,\min}$ denote the lowest measured loss of group $g$ , and $(M_{g},D_{g})$ denote its model scale and token count. The first stage fits the base surface of Equation 3 to these group minima by optimizing $\phi=(L_{\infty},\allowbreak\alpha_{M},\allowbreak\gamma_{M},\allowbreak\alpha_{D},\allowbreak\gamma_{D},\allowbreak\gamma_{MD})$ :

[Tableau] $$\min_{\phi}\sum_{g}H_{\delta}\!\left(\ln L^{*}(M_{g},D_{g};\phi)-\ln L_{g,\min}\right),\qquad H_{\delta}(r)=\begin{cases}r^{2}/2,&|r|\leq\delta,\\ \delta\left(|r|-\delta/2\right),&|r|>\delta,\end{cases}$$ (17)

with equal weight for every group. We use the threshold of OpenEuroLLM [ 1 ] , $\delta=0.001$ . Table 19 compares Huber and squared losses on log-loss residuals.

The second stage holds $L^{*}$ fixed and fits the parameters $\theta$ of $\Delta L$ using the measured loss of every run $i$ in every group:

[Tableau] $$\min_{\theta}\sum_{i}H_{\delta}\!\left(\ln\!\left[L^{*}(M_{i},D_{i})+\Delta L(M_{i},D_{i},\eta_{i},B_{i};\theta)\right]-\ln L_{i}\right),$$ (18)

with the same Huber loss and threshold as Equation 17 and equal weight for every run. Both fitting stages use bounded nonlinear optimization in log-normalized coordinates, retaining the solution with the lowest objective across several random starts. Tables 1 and 2 in Section 4.2 report the fitted base-surface coefficients and LR–BS parameters.

Following the Chinchilla analysis [ 9 ] and its extension in Skaling [ 27 ] , we minimize $L^{*}(M,D)$ subject to $MD=C$ to obtain the optimal model size and training token count. Substituting $D=C/M$ into Equation 3 gives the stationarity condition $\gamma_{M}\alpha_{M}(M^{*})^{-\gamma_{M}}=\gamma_{D}\alpha_{D}(D^{*})^{-\gamma_{D}}$ . The positive outer exponent does not change the minimizer, so with $M^{*}D^{*}=C$ ,

[Tableau] $\displaystyle M^{*}(C)$ $\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\gamma_{M}\alpha_{M}}{\gamma_{D}\alpha_{D}}\right)^{1/(\gamma_{M}+\gamma_{D})}C^{\gamma_{D}/(\gamma_{M}+\gamma_{D})},$ $\displaystyle D^{*}(C)$ $\displaystyle=C/M^{*}(C),$ (19) $\displaystyle D^{*}(M)$ $\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\gamma_{D}\alpha_{D}}{\gamma_{M}\alpha_{M}}\right)^{1/\gamma_{D}}M^{\gamma_{M}/\gamma_{D}}.$ (20)

If the BS is fixed, minimizing over the LR leaves the term $2\rho h\,[\cosh\ln(B/B^{*})-1]$ , which must also be included when re-optimizing model size and training token count.

Substituting Equation 19 into Equation 3 makes both the model term and the data term proportional to $C^{-\gamma_{M}\gamma_{D}/(\gamma_{M}+\gamma_{D})}$ . The resulting loss has the form of Equation 1 , with $\gamma_{C}=\gamma_{MD}\gamma_{M}\gamma_{D}/(\gamma_{M}+\gamma_{D})$ .

Setting 1 of Section 3.1 uses the baseline’s compute-optimal curve to choose the model size and training token count. The training token count is then set in one of two ways:

[Tableau] size first: $\displaystyle D=D^{*}(M),$ $\displaystyle\text{training budget }M\,D^{*}(M);$ (21) budget first: $\displaystyle M\approx M^{*}(C_{j}),$ $\displaystyle D=C_{j}/M,\ \text{training budget }C_{j}.$ (22)

In the size-first construction, the model scale is fixed and the token count should be chosen so that the model scale and token count lie exactly on the baseline’s optimal curve; the training budget can differ from $C_{j}$ . #### D.4 Shared versus per-group estimation of the optima

We test whether estimating the optima separately for each model–budget group is more accurate than using the shared LR–BS term. OpenEuroLLM estimates the optima by fitting a quadratic surface to the losses in each group [ 1 ] . We fit each quadratic in log LR and log BS, retaining fits with a full-rank design matrix, a positive-definite quadratic, and a minimum strictly inside the convex hull of the tested points. These fits cover 24 of the 27 groups for Qwen3.5 and 25 of 27 for OLMo3. We then fit power laws in $M$ and $D$ to the retained minima.

When evaluated against these minima, the per-group power laws attain lower MAPE than the shared LR–BS term, which is fitted to losses from all swept runs. The LR errors are 11.3% versus 16.6% for Qwen3.5 and 5.4% versus 12.5% for OLMo3. The BS errors are 9.9% versus 10.5% for Qwen3.5 and 6.2% versus 7.5% for OLMo3.

The per-group power laws, however, predict only the optimal LR and BS. The shared LR–BS term also predicts loss away from its optima and the optimal LR at a fixed BS (Equation 12 ). We therefore retain the shared term, using the per-group minima as a separate check of its predicted optima. At an imposed BS, the LR predicted by the shared term should be used to initialize the Setting 3 search (Section 3.1 ).


### Appendix E Phase II Baselines: Configuration and Results


#### E.1 Configuration and evaluation

The Qwen3.5 and OLMo3 Phase II baselines use the 24-layer shapes in Table 12 and the shared optimizer and initialization settings in Appendix B . Pretraining uses 8K context and a 2,000-update warmup. A square-root decay over the final 20% of updates reduces the LR to 10% of its peak.

Each long-context mid-training run then restores the model and optimizer state of the selected pretrained checkpoint and trains on about 10B tokens of the Dolma 3 Longmino Mix (50B) at 64K context. This stage uses a 200-update warmup followed by a stable LR and a square-root decay to zero over the final 20% of updates.

Long-context mid-training retains document boundaries within the 64K sequences [ 19 ] . Attention is causal within each document and masked across documents, and rotary positions restart at each boundary.

At 64K context, Qwen3.5 retains its pretraining RoPE base of $1\times 10^{7}$ and rotary fraction of 0.25 without further positional scaling. By contrast, OLMo3 applies YaRN scaling [ 21 ] with factor 8 to the rotary embeddings of its full-attention layers; its sliding-window layers retain their pretraining position encoding.

### Appendix F Literature Survey Methodology

The survey covers language-model architecture studies from November 2023 to October 2026, including multimodal models, with no minimum parameter count. Eligible papers described changes to a model’s internal computation and reported training or evaluation experiments. Studies limited to data, optimization, prompting, or execution efficiency were excluded unless they included architectural changes.

To identify eligible papers, we searched titles and abstracts across all arXiv categories, then used AI-assisted screening and targeted full-text checks.

Figure 1 compares work from universities and independent researchers alone with work involving industry. We classified a paper as work involving industry if at least one author was affiliated with a company, government body, independent research institution, or non-university nonprofit, including dual affiliations. We obtained affiliations from formal publications or arXiv versions and also used verified information about authors’ employment at the time of publication; funding alone did not determine the group.

Each paper was counted once by publication date, using its earliest documented public release or its first arXiv submission when no earlier date was established. The figure spans 23Q4 – 26Q3 , with 2,919 papers in the paper-count plot. The three-month periods are labeled Q1 for February–April, Q2 for May–July, Q3 for August–October, and Q4 for November–January of the following year.

The citation plot contains 117,629 cumulative citations to 2,859 papers from the same periods. We retrieved citation counts from Semantic Scholar on October 31, 2026. We matched papers to Semantic Scholar records only when both the arXiv identifiers and normalized titles agreed; unmatched papers were excluded. In each plot, bars show group totals and the line shows the share of papers or citations for work involving industry. Citation totals are grouped by publication date and are unadjusted for paper count or age.
