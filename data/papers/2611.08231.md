## From Twisted Facts to Rogue Agents: Can LLMs Reliably Detect Multi-Dimensional Hallucinations?

Large language models (LLMs) are widely used for information access, content production, and agent-based task execution, but their outputs remain vulnerable to subtle, high-risk hallucinations. To systematically evaluate and advance post-generation hallucination detection, we introduce HaluCheck , a large-scale, bilingual benchmark comprising over 42k instances spanning both factual hallucinations in text and faithfulness hallucinations in agent trajectories. HaluCheck features fine-grained, multi-level annotations for detection, localization, and correction, covering different tasks and perturbation degrees. Experiments on fourteen LLMs reveal that, while models show basic sample-level detection ability, they struggle with fine-grained localization, correction, and agent hallucination detection. Further analysis demonstrates that thinking, web search, and supervised fine-tuning each offer partial improvements, but none fully solve the problem. HaluCheck thus provides a solid basis for benchmarking and diagnosing LLM reliability across text and agent scenarios.


### 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are rapidly evolving from conversational tools into core infrastructure for content creation and task-oriented automation. Recent studies show that generative AI systems have achieved broad adoption in writing, information seeking, and productivity applications ( Bick et al., 2024 ; Chatterji et al., 2025 ) . At this scale, even a low rate of hallucination can still lead to substantial real-world risks as erroneous content propagates into downstream use.

> Figure 1: Representative hallucination risks in real-world LLM applications.

LLM hallucinations are often subtle and difficult to detect: state-of-the-art models frequently generate fluent, confident-sounding errors. As illustrated in Figure 1 , such risks have been observed in legal documents ( United States District Court for the Southern District of New York, 2023 ) , academic writing ( Sakai et al., 2026 ) , news and public information ( Bandara, 2024 ) , and code generation ( Zhang et al., 2025b ) . The consequences are even more severe in LLM-powered agents, where textual hallucinations can translate into incorrect or unsafe actions ( Lin et al., 2025 ) . Given the sheer volume of AI-generated outputs, manual verification is infeasible. This raises a key question: can LLMs reliably detect these hallucinations?

> Figure 2: Overview of HaluCheck . We construct a bilingual, multi-level benchmark covering factuality hallucinations in text and faithfulness hallucinations in agent trajectories. By injecting controllable perturbations into Wikipedia/QA passages and real agent interactions, HaluCheck supports hallucination detection, span localization and correction, and root-turn tracing in agent trajectories.

To address this, we present HaluCheck (Figure 2 ), a large-scale, bilingual benchmark containing over 42k annotated examples across both factual hallucinations in twisted texts and faithfulness hallucinations in agent trajectories. 1 1 Code repository: github.com/xMiles/HaluCheck . The benchmark provides multi-level, fine-grained annotations for detection, span localization, correction, and attribution, covering diverse tasks and perturbation degrees through controlled perturbations and moderate fabrications. Experiments show that, while models demonstrate moderate capability in factual hallucination detection, substantial gaps remain, especially in precise localization and correction. Stronger models express uncertainty more appropriately, reflecting greater self-awareness, but agent scenarios still pose major challenges: most models struggle with context integration and process tracking, leading to frequent failures in accurately detecting and attributing agent hallucinations. Further analysis reveals that explicit thinking and access to external knowledge can partially improve detection but are still limited by the models’ inherent capabilities. Supervised fine-tuning helps models recognize specific patterns of hallucination but cannot generalize stably to out-of-distribution data. Overall, hallucination detection remains an unsolved problem. HaluCheck thus provides a rigorous, large-scale foundation for benchmarking and advancing the reliability of LLMs across both text and agent-based applications.

### 2 Related Work

Hallucination generally refers to LLM-generated content that conflicts with real-world facts or user instructions ( Ji et al., 2023 ; Zhang et al., 2023 ) . With the rise of LLM-based agents, hallucinations in agentic scenarios have also gained attention ( Lin et al., 2025 ) . Following Huang et al. (2023) , we discuss LLM hallucinations through the distinction between factuality and faithfulness hallucinations, and situate agent hallucinations within the latter.


#### 2.1 Hallucination detection

Existing hallucination detection methods can be divided into reference-free and reference-based approaches. Reference-free methods do not rely on external evidence, but instead estimate output reliability from the model itself, such as sampling-based self-consistency ( Manakul et al., 2023 ) , semantic uncertainty ( Farquhar et al., 2024 ) , confidence estimation ( Ji et al., 2025 ) , or probes over internal representations ( Azaria and Mitchell, 2023 ; Han et al., 2025 ) . Reference-based methods verify model outputs against external evidence. Such methods have been widely studied in long-form factuality evaluation ( Min et al., 2023 ; Wei et al., 2024 ) , tool-augmented verification frameworks ( Chern et al., 2023 ) , and agent-based hallucination-detection systems ( Cheng et al., 2024 ) .

Building on these prior studies, we analyze real-world hallucination cases and existing hallucination taxonomies, from which we derive hallucination patterns for constructing controllable perturbations in HaluCheck .


#### 2.2 Hallucination Benchmarks

Existing hallucination benchmarks mainly focus on English text, while hallucinations in LLM-based agents and non-English contexts remain less explored. TruthfulQA tests truthfulness with misleading open-domain QA questions ( Lin et al., 2022 ) ; HaluEval constructs hallucination samples for QA, dialogue, and summarization ( Li et al., 2023b ) ; and HaloGen analyzes where hallucinations arise in model generations ( Ravichander et al., 2025 ) . Chinese benchmarks such as HalluQA ( Cheng et al., 2023 ) and C-Faith ( Zhang et al., 2025a ) further extend hallucination evaluation to Chinese QA and fine-grained hallucination taxonomies.

While most research focuses on measuring hallucination rates, post-generation detection remains underexplored. Existing detection benchmarks tend to be task-specific, such as claim verification in FEVER ( Thorne et al., 2018 ) , RAG hallucination detection in RAGTruth ( Niu et al., 2024 ) , unconstrained Chinese generation in UHGEval ( Liang et al., 2024 ) , and agent trajectory attribution in AgentHallu ( Liu et al., 2026 ) . These resources are fragmented across tasks and languages, often limited to sample-level detection, and rarely support localization or correction. HaluCheck addresses these gaps with a bilingual, fine-grained benchmark evaluating hallucination presence, span location, corrections, and root turns in agent trajectories.


### 3 HaluCheck : The Proposed Benchmark

A straightforward way to construct hallucination data is to annotate hallucinated content in model responses. However, naturally occurring hallucinations are relatively infrequent , appear unpredictably , vary across models, and are costly to annotate . We therefore adopt a synthetic data construction strategy: by injecting controlled hallucinations into source data, we obtain both sample-level labels and span-level annotations, since each hallucinated fragment is explicitly traceable. This design supports fine-grained hallucination detection and helps mitigate benchmark leakage through dynamic sample generation. Table 2 compares HaluCheck with representative hallucination and factuality benchmarks.

This perturbation-based design is grounded in observed hallucination patterns rather than arbitrary edits. Specifically, we consider two representative scenarios: Text and Agent . For twisted text hallucination, we focus on atomic factual errors in static factual passages. For rogue agent scenarios, we focus on hallucinations that may arise in multi-turn interaction. Figure 2 illustrates the overall construction and evaluation pipeline of HaluCheck .


#### 3.1 HaluText

HaluText targets hallucination detection in static factual texts. It mainly evaluates models’ ability to verify factual information about real entities and localize hallucinated spans. To this end, HaluText consists of two components: HaluReal and HaluFake . HaluReal is constructed by perturbing real Wikipedia passages and factual QA benchmarks, and can be viewed as perturbation-based data. HaluFake , in contrast, generates encyclopedia-style descriptions of fabricated entities and can be viewed as fabrication-based data.

To identify the main perturbation strategies for factual texts, we first analyze the hallucination patterns of LLMs using FactScore ( Min et al., 2023 ) . We then apply spaCy 2 2 We use {zh,en}_core_web_trf for NER. to perform named entity recognition on each atomic fact and compute the proportion of entities appearing in hallucinated atomic facts among all extracted entities as the entity-level hallucination rate.

> Figure 3: Hallucination rates across different entities.

As shown in Figure 3 , LLMs exhibit relatively high hallucination rates in expressions related to entities, especially for numerical entities such as numbers and dates. Entities related to humans and artifacts also show notable hallucination rates. Based on these observations, we summarize factual hallucinations in HaluText into three major perturbation patterns: Misattribution, Numerical deviation, and Detail Invention:

- • Misattribution mainly targets entity types such as persons, organizations, and locations, where attributes, relations, or events associated with entity A are incorrectly transferred to entity B.

- • Numerical deviation mainly targets numerical entities such as numbers, dates, and ratios, introducing either slight or substantial changes to the original values.

- • Detail Invention introduces entities, events, or details that appear plausible in a real context or encyclopedia-style passage but do not exist in objective facts.

In the construction pipeline, HaluReal is built from two types of factual sources: Wikipedia passages and factual QA benchmarks including HotPotQA ( Yang et al., 2018 ) , PopQA ( Mallen et al., 2022 ) , HalluQA ( Cheng et al., 2023 ) , and CMRC2018 ( Cui et al., 2019 ) . Wikipedia data provide entity-centered factual passages, while QA benchmarks provide questions, answers or supporting evidence to construct factual verification samples. In contrast, HaluFake is generated in an encyclopedia-style format, describing fabricated entities that do not exist in real-world knowledge.

The Chinese and English subsets are collected and constructed independently from language-specific source data. For span-level annotations, the original Wikipedia passages, correct answers, and supporting evidence are treated as hallucination-free source texts. We then instruct LLMs to modify designated spans under predefined perturbation strategies, allowing us to retain both the original factual spans and their hallucinated counterparts as explicit span-level annotations.

To evaluate models under different perturbation degrees, HaluText divides samples into three tiers: Obvious, Moderate, and Subtle. Obvious samples contain identifiable hallucinations, such as obvious entity mismatches or large numerical errors. Moderate samples contain factually incorrect but moderate perturbations, where the perturbed content is close to credible alternatives, such as similar entities, same-type attributes, or moderate numerical shifts. Subtle samples contain fine-grained hallucinations, including subtle attribute errors, implicit relation errors, or fabricated details that are difficult to verify directly. Specific examples for each perturbation degree are provided in Table 7 in Appendix B.3 .

To mitigate potential model-family bias, we employ LLMs from different model families for hallucination generation. Furthermore, combining perturbation strategies with perturbation degrees promotes diversity in the generated hallucination samples. Each candidate is further evaluated through multi-model cross-checking, where different models score the perturbation quality. Only samples that receive high scores across multiple models are retained in the final dataset.

Beyond automated quality control, we conduct two additional assessments: perplexity-based fluency evaluation and manual verification of sample and perturbation validity. Similar quality-control and manual-verification procedures are applied to HaluAgent . Detailed data-quality results are reported in Appendix B.2 .

#### 3.2 HaluAgent

HaluAgent targets faithfulness hallucination detection in multi-turn interaction and tool-use scenarios. Unlike static text, agent hallucinations may occur not only in the final response, but also in intermediate stages such as planning, tool calling, and tool-result interpretation. Since existing agent frameworks often constrain obvious execution errors through rule-based checks, hallucinations that bypass format validation and continue execution can be particularly risky. We therefore focus on subtle agent hallucinations that are more likely to cause practical harm.

Following prior studies on agent hallucination ( Lin et al., 2025 ; Liu et al., 2026 ) , we focus on three common but high-risk hallucination types:

- • Planning hallucination : the agent fabricates unsupported premises during planning and continues reasoning or acting on them.

- • Tool-use hallucination : the agent fabricates tool names or parameters, misplaces arguments, or passes task-inconsistent inputs.

- • Perception hallucination : the agent correctly plans and calls tools, but misinterprets, omits, or distorts the returned results.

HaluAgent is constructed from real agent trajectories drawn from AgentFLAN ( Chen et al., 2024 ) , AgentBank ( Song et al., 2024 ) , AgentTraj-L ( Xi et al., 2025 ) , ToolACE ( Liu et al., 2025 ) , and MSAgent-Bench ( Li et al., 2023a ) . These trajectories contain user instructions, tool definitions, reasoning traces, tool calls, and tool outputs. Following a process similar to HaluText , we synthesize hallucinated trajectories by applying targeted perturbations to agent interactions.

After these processes, we obtain the full HaluCheck dataset, comprising approximately 42k factual–hallucinated sample pairs with fine-grained annotations, multiple perturbation degrees, and predefined perturbation strategies. The Lite evaluation set is created through balanced stratified sampling from the full set. During sampling, we ensure that no factual sample is included together with its paired hallucinated counterpart, while maintaining coverage across perturbation degrees and perturbation types in both HaluText and HaluAgent . #### 3.3 Evaluation Metrics

We design separate evaluation protocols for HaluText and HaluAgent . In both tasks, the model first determines whether the input contains hallucinations. Inspired by Kalai et al. (2026) , we allow Uncertain as a valid response to capture abstention when evidence is insufficient. If the model predicts hallucination, we further evaluate fine-grained detection: HaluReal requires hallucinated-span localization and correction, HaluFake tests the model’s ability to identify fabricated entities, while HaluAgent requires identifying the earliest hallucinated step in the agent trajectory.

At the sample level, predictions are categorized as Hallucinated , Non-hallucinated , or Uncertain , while gold labels are binary. We report Accuracy , assigning Uncertain a partial score of 0.5 as a conservative judgment between correct and incorrect predictions 3 3 We assign rewards of 1, 0, and 0.5 to correct, incorrect, and uncertain responses, respectively, as Uncertain is preferable to an incorrect guess but less valuable than a correct answer. , following cost-sensitive evaluation ( Elkan, 2001 ) and the alignment goal of avoiding unsupported answers ( Bai et al., 2022 ) . We also report UncertainRate to measure how often the model abstains.

For HaluReal , we evaluate span-level localization with SpanF1 , where predicted spans are matched against gold hallucinated spans by an LLM. We further evaluate CorrectionRate , which measures whether correctly localized hallucinated spans are also corrected accurately. For HaluFake , we report Recall and UncertainRate at the sample level, examining whether models can identify fabricated entities and express uncertainty.

For HaluAgent , we report sample-level detection metrics and RootTurnAcc , which measures whether the model correctly identifies the earliest hallucinated step among hallucinated trajectories. Together, these metrics evaluate overall hallucination detection, abstention behavior, span-level localization and correction, and root-cause attribution in agent trajectories. We use rule-based and LLM-judge 4 4 The LLM judge checks agreement between model outputs and ground-truth annotations, rather than directly detecting hallucinations. span evaluation, with metric definitions and robustness checks detailed in Appendix C .

#### 4.2 Results

Table 3 presents the main experimental results. Across both the text and agent tasks, we observe substantial performance gaps among models, highlighting the strong discriminative power of our benchmark. The two tasks emphasize different types of hallucinations: the text task targets factual hallucinations, while the agent task focuses on faithfulness hallucinations, leading to distinct model behaviors.


##### HaluReal .

Among all evaluated models, Gemini3.1-Pro achieves the highest detection accuracy of around 84% and delivers the top performance in localization and correction. Interestingly, it also shows a higher tendency to express uncertainty than Gemini3-Flash. In contrast, most other models still struggle with hallucination detection, yielding accuracies between 50% and 70% on both Chinese and English datasets. Furthermore, localizing and correcting hallucinations present an even greater challenge. Across almost all models, SpanF1 and CorrectionRate are substantially lower than detection accuracy, highlighting a gap between detecting the presence of a hallucination and accurately localizing and correcting it.


##### HaluFake .

Models exhibit distinct uncertainty strategies when facing fabricated entities. In general, stronger models are more likely to express uncertainty about unknown entities, suggesting that they better “know what they do not know.” When refusal or uncertainty is allowed, some frontier/pro models adopt a more conservative strategy for fabricated entities. For instance, Gemini3.1-Pro and GPT5.4 show high UncertainRate on English HaluFake , accompanied by relatively lower Recall. In contrast, some lightweight or mini models tend to give direct judgments, resulting in higher Recall and lower UncertainRate, which may also reflect overconfidence in hallucination detection. This trade-off suggests that HaluFake evaluates not only whether models can recognize fabricated content, but also how they navigate the delicate balance between helpfulness (attempting to answer) and honesty (admitting ignorance).


##### HaluAgent .

HaluAgent is more challenging than text-based tasks: the best sample-level accuracy reaches only 64.55% in Chinese and 72.36% in English. Unlike factual hallucination detection, which can draw on pre-training knowledge, agent hallucinations require evaluating faithfulness, demanding precise integration of contextual information rather than simple fact-checking. In this setting, models rarely express uncertainty, yet their judgments are often unreliable. Model performance remains weak on both sample-level detection and root-cause localization, suggesting that identifying whether an agent trajectory contains hallucinations and where it first occurs is difficult.

Agent trajectories are longer, contain more intermediate states and step dependencies, and require faithfulness judgments over user instructions, reasoning steps, tool calls, and observations. Thus, HaluAgent exposes current models’ weaknesses in long-context understanding, process tracking, and error attribution.

Overall, LLMs demonstrate some capability in detecting hallucinations on Twisted Facts, but substantial gaps persist between models, particularly in precise localization and correction. Stronger models are better at expressing uncertainty when evidence is insufficient, reflecting greater self-awareness. In contrast, agent hallucination poses a far greater challenge: current models struggle with context integration and process tracking, highlighting substantial limitations in detecting and attributing agent hallucinations. Considerable progress is still needed for robust faithfulness in complex, multi-step scenarios.

Additional analyses further validate the practical relevance of HaluCheck . Across 14 evaluated models, benchmark performance shows strong correlations with natural-hallucination detection in factual and agent metrics. These findings suggest that HaluCheck can serve as a useful proxy for evaluating natural-hallucination detection. Furthermore, models that hallucinate less during generation tend to localize hallucinated spans more accurately, suggesting a shared reliance on factual knowledge across generation and detection tasks. ### 5 Discussion

Figure 4 shows the average performance differences of the Qwen3 series with and without thinking. By enabling thinking, the models improve on HaluReal : they identify hallucinated spans more accurately and better correct erroneous content. On HaluFake , thinking mode increases Recall while reducing UncertainRate, suggesting that reasoning makes models more confident and more willing to provide definitive judgments.

Table 4 shows representative cases where thinking mode changes the model’s hallucination judgments. The examples indicate that thinking enables models to explicitly check entity attributes, temporal relations, institutional affiliations, and geographic consistency before making a final decision. By recalling and verifying relevant knowledge, thinking helps models recover missed hallucinations, avoid false positives, and provide more accurate span-level corrections.


#### 5.2 Impact of Web Search Integration

Web search ( Cheng et al., 2024 ) and RAG ( Lewis et al., 2020 ) are commonly used to mitigate factual hallucinations. As shown in Figure 5 , integrating web search results 6 6 We obtain the web search results from the GLM Web Search API: https://docs.bigmodel.cn/cn/guide/tools/web-search consistently enhances model performance in hallucination detection, localization, and correction, while also reducing the rate of uncertain responses. The improvement is generally greater than that achieved by enabling explicit reasoning alone, highlighting the direct value of external knowledge for factual verification. However, the gains are not uniform: stronger models see marginal improvement, whereas weaker models benefit more yet may still lag behind top models without retrieval. This suggests that retrieval augmentation cannot fully substitute for a model’s inherent factual understanding and information integration capabilities. In particular, the effectiveness of web search depends on both the quality of retrieved evidence and the model’s ability to filter and utilize this information. Retrieved evidence can be incomplete, noisy, or poorly matched, and weaker models may struggle to make use of it effectively. Thus, while web search can help mitigate factual hallucinations, its overall benefit is limited by both retrieval quality and the model’s external knowledge integration.

#### 5.3 Impact of Supervised Fine-tuning

For supervised fine-tuning (SFT), we sample 5,000 paired positive-negative instances from the full HaluCheck set and verify that there is no overlap with the balanced lite set used as our test set. We fine-tune Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B using LoRA ( Hu et al., 2022 ) on this data. See detailed settings in Appendix E.1 . As shown in Figure 6 , SFT yields consistent performance gains in hallucination detection, localization, and correction. Given that the fine-tuning and test sets share no knowledge or instance overlap, we attribute these improvements primarily to the model’s enhanced ability to capture hallucination patterns and to detect subtle inconsistencies in the data.

To further evaluate the generalization ability of the fine-tuned models, we construct external test sets from HaluEval, C-Faith, and AgentHallu 7 7 These datasets have only sample-level labels, so we use binary classification without Uncertain . . Notably, while the fine-tuned models show substantial improvement on the Chinese text tasks, we observe performance drops on English text and agent-based tasks. We hypothesize that this gap is caused by distributional differences between the synthetic training data and the external benchmarks, particularly with respect to error types and contextual complexity. This finding highlights the challenge of achieving robust out-of-distribution generalization and points to the need for more diverse and representative training data for hallucination detection.

### 6 Conclusions

This paper investigates whether LLMs can reliably detect hallucinations after generation and proposes HaluCheck , a bilingual, fine-grained benchmark covering factual hallucinations in text and faithfulness hallucinations in agent trajectories. Unlike benchmarks that mainly measure whether models generate hallucinations, HaluCheck evaluates whether models can identify existing hallucinations, localize “what is wrong,” and determine “where the error starts.” Experiments show that current LLMs have some hallucination detection ability, but remain limited in span localization, correction, and agent root-step attribution. Reasoning mode, web search, and supervised fine-tuning can improve detection performance to some extent, but their gains are constrained by model capability, evidence quality, and training data coverage, indicating that reliable hallucination detection remains an open challenge.


### Limitations

Although HaluCheck provides a controlled evaluation of hallucination detection, several limitations remain. First, although constructed hallucinations may differ from naturally occurring ones, the observed correlations in evaluation metrics support their use as a practical evaluation proxy. Second, the benchmark focuses on representative factual-text and agent scenarios, leaving broader real-world settings for future exploration. Third, an independent human detection baseline is challenging due to the extensive factual verification and trajectory tracing required, although we have conducted manual verification to ensure data quality. Finally, potential model-based biases are a common challenge in LLM-assisted generation and evaluation, which we address through multi-model validation and cross-judge evaluations. Future work will further expand scenario coverage and strengthen real-world validation.


### Ethical Considerations

This work focuses on hallucination detection and uses only publicly available open-source datasets, without involving private, sensitive, or personally identifiable information. The benchmark is constructed through controlled synthetic perturbations, with rule-based checks and multi-model validation applied to improve data quality and reduce potential bias. Since the dataset contains hallucinated texts and perturbed agent trajectories, it should be used only for reliability evaluation and safety research, not for spreading misinformation or unsafe agent behavior. ### Appendix A Data Sources and Licenses

This section summarizes the data sources used to construct HaluText , HaluAgent , and the external test sets, and documents the corresponding license information. We distinguish perturbation sources from evaluation-only resources to clarify data provenance, reproducibility, and compliance with the usage terms of each source dataset.


#### A.1 Data Sources


##### Perturbation Data Sources.

For HaluText , we use both English and Chinese Wikipedia, together with factual QA datasets including HotPotQA ( Yang et al., 2018 ) , PopQA ( Mallen et al., 2022 ) , HalluQA ( Cheng et al., 2023 ) , and CMRC2018 ( Cui et al., 2019 ) . For HaluAgent , we use agent SFT data from AgentFLAN ( Chen et al., 2024 ) , AgentBank ( Song et al., 2024 ) , AgentTraj-L ( Xi et al., 2025 ) , ToolACE ( Liu et al., 2025 ) , and MSAgent-Bench ( Li et al., 2023a ) . All hallucinated samples are constructed by perturbing real, non-hallucinated data, so that each hallucination can be paired with its original factual counterpart. Chinese and English examples are collected from language-specific source datasets and constructed independently, rather than translated from one language to the other.


##### External Test Set Sources.

To evaluate the out-of-distribution generalization ability of the synthetic data constructed in this work, we build external test sets from public datasets, including HaluEval ( Li et al., 2023b ) , C-Faith ( Zhang et al., 2025a ) , and AgentHallu ( Liu et al., 2026 ) . For HaluEval, we use the provided evidence and correct answers, and prompt an LLM to generate corresponding hallucinated answers. The correct answers are used as non-hallucinated samples, while the generated incorrect answers are used as hallucinated samples, with 1,000 samples for each class. For C-Faith, we directly use the correct_answer field as non-hallucinated samples and the hallucinated_answer field as hallucinated samples, again sampling 1,000 instances for each class. For AgentHallu , we reuse its original annotations, which contain 693 samples in total, including 443 hallucinated samples and 250 non-hallucinated samples. The internal test set is the same as that used in the main experiments.

### Appendix B Details of HaluCheck Construction

This section describes the construction process of HaluCheck , including model setup, the multi-model synthesis pipeline, quality control, and human verification. It explains how text, QA, and agent sources are routed, perturbed, filtered, and validated to produce diverse, traceable, and reliable hallucination annotations.


#### B.1 Setup


##### Model selection.

For synthetic data generation, we use three frontier open-sourced large models: Qwen3.5-397B-A17B ( Qwen Team, 2026 ) , GLM4.7 ( GLM Team et al., 2025 ) , and Llama4-Maverick ( Meta AI, 2025 ) . All three models are deployed locally with FP8 quantization through SGLang on a single node with 8 H20 GPUs. During the synthesis stage, each model independently perturbs a subset of the source data. During the quality-control stage, every generated sample is evaluated by all three models in turn. A sample is retained in the final dataset only if all three models assign quality scores above the predefined threshold.

The use of multiple model families is intended to reduce family-specific artifacts in the generated perturbations. We also vary source texts, perturbation targets, and perturbation strategies so that the final labels are not determined by any single generator family.


#### B.2 Construction Pipeline

To ensure diversity and quality, we build a multi-model collaborative data synthesis pipeline consisting of three stages: data filtering and routing, perturbation, and quality control.


##### Text and QA Processing.

We preprocess the different data sources according to their formats and task types. For Wikipedia, we remove entries with abnormal text length or paragraph counts, use the first paragraph as the core factual passage, and treat the remaining paragraphs as supplementary context. For each entry, we randomly select either the core paragraph or a supplementary paragraph as the perturbation source. We then perform NER and sample entities under three perturbation-ratio settings, 0.1, 0.25, and 0.6. These ratio settings control the number of perturbed entities and are randomly combined with the Obvious, Moderate, and Subtle perturbation-degree prompts. For fabricated-entity cases, we sample Wikipedia core paragraphs and prompt models to imitate their encyclopedic style while generating entirely fictional entity descriptions. For QA data, we filter questions with overly short or long answers, and provide the model with the question, correct answer, incorrect answer, and supporting evidence when available.


##### Agent Trajectory Selection.

For agent data, we select trajectories aligned with text-based agent reasoning and tool use. AgentFLAN contributes ToolBench-style ReAct, CoT, and instruction-following trajectories; AgentTraj-L contributes weather, movie, todo, and SQLGym trajectories; ToolACE provides multi-turn tool-use examples; and MSAgent-Bench provides Chinese ModelScope plugin-based dialogues. For AgentBank, we treat each subset as an independent source for provenance tracking and flexible filtering, and retain text-oriented subsets covering code generation, mathematical reasoning, search-based QA, bash and SQL execution, web interaction, and online shopping. We exclude embodied or visually grounded subsets such as ALFRED, ALFWorld, IQA, and Rearrange, since their physical actions and visual observations are less aligned with text-based agent hallucinations. To control trajectory quality and context length, we keep ToolACE examples with 2–10 turns, constrain long instances to a maximum budget of 8,192 tokens, and use a fixed random seed of 42 for reproducible sampling.


##### Perturbation Generation.

The perturbation stage uses three LLMs to independently generate hallucinated samples according to predefined perturbation strategies and perturbation degrees. For HaluText , perturbations include substitution, displacement, and fabrication over factual entities or evidence. For HaluAgent , we perturb only one turn in each trajectory to introduce a planning, tool-use, or perception hallucination, and then update the subsequent trajectory to keep the interaction coherent. Each generated sample preserves metadata such as the original content, perturbed content, perturbation position, hallucination type, and perturbation degree, enabling traceable span-level or turn-level annotation.


##### Quality Control.

The quality control stage combines rule-based and model-based checks. Rule-based checks verify whether model outputs follow the predefined schema, whether the original and perturbed fields correspond correctly, whether factual and hallucinated samples are properly paired, and whether agent perturbations keep the original trajectory length unchanged. Model-based quality control asks multiple models to score each generated sample in terms of perturbation validity, strategy consistency, fluency, coherence, causal consistency, and naturalness. A sample is retained in the final dataset only if all model-based scores meet the predefined quality threshold. We further validate whether the controlled perturbations in HaluCheck provide a useful proxy for naturally occurring hallucinations. We compare the fluency of hallucinated and clean texts using Qwen3-8B-Base perplexity. As shown in Table 5 , hallucinated texts have perplexity values close to clean texts in both languages, suggesting that the perturbations do not create visibly unnatural artifacts.

In addition to the automatic quality and fluency checks, we manually inspect 100 sampled instances from each subset to assess label reliability. Table 6 reports both sample validity and perturbation validity. The results show high sample validity across subsets, while also revealing that agent perturbations are more challenging to construct than text perturbations. This human inspection is designed to verify known labels rather than to establish a from-scratch human baseline. A human baseline for this task would require annotators to independently detect subtle factual errors and trace multi-step agent trajectories without access to the injected labels, which is substantially more demanding than validating label correctness. We therefore focus on human verification of sample and perturbation validity in this version.

### Appendix C Metric Definitions

This section provides the detailed definitions of the evaluation metrics used in HaluCheck . We evaluate models at three levels: sample-level hallucination detection, span-level localization and correction for HaluText , and root-step localization for HaluAgent .


#### C.1 Detailed Metric Definitions


##### Sample-level metrics.

At the sample level, the gold label is binary, indicating whether the input contains hallucinations. Model predictions, however, may take three values: Hallucinated , Non-hallucinated , and Uncertain . For hallucinated samples, predictions of Hallucinated , Non-hallucinated , and Uncertain are counted as $TP$ , $FN$ , and $U^{+}$ , respectively. For non-hallucinated samples, predictions of Hallucinated , Non-hallucinated , and Uncertain are counted as $FP$ , $TN$ , and $U^{-}$ , respectively. The total number of samples is:

[Tableau] $$N=TP+FN+FP+TN+U^{+}+U^{-}.$$ (1)

We treat Uncertain as a conservative prediction between correct and incorrect answers, and assign it a partial score of 0.5 in accuracy and recall. Accuracy measures the overall correctness of sample-level judgments:

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{Acc}=\frac{TP+TN+0.5(U^{+}+U^{-})}{N}.$$ (2)

Recall measures the ability to identify hallucinated samples:

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{Recall}=\frac{TP+0.5U^{+}}{TP+FN+U^{+}}.$$ (3)

We also report the uncertainty rate, which measures how often the model abstains from making a definitive judgment:

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{UncertainRate}=\frac{U^{+}+U^{-}}{N}.$$ (4)

This scoring follows a cost-sensitive view in which abstention is treated as an intermediate outcome: it is less informative than a correct prediction, but preferable to an unsupported incorrect prediction.


##### Span-level metrics for HaluReal .

For HaluReal , we evaluate not only whether a model detects hallucinations at the sample level, but also whether it can localize hallucinated spans and correct them. Since the dataset does not exhaustively annotate all non-hallucinated spans, $\mathrm{TN}_{\mathrm{span}}$ is not defined.

In our final evaluation, span matching and correction assessment are performed by an LLM judge . Given the ground-truth hallucinated spans and the model-predicted spans, the judge identifies their correspondence, determines whether each predicted span refers to the same hallucinated content as a gold span, and assesses whether the proposed correction is correct. It then outputs a structured result containing $\mathrm{TP}_{\mathrm{span}}$ , $\mathrm{FP}_{\mathrm{span}}$ , $\mathrm{FN}_{\mathrm{span}}$ , $\#\mathrm{MatchedGT}$ , and $\#\mathrm{CorrectSpan}$ . A predicted span is counted as $\mathrm{TP}_{\mathrm{span}}$ if it matches a gold hallucinated span, and as $\mathrm{FP}_{\mathrm{span}}$ otherwise. A gold hallucinated span not matched by any prediction is counted as $\mathrm{FN}_{\mathrm{span}}$ . $\#\mathrm{MatchedGT}$ denotes the number of gold spans matched by at least one prediction, and $\#\mathrm{CorrectSpan}$ denotes the number of matched hallucinated spans whose corrections are also judged correct. We use Qwen3.5-397B-A22B, locally deployed in FP8, as the LLM judge.

Sample-level errors are propagated to span-level statistics. If a factual sample is predicted as hallucinated, all predicted spans are counted as $\mathrm{FP}_{\mathrm{span}}$ . If a hallucinated sample is predicted as non-hallucinated, all gold hallucinated spans are counted as $\mathrm{FN}_{\mathrm{span}}$ .

Span-level precision, recall, and F1 are computed as:

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{Precision}_{\mathrm{span}}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}_{\mathrm{span}}}{\mathrm{TP}_{\mathrm{span}}+\mathrm{FP}_{\mathrm{span}}},$$ (5)

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{Recall}_{\mathrm{span}}=\frac{\#\mathrm{MatchedGT}}{\#\mathrm{GT}},$$ (6)

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{F1}_{\mathrm{span}}=\frac{2\cdot\mathrm{Precision}_{\mathrm{span}}\cdot\mathrm{Recall}_{\mathrm{span}}}{\mathrm{Precision}_{\mathrm{span}}+\mathrm{Recall}_{\mathrm{span}}}.$$ (7)

For correction evaluation, we only consider predicted spans that are correctly matched to gold hallucinated spans. The correction accuracy is:

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{corr}}=\frac{\#\mathrm{CorrectSpan}}{\mathrm{TP}_{\mathrm{span}}}.$$ (8)

This metric is computed only over correctly localized hallucinated spans, and therefore reflects the model’s ability to repair errors after successful localization.


##### Root-step localization for HaluAgent .

For HaluAgent , in addition to sample-level hallucination detection, we evaluate whether the model can identify the earliest hallucinated step in the agent trajectory. This metric is computed only for hallucinated samples that the model correctly predicts as hallucinated. Let $\mathrm{RootRight}$ denote the number of such samples where the earliest hallucinated step is correctly localized. The root-step localization accuracy is:

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{RootTurnAcc}=\frac{\mathrm{\#RootRight}}{TP}.$$ (9)

This metric measures whether the model can attribute the hallucination to its root cause rather than merely detecting that an error exists somewhere in the trajectory.

### Appendix F Summary of Prompt Design

This section summarizes the prompt suite used for hallucination generation, detection, fine-tuning, and quality control. It documents how the prompts operationalize controlled perturbations, structured outputs, perturbation-degree labels, and automated validation across factual text, QA, fake-content, and agentic trajectory settings.


##### Prompt Suite Overview.

The appendix presents a structured prompt suite for hallucination data generation, hallucination detection, fine-tuning, and quality control. The prompts cover multiple scenarios, including Wikipedia-style factual text, question answering, fake-content detection, and agent-based task trajectories.


##### Synthetic Data Construction.

For synthetic data construction, the prompts are designed to generate paired samples consisting of a factual reference output and a hallucinated counterpart. In the Wiki passage setting, the main strategy is entity-level perturbation. Given a Wikipedia title, a source passage, and a named-entity list, the prompt validates the entities, determines the number of entities to modify, applies controlled replacements, and records all modifications in a structured format. The generated output includes true_text , hallucinated_text , and explicit modification annotations.


##### Perturbation Degree Design.

The prompt framework adopts a three-level perturbation-degree design: Obvious, Moderate, and Subtle. Obvious cases introduce easy-to-identify hallucinations through unrelated entity substitutions or large numerical and temporal deviations. Moderate cases use same-category or semantically similar substitutions that require external knowledge to verify. Subtle cases introduce fine-grained and highly believable perturbations, such as closely related entity swaps, fine-grained numerical changes, minor date shifts, or fabricated contextual details.


##### Fabrication Prompts.

In addition to entity replacement, the Wiki passage pipeline includes a fabrication-oriented prompt for subtle examples. This prompt adds moderate but false details to an already modified passage, making the hallucination more implicit while preserving fluency and contextual coherence.


##### QA Prompts.

For QA data, the prompts generate hallucinated answers from question-answering inputs. Given a question, a correct answer, and optional evidence or incorrect answers, the model is instructed to produce both a correct response and a hallucinated response, together with annotations of the modified factual information.


##### Agent Prompts.

For Agent data, the prompts focus on hallucinations in agentic workflows. They cover perception hallucination, plan hallucination, and tool-call hallucination. Perception hallucination changes the assistant’s interpretation of tool results while keeping tool outputs unchanged. Plan hallucination modifies reasoning steps or task decomposition. Tool-call hallucination perturbs tool selection or tool parameters. These prompts preserve the original interaction structure while introducing controlled factual or procedural errors.


##### Quality-control Prompts.

The quality-control prompts evaluate generated samples along dimensions such as perturbation validity, causal consistency, surface coherence, turn integrity, entity accuracy, text consistency, and fabrication plausibility. These prompts help filter low-quality samples and ensure that hallucinated examples are factually incorrect, linguistically natural, and consistent with the intended perturbation degree.


##### Design Principles.

Overall, the prompt suite is built around five core design principles: controlled perturbation, explicit perturbation-degree grading, structured output normalization, self-annotation of modified content, and automated quality assessment. It supports both hallucination generation and hallucination detection across factual text, question answering, fake-content identification, and agentic reasoning scenarios. This design enables the construction of hallucination datasets that are diverse, verifiable, perturbation-aware, and suitable for both evaluation and supervised fine-tuning.
