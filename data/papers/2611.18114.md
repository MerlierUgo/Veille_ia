## Interpretable weight-space programming builds transparent transformers and brain encoding models

Neural networks are powerful predictors, yet their opacity limits their usefulness in science, where the goal is explanation rather than mere prediction. In neuroscience, for example, deep encoding models accurately predict brain activity in response to natural stimuli, but struggle to reveal which stimulus features drive those predictions. Here we introduce interpretable weight-space programming (iWSP), a method in which an LLM agent iteratively builds interpretable models by explicitly programming their weights and features in code, without gradient-based training (backpropagation). Without backpropagation to tune parameters, the agent must draw on its prior knowledge and reason over previous trials to hypothesize, “hand-craft”, and refine human-interpretable computations mirroring the workflow of a human scientist. LLM audits confirm the interpretability of the hand-crafted models. We first validate iWSP by tasking it with constructing transformers for canonical benchmarks such as arithmetic and sentiment classification, finding that LLM agents can successfully craft compact, accurate networks directly. Next, we apply iWSP to build neural encoding models across vision, language, and audition. In the visual cortex, iWSP outpredicts existing interpretable baselines, uncovers distinct computations across early/higher visual areas, and generalizes to unseen data. In language fMRI, iWSP surfaces a confound in the data and in auditory ECoG, it systematically discovers interpretable acoustic features that predict neural activity. Together, these results establish iWSP as a general paradigm for aligning high predictive performance with human-interpretable scientific discovery.


### 1 Introduction

Figure 1: The iWSP loop and its interpretability audit. a , An LLM agent reads a task prompt and writes a model whose weights are set directly in code. No training is allowed. A fixed evaluator compares the model’s output with the target $y$ and returns a performance metric, which the agent uses to revise the code. On algorithmic/NLP tasks the model is a neural network whose weights are all set by the agent to generate the exact answer. For brain encoding, the hand-written network computes fixed features, and the evaluator fits a ridge regression from those features to recorded responses and evaluates it on visible stimuli. b , After the search, an LLM judge audits every saved model for training, rule violations and uninterpretable edits. To test interpretability, an agent (Opus 5.5) describes the best model in at most 200 words and a fresh session of the agent rebuilds it from that text alone, shown here for an addition task. c , In the algorithmic/NLP tasks for example, we find that the original and rebuilt models achieve very similar performance (2 rebuilds per task).

In the early days of artificial neural networks, McCulloch and Pitts wrote every connection of small networks by hand to make them compute logical functions McCulloch and Pitts, 1943 . The values of these weights were interpretable, allowing one to understand how the models worked. In the modern era, as massive networks trained with backpropagation have improved their performance, models have sacrificed this interpretability. The resulting opacity raises concerns around regulatory compliance, safety, and alignment Goodman and Flaxman, 2017 ; Amodei et al., 2016 ; Gabriel, 2020 ; Singh et al., 2024 . It is particularly limiting in fields such as neuroscience Jain et al., 2024 and social science Ziems et al., 2024 , where trustworthy interpretation is often itself the end goal. Neuroscience has seen the rise of large neural-network based “encoding models” that predict brain responses to natural stimuli, like images or text Naselaris et al., 2011 ; Yamins et al., 2014 ; Cadena et al., 2019 ; Zhuang et al., 2021 ; Konkle and Alvarez, 2022 ; Jain and Huth, 2018 . However, these models are often difficult to interpret, making it difficult to connect features of the input stimulus to brain activity Toneva and Wehbe, 2019 ; Goldstein et al., 2022 ; Caucheteux et al., 2023 ; Antonello et al., 2023 .

To tackle this challenge, we introduce interpretable weight-space programming (iWSP). iWSP guides a frontier agent to directly write a model while disallowing any training. Then, a fixed evaluator measures its performance, and the agent revises it iteratively ( Fig. 1 ). Disallowing training requires the agent to reason and implement each change in text/code, so weight changes are generally guided by the agent’s prior knowledge and each change is accompanied by a written explanation and resulting change in performance.

iWSP builds on a vast literature. One line of work builds inherently interpretable models Friedman et al., 2023 ; Rudin, 2019 ; Huth et al., 2016 ; Liu et al., 2024 ; Singh et al., 2023a ; Koh et al., 2020 ; Kim et al., 2024 ; Sun et al., 2024 ; Guide Labs Team et al., 2026 ; Benara et al., 2024 ; Feng et al., 2026 ; Ludan et al., 2023 ; Tan et al., 2024 , though their constrained form often limits accuracy on hard problems. To get around this limit, recent work uses autoresearch to discover effective programs to solve hard problems Romera-Paredes et al., 2023 ; Novikov et al., 2025 ; Georgiev et al., 2025 ; Singh et al., 2026 ; Kasenberg et al., 2026 ; Lu et al., 2024 ; Gottweis et al., 2025 ; Swanson et al., 2025 ; Ifargan et al., 2024 ; Tang et al., 2025 ; Mitchener et al., 2025 . Such programs can be translated into neural networks using a domain-specific language Weiss et al., 2021 ; Lindner et al., 2023 or alternatively these weights can be generated by training a network to do so Ha et al., 2016 ; Chauhan et al., 2024 ; Stanley et al., 2009 ; Schürholt et al., 2022 ; Peebles et al., 2022 ; Knyazev et al., 2021 ; Han et al., 2026 . In weight-space programming Takehi et al., 2026 , the agent instead reads and edits the weights itself, potentially guided by mechanistic interpretability approaches, such as computing activation differences Elhage et al., 2021 ; Wang et al., 2022 ; Turner et al., 2023 ; Li et al., 2023 ; Meng et al., 2022 . We extend this work with a simple modification: we require that weight-space edits be interpretable and then iteratively update weights to optimize a performance objective. To ensure interpretability, we audit the resulting models (both manually and with LLMs as a judge), and quantitatively test whether they can be rebuilt from a short text description ( Fig. 1 b).

We first evaluate iWSP on relatively simple algorithmic/NLP tasks. Using frontier agents (e.g. Claude Opus 5.5), iWSP writes transformers from scratch that effectively solve 13 different algorithmic tasks, and achieve respectable performance on 3 out of 4 standard NLP tasks like sentiment classification ( Sec. 3 ). We then study iWSP in three settings that predict brain responses to naturalistic stimuli. First, in visual cortex, an iWSP model written on one functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) dataset predicts responses in another better than interpretable baselines, and its computations separate early and higher visual areas ( Sec. 4.1 ). Second, in language fMRI, the interpretability of iWSP is useful for debugging, helping to identify a dataset confound ( Sec. 4.2 ). Third, in electrocorticography (ECoG) responses to audio ( Sec. 4.3 ), iWSP finds accurate interpretable models that can improve the predictive performance of black-box models. Taken together, these results suggest that frontier agents can help close the gap between highly accurate neural networks, and ones that we can usefully understand.


### 2 Method: Interpretable weight-space programming

Motivated by the search for interpretable models that can help scientific domains, we seek to improve the performance of human-understandable neural networks. To do so, we follow an approach similar to weight-space programming Takehi et al., 2026 , which prompts an agent to directly read and edit neural network weights. We study interpretable weight-space programming (iWSP), where we further require that weight-space edits be interpretable and then iteratively update weights to optimize a performance objective. Interpretability is suggested in the prompt, and then enforced by an auditing agent that judges whether model changes at each iteration are human-interpretable. This approach enables an agent to search for an interpretable model much like a human would, by using its priors and domain knowledge.

At a high level, each iWSP run has the same setup ( Fig. 1 ). A frontier coding agent is given a prompt that describes the task, and a harness that includes a starting file and a non-editable evaluation script that evaluates the current model. The prompt and harness forbid training, including optimizers, fitting loops, and downloading pretrained weights. The agent remains free to use known intepretable operations (e.g. Gabor filters), but must implement them using only its given basic packages, such as pytorch. It then repeatedly writes a model, evaluates it on a “visible set” of examples and revises it for a fixed number of iterations. Throughout, the agent is prompted to search for a human-understandable solution.


##### Interpretability audits

Each saved model (and the agent’s corresponding logs) is audited by a separate agent to test for the interpretability of the edits and any violations of the constraints outlined in the prompt and harness ( Fig. 1 b). As an additional check on whether a model uses any training data explicitly, we verify that rerunning each model’s weight-writing code on altered task data leaves its weights unchanged (algorithmic and NLP tasks). After the run concludes, the models are reevaluated on a “hidden set” that they never saw during the iterative loop. We further test the interpretability of the final models by checking whether the models can be accurately described with a short text description using a frontier LLM (Opus 5.5). We ask the LLM to describe the best model for each task in at most 200 words, and then give only that description and the task to a fresh session, which rebuilds the model ( Fig. 1 b). We then evaluate how well the rebuilt model reflects the original, e.g. does it maintain similar predictive performance?


##### Algorithmic/NLP tasks.

In the algorithmic/NLP tasks, each run starts from one Python file and one instruction document. The file defines a small GPT-style transformer, with three layers of width 64 by default, a function write_weights(model, task) for the agent to fill in, and fixed code that evaluates the result. The prompt names the task and tells the agent to follow the document until it has run 30 to 50 iterations, depending on the task. The document states the rules, including:

The model is NEVER trained. The agent must write the weights directly into interpretable_transformer.py . […] What you CANNOT do: Run any kind of training, gradient descent, optimizer step, fitting loop, or backprop. The model parameters must be set in closed form. […] The goal is simple: maximize accuracy […] All else being equal, more interpretable is better. A small improvement that adds ugly complexity is not worth it. Number of model parameters can help serve as a proxy for interpretability, but the real criterion is human-understandable structure in the weights.

The agent may change the architecture and hard-code constants, including hand-built attention circuits and lookup tables; it may not compute the answer with anything outside the network (e.g. a Python function). Each run has its own folder, and the agent works in a restricted Claude Code session with six file and shell tools, no web access and file reads confined to that folder. Its only shell command, apart from read-only listings, runs this file. The harness cannot stop the agent’s own Python from training or from computing the answer outside the network, so those rules remain instructions that we audit afterward. The agent learns the task format by printing examples from inside its code, and after each iteration it sees its visible accuracy on a fixed visible set of 200 examples, where the answer is generated token by token and must match exactly. For these experiments, the iWSP agent is Claude Code running Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 or Haiku 5.5 and audits for constraint-following are done by Opus 5.5.


##### Brain encoding models.

In our experiments involving brain data, the agent writes a model $f$ that maps each stimulus $x$ to features $f(x)$ . A fixed evaluator then fits a ridge regression model $W$ from $f(x)$ that linearly predicts recorded responses $y$ on training stimuli, predicts $\hat{y}=Wf(x)$ on the visible set, and returns the pearson correlation between $\hat{y}$ matches $y$ . The agent receives only this metric and every candidate within a dataset uses the same stimulus split, ridge regression procedure, and metric. In addition to the standard constraints above, the rules forbid a model from reading any of the brain responses and the evaluation code rejects a candidate with 10,000 or more features. Unless otherwise specified, the agentic system is Claude Code with Opus 5.5; the visual cortex search uses Gemini 4 Carbon ( Sec. 4.1 ). The visual cortex search runs in a sandbox with no network and no access to the datasets, run directories or repository. The agents work in their own sandbox, which shows only their workspace, and they receive performance metrics through a restricted interface. The story-listening fMRI and ECoG searches do not isolate in a sandbox and instead rely on the prompt with a post-hoc audit ( Secs. 4.2 and 4.3 ).

In total, the paper’s experiments cost about $17,500 at API list prices, including estimated exploratory runs ( Table A7 ).


### 3 Algorithmic/NLP results: iWSP builds effective, interpretable transformers


##### Experimental setup.

To build our intuition, we first evaluate iWSP on 17 diverse tasks. The tasks include 13 algorithmic tasks such as multiplication, sorting, edit distance and date normalization (inspired by the AdderBoard addition task Papailiopoulos, 2026 ), and four NLP tasks: 6-class question classification on TREC Li and Roth, 2002 , binary sentiment classification on SST-2 Socher et al., 2013 , binary paraphrase detection on MRPC Dolan et al., 2004 and three-way natural language inference on SNLI Bowman et al., 2015 (see full descriptions in Table A1 ). We run iWSP on each task twice with each of Claude Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Haiku 5.5, for about 1,340 iterations per LLM (about 39 per run). The hidden set is 2,000 new examples for algorithmic tasks and the official test split for NLP tasks. After the runs, we audit the iWSP models as described in Sec. 2 and exclude the few that do not pass from the results (see full details in Table A2 ).


##### Models generalize on algorithmic tasks and on 3 out of 4 NLP tasks.

We first describe the Opus runs. All 26 runs on algorithmic tasks reach 100% accuracy both on the visible set and the hidden set ( Fig. 2 a), often by implementing a known algorithm in the weights. The NLP tasks are harder as they have no known full solution. Nevertheless, on 3 of the 4 NLP datasets iWSP builds models that achieve respectable performance well above a naive majority baseline (it fails on the relatively difficult Paraphrase task).

##### iWSP compresses models after making them accurate.

Over iterations, both types of tasks improve in their accuracy and then start to compress the model parameters ( Fig. 2 b). On average, algorithmic runs reach 99% visible accuracy within the first tenth of their iterations and spend the rest compressing from about 3,400 parameters to about 200. The NLP runs’ performance plateaus after about 75% of their iterations and stay near 190,000 parameters. On algorithmic tasks, the smallest model that reaches 100% visible accuracy has a median of 166 parameters ( Fig. 2 c), and in the median run it is 29 times smaller than the first model to reach 100%.


##### Bigger LLMs mostly achieve similar performance but are better at compressing models.

When comparing different Claude models (Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5, and Haiku 5.5), we find that they generally all achieve about 100% accuracy on the algorithmic tasks, but that Opus and Sonnet outperform Haiku on the NLP tasks ( Table A2 ). Beyond predictive accuracy, better LLMs are better able to compress an accurate model ( Fig. 2 c), with the smallest model that reaches 100% visible accuracy having a median of 166 parameters for Opus, 41,600 for Sonnet and 100,000 for Haiku.


##### iWSP models translate known algorithms into transformers.

For algorithmic problems, the agent tends to implement known algorithms rather than conceiving of new ideas. For example, the edit distance task uses one block per row of the dynamic-programming table, and multiplication adds shifted partial products. For greatest common divisor (GCD), the subtractive Euclidean algorithm Yao and Knuth, 1975 uses the fact that any common divisor of $A$ and $B$ also divides $A-B$ , so replacing the pair with $(|A-B|,\min(A,B))$ preserves the greatest common divisor until the pair reaches $(g,0)$ ( Fig. 2 d). iWSP implements this as a one block of three ReLU units, repeated 1,000 times ( Fig. 2 e). Note that 100% performance on our eval tasks does not guarantee correct outputs on all inputs, there could be adversarial inputs not considered in the task or generalizations for which the network completely fails, e.g. our GCD test only specifies 3-digit inputs.


##### iWSP NLP models are crude but interpretable.

Without a known algorithm, iWSP builds simple rules that approximate what an NLP task requires. For example, the best sentiment classification model has three stages, each built from standard transformer parts ( Fig. 2 f). A shift register made of attention heads feeds one neuron for each of 1,515 hand-written word patterns, a second attention layer looks back for a negator and flips positive evidence that follows it, and a final layer adds up the evidence. The negator is a crude rule, but is effective at composing terms in a way that simple words/ngrams fail to.


##### Vetting interpretability: a 200-word description is sufficient to rebuild a model.

Beyond qualitative analysis, we further run the text bottleneck check described in Sec. 2 ( Fig. 1 b). We find that all 13 rebuilt models (with 2 seeds each) reach 100% hidden accuracy on the algorithmic tasks, and on NLP tasks rebuilt models lose about 1.4 points of hidden accuracy on average ( Fig. 1 c). We further audit that this is done properly: (i) no rebuilt model computes its weights from task data (either through training or corpus statistics), (ii) rerunning the weight-writing code of all 34 rebuilt models on altered task data leaves their weights unchanged, and (iii) no session read files outside its sandbox. The rebuilt models are also much closer to the original than a control that rebuilds models without a description. On algorithmic tasks, rebuilt models have 1.74 times the original’s parameters on a geometric mean, against 2,043 times for the control ( Fig. A2 b). On NLP tasks, rebuilt models also perform closer to the original than the control does, e.g. their hidden-set predictions correlate significantly more on average (0.74 against 0.51; Fig. A2 c).


### 4 Neuroscience results: using iWSP to build brain encoding models


#### 4.1 iWSP builds highly predictive, interpretable models of the visual cortex

We highlight five of the computations here, showing the filters and operations of one computation, its response to the example image (for channel correlations, the matrix of correlations between channel maps), and the share of each visual area’s prediction that it accounts for. Error bars in c and d show the standard error of the mean over subjects, which is often within the points

In visual neuroscience, the most accurate encoding models are deep networks trained for object recognition, self-supervised learning, or image–text alignment Yamins et al., 2014 ; Khaligh-Razavi and Kriegeskorte, 2014 ; Cadena et al., 2019 ; Zhuang et al., 2021 ; Konkle and Alvarez, 2022 ; Schrimpf et al., 2018 ; Chen and Bonner, 2025 , whose learned weights make it hard to state what image features they compute. In contrast, classical models built from explicit operations such as Gabor filters Kay et al., 2008 , divisive normalization Heeger, 1992 , scene gist Oliva and Torralba, 2001 , or texture statistics Portilla and Simoncelli, 2000 are interpretable but predict brain responses less accurately. Here we use iWSP to bridge this gap by searching for a neural network that builds interpretable features from images that are highly predictive of brain responses ( Sec. 2 ).


##### Experimental setup.

We run iWSP on LAION-fMRI Zerbe et al., 2026 (7 T visual cortex responses from five subjects viewing 25,052 natural images) and hold out the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD) for out-of-dataset testing ( Fig. 3 a). Following Sec. 2 , each candidate maps raw images to fewer than 10,000 features using fewer than 100,000 explicit parameters, and a fixed ridge regression fit on each subject’s unique images is scored by Pearson correlation on shared validation images. Search maximizes mean correlation pooled across subjects and visual areas while also returning per-subject and per-area scores (such as V1, V2, V3, hV4, FFA, and PPA). Once search on all five LAION-fMRI subjects finishes, we freeze the best program (the iWSP model) and test it zero-shot on NSD. To test unseen subjects and images within LAION-fMRI, we also repeat the search on three subjects and half of the shared images, scoring its best model once on the other two subjects and 747 shared images. On both datasets, we compare iWSP against seven classical interpretable baselines (including Gabor filters with divisive normalization and Portilla–Simoncelli texture statistics Portilla and Simoncelli, 2000 ), a single ridge regression fit to all seven combined, and pretrained AlexNet Krizhevsky et al., 2012 ( Sec. A.2 ).


##### iWSP builds from classical V1 filtering to 14 explicit computations.

Across the 600 search iterations on LAION-fMRI, performance steadily increases ( Fig. 3 b), with most of the gain occuring within the first nine iterations as the agents recover a classical V1 front end of multi-scale oriented Gabor filters and divisive normalization Heeger, 1992 on luminance and color-opponent channels. The final 865-line program produces 9,996 features from 14 explicit computations ( Sec. A.2 ), five of which appear in Fig. 3 d: (1) Gabor energy and (2) color contrast compute locally normalized filter energy on luminance and red–green or blue–yellow channels; (3) surface geometry uses second derivatives of brightness to classify local shading into pits, valleys, saddles, ridges, and bumps; (4) contour curvature tracks how fast local orientation changes across space; and (5) channel correlations average pairwise products of feature maps to capture co-occurrence. Confirming that the program is readable, fresh sessions given only a 185-word summary rebuild models that reach 0.451 pooled correlation (versus 0.488) and match the original’s predictions more closely than AlexNet conv2, while rebuilds across eight search checkpoints retain 96% of original performance on average ( Fig. A5 ; Sec. A.2 ).


##### iWSP generalizes to an unseen dataset and to held-out subjects.

Evaluating the same iWSP hand-crafted features on NSD ( Fig. 3 c, left) shows that they transfer across datasets and subjects. In every one of the four NSD subjects, iWSP beats all 7 interpretable baselines in both early (0.443 versus 0.400 for the best baseline) and ventral visual cortex (0.317 versus 0.256), and outperforms a single ridge regression fit jointly to all seven baselines (0.283 in ventral visual cortex). Compared with pretrained AlexNet, iWSP matches black-box accuracy in early visual cortex while trailing in ventral visual cortex (0.391). Repeating the search on three LAION-fMRI subjects ( Fig. 3 c, right) replicates every comparison on the two held-out subjects and hidden images (early 0.518 versus 0.472; ventral 0.409 versus 0.309), with identical accuracy on hidden and visible images in the visible subjects ( Sec. A.2 ).


##### Different computations explain early versus category-selective visual areas.

Because the ridge regression sums over features, each computation’s covariance with the measured response gives its exact percentage share of a voxel’s test correlation, summing to 100% in every area ( Sec. A.2 ). Although search optimizes a single pooled score without anatomical labels, these shares shift systematically across the visual hierarchy ( Fig. 3 d and Fig. A3 ). Early areas rely more on local shading and edge energy: local-mean surface geometry falls from 38% in V1 to 15% in PPA, and local-mean Gabor energy drops from about 10% in V1–V3 to under 3% in FFA and PPA, showing that the final V1 ridge regression relies more on later-added surface geometry than on the initial Gabor stage. Category-selective areas instead draw more on mid-level structure, with contour curvature rising from 5% or less in V1–hV4 to 15% in FFA, channel correlations rising from about 3% in V1–hV4 to about 10% in FFA and PPA, and color contrast peaking in hV4 and FFA. Because correlated computations share 88% to 96% of the model’s squared correlation, we verify these profiles by refitting the ridge regression without each computation ( Table A4 ): in all five subjects, local-mean Gabor energy uniquely explains 1.4% to 2.0% in V1–V3 and none in FFA and PPA, local-mean surface geometry uniquely explains 0.7% to 1.5% in every area, and channel correlations uniquely explain 4.9% in FFA and 1.2% in PPA versus none in V1–V3.


#### 4.2 iWSP identifies a data confound in story-listening fMRI

We next apply iWSP to a language fMRI dataset, in which subjects listen to narrative stories and the agent builds a model to predict responses from the words. iWSP rewards anything that raises its prediction performance, including confounds. Because iWSP models are interpretable, we can use this as an advantage: by reading the best-performing iWSP model, we identify a data confound at the edge of each fMRI scan that, once understood, can be removed.


##### Experimental setup.

Encoding models of fMRI responses to natural language are often used to study language processing in the brain Huth et al., 2016 ; Schrimpf et al., 2021 ; Antonello et al., 2023 ; Jain et al., 2024 ; Singh et al., 2025 ; Mitchell et al., 2008 ; Wehbe et al., 2014 ; Nishida and Nishimoto, 2018 ; Kumar et al., 2022 ; Caucheteux et al., 2023 . We use data from two recent studies LeBel et al., 2022 ; Tang et al., 2023 , with fMRI responses from three subjects listening to more than 20 hours of narrative stories from podcasts. We analyze one subject (UTS03) across 84 story scans, each 157 to 521 volumes long at one volume per 2 s (one TR). Each scan covers 95,556 voxels. The preprocessed release z-scores every voxel within each scan and has already removed 20 TRs per scan. The search fits ridge regression on 8 stories and evaluates it on 3 other stories, which form the visible evaluation set: the agent sees this performance at every iteration. After the search, we also test selected models on a hidden evaluation set of 10 other stories, drawn at random and never seen by the agent, with ridge regression still fit on the original 8 stories. Unless noted, performance below is visible performance.

We run iWSP with Claude Opus 4.7, and use a fixed evaluator that (i) aligns the features to the TRs, (ii) adds delays of one to four TRs for the slow hemodynamic response, (iii) fits ridge regression and (iv) returns the mean correlation across voxels on the visible stories. For every word in a story, the model is fed the word and the 10 words before it, and returns one feature vector per word, which the evaluator then aligns to the TRs. As a black-box baseline, we feed these same contexts to GPT-2 XL Radford et al., 2019 and take the hidden state of the final token at layer 24 out of 48 (chosen via cross-validation over the layers 12, 24, and 36).

Figure 4: A jump in performance during iWSP iterations identifies a dataset confound. a , After growing gradually, the performance jumps in a single step by adding features that code for a word’s position in the story rather than its semantics. b , Debugging this feature identifies that the beginning/end of each scan has a characteristic pattern unrelated to the content of the stories (black line). Predicting using just position features (blue line) can track this measured response well, providing potentially misleading improvements for models that are not explaining genuine signal in brain activity.


##### A data confound unrelated to language processing.

For most of the iWSP run, features of word content improve slowly, scoring well below the 0.079 correlation achieved by GPT-2 XL ( Fig. 4 a). Then, performance suddenly jumps from 0.067 to 0.104 in a single step. We find that the new features added at this step surprisingly do not encode a word’s semantic content, but rather only its position in the story. Further analysis shows that these position features alone reach about 0.090, above even the strong GPT-2 XL black-box baseline. To investigate these position features, we average the response across voxels and scans at each position in the scan, and find that it starts abnormally high and ends abnormally low compared to the rest of the scan ( Fig. 4 b, black line); this signal appears in all three subjects ( Fig. A8 ). The position features closely track this signal: fit to half of the scans and tested on the other half, they follow this average response curve at a correlation of 0.97, and the jump they cause also appears on the hidden set ( Table A6 ).

This signal is potentially problematic because it follows scan timing rather than story content, potentially providing misleading changes in measured performance for different encoding models. This signal is not entirely unknown: the dataset release removes 10 TRs at each end of every scan to avoid onset transients and poor detrending LeBel et al., 2022 , and Antonello et al. noted that responses in some voxels drift over the first minute of each scan Antonello et al., 2023 , which lets long-context features inflate measured performance by up to 20%. To correct for this, they excluded the first 100 seconds of each test story when evaluating, a convention later adopted by other work Moussa et al., 2024 ; Binhuraib et al., 2025 . Our results suggest that for future studies that are sensitive to context lengths or story position, they should go further in addressing this confound by trimming the edges of each scan (in training as well as testing) by roughly 40 TRs. Note that this dataset remains of exceptional quality and that the many valuable conclusions drawn from it (which essentially all use word-level or short-context encoding models) stand; identifying this confound is more preventative, as non-experts and agents continue doing analyses on this type of data in the future.


##### Interpreting the fitted iWSP model.

After this trimming, we find that iWSP can still build interpretable models that focus on semantics, performing on par with GPT-2 XL embeddings ( Fig. A6 ). Each feature of these models is readable and has its own ridge regression weights, so it can be traced onto the cortex; Fig. A7 shows one feature that encodes words related to mental processing or speech. As in the previous sections, we again describe the iWSP models (from 8 points along the iWSP iterations) in a text description of at most 200 words. We find that this is sufficient for a fresh agent to rebuild each of them closely enough to achieve similar performance, including the story-position features of the first model after the jump ( Fig. A9 ).


#### 4.3 iWSP identifies interpretable, performant features for predicting ECoG responses to audio

We next study ECoG recordings of responses to natural audio, where we compare iWSP with embeddings from Whisper Radford et al., 2022 , a standard, pretrained speech model, which is established as a highly performant feature space for encoding Antonello et al., 2023 . We find that interpretable raw-waveform features from iWSP reach similar prediction performance as Whisper, and that they can explain part of the response that Whisper fails to predict.


##### Experimental setup.

We predict high-gamma activity at 30 Hz from 166 intracranial electrodes in one patient, recorded continuously over 7 days Zada et al., 2025 ; Goldstein et al., 2023 . The stimulus is the raw audio from the room, cut into segments of about two minutes. Ridge regression is fit on 500 segments (about 16 hours of data). The visible evaluation set is 23 consecutive segments (about 44 minutes) during which a podcast was playing: the agent sees the prediction performance on this set at every iteration, and uses it to make inclusion and exclusion decisions about its feature choices. The hidden evaluation set is another 317 speech-rich segments from the same session (about 10 hours of data).

We run iWSP with Claude Opus 4.7, editing a model that maps the audio waveform to features. A fixed evaluator adds 4 time-lagged delays, shifts the features by 100 ms, fits a ridge regression for each electrode and returns the mean correlation on the visible evaluation set. Whisper-large-v3 encoder features go through the same evaluator. As an interpretable baseline, five isolated Claude Opus 5.5 sessions each write, in one shot and without performance feedback, the feature space an expert would assemble from standard acoustic features (spectrograms, cepstral coefficients, pitch and loudness), using any open-source library. These textbook features also go through the same evaluator. The search ran 902 iterations, plateauing after about 500 iterations. Partway through the loop, we switched to optimizing the 125 surface-grid electrodes, where Whisper’s advantage was concentrated. We still evaluate on all 166 electrodes using the hidden set.

##### iWSP on its own slightly underperforms Whisper.

On the hidden set, using the grid electrodes, iWSP starts at 0.039 with a plain gammatone cochleagram and ends at 0.074, below the 0.082 of Whisper ( Fig. 5 a). The 3,852 features in the final model are combinations of known, interpretable kernels, and each gain comes from hand-implementing the following: compression and adaptation, spectro-temporal modulation filters Chi et al., 2005 , slow context over the previous seconds, and wavelet scattering Andén and Mallat, 2013 ( Fig. 5 b). On the hidden set, over all 166 electrodes, iWSP reaches 0.065 against 0.070 for Whisper and leads on 77 electrodes. A 197-word description of these features is enough for fresh agents to rebuild the model with nearly the same hidden-set performance ( Fig. A12 ). The search goes beyond the textbook features: the iWSP model passes them early in the search, while it adds the modulation filters, and stays well above them ( Fig. 5 a, d). It also outperforms them on all five more patients described in Sec. A.4 .


##### iWSP predicts signal that Whisper misses.

On the hidden set, the iWSP features capture unique variance that Whisper does not: they alone explain 6% of the combined model’s variance, Whisper alone explains 25% and the two share 69% ( Fig. 5 c). As a result, combining both in one ridge regression significantly improves on Whisper alone, raising performance from 0.070 to 0.075 (this gain of 0.0052 has a 95% interval of 0.0039 to 0.0064 from a bootstrap over electrodes). The improvement happens across many electrodes ( Fig. 5 e): 126 of 166 improve and 40 get worse. On five more patients, each evaluated on a hidden recording session, Whisper outperforms the iWSP model on every patient, but the iWSP features still explain variance that Whisper misses in four of the six ( Fig. A11 ).


##### Which features are important.

Because every feature has a formula, we can ask which ones the model relies on ( Fig. A10 ). On the visible set, the feature families overlap heavily, and 87% of the variance explained in the brain responses cannot be assigned to any one of them. The cochleagram with slow context and a set of modulation filters predict the most unique variance, and one feature, spectral flatness, reaches 29% of the full model alone.


### 5 Related works


##### Inherently interpretable models.

A long line of work aims to build highly accurate models without sacrificing interpretability Rudin, 2019 ; Singh et al., 2021 . In text, this began with word-level and ngram-level models Huth et al., 2016 ; Lewis, 1998 ; Bojanowski et al., 2016 ; Joulin et al., 2016 , which have continued to improve by using modern techniques to improve their construction Liu et al., 2024 ; Kim et al., 2024 ; Singh et al., 2023a . At the same time, concept-based models have become popular by routing predictions through a bottleneck of interpretable concepts Koh et al., 2020 ; Sun et al., 2024 ; Benara et al., 2024 ; Guide Labs Team et al., 2026 ; Feng et al., 2026 ; Ludan et al., 2023 ; Tan et al., 2024 . However, the constrained form of these models has struggled to make accurate predictions for many hard problems, which iWSP aims to solve.


##### Agents that search for programs.

Iterative coding agents offer a way around constrained model forms, because they can search a far larger space of readable models than traditional interpretable models can. AlphaEvolve and related works Romera-Paredes et al., 2023 ; Novikov et al., 2025 ; Georgiev et al., 2025 iteratively write programs to solve particular problems in domains like math, and have been applied in domains like human and animal learning from data Kasenberg et al., 2026 , or more broadly in automating the entire scientific pipeline Lu et al., 2024 ; Gottweis et al., 2025 ; Swanson et al., 2025 ; Ifargan et al., 2024 ; Tang et al., 2025 ; Mitchener et al., 2025 (although benchmarks and critiques question their reliability Chen et al., 2024 ; Luo et al., 2025 ). iWSP follows a similar loop to these methods, but instead focuses on writing model weights and features.


##### Translating programs into weights.

The systems above write programs rather than neural network weights. A separate line of work connects the two by compiling programs into networks. RASP is a small language whose primitives map onto attention and MLP operations Weiss et al., 2021 , and Tracr compiles RASP programs into transformer weights Lindner et al., 2023 . Learning Transformer Programs goes the other way, training transformers constrained to decompile into readable code, or decompiling them afterward Friedman et al., 2023 ; Thurnherr and Riesen, 2024 ; Huang et al., 2026 . Unlike these methods, iWSP produces networks without an intermediate domain-specific language or compiler.


##### Networks that generate weights directly.

Weights can also be written by another network instead of an agent. Hypernetworks are networks that write the weights of other networks Ha et al., 2016 ; Chauhan et al., 2024 ; Han et al., 2026 , and HyperNEAT evolves a compact network whose outputs encode the weights of a much larger one Stanley et al., 2009 . Later work generates weights from collections of trained models, by sampling from learned representations of model zoos Schürholt et al., 2022 , by running a diffusion model on checkpoints Peebles et al., 2022 or by predicting parameters for unseen architectures Knyazev et al., 2021 . Weight-space architectures respect the symmetries of these inputs Navon et al., 2023 . Like iWSP, these methods can set a target network’s weights without running gradient descent on it, but they generally fail to provide explanations for their weight choices.


##### Post-hoc interpretation.

All of the work above builds models, whereas the related field of post-hoc interpretability explains models that are already trained. Attribution methods estimate how much each input contributes to a prediction Ribeiro et al., 2016 ; Lundberg and Lee, 2017 ; Selvaraju et al., 2017 ; Singh et al., 2019 . Mechanistic interpretability instead reads a model’s internals Elhage et al., 2021 ; Geva et al., 2021 ; Olsson et al., 2022 ; Wang et al., 2022 ; Merullo et al., 2023 ; Lieberum et al., 2023 ; Wu et al., 2024 ; Gao et al., 2024 and reverse-engineers small trained models Nanda et al., 2023 ; Quirke and Barez, 2023 . LLMs and agents now automate much of this work by explaining in language what parts of a network do Bills et al., 2023 ; Singh et al., 2023b ; Hernandez et al., 2022 ; Paulo et al., 2024 ; Shaham et al., 2024 ; Liu et al., 2026 ; Marin-Llobet and Ferrando, 2026 ; Zaigrajew et al., 2026 ; Khan et al., 2026 . In contrast, iWSP starts from explanations and uses these to implement and test weights.


### 6 Discussion

The main advance in this work is a method to build models that are both predictive and interpretable: instead of training a model and explaining it afterward, an agent writes it by hand and revises it based on its measured performance. By applying iWSP across domains, we show that it can produce readable transformers for algorithmic tasks, and fairly accurate encoding models in predicting brain responses to different modalities. More broadly, iWSP materializes explicit hypotheses as model weights that can be read, tested and revised, offering a path to align the explanations that science seeks with the predictive power of data-driven models.

In neuroscience specifically, the field has always been concerned with finding the features of stimuli that selectively drive activity in the brain. Traditionally this problem has been approached by manually searching for these stimulus properties across multiple experiments Kanwisher and Yovel, 2006 ; Kamps et al., 2016 while data-driven methods Jain and Huth, 2018 ; Toneva and Wehbe, 2019 ; Abnar et al., 2019 ; Goldstein et al., 2022 ; Antonello et al., 2023 have explored a much larger model space, but sometimes failed to translate their results to human-understandable findings. iWSP enables a more grounded, prior-based search to draw interpretable conclusions from data.

An important ancillary benefit of iWSP in neuroscience will be to help make datasets usable for future data-driven discovery. The signal we found at the start and end of each scan in the language dataset ( Sec. 4.2 ) echoes the usefulness of earlier work on inherently interpretable models. For example, in a past study Caruana et al., 2015 , a generalized additive model predicting pneumonia risk identified that asthma lowered predicted risk of death, because patients with asthma received more aggressive care, helping identify issues with confounding that can be used to correct a model. As data-driven methods become increasingly powerful, interpretability will be more important in identifying and mitigating such confounds (and reward hacking more generally Amodei et al., 2016 ; Skalse et al., 2022 ; Bergen et al., 2026 ).


##### Limitations.

Despite these strengths, iWSP has important limitations in both the models it discovers and the evidence they provide. First, current agents do not invent genuinely new computational principles. Instead, they explore and combine known motifs drawn from their prior knowledge, such as textbook algorithms on synthetic tasks ( Sec. 3 ) or established cortical operations like Gabor filters, divisive normalization, and modulation filters on brain data ( Secs. 4.1 and 4.3 ). Systematically combining known motifs is already productive, but proposing creatively distinct, unfamiliar hypotheses remains beyond current models. Relatedly, the discovered models rely on shallow motifs rather than deep, multi-layer hierarchies: our brain encoding searches assemble parallel banks of one- or two-stage computations into a linear readout, and our NLP transformers stack only a few rule-based stages. Programming deep hierarchical representations without backpropagation remains an open problem, which likely explains why iWSP matches pretrained networks in early visual cortex while trailing deeper models in ventral visual cortex, harder language tasks, and speech ECoG. At the method level, iWSP relies on an agent’s written explanations remaining faithful to its weight edits; while our audits and text-bottleneck rebuilds ( Secs. 3 , 4.1 , 4.2 and 4.3 ) support this for current frontier models, stronger agents will require continued vetting to ensure they do not embed opaque heuristics. Running search on neural datasets is also slow and costly, requiring hundreds of candidate evaluations and a large token budget, although these costs continue to fall as LLMs become faster and cheaper. Finally, our neuroscience experiments are observational: they include relatively few subjects, test generalization within similar populations, and lack causal interventions. In visual cortex, for instance, area attributions and driving images reflect the specific hypotheses written into one 865-line model on the data that guided its search, showing which explicit computations predict responses rather than identifying unique causal mechanisms in the brain.


##### Future work.

The work here opens the door to a great deal of future research. One idea is to use iWSP to explain black-box models by approximating them with an interpretable network: this could help build a mechanistic understanding of when they work/fail, improving over modern toy models used in mechanistic interpretability Elhage et al., 2022 ; Nanda et al., 2023 ; Chughtai et al., 2023 ; Quirke and Barez, 2023 ; Lindner et al., 2023 . In data-scarce domains (often the case in neuroscience and medicine), another path is to leverage the explainability offered by iWSP to allow domain experts to help improve model adaptation Amershi et al., 2014 ; Holzinger, 2016 ; Ross et al., 2017 ; Teso and Kersting, 2019 ; Feng et al., 2026 ; Schramowski et al., 2020 . Finally, iWSP could also serve as an automated audit of widely used datasets, and could be applied to a much broader range of domains and tasks, including other neuroscience modalities and beyond. The capability of frontier models to do these weight-level tasks is exciting for domains like science, and is highly verifiable, making it possible to rapidly improve at this capability. We hope iWSP will help move toward a future in which the models we rely on for science are ones that we can interpret and trust.

#### A.2 Computations of the visual cortex model


##### The iWSP model cascades a V1-like filter bank into five explicit mid-level modules.

The iWSP model is a Python program of 865 lines with no learned weights that mirrors the hierarchical organization of biological vision. At a high level, the model first passes the image through a shared V1-like frontend to extract normalized local edge and contrast maps, branches into five parallel mid-level modules that capture higher-order structure, and finally pools these maps over a foveated spatial grid. In the frontend, pixel intensities undergo power-law compression and are split into one luminance and two color-opponent channels (red–green and blue–yellow). Each channel is convolved at three spatial scales with center–surround filters and oriented Gabor filters spanning eight orientations. The responses are then passed through standard cortical nonlinearities: quadrature Gabor pairs are combined into phase-invariant complex-cell energy [ Adelson and Bergen, 1985 ] (the square root of summed squared responses), center–surround responses are half-wave rectified into ON and OFF channels, and all channels undergo local divisive normalization followed by logarithmic compression to encode contrast relative to the surrounding image context.

From this normalized V1 representation, the model computes five kinds of mid-level features: (i) texture boundaries , which pass the first-stage energy maps through a second bank of Gabor filters and energy pooling (a classical filter–rectify–filter cascade) to detect where patterns of oriented contrast change; (ii) contour curvature , which takes spatial derivatives of the local orientation field to measure how rapidly edge orientation bends across space and relative to its surround; (iii) edge phase , which compares even and odd Gabor responses rectified by sign to distinguish thin bright ridges, dark creases, and step edges between surfaces; (iv) feature co-occurrences , which multiply pairs of centered, normalized channels and average them spatially to measure which orientations, colors, curvatures, and phases appear together within and across spatial scales, following classical parametric texture models [ Portilla and Simoncelli, 2000 ] ; and (v) surface geometry , which acts directly on smoothed luminance and color derivatives to classify local brightness surfaces into five 3D shading primitives (pits, valleys, saddles, ridges, and bumps) alongside corner strength and isophote curvature. Fig. 3 d shows the weights and operations of five of the model’s computations. Finally, every feature map is summarized by its mean and spatial standard deviation over a foveated grid of image windows (finer near the center of gaze) and normalized per block, yielding 9,996 explicit features. Aside from the analytic tuning curves used to categorize the five surface shapes, nearly every nonlinearity in the program reduces to one of five canonical operations from visual neuroscience: power/log compression, half-wave rectification, quadrature energy, divisive normalization, and pairwise channel multiplication.


##### Attributing each area’s prediction to computations.

Each of the 9,996 output features is written by one stage of the program, and we group the features into 14 computations by that stage. Where a stage summarizes a map both by its average within the cells of a spatial grid and by its variability within those cells, we treat the two summaries as separate computations. Fig. A3 lists the 14 computations with their feature counts. To attribute an area’s prediction, we fit the ridge regression once with all features, as in the search. For one voxel, the prediction on the shared images is a sum over computations, $\hat{y}=\sum_{c}\hat{y}_{c}$ , where $\hat{y}_{c}$ uses only the features and ridge weights of computation $c$ . The correlation between the voxel’s prediction and the measured response $y$ then splits exactly into one term per computation:

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{corr}(\hat{y},y)=\sum_{c}\frac{\mathrm{cov}(\hat{y}_{c},y)}{\sigma_{\hat{y}}\,\sigma_{y}}.$$

We average each term over the voxels of an area, divide by the area’s mean correlation so that the shares sum to 100%, and then average over the five LAION-fMRI subjects. A share is negative when a computation’s part of the prediction is anticorrelated with the response. Correlated computations divide the credit between them, and the model scales its feature blocks differently, so a share describes what the fitted ridge regression uses, not what it needs. We therefore also measure each computation’s unique variance: we refit ridge without the computation’s features and report the drop in squared correlation as a percentage of the full model’s squared correlation ( Table A4 ). The maps in Fig. 3 d use the finest image scale, and the matrix of channel correlations uses half that resolution, as in the model. The five surface-shape tiles in Fig. 3 d illustrate the brightness profiles that the code separates and are not filter weights. The matrix of channel correlations has $24\times 24$ entries, with positive values in blue and negative values in orange.

##### Search constraints and held-out evaluation.

In every candidate, each filter and weight is set analytically or generated from a fixed random seed. Candidates are limited to fewer than 10,000 output features so that a model cannot succeed merely by handing the ridge regression an overcomplete random basis. On NSD, we evaluate the four subjects who completed all scanning sessions, fitting the linear readout on each subject’s unique images and testing on the shared images after excluding any stimulus that also appears in LAION-fMRI, which leaves 998 test images. The seven interpretable baselines are Gabor filters, Gabor filters with divisive normalization [ Heeger, 1992 ] , GIST [ Oliva and Torralba, 2001 ] , HMAX [ Riesenhuber and Poggio, 1999 ] , wavelet scattering [ Bruna and Mallat, 2013 ] , Portilla–Simoncelli texture statistics [ Portilla and Simoncelli, 2000 ] , and a contour curvature model. For AlexNet, we use for each stream the layer that scores best on the three search subjects and visible images of LAION-fMRI, averaged over subjects and fixed before any held-out evaluation: conv3 for early and fc6 for ventral visual cortex. The same two layers are used for held-out LAION-fMRI and for NSD. For the combined baseline, we concatenate the features of the seven baselines (18,386 features in total) and fit the same ridge regression, with one penalty per voxel. Before concatenation, we center each baseline’s features and divide them by their root mean squared norm over the training images, so that the seven baselines enter the regression with equal weight; without this rescaling, the combined baseline scores lower in ventral visual cortex on both datasets. Across individual subjects, the iWSP model exceeds the strongest single baseline in every NSD subject by 0.035 to 0.054 in early visual cortex and by 0.057 to 0.064 in ventral visual cortex. On the two held-out LAION-fMRI subjects, the best model of the three-subject ablation search reaches 0.466 pooled correlation (0.518 versus 0.472 for Gabor filters with divisive normalization in early visual cortex, higher by 0.044 to 0.047 per subject; 0.409 versus 0.309 for Gabor filters with divisive normalization in ventral visual cortex, higher by 0.096 to 0.105 per subject). On the three search subjects of that run, the model scores identically on held-out and visible shared images (0.536 versus 0.536 pooled correlation), showing that the lower absolute correlation on the two held-out subjects reflects subject differences that affect baselines as well (Gabor filters with divisive normalization fall from 0.451 on the search subjects to 0.395 on the held-out subjects).


##### Area readouts and synthesized images.

To see which images drive the model’s prediction for each area ( Fig. A4 ), we fit the ridge readout for every voxel of an area as in the search, average the voxel weight vectors within the area, and then average over the five LAION-fMRI subjects. The resulting area readout predicts the area-mean response, and the shared images are held out of every readout fit. It predicts the measured area-mean response to the shared images with a correlation of 0.82 in hV4, 0.53 in FFA, and 0.70 in PPA. The images with the highest predicted response are dense, multicolored objects for hV4, faces and people for FFA, and buildings and other rectilinear scenes for PPA; the 20 highest-ranked images of each area evoke measured responses 1.3 to 1.7 standard deviations above the area’s mean. Because the model is differentiable, we can also synthesize an image that maximizes each area readout. To synthesize an image, we start from noise and run gradient ascent on the area readout, with the image parametrized in the Fourier domain and shifted by up to 6 pixels at random on each step. We fix the image mean at 0.5 and the pixel standard deviation at 0.2, because without this constraint the optimum is a blank image. Two blocks of global image statistics (82 features) have no usable gradient and are held constant. The synthesized images reach predicted responses 6.5 (hV4), 9.6 (FFA), and 10.3 (PPA) standard deviations above the mean prediction for the shared images, where the highest natural image reaches about 2.5, so they show the direction the readout favors rather than a response the model has been tested on. The synthesized images agree with the natural ones: fine multicolored texture for hV4, a central skin-toned blob for FFA, and a lattice of horizontal and vertical lines for PPA. These images show which image statistics the model associates with each area; they do not show that the model represents faces or scenes as categories.

##### Rebuilding the visual cortex model from text.

To test whether a short description preserves the iWSP model’s computations, Claude Opus 5.5 reads the model’s code and summarizes it in 185 words ( Fig. A5 d). We give only that description and the task to four independent rebuilding sessions. Each rebuilt feature extractor receives a newly fitted ridge readout under the original five-subject LAION-fMRI protocol. Mean pooled correlation is 0.451 for the rebuilds and 0.488 for the original, and in each of six visual areas every rebuild scores below the original and above normalized Gabor features ( Fig. A5 a, b). The rebuilds recover 37% of the original’s correlation gain over normalized Gabor features (correlation 0.430), indicating partial reconstruction of the improvement beyond this baseline.

Similar accuracy does not show that the rebuilds compute the same thing, so we also compare what the models predict. For each voxel, we correlate two models’ predicted responses to the 1,492 shared images. The rebuilds’ predictions correlate with the original’s at 0.86 to 0.88, compared with 0.81 for pretrained AlexNet conv2, a model of similar accuracy (correlation 0.461), and 0.80 for normalized Gabor features. Because every model shares a large Gabor-like component, we repeat the comparison after regressing each prediction on the normalized Gabor prediction, voxel by voxel. On this remainder, the rebuilds correlate with the original at 0.64 to 0.69, against 0.50 for AlexNet conv2, and every rebuild exceeds AlexNet conv2 in each of the five subjects. Over each subject’s 10% of voxels best predicted by the original, the original correlates with the rebuilds at 0.94 (0.74 to 0.76 after the regression) and with AlexNet conv2 at 0.91 (0.57 after the regression). The description therefore carries computations specific to the original, but only in part: the rebuilds agree more with one another (0.77 to 0.83) than with the original, so they share a reading of the text that differs from the code. Two further comparisons do not favor the rebuilds. Across voxels, the gain of each model over normalized Gabor features correlates with the original’s gain at 0.61 to 0.73 for the rebuilds and at 0.73 for AlexNet conv2. The pattern of per-voxel accuracy correlates at 0.96 or above for every pair of models, including both references, so it does not distinguish them. Linear centered kernel alignment between original and rebuilt features ranges from 0.71 to 0.76 on 1,000 test images. The rebuilds are also smaller than the original, with 292 to 358 lines of code against 742 and about a third of its numeric constants ( Table A5 ).

##### The text bottleneck along the search.

The comparison above uses only the final model, so we repeat the text bottleneck on the search’s best model at eight evenly spaced iterations: 1, 87, 172, 258, 343, 429, 514, and 600. As before, Claude Opus 5.5 describes each model in at most 200 words (188 to 200 used), and two independent sessions rebuild each model from its description and the task alone. We score the originals and the rebuilds under the five-subject LAION-fMRI protocol of the search. One of the 16 rebuilds, at iteration 429, fails when run on the GPU and is left out.

The first model is a random projection of the pixels (pooled correlation of 0.148), and both of its rebuilds reproduce its score exactly. Across the eight models, the rebuilds keep 96% of the original’s performance on average. For the seven later models they keep between 92% (iteration 514) and 98% (iteration 87), and every rebuild outperforms normalized Gabor features (correlation 0.430; Fig. A5 a). No rebuild reaches its original: the originals improve from 0.467 at iteration 87 to 0.488 at iteration 600, while the rebuilds stay between 0.445 and 0.470. Over the same iterations the program grows from 373 to 865 lines. A description of 200 words thus conveys most of each model, and the small improvements of the later iterations are the part it conveys least. The two rebuilds of the final model, which come from a new description, reach 0.456 and 0.470, in line with the four rebuilds above (0.447 to 0.454; open circles in Fig. A5 a).

We also ask whether the rebuilds of a model predict like their own original more than like the other checkpoints and standard baselines ( Fig. A5 c). Comparing the seven sets of rebuilds against a 17-row reference set consisting of the seven later originals, the seven classical baselines, and AlexNet conv2, conv3, and fc6 requires no additional rebuilding runs. Each original correlates with its own rebuilds at 0.895 on average, versus 0.753 across all 112 off-diagonal pairs ( $p=4.7\times 10^{-7}$ , exact permutation test over all 98,017,920 ways of pairing seven of the 17 models with the seven sets of rebuilds). The ten baselines correlate with the rebuilds at 0.679 on average, from 0.520 for HMAX to 0.773 for normalized Gabor features and AlexNet conv2, and for every rebuilt checkpoint all seven search originals agree more closely with its predictions than any baseline does. Within the seven search checkpoints alone, off-diagonal agreement is 0.877 ( $p=0.009$ over all 5,040 pairings among the seven originals) because checkpoints from one search share most of their computations; from iteration 258 onward, the originals agree almost equally with each set of rebuilds, and an original agrees most with its own rebuilds for 3 of the 7 models.
