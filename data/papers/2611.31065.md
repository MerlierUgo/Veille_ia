## Decoupling Solving from Abstention: Single-Rollout Conformal Risk Control for Hard-Budget LLM Reasoning

Reasoning with large language models (LLMs) relies on extended intermediate computation, so enforcing a hard token budget often exhausts the limit before an answer is produced. Forcing thinking to terminate recovers many correct solutions, yet it also exposes far more confident mistakes—turning budgeted reasoning into a coupled challenge of solving within the cap and deciding when to abstain. Existing approaches address these goals at high cost: supervised fine-tuning provides no deployment error bound, wrong-answer penalties in reinforcement learning (RL) can discourage weak solvers from attempting hard problems, and multi-sample conformal methods multiply inference compute. We introduce Tail-CRC, a single-rollout framework that decouples candidate generation from submission control. Tail-CRC first adapts a solver to a budget-forcing environment using pure binary correctness rewards, then freezes the solver and applies conformal risk control to the upper-tail token surprise of its answer span. A single trajectory supplies both the answer and its uncertainty score using only returned token log probabilities, yielding finite-sample control of expected wrong submissions under exchangeability while allowing risk targets or target domains to be recalibrated without retraining. Theoretically, we show how wrong-answer penalties introduce failure-only comparisons into group-relative RL, derive an exact exploration-starvation boundary in an attempt–solver learning model, and characterize length-induced ranking reversal and single-token spike sensitivity in answer scoring. Across Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3-8B on DAPO and out-of-domain Omni-MATH, 50 RL updates on 400 problems raise correct-submission rates by 15.0–29.6 and 4.3–12.8 percentage points over inference-only budget forcing while reducing generation cost by 3.5–12.6%. On 8B Omni-MATH, target-domain calibration lowers wrong submissions from 30.3% to 5.8% at a 4K budget and from 41.8% to 6.5% at 8K. Finally, 4K generation runs 2.05 $\times$ faster for 4B and 2.23 $\times$ faster for 8B than unforced 8K base decoding in local amortized batch time, demonstrating that decoupled solving and single-rollout calibration offer a practical path to reliable, efficient budgeted reasoning.


### 1 Introduction

Chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning and adaptive test-time compute have substantially expanded what large language models (LLMs) can solve [ 34 , 31 ] , yet real deployments rarely offer an unbounded token budget. Under a hard output cap, a reasoning model must convert its intermediate derivation into a finished answer before tokens run out. This constraint turns inference into a three-way outcome: a correct submission, a wrong submission, or no submission at all. Standard accuracy treats the latter two identically, even though withholding an answer and exposing a confident mistake carry very different costs for downstream users. Reliable budgeted reasoning therefore requires solving two distinct problems: producing more correct answers within the cap, and deciding which completed answers are safe to submit.

Our evaluation of Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3-8B [ 27 , 35 ] highlights how sharply these two goals collide. At a 4K token cap, uninterrupted Native decoding exhausts the budget without submitting an answer on 88.0–99.2% of problems across two mathematics benchmarks. Following s1’s budget-forcing strategy [ 25 ] , injecting an end-of-thinking delimiter reserves tokens for answer synthesis and immediately recovers many solutions: on Qwen3-8B DAPO [ 36 ] , correct submissions jump from 7.6% to 28.6%. At the same time, however, wrong submissions surge more than twentyfold, from 0.4% to 10.6% (Figure 1 ). Meeting the deadline does not eliminate failure; it shifts hidden truncations into visible errors.

Native is uninterrupted base-model generation; Budget forcing applies s1-style thinking termination [ 25 ] . Binary RL trains with GRPO Done Right (Dr. GRPO) using binary correctness rewards [ 22 ] ; Penalty RL adds a $-1$ wrong-answer penalty, and Explicit-Abstain RL additionally allows an explicit abstention action. On DAPO, Tail-CRC strictly dominates Budget forcing and Penalty RL—submitting more right answers and fewer wrong answers while abstaining less. On Omni-MATH, calibration reduces wrong submissions to 5.8% at the 6.5% target and yields the highest net score among the displayed methods. Tail-CRC also leads all three penalty-based RL baselines after matched post-hoc calibration (Table 2 ).

Existing remedies struggle because they either leave deployment risk uncontrolled or pay a steep price in capability or compute. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) can teach a model to reason from worked examples [ 25 ] , but it leaves the deployment error rate uncontrolled. Incorporating wrong-answer penalties or abstention rewards into reinforcement learning (RL) couples caution directly into training [ 23 ] ; under a hard cap, however, a weak solver can avoid penalties simply by exhausting the budget, starving itself of the very attempts needed to improve. Conformal methods can calibrate a rule for constructing candidate sets or aggregating reasoning paths [ 26 , 12 ] . Each additional sample adds inference cost.

We instead decouple learning to solve from deciding when to abstain. We introduce Tail-CRC , a two-stage, single-rollout framework that keeps candidate generation and risk control separate. In the first stage, we train the solver inside the budget-forcing environment using RL with pure binary correctness rewards, encouraging it to finish as many correct solutions as possible within the cap. We then freeze the solver weights and apply conformal risk control (CRC) [ 3 ] to an uncertainty score computed from the upper tail of token surprises across the answer span. Because a single rollout supplies both the candidate answer and its score using only the token log probabilities routinely returned by hosted APIs, deployment requires no extra model calls and no auxiliary verifier. Under exchangeability, CRC guarantees finite-sample control of expected wrong submissions regardless of solver accuracy. Because calibration is decoupled from training, operators can change risk targets by adjusting the cutoff, or adapt to a new domain by recalibrating on a few hundred labeled target problems, without retraining the solver.

Figure 1 shows that Tail-CRC’s reliability gains do not come from trivially abstaining more. On DAPO at 4K, Tail-CRC actually submits more answers overall than both Budget forcing and Penalty RL (41.0% vs. 39.2% and 39.6% coverage), yet achieves higher correct submissions (33.9% vs. 28.6% and 29.4%) and lower wrong submissions (7.1% vs. 10.6% and 10.2%)—strictly dominating both baselines. On the harder out-of-domain Omni-MATH benchmark, Budget forcing and the unfiltered RL policies expose wrong answers on roughly one in five to nearly one in three problems (18.7–30.3%). There, Tail-CRC targets a 6.5% wrong-submission rate by withholding more predictions, cutting errors to 5.8% and more than doubling net score ( $R-W$ ) over Penalty RL (10.8% vs. 5.3%). When every penalty and explicit-abstention baseline is granted the same post-hoc tail-score calibration, Binary RL with Tail-CRC still achieves the highest net score across all twelve matched 4K comparisons (Section 5 , Table 2 ).

Both halves of this design are grounded in concrete failure modes. On the training side, we show exactly how wrong-answer penalties activate failure-only group comparisons in group-relative RL, and derive a sharp exploration-starvation boundary in an attempt–solver model explaining how premature penalties can halt improvement. On the scoring side, relying only on final boxed-answer likelihood can miss uncertainty in the derivation and favor short wrong integers over longer correct symbolic expressions, while summing negative log likelihood (NLL) over the answer span introduces a length multiplier that can invert rankings altogether. Averaging the largest 10% of token surprises across the answer span removes this explicit length factor and limits the influence of any single lexical spike. As illustrated in Section 3.3 (Figure 3 (b)), this allows Tail-CRC to preserve a correct radical answer rejected by Box NLL while filtering out a wrong integer that Box NLL confidently accepts.

The paper makes three contributions:

- • A decoupled, single-rollout framework for hard-budget LLM reasoning. Tail-CRC separates binary-reward budget adaptation from answer-span CRC, providing finite-sample wrong-submission guarantees and retraining-free risk adaptation using one rollout’s returned token log probabilities.

- • Theoretical mechanisms for training and single-rollout scoring. Exact group-advantage identities and an analytically solved attempt–solver flow explain penalty-induced exploration starvation, while score-anatomy results isolate length-induced ranking reversal and single-token spike sensitivity (complete proofs in Appendix A ).

- • Empirical gains in accuracy, controlled risk, and inference efficiency. Across Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3-8B, two domains, and 4K/8K budgets, 50 RL updates improve correct submissions by 15.0–29.6 and 4.3–12.8 percentage points over budget forcing while lowering generation cost by 3.5–12.6%; target calibration cuts 8B Omni-MATH errors from 30.3/41.8% to 5.8/6.5%, and local 4K generation runs 2.05 $\times$ /2.23 $\times$ faster than unforced 8K base decoding.


### 2 Problem Formulation

We study a reasoner that receives one problem, generates one trajectory within a hard token budget, and either submits its answer or withholds it. The objective is to retain as many correct submissions as possible at a chosen wrong-submission risk, with the generation cost of one rollout.

Let $x$ be a problem, $z^{\star}$ its reference answer, and $\pi_{\theta}$ an autoregressive model. A fixed stochastic decoder $K_{\theta,B}(\cdot\mid x)$ generates trajectory $y$ under total token cap $B$ . Native decoding runs continuously; budget forcing reserves tokens for answer synthesis by inserting the end-of-thinking delimiter </think> if thinking exceeds its allocation. The answer span contains all sampled explanation and answer tokens after the first end-of-thinking delimiter and before end-of-sequence (EOS), whether thinking ended naturally or externally. Injected tokens and EOS are excluded from scoring. Exact 4K/8K allocations and early-ending rules are specified in Appendix B .

The observable indicator $v(x,y)$ records an EOS-terminated, extractable answer with consistent boxed contents. A valid candidate is right ( $Y=R$ ) or wrong ( $Y=W$ ) by mathematical equivalence; truncation, missing or conflicting answers, and explicit abstention give $Y=U$ . Labels require a reference answer during calibration and evaluation; validity and deployment selection do not. For a nonconformity score $S(x,y)$ , larger means more uncertain, and invalid or empty spans receive $S=+\infty$ . We submit iff

[Tableau] $$g_{\tau}(x,y)=\mathbb{I}\{v(x,y)=1,\ S(x,y)<\tau\}.$$ (1)

Define $R_{\tau}=P(Y=R,g_{\tau}=1)$ , $W_{\tau}=P(Y=W,g_{\tau}=1)$ , and $U_{\tau}=1-R_{\tau}-W_{\tau}$ . Suppressing the cutoff subscript, coverage, net score, precision, and selective error are

[Tableau] $$C=R+W,\qquad\mathrm{Net}=R-W,\qquad\mathrm{Prec}=\frac{R}{R+W},\qquad r_{\rm sel}=\frac{W}{R+W}.$$ (2)

Marginal rates use all problem families; precision and selective error use submitted answers. Generation cost includes rejected trajectories. Rates are percentages; differences are percentage points (pp).

The loss $L_{\tau}=\mathbb{I}\{Y=W,S<\tau\}$ records an exposed wrong answer. With the solver, decoder, validity rule, and score frozen independently of calibration, we select a cutoff from one rollout per labeled family so that

[Tableau] $$\E_{\mathcal{D}_{\rm cal},\,{\rm test}}[L_{\widehat{\tau}}]\leq\alpha.$$ (3)

This expected marginal risk averages over calibration, a new test family, and stochastic generation; it differs from the error rate conditional on submission. Sections 3.1 and 3.4 give the construction and guarantee.


### 3 Tail-CRC: Method and Theory


#### 3.1 Learning to solve before choosing when to submit

Tail-CRC first trains a solver to produce correct answers within the hard budget, then calibrates which candidates to submit. Separating these stages lets the submission rule adapt to a new risk target or population while keeping candidate generation fixed. Figure 2 summarizes the pipeline.

> Figure 2 : Train, score, and calibrate with Tail-CRC. Binary RL adapts the solver to the hard budget; a fixed answer-span score then measures token surprise. Labeled calibration families determine a cutoff for that score. Deployment generates one rollout and either submits or abstains. The answer span begins when thinking ends, naturally or through budget forcing; tail aggregation selects surprises across that span. Calibration is offline.

Binary RL uses terminal reward $r_{0}(Y)=\mathbb{I}\{Y=R\}$ in the same two-stage budget-forcing environment used at deployment. Dr. GRPO [ 22 ] , a variant of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) [ 30 ] , centers each group of $G$ rollout rewards, $A_{g}=r_{g}-G^{-1}\sum_{j}r_{j}$ , without standard-deviation normalization. Sampled thinking and answer tokens receive the terminal advantage with a fixed token denominator; deterministic prompt and injected delimiter tokens are masked. We freeze the final solver after training. The objective rewards a successful budgeted trajectory as a whole. Giving both sampled phases its terminal advantage allows training to improve the partial derivation and its final synthesis together. The learned solver thus supplies candidates before any deployment-specific choice about how many of their errors to expose.

A boxed answer can be a single confident token, whereas its preceding answer span exposes the transitions used to construct it. Let $\ell_{t}$ be the negative log likelihood (NLL) of sampled answer token $t$ , after the first end-of-thinking delimiter and before EOS. Natural termination and budget forcing use the same span. Order the $L$ token surprises as $\ell_{(1)}\geq\cdots\geq\ell_{(L)}$ ; Tail NLL is

[Tableau] $$S=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\ell_{(t)},\qquad k=\lceil 0.1L\rceil.$$ (4)

The tail contains the largest surprises anywhere in the answer span. Invalid outputs and empty spans receive $S=+\infty$ . The API version uses token log probabilities from the generated trajectory; Tail entropy instead uses full-vocabulary entropy at the same prefixes (Appendix A.6.4 ).

With the solver, decoder, and score frozen, calibration generates and grades one trajectory per held-out family and applies standard CRC to choose a cutoff. Deployment generates one trajectory and submits it when it is valid and $S<\widehat{\tau}$ . References are needed for calibration; submission uses only the output and score. Changing the risk target changes the cutoff without retraining or extra generation.


#### 3.2 Why binary rewards preserve learning opportunities

Under a hard cap, avoiding a wrong answer can mean exhausting the budget without submitting. To identify the training signal behind this behavior, consider Penalty RL rewards $r_{\lambda}(R)=1$ , $r_{\lambda}(W)=-\lambda$ , and $r_{\lambda}(U)=0$ , for $\lambda\geq 0$ . Probabilities below refer to the actual stochastic rollout distribution.

For $G\geq 2$ independent rollouts of a fixed prompt with probabilities $(p_{R},p_{W},p_{U})$ , set $\gamma_{G}=(G-1)/G$ . For outcomes of positive probability,

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\E[A_{1}\mid R]$ $\displaystyle=\gamma_{G}(1-p_{R}+\lambda p_{W}),$ $\displaystyle\E[A_{1}\mid W]$ $\displaystyle=-\gamma_{G}\{\lambda(1-p_{W})+p_{R}\},$ $\displaystyle\E[A_{1}\mid U]$ $\displaystyle=\gamma_{G}(\lambda p_{W}-p_{R}).$ (5)

When $p_{R}+p_{W}>0$ , the last expression is positive exactly when $p_{R}/(p_{R}+p_{W})<\lambda/(1+\lambda)$ . For $\lambda>0$ , a reward-varying group with no correct rollout has probability

[Tableau] $$(1-p_{R})^{G}-p_{W}^{G}-p_{U}^{G}.$$ (6)

Every such group has zero advantages under binary reward. For any integrable per-rollout feature $\psi$ ,

[Tableau] $$\E\!\left[G^{-1}\sum_{g}A_{g}\psi_{g}\right]=\gamma_{G}\operatorname{Cov}(r_{\lambda},\psi).$$ (7)

Proof. Appendix A.4 derives all three identities.

Non-submission has positive expected advantage below 50% submitted-answer precision for $\lambda=1$ , or 20% for $\lambda=0.25$ . Failure-only groups then favor $U$ over wrong attempts without containing a successful solution. Binary reward gives the whole group zero advantage. The comparison is prompt-specific: a difficult prompt can fall below the break-even precision even when the benchmark average is higher. A common non-submission reward $0\leq\rho<1$ is equivalent to effective penalty $(\lambda+\rho)/(1-\rho)$ up to positive reward rescaling , which group centering retains (Corollary A.1 ).

An explanatory attempt–solver model connects this signal to learning opportunities. Let $a$ be attempt probability and $q$ success probability given an attempt, so $(p_{R},p_{W},p_{U})=(aq,a(1-q),1-a)$ . The product Bernoulli mirror-gradient flow with relative solver learning rate $\kappa>0$ is

[Tableau] $$\dot{a}=a(1-a)\{(1+\lambda)q-\lambda\},\qquad\dot{q}=\kappa(1+\lambda)aq(1-q).$$ (8)

Attempts decline below break-even precision while solver improvement slows with fewer attempts. For interior $(a_{0},q_{0})$ , $\lambda>0$ , and $q_{0}<q_{c}:=\lambda/(1+\lambda)$ , starvation occurs exactly when

[Tableau] $$D_{\rm Ber}(q_{c}\|q_{0})\geq-\kappa\log(1-a_{0}),$$ (9)

where $D_{\rm Ber}$ is Bernoulli Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence. Then $a\to 0$ and $q\to q_{\infty}\leq q_{c}$ , with finite total attempt and correct-rollout exposure; otherwise $a,q\to 1$ . Binary reward reaches $(1,1)$ from every interior initialization. Appendix A.4.4 proves the invariant and boundary cases for this specified flow.

Figure 3 (a) shows the exact boundary at $\lambda=1$ , $\kappa=0.5$ : for $q_{0}=0.2$ , recovery requires $a_{0}>0.36$ . Starting at $a_{0}=0.25$ gives vanishing attempts and $q_{\infty}\approx 0.309$ ; starting at $a_{0}=0.50$ leads to recovery. The model explains how attempts can disappear before solver quality crosses break-even precision. The reward ablation tests the corresponding observable pattern: Penalty RL produces more truncation, fewer correct submissions, and longer rollouts (Table 2 ).

Equal rewards for explicit abstention and truncation express no preference between them: moving probability between these outcomes at fixed $(p_{R},p_{W})$ leaves the objective unchanged. Rewarding explicit refusal alone would break that tie. Deployment selection is a separate choice: the optimal correctness threshold generally depends on both the target and population, even when posterior correctness is unchanged. Proposition A.1 in Appendix A.5 formalizes this dependence. Tail-CRC adjusts submission by recalibration; we compare training rewards after matched calibration.


#### 3.3 Why score the upper tail of the answer span?

With the solver fixed, selection depends on score orderings. Box NLL omits the reasoning leading to an answer; Sum NLL incorporates that span but also its length; a maximum can be dominated by one unusual token. The following identities isolate these effects. For independent valid wrong and right draws, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) is $\operatorname{AUC}(S)=P(S_{W}>S_{R})+\tfrac{1}{2}P(S_{W}=S_{R})$ , with wrong answers as the positive class.

For a nonempty span, $S_{\rm sum}=L\bar{\ell}$ . With positive average surprises, define $D=\log\bar{\ell}_{W}-\log\bar{\ell}_{R}$ , $B_{\ell}=\log L_{R}-\log L_{W}$ , and $\psi(u)=\mathbb{I}\{u>0\}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{I}\{u=0\}$ .

[Tableau] $$\operatorname{AUC}(S_{\rm sum})=\E\psi(D-B_{\ell}),\qquad\operatorname{AUC}(\bar{\ell})=\E\psi(D).$$ (10)

Proof. Appendix A.6.1 factors the pairwise comparison and gives a Gaussian surrogate for ranking reversal.

A correct symbolic derivation can have lower average surprise yet greater total surprise than a short wrong answer. Mean and tail averages remove the explicit length multiplier. Figure 3 (b) illustrates a complementary problem with final-box scoring: it rejects a correct radical answer while accepting a confident wrong integer. Answer-span tail scoring reverses both decisions in these observed cases.

For $T_{k}(z)$ , the average of the $k$ largest vector entries, the standard conditional value-at-risk (CVaR) representation [ 29 ] quantifies sensitivity to single tokens.

For $z\in\mathbb{R}^{L}$ and $k\in\{1,\ldots,L\}$ ,

[Tableau] $$T_{k}(z)=\min_{\eta}\left\{\eta+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{L}(z_{t}-\eta)_{+}\right\}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq w_{t}\leq 1/k\\ \sum_{t}w_{t}=1\end{subarray}}w^{\top}z.$$ (11)

This is empirical CVaR at tail mass $k/L$ . A coordinate increase $u\geq 0$ changes $T_{k}$ by at most $u/k$ .

Proof. Appendix A.6.2 proves the identities and bound, including ties, rounding, and replication effects.

The dual weights retain high-surprise transitions while capping each token’s influence at $1/k$ ; a maximum permits the full change $u$ . The mean weights every token equally; the tail concentrates on surprising transitions while averaging across them. These score properties hold regardless of autoregressive dependence. In the sparse-error explanatory model of Appendix A.6.3 , tail averaging can improve separation by excluding background noise, whereas deterministic common background gives exact affine ranking equality with the mean. Small background variance relative to critical variance gives only a small standardized-separation gain, without establishing ranking equivalence. Empirically (Section 5 ), Tail NLL and Mean NLL perform comparably across our 36 model–domain–budget settings, splitting them 18–18, while both improve on Box NLL for 8B Binary RL on out-of-domain Omni-MATH.

(a) The explanatory attempt–solver flow at $\lambda=1$ , $\kappa=0.5$ has an exact recovery–starvation boundary; for $q_{0}=0.2$ , the critical initial attempt probability is $a_{0}=0.36$ . #### 3.4 Single-rollout finite-sample risk control

To calibrate submission, treat each problem family and its fixed-decoder output as one unit. Internal token dependence and variable trace length do not prevent exchangeability across families. Let $n$ be the calibration size, $0<\alpha<1$ the marginal wrong-submission target, $j=\lfloor(n+1)\alpha\rfloor$ , and $S^{W}_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq S^{W}_{(m)}$ the wrong calibration scores. The bounded-loss CRC rule [ 3 ] specializes to

[Tableau] $$\widehat{\tau}_{\alpha}=\begin{cases}-\infty,&j=0,\\ +\infty,&j\geq 1,\ m<j,\\ S^{W}_{(j)},&j\geq 1,\ m\geq j.\end{cases}$$ (12)

Strict acceptance rejects cutoff ties. When $j\geq 1$ , the rule allows at most $j-1$ wrong calibration submissions, accounting for the finite-sample correction for the unseen family.

Let calibration and test families be exchangeable. Fix the solver, decoder, score, and verifier independently of these data. Applying the same stochastic kernel independently to each family preserves outcome–score exchangeability, and the cutoff ( 12 ) satisfies

[Tableau] $$\E_{\mathcal{D}_{\rm cal},\,{\rm test}}\mathbb{I}\{Y_{\rm test}=W,\ S_{\rm test}<\widehat{\tau}_{\alpha}\}\leq\frac{\lfloor(n+1)\alpha\rfloor}{n+1}\leq\alpha.$$ (13)

This holds at any solver accuracy, with one test trajectory.

Proof. Appendix A.1 proves decoder-preserved exchangeability and the rank bound, including ties.

The guarantee averages over calibration and test draws. Useful retention still depends on the solver supplying correct candidates and the score ranking them above wrong ones in confidence. For $n=300$ and $\alpha=0.065$ , $j=19$ : at most 18 wrong calibration answers pass, and expected test risk is at most $19/301\approx 6.31\%$ . A realized test-panel rate, or its confidence bound, can exceed the target. Choosing the largest admissible cutoff retains every lower-score candidate consistent with the corrected calibration count. Better discrimination therefore creates room for more correct submissions under the same risk rule. This separates two empirical questions: whether training increases the correct-candidate supply, and whether the score retains that supply when submission risk is controlled.

For source and target laws $P,Q$ with outcome–score marginals $P_{Z},Q_{Z}$ , define $F_{W}^{P}(t)=P(Y=W,S<t)$ .

[Tableau] $$\E_{\mathcal{D}_{\rm cal}\sim P^{n}}F_{W}^{Q}(\widehat{\tau}_{\alpha})\leq\alpha+\sup_{t}\{F_{W}^{Q}(t)-F_{W}^{P}(t)\}\leq\alpha+\operatorname{TV}(P_{Z},Q_{Z}).$$ (14)

Proof. Appendix A.2 bounds the additional target wrong-answer mass below source cutoffs. Target-domain calibration restores the exchangeable setting without changing the solver. This motivates testing solver transfer and cutoff transfer separately: a model can retain its ability to solve target problems while the source cutoff admits too much of the target’s wrong-answer score distribution.

CRC controls marginal wrong submissions. Learn-then-Test (LTT) certifies error among submitted answers with high probability over calibration [ 2 ] . Even zero errors require 44 accepted examples for a 10% conditional-error target with ten fixed cutoffs and $\delta=0.1$ . Appendix A.3 gives the test and Theorem A.1 .


### 4 Experimental Setup

We evaluate Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3-8B at total caps of 4K and 8K tokens [ 27 , 35 ] . Binary RL uses 50 Dr. GRPO updates on 400 DAPO families: 16 prompts with eight rollouts each per update, or 6,400 trajectories per seed. Rank-32 LoRA [ 15 ] updates attention, MLP, and output projections. Training uses 4K external budget forcing, binary correctness reward, no KL penalty, and loss denominator $16\times 8\times 4096$ ; the final weights serve both caps. Controls are the pretrained model with Native decoding or Budget forcing , Answer-SFT , and Penalty RL ( $\lambda=1$ ). Two ablations use Mild-Penalty RL ( $\lambda=0.25$ ) or Explicit-Abstain RL , which adds an abstention instruction and action to the $\lambda=1$ reward. Binary RL has seeds 41 and 73; penalty arms use seed 41. All decoding uses temperature 1, top- $p=0.95$ , and top- $k=20$ . Tail-CRC filters Binary RL with calibrated Tail NLL.

DAPO supplies 400 training, 200 development, 500 calibration, and 500 test families in disjoint panels [ 36 ] . Independent Omni-MATH confirmation uses 600 verifiable families absent from tracked prior panels, split into 300 calibration and 300 test families without answer-shape quotas [ 10 ] ; test references include 37 bare integers and 263 other forms. To preserve blinding, all confirmation trajectories were generated before calibration labels were opened, and calibration cutoffs were locked before test labels were revealed. Native controls, additional penalty arms, white-box scoring, and DAPO Tail NLL analyses are supplementary studies on released panels; full protocols appear in Appendix B .

Binary RL averages its two frozen seeds within family , estimating single-seed deployment without answer aggregation. $R$ , $W$ , net score $R-W$ , and cost use all test families: 500 on DAPO and 300 on Omni; conditional error uses accepted submissions. Rates are percentages, absolute differences are percentage points (pp), and cost savings are relative to the stated comparator. AUROC uses valid right/wrong submissions with wrong answers positive; two-seed AUROC averages the seed-specific values. Pointwise 95% confidence intervals (CIs) use 20,000 paired family bootstrap resamples conditional on trained models and fitted thresholds; white-box AUROC uses up to 2,000 estimable resamples. Full estimation and calibration diagnostics appear in Appendices B and C .

#### 5.2 Q2: Which single-rollout score supports selection?

In an earlier preregistered DAPO study using Box NLL, the 8B model missed its joint risk-and-gain gates (Appendix B.5 ), motivating our shift to answer-span tail scoring. Box NLL loses information when correctness depends on a long or symbolic answer. The observed cases in Figure 3 (b) illustrate this mismatch; the independent Omni test quantifies it. For 8B Binary RL, Tail NLL versus Box NLL AUROCs are 0.756 versus 0.628 at 4K and 0.724 versus 0.622 at 8K. The paired improvements are 0.128 (95% CI $[0.043,0.216]$ ) and 0.102 ( $[0.025,0.180]$ ). Under source-calibrated cuts at $\alpha=0.065$ , accepted correct answers whose references are not written as a single integer increase from 42 to 90 at 4K and 44 to 94 at 8K, across 526 seed–family records (263 families, two seeds).

Sum NLL has a separate length problem: its AUROC is 0.111 for the 4B Budget forcing baseline on DAPO 4K and 0.289 for Answer-SFT. Proposition 2 isolates this multiplicative length confounding. Across 36 model–domain–budget cells (nine model arms $\times$ two domains $\times$ two caps; Table 7 ), Tail NLL and Mean NLL have AUROC ranges 0.630–0.862 and 0.626–0.854, respectively. Tail NLL wins 18 cells and loses 18, so the two normalized scores perform comparably across these settings. Tail NLL’s demonstrated strength is correcting the Box NLL and Sum NLL failures (Figure 5 ).

Full-vocabulary access provides an optional score upgrade. Replaying 28,800 trajectories and 15,461,536 valid answer tokens finds seven significant Tail entropy gains: six 8B DAPO cells and one 4B DAPO cell. The other intervals cross zero, including all 18 Omni cells. At 8B DAPO 8K, replacing Tail NLL with Tail entropy at $\alpha=0.065$ raises Binary RL’s $R$ from 39.1% to 43.7%, changes $W$ from 5.9% to 6.2%, and raises net score from 33.2% to 37.5%.

Writing $p_{t}$ for the model’s next-token distribution at step $t$ and $Y_{t}$ for the sampled token, sampled surprise decomposes as $-\log p_{t}(Y_{t})=H(p_{t})+b_{t}+\xi_{t}$ , where $H(p_{t})$ is full-vocabulary Shannon entropy, $b_{t}$ is predictable drift from the truncated decoder, and $\xi_{t}$ is conditionally centered sampling noise. Under raw-model sampling, the conditional variance of $\xi_{t}$ is varentropy. Tail selection can retain extreme drift or noise as well as entropy, so tokenwise centering does not equate the tail scores. Appendices A.6.4 and A.6.5 give the decomposition and its pathwise bound.

### 6 Related Work

CoT prompting and self-consistency use intermediate computation and multiple reasoning paths [ 34 , 33 ] ; test-time scaling studies how to allocate that computation [ 31 ] . STaR trains on self-generated rationales that yield correct answers [ 37 ] . Rationale distillation uses teacher-generated explanations as supervision [ 14 ] , and RL can build reasoning behavior directly [ 7 ] . For explicit budgets, s1 introduces inference-time forcing, L1 trains for chosen lengths, and ThinkPrune trains under a token limit [ 25 , 1 , 13 ] . Tail-CRC couples budget adaptation to a separate decision about which completed answers a deployment should expose.

The reject option and selective classification formalize prediction with withholding [ 6 , 11 ] . Refusal-Aware Instruction Tuning (R-Tuning) teaches uncertainty responses [ 38 ] ; reinforced hesitation distinguishes correct, incorrect, and abstaining outputs through rewards [ 23 ] . Our analysis isolates how these rewards interact with hard-budget non-submission under GRPO and Dr. GRPO [ 30 , 22 , 39 ] . Matched calibration tests which training objective supplies better answers at the same risk target.

Conformal prediction calibrates set coverage [ 32 , 20 ] ; risk-controlling prediction sets and CRC extend calibration to loss targets [ 4 , 3 ] , while LTT tests candidate policies for risk certification [ 2 ] . Table 4 compares the decisions these methods calibrate.

Language-generation methods control candidate-set success, retained claims, or an answer aggregated from several paths. Tail-CRC controls the wrong-submission event from a single budget-adapted trajectory, using its answer-span token probabilities.

Confidence can come from answer probabilities, self-evaluation, or verbal uncertainty [ 16 , 17 , 21 , 28 ] . Semantic entropy compares meaning across sampled answers [ 8 ] ; Deep Think with Confidence (DeepConf) filters parallel reasoning traces by token confidence [ 9 ] . Our single-rollout comparison separates representation and length effects from the choice of token aggregation. CVaR supplies a precise tail-score interpretation [ 29 ] ; the entropy extension relates sampled surprise to decoder drift and token varentropy [ 18 ] .


### 7 Discussion and Limitations

Training supplies correct candidates, scoring separates them from errors, and calibration sets the marginal wrong-submission target. Binary RL with Tail-CRC is useful under tight budgets and API log-probability access. Tail entropy improves the demonstrated 8B DAPO operating point when full-vocabulary access is available. With costly errors and an 8K budget, conservative Native decoding remains competitive. The deployment choice therefore depends jointly on $R$ , $W$ , and cost.

Calibration cannot create correct answers or recover separation absent from the score. On 8B Omni at $\alpha=0.065$ , Tail-CRC’s 4K net-score gains over identically calibrated Budget forcing and Answer-SFT are 0.17 and 0.50 pp; at 8K it retains 4.0 pp fewer correct answers than calibrated Penalty RL ( $[-7.67,-0.33]$ pp). Better raw solving does not guarantee the best small accepted subset. Likewise, the sparse-signal model provides a conditional explanation for tail scoring, and the evaluated 10% fraction is not universally optimal. The 18–18 Tail/Mean NLL split leaves both normalized scores viable. Similarly, white-box score gains do not identify decoder drift or varentropy as their cause, and zero-crossing intervals do not establish score equivalence.

The study covers two Qwen model generations and two verifiable mathematics domains. Binary RL has two training seeds; penalty arms have one, and Explicit-Abstain RL changes instruction and parser together. Equal zero rewards for refusal and truncation leave their relative value unspecified; rewarding prompt refusal would be a different intervention. The solved dynamics isolate a mechanism rather than identify the actual AdamW trajectory. A longer-budget Native-GRPO training control remains untested. Small exploratory training and two-draw studies are confined to Appendix D .

Guarantees require the stated calibration/deployment relationship and correct labels. Three hundred reference-keyed calibration examples cost an estimated $0.73 for 8B or $1.31 for 4B at single-seed 4K generation rates; trustworthy labels in open-ended domains may be harder to obtain. Public-benchmark pretraining exposure is unknown, and supplementary released-panel analyses are not independent confirmations. At a matched 4K cap, extra prefill can make budget forcing slightly more expensive than Native; rejection refunds no generated tokens. Full-vocabulary extraction remains untimed, and open-engine reconstruction does not establish identical generation distributions. Future work can improve solvers, trajectory ranking, and calibration under shift.

### Appendix A Complete proofs and theoretical extensions

The first three sections establish finite-sample guarantees. The next sections prove the reward and score results and their explanatory models. Counterexamples clarify the assumptions behind each conclusion.


#### A.1 Proof of Theorem 1

Let $F_{i}$ be the complete problem family and $K(\cdot\mid F_{i})$ the fixed kernel for trajectory generation, budget forcing, score extraction, and outcome grading. The distribution of $((F_{i},Y_{i},S_{i}))_{i=1}^{n+1}$ integrates the product $\prod_{i}K(dy_{i},ds_{i}\mid F_{i})$ against the exchangeable law of $(F_{i})_{i=1}^{n+1}$ . Permuting all indices leaves both factors unchanged. Therefore the augmented observations are exchangeable. Token dependence, stochastic decoding, and deterministic external delimiters are internal to $K$ ; they do not change this argument. Independent random seeds across families implement the product-kernel assumption; pairing seeds across comparison arms preserves each arm’s argument.

Write $N=n+1$ and define $Z_{i}=S_{i}$ when $Y_{i}=W$ , and $Z_{i}=+\infty$ otherwise. For $j=\lfloor N\alpha\rfloor\geq 1$ , the threshold from $n$ calibration observations is their $j$ th ordered $Z$ value: when fewer than $j$ wrong scores are finite, it is $+\infty$ . Since $\alpha<1$ , $j\leq n$ . For each $i\leq N$ , let $Z_{(j)}^{-i}$ be the $j$ th order statistic after omitting $i$ , and set

[Tableau] $$E_{i}=\mathbf{1}\{Y_{i}=W,\ Z_{i}<Z_{(j)}^{-i}\}.$$

For any deterministic collection of $N$ observations,

[Tableau] $$\sum_{i=1}^{N}E_{i}\leq j.$$ (A.1)

To see this with ties, group finite wrong scores by value. A score in a group whose cumulative count is $r$ is strictly below the $j$ th leave-one-out value only if $r\leq j$ : the other $r-1$ observations at or below that value would otherwise already fill the first $j$ ranks. Thus only entire groups contained within the first $j$ ranks can count. If there are at most $j$ finite wrong scores, all of them can count and their number is still at most $j$ . Infinite scores never satisfy a strict comparison with an infinite threshold.

Exchangeability gives $\mathbb{E}E_{N}=N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\sum_{i}E_{i}$ . Equation ( A.1 ) yields $\mathbb{E}E_{N}\leq j/N\leq\alpha$ . When $j=0$ , the declared reject-all rule has risk zero. This proves every case in ( 12 ). If all wrong scores are finite and almost surely distinct, and $M_{W}$ is the number of wrong outputs among all $N$ observations, the proof additionally gives the exact identity

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}E_{N}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\min\{j,M_{W}\}]}{N}.$$

Consequently the rule can accept every valid answer when wrong outputs are already rare; reaching the risk target is not required for validity.

For $j\geq 1$ , the inequality $1+\sum_{i}\mathbf{1}\{Y_{i}=W,S_{i}<\tau\}\leq N\alpha$ is equivalent to allowing at most $j-1$ calibration wrong submissions. The largest admissible threshold is the $j$ th wrong score, unless fewer than $j$ such scores exist, in which case all finite valid scores can pass. With strict acceptance, $\mathbf{1}\{S<\tau\}$ is left-continuous in $\tau$ , and a tied threshold rejects the entire tie group. The rank proof above avoids any continuity assumption. ∎


#### A.2 Risk under shift, repeated test items, and seed averaging

For any fixed threshold $t$ , $F_{W}^{Q}(t)\leq F_{W}^{P}(t)+d_{W}$ where $d_{W}=\sup_{t}(F_{W}^{Q}(t)-F_{W}^{P}(t))$ . Taking the expectation over a source calibration set and using Theorem 1 proves the first inequality in ( 14 ); the second follows because each event $\{Y=W,S<t\}$ is measurable and bounded by total variation. When both domains use the same decoder and verifier kernel, the data-processing inequality further bounds induced total variation by that between the complete problem-family distributions. The bound is an expectation over calibration sets ; it does not assert $F_{W}^{P}(\widehat{\tau})\leq\alpha$ for every realized calibration split.

If calibration and the $m$ future test families are jointly exchangeable, linearity gives $\mathbb{E}[m^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}L_{i}(\widehat{\tau})]\leq\alpha$ . Under i.i.d. test draws, conditionally on calibration this test average has mean $F_{W}^{P}(\widehat{\tau})$ and variance $F_{W}^{P}(\widehat{\tau})(1-F_{W}^{P}(\widehat{\tau}))/m$ . The conditional mean itself varies with calibration. This is why an empirical test rate or its one-sided confidence limit can exceed $\alpha$ without contradicting the expected-risk statement.

If two frozen training seeds are calibrated separately and one is selected uniformly for deployment, the deployed risk is the average of their risks. Linearity preserves the same expected-risk bound. Reporting this average within each family estimates the randomized single-seed deployment policy; it is not a two-rollout voting procedure, and the seeds do not double the independent family count.


#### A.3 Conditional selective-error certificate and proof

Fix a solver, decoder, verifier, score, and calibration-independent grid $\Lambda$ of $M$ cutoffs, a selective-error target $\alpha_{\rm sel}\in(0,1)$ , and error probability $\delta\in(0,1)$ . For $n$ calibration families, define

[Tableau] $$N_{\tau}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\{S_{i}<\tau\},\qquad W_{\tau}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\{Y_{i}=W,S_{i}<\tau\}.$$

The one-sided exact binomial test is

[Tableau] $$p_{\tau}=\Pr\{\operatorname{Bin}(N_{\tau},\alpha_{\rm sel})\leq W_{\tau}\},\qquad p_{\tau}=1\ \text{if }N_{\tau}=0.$$ (A.2)

Let calibration families and an independent test family be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.). Apply the same frozen decoding kernel independently to each family, with the score and grid fixed independently of these data. Select the largest $\tau\in\Lambda$ satisfying $p_{\tau}\leq\delta/M$ , or issue no certificate. With probability at least $1-\delta$ over calibration, every issued cutoff has $P(Y_{\rm test}=W\mid S_{\rm test}<\widehat{\tau},\mathcal{D}_{\rm cal})\leq\alpha_{\rm sel}$ .

Proof. The conditional binomial argument and simultaneous testing steps follow.

For a fixed calibration-independent threshold $\tau$ , set $A_{i}=\mathbf{1}\{S_{i}<\tau\}$ and $B_{i}=\mathbf{1}\{Y_{i}=W,S_{i}<\tau\}$ . Under i.i.d. families the pairs $(A_{i},B_{i})$ are i.i.d. If $c_{\tau}=P(A_{i}=1)>0$ , then conditioning on the set of accepted indices makes their wrong indicators independent Bernoulli variables with common parameter $r_{\tau}=P(B_{i}=1\mid A_{i}=1)$ . Hence

[Tableau] $$W_{\tau}\mid N_{\tau}=k\sim\operatorname{Bin}(k,r_{\tau}).$$

If $c_{\tau}=0$ , $N_{\tau}=0$ almost surely and the threshold cannot be certified.

Let $F_{k,a}$ be the binomial CDF with parameters $(k,a)$ . For $r_{\tau}\geq a=\alpha_{\rm sel}$ and $u\in[0,1]$ , the event $F_{k,a}(W_{\tau})\leq u$ is a lower-tail event $W_{\tau}\leq b$ for some integer $b$ , or is empty. By binomial stochastic ordering, a larger parameter has no larger lower-tail probability:

[Tableau] $$P_{r_{\tau}}\{F_{k,a}(W_{\tau})\leq u\mid N_{\tau}=k\}\leq F_{k,a}(b)\leq u.$$

The $k=0$ convention $p_{\tau}=1$ is valid as well. Averaging over $N_{\tau}$ proves super-uniformity under every bad threshold with $r_{\tau}>\alpha_{\rm sel}$ .

Let $\Lambda_{\rm bad}=\{\tau\in\Lambda:r_{\tau}>\alpha_{\rm sel}\}$ . A union bound yields

[Tableau] $$P\{\exists\tau\in\Lambda_{\rm bad}:p_{\tau}\leq\delta/M\}\leq|\Lambda_{\rm bad}|\delta/M\leq\delta.$$

Outside this event, all passing thresholds have conditional error at most $\alpha_{\rm sel}$ , so selecting the largest one is valid. No monotonicity of $r_{\tau}$ is needed. If none passes, no conditional-risk certificate is issued. The independent future family’s risk conditional on calibration and the selected threshold is $r_{\widehat{\tau}}$ . ∎

At $W_{\tau}=0$ , the p-value is $(1-\alpha_{\rm sel})^{N_{\tau}}$ . The binomial CDF increases with the wrong count, so zero errors minimize the p-value at fixed $N_{\tau}$ ; issuance requires

[Tableau] $$N_{\tau}\geq\left\lceil\frac{\log(\delta/M)}{\log(1-\alpha_{\rm sel})}\right\rceil.$$

With $M=10,\delta=0.1$ , the necessary accepted counts at conditional-error targets 10%, 15%, 20%, and 30% are respectively 44, 29, 21, and 13. These are necessary volume conditions, not a guarantee of issuance. The family-wise guarantee covers one declared model–domain–budget and error-target configuration; reporting multiple configurations gives no joint guarantee across them.


#### A.4 Proofs and scope of Proposition 1


##### A.4.1 Group advantages and mixed failures

Write $\mu_{\lambda}=p_{R}-\lambda p_{W}$ for the expected reward. Condition on the first trajectory. Independence of the remaining $G-1$ rollouts gives

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[A_{1}\mid Y_{1}=y]$ $\displaystyle=r_{\lambda}(y)-\frac{r_{\lambda}(y)+(G-1)\mu_{\lambda}}{G}$ $\displaystyle=\gamma_{G}\{r_{\lambda}(y)-\mu_{\lambda}\}.$

Substituting $r_{\lambda}(R)=1$ , $r_{\lambda}(W)=-\lambda$ , and $r_{\lambda}(U)=0$ proves ( 5 ). Conditional statements are understood only for outcomes having positive probability. If $p_{R}+p_{W}>0$ , then

[Tableau] $$\lambda p_{W}-p_{R}>0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ (1+\lambda)p_{R}<\lambda(p_{R}+p_{W})\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \frac{p_{R}}{p_{R}+p_{W}}<\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}.$$

If $p_{R}=p_{W}=0$ , all rewards and advantages are zero and the precision ratio is undefined, so no phase-transition claim is needed. For an actual group with counts $(N_{R},N_{W},N_{U})$ , every $U$ rollout has

[Tableau] $$A_{U}=\frac{\lambda N_{W}-N_{R}}{G}.$$

At $\lambda=0$ , this is nonpositive in every group, strengthening the conditional-expectation statement. Sampled $R$ advantages are always nonnegative, and sampled $W$ advantages are always nonpositive.

For $\lambda=0$ , a group has a nonconstant reward vector exactly when it contains both an $R$ and a non- $R$ . Its probability is

[Tableau] $$1-p_{R}^{G}-(1-p_{R})^{G}.$$

For $\lambda>0$ , the three outcome classes have distinct rewards, so the corresponding probability is $1-p_{R}^{G}-p_{W}^{G}-p_{U}^{G}$ . Subtracting proves ( 6 ). Equivalently, the additional event is $\{N_{R}=0,N_{W}>0,N_{U}>0\}$ , on which $A_{U}=\lambda N_{W}/G>0$ and $A_{W}=-\lambda N_{U}/G<0$ . The phrase reward-varying does not assert that an arbitrary shared parameterization must have a nonzero parameter gradient on that group.


##### A.4.2 Positive non-submission rewards: proof of Corollary A.1

If $r(R)=1$ , $r(W)=-\lambda$ , and $r(U)=\rho$ with $\lambda\geq 0$ and $0\leq\rho<1$ , the group advantages satisfy

[Tableau] $$A_{g}^{\lambda,\rho}=(1-\rho)A_{g}^{\lambda_{\rm eff},0},\qquad\lambda_{\rm eff}=\frac{\lambda+\rho}{1-\rho}.$$ (A.3)

The break-even submitted-answer precision becomes $(\lambda+\rho)/(1+\lambda)$ .

Proof. The conditional calculation and affine reward identity are as follows.

Let $r_{\lambda,\rho}(R)=1$ , $r_{\lambda,\rho}(W)=-\lambda$ , and $r_{\lambda,\rho}(U)=\rho$ , with $\lambda\geq 0$ and $0\leq\rho<1$ . For the same independent group of $G\geq 2$ rollouts, write $\gamma_{G}=(G-1)/G$ . Conditioning as in Proposition 1 gives, whenever $p_{U}>0$ ,

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}[A_{1}\mid U]=\gamma_{G}\{\rho(p_{R}+p_{W})+\lambda p_{W}-p_{R}\}.$$ (A.4)

Indeed, the mean reward is $\mu_{\lambda,\rho}=p_{R}-\lambda p_{W}+\rho p_{U}$ and the conditional advantage is $\gamma_{G}(\rho-\mu_{\lambda,\rho})$ . For $p_{R}+p_{W}>0$ , its sign is positive exactly when

[Tableau] $$\frac{p_{R}}{p_{R}+p_{W}}<\frac{\lambda+\rho}{1+\lambda}.$$ (A.5)

If $p_{R}=p_{W}=0$ , every group reward equals $\rho$ and every advantage is zero.

The affine transformation

[Tableau] $$r^{\prime}=\frac{r_{\lambda,\rho}-\rho}{1-\rho}=\left(1,-\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}},0\right),\qquad\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{\lambda+\rho}{1-\rho},$$

has an exact groupwise consequence: $A_{g}^{\lambda,\rho}=(1-\rho)A_{g}^{\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}},0}$ . The additive constant cancels in group centering, and the positive rescaling multiplies the raw advantage-weighted gradient by $1-\rho$ . This is an identity of rewards, advantages, and the corresponding raw surrogate gradient; it does not identify a shared neural policy’s trajectory under AdamW, gradient clipping, or additional loss terms. The restriction $\rho<1$ ensures a positive scaling and keeps a correct answer strictly preferable to non-submission. At $\rho=1$ , those outcomes tie and this reduction is undefined.

In the specified attempt–solver model, the objective becomes

[Tableau] $$J_{\lambda,\rho}(a,q)=\rho+a\{(1+\lambda)q-\lambda-\rho\}.$$

With the same product Bernoulli mirror metric, the exact flow is

[Tableau] $$\dot{a}=a(1-a)\{(1+\lambda)q-\lambda-\rho\},\qquad\dot{q}=\kappa(1+\lambda)aq(1-q).$$

For $\lambda+\rho>0$ , put $q_{c}=(\lambda+\rho)/(1+\lambda)\in(0,1)$ . The invariant $-q_{c}\log q-(1-q_{c})\log(1-q)+\kappa\log(1-a)$ is unchanged in form. Consequently, for $a_{0},q_{0}\in(0,1)$ with $q_{0}<q_{c}$ , the exact starvation criterion remains

[Tableau] $$D_{\mathrm{Ber}}(q_{c}\|q_{0})\geq-\kappa\log(1-a_{0}).$$

The same proof gives $(a,q)\to(0,q_{\infty})$ with $q_{\infty}\leq q_{c}$ under this inequality, and recovery to $(1,1)$ under the reverse strict inequality; $q_{0}\geq q_{c}$ also gives recovery. Thus a common positive non-submission reward raises break-even precision even without an explicit wrong-answer penalty: at $\lambda=0$ , $q_{c}=\rho$ .

Rewarding only explicit refusal is a different four-outcome intervention. For rewards $(1,-\lambda,\rho_{A},0)$ on $(R,W,U_{A},U_{T})$ , where $U_{A}$ is explicit refusal and $U_{T}$ is truncation, the common- $U$ reduction above does not apply. In particular, when both subtypes have positive probability,

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}[A_{1}\mid U_{A}]-\mathbb{E}[A_{1}\mid U_{T}]=\gamma_{G}\rho_{A}.$$

Positive $\rho_{A}$ breaks their reward tie and expressly favors refusal over truncation at the reward level. It does not by itself specify probability flow or a deployment marginal-risk guarantee. These positive-reward shaping variants were not evaluated in the experiments.


##### A.4.3 Gradient identity and policy parameterization

Group-centered policy gradients also admit a U-statistic interpretation [ 39 ] . The direct covariance derivation below isolates the effect of the ternary reward and applies to arbitrary rollout features. Exchangeability within the group gives $\mathbb{E}[G^{-1}\sum_{g}A_{g}\psi_{g}]=\mathbb{E}[A_{1}\psi_{1}]$ . Using independence for $j\neq 1$ ,

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[A_{1}\psi_{1}]$ $\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[r_{1}\psi_{1}]-\frac{1}{G}\mathbb{E}[r_{1}\psi_{1}]-\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=2}^{G}\mathbb{E}[r_{j}]\mathbb{E}[\psi_{1}]$ $\displaystyle=\gamma_{G}\{\mathbb{E}[r_{\lambda}\psi]-\mathbb{E}[r_{\lambda}]\mathbb{E}[\psi]\}.$

Here $\psi$ need not be a score function. If $Q_{\theta}$ has a differentiable density on a locally fixed support, rewards have no direct dependence on $\theta$ , and differentiation can be interchanged with integration, then

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}_{Q_{\theta}}\nabla_{\theta}\log Q_{\theta}=0,\qquad\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{\theta}}r_{\lambda}=\mathbb{E}_{Q_{\theta}}[r_{\lambda}\nabla_{\theta}\log Q_{\theta}],$$

which proves the score-function specialization.

Fix token denominator $GB$ and mask deterministic environment tokens. The unclipped token-ratio surrogate’s gradient at the behavior-policy point is

[Tableau] $$\widehat{g}=\frac{1}{GB}\sum_{g=1}^{G}A_{g}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{g}^{\mathrm{sampled}}}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{g,t}\mid h_{g,t}).$$

Thus ( 7 ) applies to the summed token feature with the constant $1/B$ . Fixed $B$ prevents per-trajectory length weighting. Deterministic prefills, injected end-of-thinking tokens, and forced stopping are unsampled and contribute no score term.

The experiments use top- $p$ and top- $k$ sampling. If the optimized log-probability is that of the raw model $\pi_{\theta}$ , while trajectories come from a transformed law $Q_{\theta}$ , the exact statement is the covariance identity, not automatically $\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{\theta}}r_{\lambda}$ . On a region with a fixed truncation set $\mathcal{V}(h)$ , for example, $q_{\theta}(v\mid h)=\pi_{\theta}(v\mid h)\mathbf{1}\{v\in\mathcal{V}(h)\}/Z_{\theta}(h)$ has score $\nabla\log q_{\theta}=\nabla\log\pi_{\theta}-\nabla\log Z_{\theta}$ . Prefix-dependent normalizer terms generally persist in the reward covariance; changing truncation sets require separate analysis.

For standard positive-width PPO clipping, ratios equal to one lie in the unclipped region, so the behavior-point derivative agrees with the unclipped derivative above. This does not identify later clipped inner-loop derivatives. AdamW, gradient clipping, parameter sharing, and finite stochastic updates also prevent equating the raw expected gradient with the trained LLM’s exact probability flow.

For diagnostic purposes, consider a terminal three-action softmax with logits $z_{i}$ , and rescale time to absorb $\gamma_{G}/B$ . Exact Euclidean gradient ascent on $J=p_{R}-\lambda p_{W}$ has

[Tableau] $$\dot{z}_{i}=p_{i}\{r_{i}-\mu_{\lambda}\}.$$

Writing $S_{2}=p_{R}^{2}+p_{W}^{2}+p_{U}^{2}$ and differentiating $p_{R}$ gives

[Tableau] $$\dot{p}_{R}=p_{R}^{2}(1-2p_{R}+S_{2})-\lambda p_{R}p_{W}(S_{2}-p_{R}-p_{W}).$$ (A.6)

Indeed, $\dot{p}_{R}=\|\nabla_{z}p_{R}\|^{2}-\lambda\langle\nabla_{z}p_{R},\nabla_{z}p_{W}\rangle$ , $\|\nabla_{z}p_{R}\|^{2}=p_{R}^{2}(1-2p_{R}+S_{2})$ , and $\langle\nabla_{z}p_{R},\nabla_{z}p_{W}\rangle=p_{R}p_{W}(S_{2}-p_{R}-p_{W})$ . At $(p_{R},p_{W},p_{U})=(0.01,0.30,0.69)$ and $\lambda=1$ ,

[Tableau] $$\dot{p}_{R}=0.00015462-0.00076860=-0.00061398.$$

Here the correct outcome has positive expected advantage, but the penalty decreases its probability at this state. On a failure-only group, the empirical logit gradient is exactly

[Tableau] $$(\widehat{g}_{R},\widehat{g}_{W},\widehat{g}_{U})=\frac{\lambda N_{W}N_{U}}{G^{2}}(0,-1,1).$$

The first-order change in $p_{R}$ from that group is therefore $-p_{R}\lambda N_{W}N_{U}(p_{U}-p_{W})/G^{2}$ , which is negative when $p_{U}>p_{W}$ . This exhibits one mechanism for diminishing correct-path exploration in this parameterization.

Even positive expected $U$ advantage need not increase $p_{U}$ under parameter sharing. For scalar $\theta$ , take

[Tableau] $$(z_{R},z_{W},z_{U})=(\log 0.4+4\theta,\log 0.5,\log 0.1+\theta),\quad\lambda=1.$$

At $\theta=0$ , $\mu_{\lambda}=-0.1$ and $\mathbb{E}[A\mid U]=0.1\gamma_{G}>0$ . However, $\partial_{\theta}p_{U}=-0.07$ and $\partial_{\theta}J=1.77$ , so gradient ascent gives $\dot{p}_{U}=-0.1239$ in rescaled time.

Conversely, the full-simplex natural-gradient/replicator flow is

[Tableau] $$\dot{p}_{i}=p_{i}(r_{i}-\mu_{\lambda}),\qquad p_{i}(t)=\frac{p_{i}(0)e^{r_{i}t}}{\sum_{j}p_{j}(0)e^{r_{j}t}}.$$ (A.7)

For every interior initial state, $p_{R}$ increases and tends to one. Moreover, from the same initial state,

[Tableau] $$p_{R}^{\lambda}(t)=\frac{p_{R}(0)e^{t}}{p_{R}(0)e^{t}+p_{U}(0)+p_{W}(0)e^{-\lambda t}}\geq p_{R}^{0}(t).$$

Thus reward ordering alone implies neither a universal all- $U$ attractor nor universal frontier contraction.


##### A.4.4 An attempt–solver model with a sharp starvation boundary

We now exhibit assumptions under which a genuine boundary attractor can occur, rather than attributing one to Dr. GRPO without a dynamics model. A gate attempts a solution with probability $a\in(0,1)$ ; conditional success probability is $q\in(0,1)$ . Otherwise it produces $U$ . Then

[Tableau] $$p_{R}=aq,\quad p_{W}=a(1-q),\quad p_{U}=1-a,\qquad J_{\lambda}(a,q)=a\{(1+\lambda)q-\lambda\}.$$

Use the product Bernoulli mirror metric, with relative solver learning rate $\kappa>0$ :

[Tableau] $$M(a,q)=\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{1}{a(1-a)},\,\frac{1}{\kappa q(1-q)}\right).$$

Its gradient-ascent flow is

[Tableau] $$\dot{a}=a(1-a)\{(1+\lambda)q-\lambda\},\qquad\dot{q}=\kappa(1+\lambda)aq(1-q).$$ (A.8)

This is a specified block mirror-flow model. In particular, its metric is not the Fisher information metric of the full gated trajectory law: the latter contains an extra visitation factor $a$ in the solver block. The factor $a$ remaining in $\dot{q}$ represents learning that slows when solution attempts become rare.

For $\lambda>0$ , write $q_{c}=\lambda/(1+\lambda)$ and suppose $q_{0}<q_{c}$ . Let

[Tableau] $$D_{\mathrm{Ber}}(q_{c}\|q_{0})=q_{c}\log\frac{q_{c}}{q_{0}}+(1-q_{c})\log\frac{1-q_{c}}{1-q_{0}}.$$

Then the exact dichotomy is

[Tableau] $$\begin{cases}a(t)\to 0,\ q(t)\to q_{\infty}\leq q_{c},&D_{\mathrm{Ber}}(q_{c}\|q_{0})\geq-\kappa\log(1-a_{0}),\\ a(t)\to 1,\ q(t)\to 1,&D_{\mathrm{Ber}}(q_{c}\|q_{0})<-\kappa\log(1-a_{0}).\end{cases}$$ (A.9)

The strict first inequality gives $q_{\infty}<q_{c}$ and exponentially vanishing attempt probability. At equality, $q_{\infty}=q_{c}$ . For $q_{0}\geq q_{c}$ , or for $\lambda=0$ with any interior initial state, both $a$ and $q$ tend to one.

To prove the dichotomy, define $h_{c}(q)=-q_{c}\log q-(1-q_{c})\log(1-q)$ . Direct differentiation of ( A.8 ) yields

[Tableau] $$\frac{d}{dt}\{h_{c}(q)+\kappa\log(1-a)\}=0.$$

Consequently,

[Tableau] $$a(q)=1-(1-a_{0})\exp\!\left\{\frac{h_{c}(q_{0})-h_{c}(q)}{\kappa}\right\}.$$ (A.10)

The function $h_{c}$ decreases strictly on $(0,q_{c})$ , has its unique minimum at $q_{c}$ , and increases strictly on $(q_{c},1)$ . Also $q(t)$ increases strictly, and both coordinates remain interior at every finite time.

If $h_{c}(q_{0})+\kappa\log(1-a_{0})>h_{c}(q_{c})$ , there is a unique $q_{\infty}\in(q_{0},q_{c})$ satisfying $h_{c}(q_{\infty})=h_{c}(q_{0})+\kappa\log(1-a_{0})$ . Equation ( A.10 ) prevents $q$ from crossing this value with $a>0$ . Its limit cannot be smaller, since then both $a$ and $q(1-q)$ would remain bounded away from zero and $\dot{q}$ would remain positive. Thus $q\to q_{\infty}$ and $a\to 0$ . Since $\frac{d}{dt}\operatorname{logit}(a)=(1+\lambda)(q-q_{c})$ tends to a strictly negative constant, $a$ vanishes exponentially on the logarithmic scale. The same argument at equality gives $(a,q)\to(0,q_{c})$ .

If $h_{c}(q_{0})+\kappa\log(1-a_{0})<h_{c}(q_{c})$ , then

[Tableau] $$a(q_{c})=1-(1-a_{0})e^{D_{\mathrm{Ber}}(q_{c}\|q_{0})/\kappa}>0.$$

The trajectory crosses $q_{c}$ in finite time. Thereafter $a$ and $q$ increase, $q$ cannot converge below one, and ( A.10 ) implies $a\to 1$ . Finally, $h_{c}(q_{0})-h_{c}(q_{c})=D_{\mathrm{Ber}}(q_{c}\|q_{0})$ , which proves ( A.9 ). For $q_{0}\geq q_{c}$ and $\lambda>0$ , $q$ immediately exceeds $q_{c}$ , after which the same monotone-recovery argument applies. For $\lambda=0$ , $\dot{a}>0$ and $a\geq a_{0}>0$ ; the equation for $q$ implies $q\to 1$ , and the equation for $\operatorname{logit}(a)$ then implies $a\to 1$ .

> Figure 7 : Exact boundary and trajectories in the specified attempt–solver model. At $\lambda=1$ , $\kappa=.5$ , and $q_{0}=.2$ , recovery requires $a_{0}>.36$ . The $a_{0}=.25$ trajectory tends to $(a,q)=(0,.3085146)$ ; $a_{0}=.50$ recovers to $(1,1)$ . All curves are model calculations, with no parameters fitted to the experiments.

The starvation regime has a finite exploration budget in the following precise sense:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}a(t)\,dt$ $\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{logit}(q_{\infty})-\operatorname{logit}(q_{0})}{\kappa(1+\lambda)}<\infty,$ $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}a(t)q(t)\,dt$ $\displaystyle=\frac{\log(1-q_{0})-\log(1-q_{\infty})}{\kappa(1+\lambda)}<\infty.$ (A.11)

These identities follow by dividing $\dot{q}$ by $q(1-q)$ and by $1-q$ , respectively, and integrating. Multiplication by a fixed rollout intensity gives expected attempted and correct-rollout exposure. The probability that a group contains any correct rollout, $1-\{1-a(t)q(t)\}^{G}$ , tends to zero; the probability of an all- $U$ group tends to one. This model is not fitted to the released training curves.

An even simpler finite-horizon comparison holds when solver quality is fixed during gate adaptation. With $v_{\lambda}=q-\lambda(1-q)$ ,

[Tableau] $$a_{\lambda}(t)=\operatorname{logit}^{-1}\{\operatorname{logit}(a_{0})+v_{\lambda}t\}.$$

For every $\lambda>0$ , $q\in(0,1)$ , and $t>0$ , $a_{\lambda}(t)<a_{0}(t)$ , where the subscript zero denotes binary reward from the same initialization. Thus both the per-rollout success probability $qa_{\lambda}(t)$ and $\int_{0}^{T}qa_{\lambda}(t)\,dt$ are strictly smaller for every $T>0$ . If $\delta=\lambda-(1+\lambda)q>0$ , then

[Tableau] $$\int_{0}^{T}a_{\lambda}(t)\,dt=-\frac{1}{\delta}\log\{1-a_{0}+a_{0}e^{-\delta T}\}\leq\frac{-\log(1-a_{0})}{\delta}.$$

The conditional solver is fixed here; a freely adapting full policy needs further assumptions.


#### A.5 Proofs and scope of Proposition A.1

For a frozen candidate generator, let $\eta(z)=P(R\mid z,\mathrm{valid})$ be posterior correctness from selector information $z$ . For $\alpha>0$ , maximizing correct submissions with $W\leq\alpha$ over randomized selectors measurable in $z$ gives

[Tableau] $$g^{\star}(z)=\mathbb{I}\{\eta(z)>\lambda^{\star}/(1+\lambda^{\star})\},$$ (A.12)

with possible randomization at equality. The optimal dual multiplier depends in general on both $\alpha$ and the deployment population, including when $\eta(z)$ is unchanged under shift. If explicit abstention and truncation both receive zero reward, redistributing probability between them at fixed $(p_{R},p_{W})$ leaves the objective unchanged; their conditional expected advantages are identical.

Proof. We derive the optimal selector, exhibit its population dependence, and verify reward equivalence.

Let $\nu_{P}$ be the subprobability law of valid candidates and their selector information, so $\nu_{P}(\mathcal{Z})=P(\mathrm{valid})$ . For a randomized selector $g\colon\mathcal{Z}\to[0,1]$ , write $R_{P}(g)=\int g\eta\,d\nu_{P}$ and $W_{P}(g)=\int g(1-\eta)\,d\nu_{P}$ . Assume $\alpha>0$ . For $\lambda\geq 0$ , the Lagrangian is

[Tableau] $${\cal L}(g,\lambda)=\lambda\alpha+\int g(z)\{\eta(z)-\lambda(1-\eta(z))\}\,d\nu_{P}(z).$$

Pointwise maximization over $g(z)\in[0,1]$ gives ( A.12 ); at zero coefficient any value in $[0,1]$ is optimal. Retaining the constant term, the resulting dual objective is

[Tableau] $$d_{P}(\lambda)=\lambda\alpha+\int\{\eta-\lambda(1-\eta)\}_{+}\,d\nu_{P}.$$

It is continuous and convex, and $d_{P}(\lambda)\geq\lambda\alpha$ , so it attains a finite minimum for $\alpha>0$ . At an interior minimizer, the subgradient condition permits a randomization on the equality set making $W_{P}(g^{\star})=\alpha$ . At $\lambda^{\star}=0$ , one can choose a maximizer with $W_{P}(g^{\star})\leq\alpha$ . Equivalently, order points by the posterior odds $\eta/(1-\eta)$ under the wrong-mass measure $(1-\eta)d\nu_{P}$ and fill up to a budget of mass $\alpha$ , randomizing at a boundary atom. Points with $\eta=1$ are accepted at no wrong-answer cost.

For completeness, any feasible $g$ and the resulting $g^{\star}$ satisfy

[Tableau] $\displaystyle R_{P}(g)-R_{P}(g^{\star})$ $\displaystyle=\int(g-g^{\star})\{\eta-\lambda^{\star}(1-\eta)\}\,d\nu_{P}$ $\displaystyle\quad+\lambda^{\star}\{W_{P}(g)-W_{P}(g^{\star})\}\leq 0.$

The first term is nonpositive by pointwise maximization; the second is nonpositive by feasibility and complementary slackness. This proves optimality directly. If the budget exceeds the raw wrong-answer mass, accepting all valid candidates is optimal and $\lambda=0$ suffices. At $\alpha=0$ , accepting only posterior-certain correct candidates is optimal, and a finite dual threshold need not exist.

Let all candidates be valid, let $Z\in[0,1]$ , and let correctness conditional on $Z=z$ be Bernoulli with probability $\eta(z)=z$ . On the source population $Z\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]$ , a cutoff $t$ has

[Tableau] $$W_{\mathrm{src}}(t)=\int_{t}^{1}(1-z)\,dz=\frac{(1-t)^{2}}{2}.$$

For $\alpha=0.02$ , the unique cutoff is $t=0.8$ and $\lambda^{\star}_{\mathrm{src}}=4$ . Now shift only the feature distribution to $Z\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0.5,1]$ , retaining the identical correctness function $\eta(z)=z$ . The same threshold has

[Tableau] $$W_{\mathrm{tgt}}(0.8)=2\int_{0.8}^{1}(1-z)\,dz=0.04.$$

The target-optimal threshold is $t^{\star}_{\mathrm{tgt}}=1-\sqrt{0.02}$ and $\lambda^{\star}_{\mathrm{tgt}}=1/\sqrt{0.02}-1\approx 6.0711$ . Even this shift toward easier candidates doubles the marginal wrong rate of the fixed rule because it changes accepted population mass. The source optimum also depends on the risk target: $\lambda^{\star}(\alpha)=1/\sqrt{2\alpha}-1$ for $0<\alpha<1/2$ .

This is non-transferability of the optimal operating point in general, not a claim that every shift changes the optimum or that no fixed penalty can ever be conservative. Under an oracle posterior threshold,

[Tableau] $$W_{P}(g_{\lambda})\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{accepted})}{1+\lambda}\leq\frac{1}{1+\lambda}.$$

Thus $\lambda\geq\alpha^{-1}-1$ is a distribution-uniform sufficient choice in this ideal Bayes problem, often at a substantial coverage cost. The bound is sharp in the supremum over populations by concentrating mass just above the acceptance threshold. A trained neural penalty policy is not assumed to implement an oracle posterior threshold. None of these facts requires full solver retraining: changing a post-hoc selector changes its operating point with weights held fixed.

With $(R,W,U_{A},U_{T})$ rewards $(1,-\lambda,0,0)$ , $J=p_{R}-\lambda p_{W}$ does not contain $p_{A}$ or $p_{T}$ separately. Hence the feasible perturbation $(p_{A},p_{T})\mapsto(p_{A}+\varepsilon,p_{T}-\varepsilon)$ has zero directional derivative. Conditioning as above gives $\mathbb{E}[A\mid U_{A}]=\mathbb{E}[A\mid U_{T}]=-\gamma_{G}\mu_{\lambda}$ .

In the full-simplex replicator geometry, $\dot{p}_{A}=-\mu_{\lambda}p_{A}$ and $\dot{p}_{T}=-\mu_{\lambda}p_{T}$ , so

[Tableau] $$\frac{d}{dt}\log\frac{p_{A}}{p_{T}}=0.$$

Their odds preserve their initialization; when both derivatives are nonzero, their absolute flow ratio equals $p_{A}/p_{T}$ . Euclidean ascent in independent outcome logits has $\dot{z}_{A}=-\mu_{\lambda}p_{A}$ and $\dot{z}_{T}=-\mu_{\lambda}p_{T}$ , giving

[Tableau] $$\frac{d}{dt}\log\frac{p_{A}}{p_{T}}=-\mu_{\lambda}(p_{A}-p_{T}).$$

Thus even two standard geometries give different relative flows. Shared neural parameters or Adam preconditioning can give still different behavior. A reward preference for explicit abstention over truncation would break the tie, but alone gives no deployment marginal-risk guarantee.

For any frozen generator and any selector, $R_{P}(g)\leq\mathbb{P}_{P}(Y=R)$ : filtering cannot create a correct candidate. Sharper set inclusion holds under an explicit thinning assumption. Suppose a second generator preserves the original candidate and its selector information but retains it with probability $h(Z)\in[0,1]$ , independently of correctness conditional on $Z$ ; otherwise it returns $U$ . Its valid-candidate law is $h\,d\nu_{P}$ . Any selector $g$ on the thinned generator attains

[Tableau] $$(R,W)=\left(\int hg\eta\,d\nu_{P},\;\int hg(1-\eta)\,d\nu_{P}\right),$$

which the original generator attains using the randomized selector $hg$ . Its attainable set of $(R,W)$ pairs is therefore a subset of the original generator’s attainable set.

With valid coverage $a$ and fixed correctness probability $q\in(0,1)$ , the attempt model gives

[Tableau] $$\max_{g:\,W(g)\leq\alpha}R(g)=q\min\left\{a,\frac{\alpha}{1-q}\right\}.$$

Reducing $a$ can strictly reduce the frontier once the smaller candidate supply binds, while sufficiently tight risk budgets give the same optimum. This is a sufficient condition for contraction, not a theorem that arbitrary penalty training is such a thinning. Training can alter both candidate quality and the score distribution. Indeed, ( A.7 ) gives a counterexample to any universal contraction assertion: increasing $\lambda$ there raises $p_{R}(t)$ and lowers $p_{W}(t)$ from the same initial state.


#### A.6 Score anatomy: complete proofs and boundaries


##### A.6.1 Length and discrimination

In a continuous length-factor surrogate with independent Gaussian log factors, suppose $\E D=d$ , $\E B_{\ell}=b$ and variances $v_{D},v_{B}>0$ . Writing $\Phi$ for the standard normal cumulative distribution function,

[Tableau] $$\operatorname{AUC}(S_{\rm sum})=\Phi\!\left(\frac{d-b}{\sqrt{v_{D}+v_{B}}}\right).$$ (A.13)

Even if $d>0$ , the ranking reverses when $b>d$ .

For the general Gaussian extension, use $B=B_{\ell}$ from Proposition 2 . In a stylized positive-factor model, now allowing $L$ to be real valued, suppose the within-class vectors $(\log L_{y},\log\bar{\ell}_{y})$ are jointly Gaussian. Define $d=\mathbb{E}\log\bar{\ell}_{W}-\mathbb{E}\log\bar{\ell}_{R}$ , $b=\mathbb{E}\log L_{R}-\mathbb{E}\log L_{W}$ , $v_{U}=\sum_{y\in\{R,W\}}\operatorname{Var}(\log\bar{\ell}_{y})$ , $v_{L}=\sum_{y}\operatorname{Var}(\log L_{y})$ , and $c=\sum_{y}\operatorname{Cov}(\log L_{y},\log\bar{\ell}_{y})$ . For $v_{U}>0$ and $v_{U}+v_{L}+2c>0$ ,

[Tableau] $$\operatorname{AUC}(\bar{\ell})=\Phi\!\left(\frac{d}{\sqrt{v_{U}}}\right),\qquad\operatorname{AUC}(S_{\rm sum})=\Phi\!\left(\frac{d-b}{\sqrt{v_{U}+v_{L}+2c}}\right).$$ (A.14)

When $c=0$ , the independent-factor specialization uses $v_{D}=v_{U}$ and $v_{B}=v_{L}$ . Consequently the summed score reverses direction exactly when $b>d$ in this model. If $d>0$ , $b\geq 0$ , and the two log factors are independent within each class, length strictly reduces AUROC whenever $b>0$ or $v_{L}>0$ .

The factorization follows directly from the definition of the arithmetic mean. Since the logarithm is strictly increasing on $(0,\infty)$ ,

[Tableau] $$L_{W}\bar{\ell}_{W}>L_{R}\bar{\ell}_{R}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\log\bar{\ell}_{W}-\log\bar{\ell}_{R}>\log L_{R}-\log L_{W}.$$

The same equivalence holds with equality. Multiplying the strict-event probability by one and the tie-event probability by one half proves ( 10 ). If a span has zero average surprise, omit only the logarithmic reparameterization; the product identity and original AUROC definition remain valid.

For the Gaussian model, independence of the two class draws and the stated within-class joint normality imply that $D$ is normal with mean $d$ and variance $v_{U}$ . Also,

[Tableau] $$D-B=(\log L_{W}+\log\bar{\ell}_{W})-(\log L_{R}+\log\bar{\ell}_{R})$$

is normal with mean $d-b$ and variance $v_{U}+v_{L}+2c$ . A normal random variable with mean $m$ and positive standard deviation $s$ exceeds zero with probability $\Phi(m/s)$ and has no atom at zero. This proves ( A.14 ). Since $\Phi$ is strictly increasing and $\Phi(0)=1/2$ , the summed score has AUROC below $1/2$ exactly when $d-b<0$ . If the factors are independent within each class, $c=0$ . When $d>0$ and $b\geq 0$ , either $d-b\leq 0$ , in which case the comparison with $d/\sqrt{v_{U}}>0$ is immediate, or

[Tableau] $$\frac{d-b}{\sqrt{v_{U}+v_{L}}}\leq\frac{d}{\sqrt{v_{U}}},$$

with strict inequality when $b>0$ or $v_{L}>0$ . This proves the stated degradation claim. With a zero Gaussian variance, the corresponding score gap is deterministic and its AUROC is $\psi$ of its mean; this also describes the degenerate boundaries omitted from the displayed positive-variance formula. ∎

An exact accounting of the direction changes is useful. If $\Pr(D=0)=\Pr(D=B)=0$ , then

[Tableau] $$\operatorname{AUC}(S_{\rm sum})-\operatorname{AUC}(\bar{\ell})=\Pr(B<D\leq 0)-\Pr(0<D\leq B).$$ (A.15)

Subtracting the indicators gives $+1$ only when an incorrect ordering becomes correct, and $-1$ only for the reverse change. Without the no-ties condition one simply uses $\mathbb{E}[\psi(D-B)-\psi(D)]$ , which is exact for all distributions. A direct sufficient condition for non-increasing AUROC is $B\geq 0$ almost surely for the independent right–wrong pair, because $\psi$ is nondecreasing. Marginal stochastic dominance of lengths is substantially weaker than this pairwise condition.

Let a right draw equal $(L_{R},\bar{\ell}_{R})=(2,1)$ with probability $0.8$ and $(4,4)$ with probability $0.2$ . Let an independent wrong draw equal $(1,3)$ with probability $0.8$ and $(3,0.8)$ with probability $0.2$ . The right length stochastically dominates the wrong length: its quantiles exceed the latter’s by one. The mean-score AUROC is $0.8\cdot 0.8=0.64$ . The summed scores are $2$ or $16$ on right draws and $3$ or $2.4$ on wrong draws, giving AUROC $0.8$ . Hence even strict length dominance and positive mean-score discrimination can coexist with an improvement after summation. Dependence between length and token surprise matters.

Let $L_{W}=1$ , $L_{R}=1$ with probability $0.49$ and $L_{R}=2$ otherwise. Let $\bar{\ell}_{R}=1$ , and independently set $\log\bar{\ell}_{W}=0.1$ with probability $0.51$ and $1$ otherwise. Then the unique median of $B$ is $\log 2$ , exceeding the unique median $0.1$ of $D$ , whereas

[Tableau] $$\operatorname{AUC}(S_{\rm sum})=0.49+0.51\cdot 0.49=0.7399>\tfrac{1}{2}.$$

The mean score has AUROC one. Means alone are insufficient under arbitrary sub-Gaussian assumptions: take $B=\log 2$ and independently $D=1$ with probability $0.51$ and $D=0.1$ otherwise. Both variables are bounded and therefore sub-Gaussian, $\mathbb{E}D=0.559<\log 2$ , but $\Pr(D>B)=0.51$ . The Gaussian sign-reversal criterion requires normality or another restriction on the full centered contrast distribution.


##### A.6.2 Finite empirical CVaR, rounding, and replication

For every $\eta$ and every $k$ -element subset $J$ ,

[Tableau] $$\eta+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{L}(z_{t}-\eta)_{+}\geq\eta+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t\in J}(z_{t}-\eta)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t\in J}z_{t}.$$

Taking $J$ to be a top- $k$ subset gives a lower bound $T_{k}(z)$ . At $\eta=z_{(k)}$ , the expression equals $T_{k}(z)$ : values tied at $z_{(k)}$ contribute zero excess, and filling their remaining positions with $z_{(k)}$ supplies exactly the top- $k$ sum. This also works for $k=L$ , or one can take any $\eta\leq z_{(L)}$ .

For the dual identity, let $w_{t}\in[0,1/k]$ and $\sum_{t}w_{t}=1$ . Writing $z_{t}=\eta+(z_{t}-\eta)$ gives

[Tableau] $$w^{\top}z\leq\eta+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t}(z_{t}-\eta)_{+}.$$

At the minimizing $\eta$ this is at most $T_{k}(z)$ . Choosing $w_{t}=\mathbf{1}\{t\in J\}/k$ for a top- $k$ subset attains $T_{k}(z)$ . Empirical CVaR has coefficient $1/(\gamma L)$ for tail mass $\gamma$ , so $\gamma=k/L$ identifies the two expressions.

For $z^{\prime}=z+ue_{i}$ , $u\geq 0$ , nonnegative dual weights imply monotonicity. An optimizer $w^{\prime}$ at $z^{\prime}$ gives

[Tableau] $$T_{k}(z^{\prime})-T_{k}(z)\leq(w^{\prime})^{\top}(z^{\prime}-z)=uw^{\prime}_{i}\leq u/k.$$

The bound is attained when coordinate $i$ already belongs to a strictly separated top- $k$ set. Setting $k=1$ gives the maximum’s sharp bound. ∎

Let $0<\beta\leq 1$ , $a=\beta L$ , $k=\lceil a\rceil$ , and $r=k-a$ . Denote fixed-tail-mass empirical CVaR by

[Tableau] $$C_{\beta}(z)=\min_{\eta}\left\{\eta+\frac{1}{\beta L}\sum_{t=1}^{L}(z_{t}-\eta)_{+}\right\}.$$

Then

[Tableau] $$C_{\beta}(z)-T_{k}(z)=\frac{r}{a}\bigl(T_{k}(z)-z_{(k)}\bigr)=\frac{r}{k}\bigl(C_{\beta}(z)-z_{(k)}\bigr),$$ (A.16)

and, with $\operatorname{range}(z)=z_{(1)}-z_{(L)}$ ,

[Tableau] $$0\leq C_{\beta}(z)-T_{k}(z)\leq\frac{k-\beta L}{k}\operatorname{range}(z).$$

If $z^{[c]}$ repeats each coordinate $c$ times, for an integer $c\geq 1$ , then

[Tableau] $$0\leq T_{\lceil\beta cL\rceil}(z^{[c]})-T_{k}(z)\leq\frac{k-\beta L}{k}\operatorname{range}(z).$$ (A.17)

Fixed- $\beta$ CVaR is exactly replication-invariant. The rounded score is exactly replication-invariant whenever $\beta L$ is integral; it need not be so otherwise.

For noninteger $a$ , write $a=m+\theta$ , where $m=\lfloor a\rfloor$ and $\theta\in(0,1)$ . Evaluating the piecewise-linear objective at $\eta=z_{(m+1)}$ gives

[Tableau] $$C_{\beta}(z)=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}z_{(j)}+\theta z_{(m+1)}}{a}.$$

To see that this is minimal, observe that on each interval between successive ordered coordinates the derivative is $1-j/a$ , where $j$ coordinates exceed $\eta$ ; the slope changes from nonpositive to nonnegative at this quantile. Ties give the same value. For integer $a$ , the analogous value is the average of the first $a$ coordinates. Thus in both cases

[Tableau] $$aC_{\beta}(z)=kT_{k}(z)-rz_{(k)}.$$

Rearrangement yields both identities in ( A.16 ). Because $z_{(k)}\leq C_{\beta}(z)\leq z_{(1)}$ , their common value is nonnegative and at most $r\operatorname{range}(z)/k$ .

Repeating coordinates preserves their empirical probability measure, so the fixed- $\beta$ CVaR objective and its value are unchanged. Moreover

[Tableau] $$\beta\leq\frac{\lceil c\beta L\rceil}{cL}\leq\frac{\lceil\beta L\rceil}{L}.$$

An upper-tail average is nonincreasing in its tail mass. This follows directly by adding lower quantile values to the averaged upper portion, or from the dual representation. It follows that

[Tableau] $$T_{k}(z)\leq T_{\lceil c\beta L\rceil}(z^{[c]})\leq C_{\beta}(z).$$

Combine this with the rounding bound. For integral $a$ , both endpoints coincide, giving exact invariance. ∎

For example, with $\beta=0.1$ , $L=11$ , one coordinate equal to one and the other ten equal to zero, the implemented score is $1/2$ . Repeating each coordinate ten times gives a score $10/11$ . Thus the rounded score removes linear growth with replication but is not exactly replication-invariant for short spans. The bound is pathwise: an absolute small-error conclusion additionally requires control of the realized score range. NLL has no universal finite upper bound. Replication also does not model an arbitrary change of tokenization, which can change the empirical surprise distribution itself.


##### A.6.3 Sparse signal, background noise, and selective submission

For an explanatory model, fix $L$ and a set $C$ of $k$ critical positions. Conditional on class $y\in\{0,1\}$ , with $0=R$ and $1=W$ , let

[Tableau] $$\ell_{t}^{(y)}=\mu_{t}+y\delta_{t}+\varepsilon_{t}^{(y)},\qquad\delta_{t}\geq 0,\quad\delta_{t}=0\ (t\notin C).$$ (A.18)

Within each class the noises are independent and centered, have equal variances across classes, $\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_{t}^{(0)})=\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_{t}^{(1)})=\sigma_{t}^{2}$ , and satisfy $|\varepsilon_{t}^{(y)}|\leq a_{t}$ . Assume separated support,

[Tableau] $$\min_{t\in C}(\mu_{t}-a_{t})>\max_{t\notin C}(\mu_{t}+a_{t}),$$ (A.19)

with the condition vacuous when $C$ is the whole span. Set $D_{C}=\sum_{t\in C}\delta_{t}>0$ , $V_{C}=\sum_{t\in C}\sigma_{t}^{2}>0$ , and $V_{B}=\sum_{t\notin C}\sigma_{t}^{2}$ . For standardized pairwise separation

[Tableau] $$d(S)=\frac{\mathbb{E}S_{W}-\mathbb{E}S_{R}}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(S_{W})+\operatorname{Var}(S_{R})}},$$

the exact separation formulas are

[Tableau] $$d(T_{k})=\frac{D_{C}}{\sqrt{2V_{C}}},\qquad d(\bar{\ell})=\frac{D_{C}}{\sqrt{2(V_{C}+V_{B})}}.$$ (A.20)

Under ( A.18 )–( A.19 ), the ratio of tail to mean separation is

[Tableau] $$\frac{d(T_{k})}{d(\bar{\ell})}=\sqrt{1+\frac{V_{B}}{V_{C}}}.$$ (A.21)

With equal per-token noise variances, the ratio is $\sqrt{L/k}$ . If the background is deterministic and common to both classes, the mean and tail scores are positive affine transforms and give identical rankings.

Condition ( A.19 ) and nonnegative shifts imply that every critical position exceeds every background position almost surely in both classes. Thus the top- $k$ set is exactly $C$ before and after the class shift, and

[Tableau] $$T_{k}(\bm{\ell}^{(y)})=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t\in C}\mu_{t}+y\frac{D_{C}}{k}+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t\in C}\varepsilon_{t}^{(y)}.$$

Independence and centering give class mean difference $D_{C}/k$ and within-class variance $V_{C}/k^{2}$ . For the full mean the corresponding quantities are $D_{C}/L$ and $(V_{C}+V_{B})/L^{2}$ . Substitution into the definition of $d$ proves ( A.20 ). With common positive variance $\sigma^{2}$ , $V_{C}=k\sigma^{2}$ and $V_{B}=(L-k)\sigma^{2}$ , giving ratio $\sqrt{L/k}$ .

If $V_{B}=0$ , each background noise has zero variance and mean zero, hence is zero almost surely. Consequently

[Tableau] $$\bar{\ell}=\frac{k}{L}T_{k}(\bm{\ell})+\frac{1}{L}\sum_{t\notin C}\mu_{t}$$

in both classes. The positive affine map preserves all comparisons and threshold acceptance sets, establishing the stronger equal-ranking claim. If $V_{C}=0$ and $D_{C}>0$ , tail scores are deterministic and perfectly separated; the finite-SNR display omits this case only to avoid division by zero. ∎

If only $m\leq k$ critical positions have a common shift $\Delta$ , then $D_{C}=m\Delta$ , the tail’s class mean gap is $m\Delta/k$ , and the whole-answer mean gap is $m\Delta/L$ . These are exact within the model. Calling a position “critical” does not assert that a language model’s mathematical correctness follows an observed independent AND gate; that causal interpretation would require additional measurements.

The top- $k$ variance is exactly a sum of variances divided by $k^{2}$ because the support separation fixes its membership. When harmless lexical spikes can enter or leave the selected set, the functional is nonlinear and that formula need not hold. Its deterministic sensitivity still holds: perturbing at most $m$ coordinates upward by at most $u$ changes $T_{k}$ by at most $\min(m/k,1)u$ . To prove this, use $\sum_{t\in I}w_{t}\leq\min(m/k,1)$ for every feasible dual weight vector. This limits spike influence relative to a maximum without implying a distribution-free variance decay rate.

Under ( A.18 )–( A.19 ), use $J=C$ , $n_{J}=k$ for the tail, or $J=\{1,\ldots,L\}$ , $n_{J}=L$ for the mean. Let $m_{R,J}=n_{J}^{-1}\sum_{t\in J}\mu_{t}$ , $m_{W,J}=m_{R,J}+D_{C}/n_{J}$ , and $A_{J}=\sum_{t\in J}a_{t}^{2}>0$ . Let $\pi_{R}$ and $\pi_{W}$ be the unfiltered marginal probabilities of right and wrong submissions. For $0<\alpha_{0}<\pi_{W}$ , define

[Tableau] $$\tau_{J}=m_{W,J}-\frac{\sqrt{2A_{J}\log(\pi_{W}/\alpha_{0})}}{n_{J}}.$$ (A.22)

Accepting valid submissions with score below $\tau_{J}$ gives $W(\tau_{J})\leq\alpha_{0}$ and

[Tableau] $$R(\tau_{J})\geq\pi_{R}\left[1-\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{D_{C}}{\sqrt{A_{J}}}-\sqrt{2\log(\pi_{W}/\alpha_{0})}\right)_{+}^{2}\right\}\right].$$ (A.23)

For independent, centered variables $\varepsilon_{t}\in[-a_{t},a_{t}]$ , Hoeffding’s inequality gives, for $u>0$ ,

[Tableau] $$\Pr\left(\sum_{t\in J}\varepsilon_{t}\leq-u\right)\leq e^{-u^{2}/(2A_{J})},\qquad\Pr\left(\sum_{t\in J}\varepsilon_{t}\geq u\right)\leq e^{-u^{2}/(2A_{J})}.$$ (A.24)

For completeness, convexity of the exponential bounds its value at $x\in[-a_{t},a_{t}]$ by the chord joining its endpoint values. Taking expectations and using $\mathbb{E}x=0$ gives $\mathbb{E}e^{sx}\leq\cosh(sa_{t})\leq e^{s^{2}a_{t}^{2}/2}$ . The second inequality follows by integrating $\tanh u\leq u$ for $u\geq 0$ and using symmetry for $u<0$ . Multiplying these bounds and minimizing $\exp(-su+s^{2}A_{J}/2)$ over $s>0$ gives the two inequalities.

For wrong draws, $n_{J}(S_{W}-m_{W,J})=\sum_{t\in J}\varepsilon_{t}^{(1)}$ . Substitution of $u=\sqrt{2A_{J}\log(\pi_{W}/\alpha_{0})}$ into ( A.24 ) yields

[Tableau] $$W(\tau_{J})=\pi_{W}\Pr(S_{W}<\tau_{J}\mid W)\leq\alpha_{0}.$$

For right draws,

[Tableau] $$n_{J}(\tau_{J}-m_{R,J})=D_{C}-\sqrt{2A_{J}\log(\pi_{W}/\alpha_{0})}.$$

If this number is positive, the upper-tail Hoeffding inequality bounds the probability that a right score is at least $\tau_{J}$ . Subtracting that bound from one and multiplying by $\pi_{R}$ proves ( A.23 ). If it is nonpositive, the displayed positive-part formula reduces to the universally valid bound $R\geq 0$ . If $A_{J}=0$ , the scores are deterministic and a threshold strictly between the two class means instead gives $W=0$ and $R=\pi_{R}$ . ∎

The bound depends on $D_{C}/\sqrt{A_{J}}$ , not mean dilution alone. Discarding background positions improves this sufficient guarantee by removing nuisance concentration terms while preserving the total class signal. This population threshold uses model parameters; deployment estimates its threshold by calibration. The next result connects these distinct constructions.

Let an i.i.d. calibration sample have size $n$ , and let the CRC rule choose the largest threshold satisfying $(1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{Y_{i}=W,S_{i}<\tau\})/(n+1)\leq\alpha$ , with the usual strict-tie convention. Fix a calibration slack satisfying

[Tableau] $$\frac{1-\alpha}{n}<\eta<\alpha,$$

and a data-independent threshold $\tau_{0}$ with population $W(\tau_{0})\leq\alpha-\eta$ . Then

[Tableau] $$\Pr(\widehat{\tau}_{\rm CRC}\geq\tau_{0})\geq 1-\exp\left[-2n\left(\eta-\frac{1-\alpha}{n}\right)^{2}\right].$$ (A.25)

On this event $R(\widehat{\tau}_{\rm CRC})\geq R(\tau_{0})$ . In particular, choosing $\tau_{0}$ from Corollary A.3 with $\alpha_{0}=\alpha-\eta<\pi_{W}$ transfers its correct-submission lower bound to the CRC policy on the displayed high-probability event.

The calibration wrong-submission indicators at $\tau_{0}$ are i.i.d. Bernoulli with mean at most $\alpha-\eta$ . The threshold is feasible whenever their average is at most $\alpha-(1-\alpha)/n$ . Hoeffding’s inequality therefore bounds the probability of infeasibility by the exponential in ( A.25 ). Whenever $\tau_{0}$ is feasible, the maximal feasible CRC threshold is at least $\tau_{0}$ , including the strict-tie implementation. Acceptance is monotone in the threshold, so its correct-submission probability is at least that at $\tau_{0}$ . The efficiency bound assumes an explicit population margin and i.i.d. calibration; the marginal CRC guarantee is separate. ∎

In the separate score-level model $S_{R}\sim N(\mu_{R},\sigma^{2})$ and $S_{W}\sim N(\mu_{W},\sigma^{2})$ , put $d_{0}=(\mu_{W}-\mu_{R})/\sigma$ . At $0<\alpha<\pi_{W}$ , the threshold $\tau=\mu_{W}+\sigma\Phi^{-1}(\alpha/\pi_{W})$ has

[Tableau] $$W=\alpha,\qquad R=\pi_{R}\Phi\left(\Phi^{-1}(\alpha/\pi_{W})+d_{0}\right),\qquad\operatorname{AUC}=\Phi(d_{0}/\sqrt{2}).$$

These identities follow from the two normal CDFs and the normal law of $S_{W}-S_{R}$ . They show how stronger standardized separation can retain more correct submissions at a fixed wrong budget. This Gaussian score model differs from the bounded, separated-support token model: nondegenerate Gaussian token noise is unbounded and cannot satisfy almost-sure support separation.

This is a separate illustrative model, not an assumption of the preceding sparse-signal analysis. Suppose all class-dependent critical scores are bounded above by $K$ , and there are $m$ independent class-independent nuisance scores with common CDF $F$ , where $F(K)<1$ . Couple the maximum score in each class to its nuisance-only maximum. They differ only if every nuisance score is at most $K$ , an event of probability $F(K)^{m}$ . The independent nuisance-only maxima have tie-corrected AUROC $1/2$ by symmetry. A union bound over the two class draws yields

[Tableau] $$\left|\operatorname{AUC}(\max)-\tfrac{1}{2}\right|\leq 2F(K)^{m}.$$

The conclusion follows because the pairwise AUROC contribution lies in $[0,1]$ and is unchanged when both coupled scores agree. This provides one precise way that a maximum can lose signal. It does not establish that every upper-tail average avoids nuisance contamination. Under a specific tail model, a growing expected maximum alone implies neither growing variance nor declining AUROC.


##### A.6.4 Decoder-aware martingales and stopping

Token log probabilities are available with many generation APIs, whereas full-vocabulary entropy requires access to the model distribution at each prefix. The additional access is valuable only if it changes the information used to rank answers. To examine that question, we separate the uncertainty of the model distribution from the random surprise of the token actually sampled, accounting for the decoder transformation used by the experiments. This distinction is essential because top- $p$ /top- $k$ sampling generally changes the distribution from which a token is drawn.

At generation history $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$ , let $p_{t}$ be the scored token distribution and $q_{t}$ the actual sampling distribution on a finite vocabulary, with $q_{t}\ll p_{t}$ and finite surprise second moment. For $Y_{t}\sim q_{t}$ conditionally,

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\ell_{t}$ $\displaystyle=-\log p_{t}(Y_{t})=H(p_{t})+b_{t}+\xi_{t},$ $\displaystyle c_{t}$ $\displaystyle=H(q_{t})+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{t}\|p_{t}),\qquad b_{t}=c_{t}-H(p_{t}),$ $\displaystyle\E[\xi_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]$ $\displaystyle=0,\qquad\E[\xi_{t}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=v_{t}:=\operatorname{Var}_{q_{t}}[-\log p_{t}(Y_{t})].$ (A.26)

For a deterministic horizon $N$ , the mean residual $N^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\xi_{t}$ has second moment $N^{-2}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\E v_{t}$ . When $q_{t}=p_{t}$ , $b_{t}=0$ and $v_{t}$ is token varentropy. For every realized scored span, write $r_{t}=\ell_{t}-H(p_{t})$ and $\bar{r}=L^{-1}\sum_{t}r_{t}$ . Then

[Tableau] $$\left|T_{k}(\bm{\ell})-T_{k}(\mathbf{H})-\bar{r}\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{L}}\,\|\mathbf{r}-\bar{r}\mathbf{1}\|_{2},$$ (A.27)

with a sharp constant, including for random-length or validity-selected spans.

Proof. The decomposition and isometry are proved below; Appendix A.6.5 proves the pathwise bound.

The decomposition identifies three contributions to sampled-token surprise: full-distribution entropy $H(p_{t})$ , decoder drift $b_{t}$ , and centered random surprise $\xi_{t}$ . At temperature one, truncation to a retained set with probability mass $Z_{t}$ gives $c_{t}=H(q_{t})-\log Z_{t}$ . Full-vocabulary entropy removes sampled-token fluctuation but also changes the decoder-dependent center, making its effect on answer ranking an empirical question.

A simple example distinguishes entropy from sampling noise. A uniform model distribution on $K$ tokens has entropy $\log K$ , yet every token has the same surprise and varentropy is zero [ 18 ] . High entropy alone therefore does not predict an entropy upgrade. In a fixed-context resampling model, convexity gives $\E T_{k}(\bm{\ell})-T_{k}(\mathbf{c})\geq 0$ , with an upper bound in terms of $v_{t}$ ; matched sampling specializes the center to entropy. Lemma A.3 translates score perturbations into ranking changes through right–wrong pair margins.

For a deterministic horizon $N$ , the precise isometry is

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\xi_{t}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}v_{t}.$$ (A.28)

Conditional on $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$ , both distributions are fixed and $Y_{t}$ is distributed according to $q_{t}$ . Therefore

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}[\ell_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=\sum_{v}q_{t}(v)[-\log p_{t}(v)]=c_{t}.$$

Centering proves both conditional-moment statements. Adding and subtracting $H(p_{t})$ gives ( A.26 ). The cross-entropy identity follows from $-\log p_{t}(v)=-\log q_{t}(v)+\log(q_{t}(v)/p_{t}(v))$ on the support of $q_{t}$ .

For $s<t$ , $\xi_{s}$ is $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$ -measurable, so integrability and the tower property imply

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}[\xi_{s}\xi_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[\xi_{s}\mathbb{E}(\xi_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})]=0.$$

Thus expanding the square of the deterministic-horizon sum eliminates all cross terms, and

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{N}\xi_{t}\right)^{2}=\sum_{t=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\xi_{t}^{2}=\sum_{t=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}v_{t}.$$

Division by $N^{2}$ proves ( A.28 ). The sum has mean zero, so this second moment is its variance. The same identities hold conditionally on the initial sigma-field $\mathcal{F}_{0}$ after replacing each outer expectation by its conditional version. Conditioning on the entire random future history need not preserve orthogonality.

When $q_{t}=p_{t}$ , $c_{t}=H(p_{t})$ and its conditional variance is $V(p_{t})=\sum_{v}p_{t}(v)[-\log p_{t}(v)-H(p_{t})]^{2}$ . For truncation, on the retained support $-\log p_{t}(v)=-\log q_{t}(v)-\log Z_{t}$ . Taking its $q_{t}$ -mean proves $c_{t}=H(q_{t})-\log Z_{t}$ ; subtracting its mean cancels the constant $-\log Z_{t}$ , proving $v_{t}=V(q_{t})$ . ∎

For a retained set consisting of the most probable tokens, every retained surprise is no greater than every omitted surprise. If $0<Z_{t}<1$ and $c_{t}^{\rm out}$ is the mean surprise conditional on omission,

[Tableau] $$b_{t}=c_{t}-H(p_{t})=(1-Z_{t})(c_{t}-c_{t}^{\rm out})\leq 0.$$

Its magnitude can vary with context. A small measured discrepancy between receipt log probabilities and recomputed raw log probabilities does not set $Z_{t}=1$ or remove this drift: it compares scoring values, not the two sampling laws. If a receipt log probability has an additional numerical or backend residual $e_{t}$ relative to $-\log p_{t}(Y_{t})$ , the exact pathwise residual becomes $r_{t}=b_{t}+\xi_{t}+e_{t}$ .

If $a_{t}$ is $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$ -measurable and the terms are square integrable, the weighted sum has zero mean and satisfies the isometry

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}\sum_{t=1}^{N}a_{t}\xi_{t}=0,\qquad\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{N}a_{t}\xi_{t}\right)^{2}=\mathbb{E}\sum_{t=1}^{N}a_{t}^{2}v_{t}.$$

In particular, for a stopping time $\tau\leq N$ ,

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\xi_{t}=0,\qquad\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\xi_{t}\right)^{2}=\mathbb{E}\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}v_{t}.$$

The weighted increments have conditional mean zero and conditional second moment $a_{t}^{2}v_{t}$ . The preceding orthogonality proof applies to them unchanged. For bounded stopping, use $a_{t}=\mathbf{1}\{\tau\geq t\}$ . This is predictable because $\{\tau\geq t\}$ is the complement of $\{\tau\leq t-1\}\in\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$ . ∎

If $\tau$ is the first EOS time, these statements include its sampled EOS token. The indicator for retaining a token strictly before EOS is $\mathbf{1}\{t<\tau\}$ , which depends on the current token and need not be predictable. Deleting EOS therefore changes the compensator. One can center a masked contribution directly, for example $g_{t}(Y_{t})=\mathbf{1}\{Y_{t}\neq\mathrm{EOS}\}[-\log p_{t}(Y_{t})]$ , by $\sum_{v}q_{t}(v)g_{t}(v)$ , but that center is not Shannon entropy. Selecting only eventually valid trajectories is another conditioning operation depending on future tokens. Neither operation preserves the unconditioned martingale statements automatically.

An example uses genuine self-information. Draw two independent tokens from $p=(2/3,1/3)$ , and let $a=(\log 2)/3$ . Their centered surprises independently equal $-a$ with probability $2/3$ and $2a$ with probability $1/3$ . Stop at $\tau=1$ if the first centered surprise is $2a$ , and at $\tau=2$ otherwise. This is a bounded stopping time, and the unnormalized stopped sum has mean zero. Normalizing by the stopping time instead gives a nonzero expectation:

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\xi_{t}\right]=\frac{a}{3}\neq 0.$$

Indeed, the normalized sum takes values $2a,a/2,-a$ with probabilities $1/3,2/9,4/9$ . Its second moment is $11a^{2}/6$ , whereas naively placing the random denominator in the deterministic-horizon formula would give

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}v_{t}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{2a^{2}}{\tau}\right]=\frac{4a^{2}}{3}.$$

The discrepancy is not a minor asymptotic technicality. It excludes an exact unbiasedness or variance claim for the random-length answer mean without additional conditions.


##### A.6.5 A pathwise bound that survives span selection

The pathwise bound is tailored to the implemented answer score. A fixed horizon gives a martingale second-moment identity, but the scored span ends at a random time, excludes EOS, and is evaluated among valid outputs. Equation ( A.27 ) continues to hold for each such realized span. It shows that the discrepancy between tail surprise and tail entropy depends on the mean residual and its variation across positions: uniform shifts and localized spikes affect the tail differently.

Let $\mathcal{W}_{k}=\{w:0\leq w_{t}\leq 1/k,\sum_{t}w_{t}=1\}$ , $u=L^{-1}\mathbf{1}$ , and $z^{\circ}=z-\bar{z}\mathbf{1}$ . For any $w\in\mathcal{W}_{k}$ , its coordinate bounds and unit sum imply

[Tableau] $$\|w-u\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{t}w_{t}^{2}-\frac{1}{L}\leq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t}w_{t}-\frac{1}{L}=\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{L}.$$

If $w_{+}$ maximizes $T_{k}(x+z)$ , then $T_{k}(x+z)-T_{k}(x)\leq w_{+}^{\top}z$ . If $w_{0}$ maximizes $T_{k}(x)$ , the analogous lower bound is $T_{k}(x+z)-T_{k}(x)\geq w_{0}^{\top}z$ . Since

[Tableau] $$w^{\top}z=\bar{z}+(w-u)^{\top}z^{\circ},$$

Cauchy–Schwarz bounds deviations from $\bar{z}$ by $\sqrt{1/k-1/L}\|z^{\circ}\|_{2}$ . Set $x=\mathbf{H}$ , $z=\bm{\ell}-\mathbf{H}$ to obtain ( A.27 ).

For sharpness when $k<L$ , choose a fixed $k$ -subset $J$ , $w=\mathbf{1}_{J}/k$ , $x=0$ , and $z=w-u$ . Then $\bar{z}=0$ , its top- $k$ subset is $J$ , and

[Tableau] $$T_{k}(z)=w^{\top}(w-u)=\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{L},\qquad\|z\|_{2}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{L}}.$$

Equality holds. When $k=L$ , $T_{k}$ is the mean and the bound is the identity with zero remainder. No part of the proof used a distribution for $x,z,L$ , so it remains true after any span or validity selection. ∎

A weaker but sometimes convenient consequence of the dual representation is

[Tableau] $$|T_{k}(x+z)-T_{k}(x)|\leq\min\{\|z\|_{\infty},\ \|z\|_{1}/k,\ \|z\|_{2}/\sqrt{k}\}.$$

The centered bound distinguishes an overall score displacement from heterogeneous fluctuations that can alter which tokens enter the tail.


##### A.6.6 Fixed-context convexity, equality, and autoregressive limits

In a separate fixed-context perturbation model, hold $p_{1:L},q_{1:L}$ fixed exogenously and draw independent tokens from the $q_{t}$ . Then

[Tableau] $$0\leq\mathbb{E}T_{k}(\bm{\ell})-T_{k}(\mathbf{c})\leq\sqrt{\left(\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{L}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{L}\right)\sum_{t=1}^{L}v_{t}}.$$ (A.29)

Equality on the left holds exactly when some fixed $k$ -element subset is a maximizing subset almost surely. Zero varentropy is sufficient in the matched-law case, but is not necessary.

Hold the distributions fixed exogenously. The deterministic mean vector is $\mathbf{c}$ and $\bm{\ell}=\mathbf{c}+\bm{\xi}$ , with $\mathbb{E}\bm{\xi}=0$ . Equation ( 11 ) expresses $T_{k}$ as a maximum of linear functions, so it is convex. Jensen’s inequality gives $\mathbb{E}T_{k}(\bm{\ell})\geq T_{k}(\mathbf{c})$ . The pathwise upper bound gives

[Tableau] $$T_{k}(\mathbf{c}+\bm{\xi})-T_{k}(\mathbf{c})\leq\bar{\xi}+\sqrt{1/k-1/L}\|\bm{\xi}-\bar{\xi}\mathbf{1}\|_{2}.$$

Its expectation is at most

[Tableau] $$\sqrt{1/k-1/L}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\|\bm{\xi}-\bar{\xi}\mathbf{1}\|_{2}^{2}}.$$

Independence and centering imply $\mathbb{E}\bar{\xi}^{2}=L^{-2}\sum_{t}v_{t}$ . Hence

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}\|\bm{\xi}-\bar{\xi}\mathbf{1}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{t}v_{t}-L\mathbb{E}\bar{\xi}^{2}=(1-1/L)\sum_{t}v_{t},$$

proving ( A.29 ). Jensen itself does not require independence. For dependent perturbations with covariance matrix $\Sigma$ , the same proof replaces the final factor by $\operatorname{tr}\Sigma-L^{-1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{1}$ .

For equality, choose a deterministic $k$ -subset $J$ maximizing the mean vector $\mathbf{c}$ . Then

[Tableau] $$T_{k}(\bm{\ell})-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t\in J}\ell_{t}\geq 0\quad\text{almost surely},$$

and its expectation equals $\mathbb{E}T_{k}(\bm{\ell})-T_{k}(\mathbf{c})$ . That expectation is zero exactly when the nonnegative random variable is zero almost surely, i.e. when $J$ is maximizing almost surely. Conversely, if a fixed $J$ is maximizing almost surely, its expected value is at least that of every other subset, so it also maximizes the mean vector and the gap is zero. This proves the stated necessary and sufficient condition, including ties and $k=L$ . ∎

For $L=2,k=1$ , take independent matched-law tokens from $p_{1}=(2/3,1/3)$ and $p_{2}=(0.3,0.3,0.2,0.2)$ . Both distributions have positive varentropy. Every first-coordinate surprise is at most $\log 3$ , while every second-coordinate surprise is at least $\log(10/3)>\log 3$ . The second coordinate is therefore always the maximum, and

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}\max(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})=H(p_{2})=\max(H(p_{1}),H(p_{2})).$$

Repeating the low-support distribution at nine positions and using the high-support distribution at one gives the same counterexample for the literal $L=10,k=1$ tail10 score. Zero varentropy is therefore not necessary, even if every coordinate has positive varentropy. For a finite distribution $p$ , $V(p)=0$ itself is equivalent to uniformity on its positive-probability support: zero variance forces $-\log p(Y)$ to be constant, so all positive probabilities coincide; the converse is immediate.

For example, let all coordinates be independent draws from $p=(0.9,0.1)$ and let $k=\lceil 0.1L\rceil$ . The two surprise values are $a=-\log 0.9$ and $b=-\log 0.1$ . If $N_{L}$ is the number of rare draws, then

[Tableau] $$T_{k}(\bm{\ell})=a+(b-a)\min(N_{L}/k,1).$$

The law of large numbers gives $N_{L}/L\to 0.1$ and $k/L\to 0.1$ , so $T_{k}(\bm{\ell})\to b$ almost surely. Bounded convergence then transfers this almost-sure limit to the expected score:

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}T_{k}(\bm{\ell})-T_{k}(\mathbf{H})\longrightarrow b-H(p)>0.$$

Thus a finite-sample upper-tail selection bias can remain of constant order at a fixed tail fraction, even with independent coordinates. The bound in ( A.29 ) is consistent: for equal $v_{t}=v$ and fixed $k/L$ , it is of order $\sqrt{v}$ , not $L^{-1/2}$ . At fixed $L$ , the bound vanishes with the coordinate varentropies.

Consider a deterministic horizon $L=10$ , $k=1$ , with matched sampling law $q_{t}=p_{t}$ . At the first step, $p_{1}=(0.9,0.1)$ . If the common token is drawn, let step two be deterministic. If the rare token is drawn, let step two be uniform on ten tokens. Steps three through ten are deterministic. On the common-token branch, $T_{1}(\bm{\ell})=-\log 0.9$ while $T_{1}(\mathbf{H})=H(p_{1})$ . On the rare-token branch, both equal $\log 10$ . Consequently

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}T_{1}(\bm{\ell})-\mathbb{E}T_{1}(\mathbf{H})=0.9[-\log 0.9-H(p_{1})]<0.$$

Every per-step conditional-mean identity is valid, but the sequence of later distributions reveals the first draw. Conditioning on that sequence is therefore not the independent resampling experiment used in ( A.29 ). This also shows why $T_{k}(\mathbf{H})$ should not be described as the exact Rao–Blackwell conditional expectation of the entire sampled tail score.


##### A.6.7 From score discrepancies to ranking discrepancies

Let two scores $S,T$ be coupled on the same trajectory. On independent right and wrong draws write $e_{y}=S_{y}-T_{y}$ . Then

[Tableau] $$|\operatorname{AUC}(S)-\operatorname{AUC}(T)|\leq\Pr\bigl(|T_{W}-T_{R}|\leq|e_{W}|+|e_{R}|\bigr).$$ (A.30)

In particular, for any $\epsilon_{R},\epsilon_{W}\geq 0$ , the right side is at most

[Tableau] $$\Pr\bigl(|T_{W}-T_{R}|\leq\epsilon_{R}+\epsilon_{W}\bigr)+\Pr(|e_{R}|>\epsilon_{R})+\Pr(|e_{W}|>\epsilon_{W}).$$

Outside the event on the right of ( A.30 ), the perturbation $e_{W}-e_{R}$ is strictly smaller in absolute value than $T_{W}-T_{R}$ , so it cannot change its sign or create a tie. Thus the two pairwise $\psi$ contributions agree there. On the event they can differ by at most one. Taking expectations proves the first bound; splitting according to the two error events proves the second. ∎

The lemma supplies a sufficient route from a small pathwise discrepancy to similar AUROC: pairwise entropy-score margins must also be large relative to that discrepancy. The converse fails. Large changes that preserve rankings can leave AUROC unchanged, and equal aggregate AUROCs can arise from different pairs changing in opposite directions. Hence neither an equal AUROC nor a confidence interval crossing zero supports an estimate of low varentropy. The empirical comparison and the conditional mathematical mechanisms must remain separate claims.
