## Résumé

Whether models trained on different modalities converge to the same representation of the world is central to AI. The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH) conjectures that they do as they scale, but without a proof it offers neither a guarantee nor a recipe. We turn the hypothesis into a theorem. First, we prove a Platonic Representation Theorem: Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) trained separately on any number of modalities learn the same representation up to one rotation whenever their positive pairs are two observations of one scene--good views--and their misalignment is bounded otherwise. Second, we show that next-token prediction is a JEPA with bad views, parts of a single document, which explains why language models align with vision only through scale. Third, we validate the theory from 145k captions to the texts of 940M web images, and uncover a scaling law of alignment that predicts unseen runs within seed noise. ## Proving and Improving the Platonic Representation Hypothesis with JEPAs


### 1 Introduction

Whether different observers of one world end up with the same picture of it is a question with roots dating back millennia, from the shadows of Plato’s cave to perception as unconscious inference [ 48 , 63 ] and the reduction of redundancy in sensory messages [ 7 ] . Deep networks revived that question. Word embeddings trained separately on different languages turned out to be related by a linear map [ 39 ] , even by a rotation that can be found without a single translated pair, enough to translate words and then sentences with no parallel data [ 11 , 29 ] . Across modalities, Huh et al. [22] observed that the representations of vision and language models become increasingly similar as models scale, and hypothesized that they converge to a shared statistical model of reality–coined the Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH).

Alas, the PRH remains an observation. Its supporting argument is an idealized contrastive learner whose kernel converges to the pointwise mutual information of co-occurring world events [ 22 ] , the closest proof we know of covers embedded deep linear networks trained by SGD [ 75 ] , and a recent theory explains why translations share representations inside multilingual language models [ 14 ] . For the self-supervised objectives used in practice, and in particular for Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) [ 30 , 5 ] , there is no proof, no prediction of when the hypothesis holds, and hence no recipe to improve alignment. As a result, scaling models and data remains the main lever used so far.

Our study proposes to turn that observation into a theorem by asking the following question: when do models, trained separately on different modalities, provably learn the same representation? We answer it for JEPAs with a short argument. A JEPA with whitened second-order anti-collapse learns the spectral embedding of its positive pairs , the pairs of inputs it declares to be views of each other [ 19 , 4 ] . If the modalities render one world and their positive pairs relate the same world states, their training problems are one problem written in different coordinate systems, and their solutions coincide up to a rotation–which we coin the Platonic Representation Theorem ( Section 2.1 ). Nothing in this argument is specific to images and text: it holds for any modalities that observe one world, e.g., images, video, text, audio or actions, and for any number of them at once. We therefore obtain the following key insight. The PRH is a statement about positive views: a JEPA whose positives are two observations of the same scene learns a representation that belongs to the world, while a JEPA whose positives are corruptions of one observation learns one that belongs to its modality ( Figure 1 ). We coin the former good-view and the latter bad-view JEPAs, and summarize our contributions below.

Contribution 1: We turn the PRH into a theorem. In an idealized model, any number of JEPAs trained separately on renderings of one world learn the same embedding up to one rotation each when their positive pairs relate the same world states and their minimizers are unique up to rotation, as we prove for whitened JEPAs under a spectral gap ( Sections 2.1 and 2.1 ); otherwise, their misalignment is bounded by the mismatch of their positive pairs ( Section 2.2 ). Positives that corrupt a single observation make an encoder spend dimensions on its modality’s nuisance whenever they keep more of it than of the world’s $k$ -th direction, which no rotation can align, while two observations of one scene spend none ( Sections 2.3 and 2.3 ), and views and data together set what a JEPA learns ( Section 2.3 ).

Contribution 2: We show that next-token prediction is a bad-view JEPA. Its positives are a prefix and its continuation within one document, and its anti-collapse term is the softmax log-partition ( Section 2.4 ). This suggests why language models align with vision only partly, and mostly through scale.

Contribution 3: We validate good views and uncover a scaling law of alignment. With identical texts and encoders, good views align best in all but a few of the comparisons we ran ( Appendix C ): from 145k Flickr captions to the texts of 940M web images, with image encoders trained from scratch or frozen, and on video ( Section 3 ). Alignment follows a scaling law in the fraction of good views and the samples seen, which predicts 85 new runs, before they are trained, as accurately as rerunning them with another seed ( Section 4.4 ).

Contribution 4: We improve alignment beyond 70B language models, which scale poorly in comparison. Good views help in three ways ( Section 4 ). They scale with data: trained on the texts of 50M web images, a 33M-parameter text JEPA reaches .125 on the PRH paper’s benchmark, against .034 on Flickr captions, level with Qwen2.5-3B and -7B. They need fewer samples: on the same corpus, a good-view JEPA reaches with 21.6M training samples the alignment that next-token prediction reaches with 900M, 42 times more ( Figure 1 ). They beat larger models: trained on the texts of 940M web images, a 109M-parameter text JEPA reaches .184, above every language model we evaluated, up to Qwen2.5-72B (.154), and above CLIP’s text encoder (.124), on two benchmarks and two metrics; it also leads the language models with MAE, I-JEPA and CLIP’s image encoders, e.g., .230 against .210 with CLIP ViT-H. For alignment, next-token prediction is thus a remarkably inefficient use of scale: our 109M encoder, which read 116B text tokens, beats models 660 times larger trained on over 100 times more.

Contribution 5: We caption images without training a network on image–caption pairs. One linear map with orthonormal rows, the only part fitted on pairs, joins frozen DINOv2 and DINOv3 image encoders to a language model fine-tuned on text alone, reaching CIDEr .838 on COCO ( Section 5.1 ). With no pair at all, the rotation can be found from the geometry of unpaired images and captions: on the benchmark of Schnaus et al. [54] , which aligns a vision and a language encoder from unpaired images and captions and scores the result by zero-shot classification, our 109M text JEPA beats every zero-shot accuracy they report, over 21 pairs of vision and language models and five settings, and it beats their strongest language model, Qwen3-8B, rerun under identical conditions, by 17 points of zero-shot ImageNet-100 accuracy ( Section 5.2 ). This demonstrates that the PRH–with JEPAs–is no longer a hypothesis but a theorem and a tool.


### 2 Shared Positives Provably Make Representations Platonic

We start by formalizing a world, the modalities that observe it and their positive pairs, then prove that JEPAs with shared positives agree up to one rotation ( Section 2.1 ), bound their misalignment otherwise ( Section 2.2 ), and show which positives make an encoder spend its embedding on its modality’s nuisance ( Sections 2.3 , 2.3 and 2.3 ), of which next-token prediction is an instance ( Section 2.4 ).


#### 2.1 The Platonic Representation Theorem


##### World, modalities, and positive pairs.

Let a world be a finite set of states $z\in\mathcal{Z}$ with probabilities $p(z)>0$ , and let $M\geq 2$ modalities observe it, modality $m$ through an injective rendering $x=g_{m}(z)$ , e.g., the pixels of a photograph or the tokens of a caption. A JEPA of modality $m$ is trained on positive pairs $(g_{m}(z),g_{m}(z^{\prime}))$ whose world states are drawn from a symmetric positive-pair distribution $(z,z^{\prime})\sim\pi_{m}$ with both marginals equal to $p$ . Its encoder $f:\mathcal{X}_{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}$ minimizes

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{m}(f)=\mathbb{E}_{(z,z^{\prime})\sim\pi_{m}}\,\ell\big(f(g_{m}(z)),f(g_{m}(z^{\prime}))\big)+\Omega(f),$$ (1)

a prediction term $\ell$ plus an anti-collapse term $\Omega$ that depends on $f$ only through the distribution of its embeddings (possibly a hard constraint, $\Omega\in\{0,\infty\}$ ). As in our experiments, the predictor is the identity: $\ell$ compares the two embeddings directly, whereas JEPAs used as world models predict one embedding from the other through a learned predictor, often conditioned on an action, which our analysis does not cover. Both $\ell$ and $\Omega$ are invariant to rotations $\boldsymbol{Q}\in\mathrm{O}(k)$ : $\ell(\boldsymbol{Q}a,\boldsymbol{Q}b)=\ell(a,b)$ , and rotating every embedding leaves $\Omega$ unchanged. Spectral contrastive learning and whitened MSE fit this form [ 19 , 16 ] , VICReg and Barlow Twins in a whitened limit [ 6 , 72 , 4 ] , and LeJEPA’s SIGReg, which pushes the embeddings toward an isotropic Gaussian, in expectation over its directions [ 5 ] ( Appendix A ). The key observation is that an injective rendering only changes coordinates: $\mathcal{L}_{m}(f)=\mathcal{L}_{\pi_{m}}(f\circ g_{m})$ , the same objective written on world states, $\mathcal{L}_{\pi}(h)=\mathbb{E}_{(z,z^{\prime})\sim\pi}\ell(h(z),h(z^{\prime}))+\Omega(h)$ for $h:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}$ ( Section A.2 ). We are now able to state when modalities learn the same representation.

In words, encoders that never see each other’s modality, and whose renderings need not be related beyond describing the same world, learn the same embedding up to one rotation each: in this idealized JEPA setting–one finite world rendered injectively, shared positives, population optima, and minimizers unique up to rotation–the PRH becomes a theorem, for images and texts as for any other set of modalities. The proof is direct: every $f_{m}^{\star}\circ g_{m}$ minimizes $\mathcal{L}_{\pi}$ . The key assumption is uniqueness up to rotation, which holds for JEPAs with second-order anti-collapse under a spectral gap, as formalized below. Write $\boldsymbol{\Pi}$ for the matrix of $\pi$ ( $\Pi_{zz^{\prime}}=\pi(z,z^{\prime})$ ), $\boldsymbol{D}=\mathrm{diag}(p)$ , and $\boldsymbol{K}_{\pi}=\boldsymbol{D}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\Pi}\boldsymbol{D}^{-1/2}$ for the normalized adjacency of the graph that positives draw on the world, with eigenvalues $1=\mu_{0}\geq\mu_{1}\geq\dots$ and orthonormal eigenvectors $\boldsymbol{v}_{0}=\boldsymbol{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\dots$ .

In words, a JEPA with second-order anti-collapse learns the top eigenvectors of the graph its positives draw, the population form of Laplacian eigenmaps [ 8 , 4 , 19 ] , unique up to rotation when the spectrum has a gap. LeJEPA’s SIGReg target $\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{I}_{k})$ is rotation invariant and has the same second moments; Section 2.1 does not cover uniqueness under its full-distribution constraint, which we leave as an assumption.


#### 2.2 When Positives Differ

Alas, in practice the modalities’ positives are not the same pairs of world states.

In words, for the objective of Section 2.1 the left side is the root-mean-square distance between the embeddings of paired observations after the best single rotation, and only two quantities matter: how different the graphs drawn by the two modalities’ positives are, and how separated the top of their spectrum is. Crucially, the same argument applied to a block of eigenvectors, with gaps on both sides of the block [ 71 ] , suggests where alignment lives: directions separated from the rest of the spectrum by large gaps are protected, while directions near the end of the spectrum need not align at all. Figure 2 validates the bound on randomly generated finite worlds, each point being one world with a pair of kernels that differ by a controlled amount: in all 300 worlds the error lies below the bound, at 18% of it at most.

We clearly observe that the error lies below the bound in every world (dashed: equality), at 18% of it at most.


##### What pairs are for.

Section 2.1 says that the embeddings agree up to a rotation, but not which one. As formalized below, the first two moments of unpaired data cannot find it, and $k$ pairs suffice.

In words, pairs enter only through one $k\times k$ orthogonal matrix, which $k$ pairs in general position determine; when the positives differ, more pairs average out the residual misalignment of Section 2.2 . Higher-order structure of a finite world can reveal the rotation without any pair, as recent work shows by aligning encoders from their shared geometry alone [ 54 ] ; with good views, whose geometry the theory ties to the world’s, this works best ( Section 5.2 ). When the two embedding dimensions differ (768 for DINOv2, 384 for our text JEPA) we fit a linear map with orthonormal rows, a case the theory does not cover; such a map between dimensions $d_{a}\geq d_{b}$ has $d_{a}d_{b}-d_{b}(d_{b}+1)/2$ free parameters, 0.22M for DINOv2 ViT-B and 1.9M for the 5,120-dimensional features of our best captioner, all fitted on pairs.


#### 2.3 Good-View and Bad-View JEPAs

Section 2.2 singles out what a JEPA must get right for the PRH: its positive pairs. Call a JEPA good-view when its positives are two observations of world states that the world itself relates, e.g., two photographs or two descriptions of one scene, and bad-view when they are corruptions of a single observation, e.g., two crops of one photograph or two maskings of one sentence ( Figure 1 ). Injective renderings cannot express this distinction: there, a state has a single observation. We therefore let each observation carry a nuisance $\nu$ of its own modality–which words a caption happens to use, from which viewpoint a photograph was taken–drawn independently of the state. It is a nuisance only for alignment: a world model that acts through a camera must track its viewpoint, but no caption determines it, so no rotation can match it across modalities. We render $x=g_{m}(z,\nu)$ injectively, so that a state has many observations. A good-view JEPA’s positives are $(g_{m}(z,\nu),g_{m}(z^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}))$ with $(z,z^{\prime})\sim\pi$ and independent nuisances; a bad-view JEPA’s are two corruptions of one observation, which share its nuisance. For an encoder $f$ centered and whitened on its observations, let $\boldsymbol{C}_{f}=\mathrm{Cov}\big(\mathbb{E}[f(x)\mid z]\big)$ be the covariance of the part of its embedding that the world state explains, and $\rho(f)=\mathrm{tr}\,\boldsymbol{C}_{f}^{1/2}/k\in[0,1]$ its scene-explained fraction . The cost of encoding nuisance is formalized below.

In words, every direction an encoder spends on its own modality’s nuisance is lost for alignment, whatever whitened embedding the other modality learns. Which JEPAs spend directions on nuisance is formalized below.

In words, a corruption that preserves the nuisance more than the world’s $k$ -th direction–spans of one caption keep its word choice, crops of one photograph its viewpoint and lighting–makes the JEPA spend embedding directions on that nuisance, which no rotation can align with another modality; two observations of one scene share only the scene, so a good-view JEPA spends none. The model still assumes that every observation determines its state, while a caption sees only part of a photograph; Section 2.2 covers that departure only through the kernel mismatch. By the same definition our image encoders, and DINOv2, are bad-view JEPAs: their positives are crops of one photograph. Our experiments mostly vary the text side against a frozen DINOv2, the vision model with which Huh et al. [22] measured the PRH, so that our text encoders compare directly with the language models measured there; DINOv3 [ 56 ] , released after that benchmark, serves in our captioner ( Section 5.1 ), and other vision families rank the text side alike ( Table 18 ). Good image views need several observations of each scene, e.g., the frames of a video: MSR-VTT tests them ( Section 3.3 ), but an image JEPA trained on its 7k clips is far weaker than DINOv2, and scaling good image views is left to future work.

That is, good views fix the world kernel by design, while bad views and next-token prediction get it from the data .


##### Views and data together.

Section 2.3 lets a corruption act on state and nuisance separately. In general, whether the positives of two different observations coincide depends on the data: masking two captions of one scene can leave the same words. In the augmentation-graph analyses of contrastive learning, such overlaps are what connect samples of one class [ 19 , 66 ] , with the function class deciding what overlaps [ 52 , 26 , 9 ] . We now state an analogue with world states in place of classes, and its cross-modal consequence ( Figure 3 ). Given any law $W_{m}$ of positive pairs over modality $m$ ’s finitely many observations–good views, corruptions, or a mix–call its induced world kernel $\pi_{W_{m}}$ the probability that a positive pair joins an observation of $z$ to one of $z^{\prime}$ .

In words, a spectral JEPA learns, up to $\tau_{m}$ , the spectral solution of the world kernel that its views and its data induce, and two such JEPAs of different modalities agree, up to $\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}$ , exactly as well as those kernels’ top eigenvectors do. Good views induce the world’s own kernel with $\tau_{m}=0$ , which gives Section 2.1 with many observations per state. Crucially, a bad-view JEPA does not choose its world kernel: $\pi_{W_{m}}$ records which states its positives connect, which depends on how the data cover each state and its neighbors. Bad-view JEPAs of two modalities therefore agree only insofar as their data make the positives connect the same states, while good views fix the kernel by design.


##### How much data, and which.

Read on finitely many samples, with positives that join two samples when their corruptions overlap–the empirical augmentation graph–weak views join few samples, so each level set of the world splits into small pieces that a spectral JEPA separates, and more samples chain the overlaps through each level set. Good views join all observations of related states in one step and need no such density. The kind of data, in contrast, sets the kernel itself: chains of overlapping positives stay within a scene for literal descriptions, but cross into states that share only a nuisance for alt-texts sharing a brand name. Figure 4 maps this trade-off on a synthetic world: the reach that weak bad views need falls as the samples per state grow, along $\Delta\propto\log N/N$ , the scale of the largest gap between a state’s samples; reach large enough to cross scenes caps alignment at any sample size; and good views align exactly from one sample per state. Mixed data, whose classes overlap, cap every bad-view JEPA lower still ( Figure 12 ). Our runs show the same trade-off: a good-view JEPA reaches the alignment of next-token prediction on the same corpus with 42 times fewer samples ( Figure 1 ), and alignment follows a scaling law in the fraction of good views and the samples seen ( Section 4.4 ). Real data agree with this reading as well: with the same bad views, a text JEPA trained on LLaVA recaptions, literal descriptions of what is visible, aligns with DINOv2 better than one trained on alt-texts ( Table 7 ); changing the data changes how positives mix state and nuisance as well as the kernel, so this comparison cannot single out the kernel.

#### 2.4 Next-Token Prediction is a Bad-View JEPA

Language models are the usual text side of the PRH [ 22 ] . We now show that their training objective is a JEPA in disguise, as formalized below.

In words, next-token prediction is a JEPA whose positives are a prefix and its continuation, and whose anti-collapse term is the log-partition. Part (iii) is the matrix-factorization view of word embeddings [ 31 ] , and (ii) plays the role of the uniformity term of contrastive learning [ 65 ] , much as masked autoencoders implicitly align the two parts that a mask creates [ 73 ] . Crucially, (i) tells us what next-token prediction’s positives are: a prefix and its continuation inside one document. Its kernel therefore relates texts through shared wording, like masked-span JEPAs, and not through shared world states. Heuristically, Section 2.3 then applies to it: a prefix and its continuation share their document’s nuisance (its author’s wording and style), so next-token prediction should spend directions on that nuisance and align with vision worse than a good-view JEPA. Section 2.3 assumes one encoder and a kernel on one space, so this is a prediction to test rather than a corollary–which we do next.

Read through Section 2.3 , again heuristically since next-token prediction is not a spectral JEPA and is identified only up to $\mathrm{GL}(k)$ , it has a second kind of positive. At the optimum (iii), $\langle h(c),w_{y}\rangle=\log p(y\mid c)+b(c)$ , and contexts followed by the same token gather around that token’s output vector, the neural collapse that language models approach with scale [ 46 , 67 ] . Contexts thus become implicit positives of each other when they share a continuation: very weak views, since one token rarely spans a scene, so joining the descriptions of one scene takes long chains of shared continuations, i.e., very dense data, while frequent continuations (function words, a document’s style) also join contexts across scenes. Good-view JEPA views instead join all descriptions of a scene at once and never leave it. This is our reading of why language models align with vision mostly through scale, and why good views need far less of it.


### 3 Good Views Improve Alignment

We now empirically validate the predictions of Section 2 and Section 2.4 : good-view JEPAs should align across modalities better than bad-view JEPAs and next-token prediction trained on identical data, whatever the data, the encoders and the modality.


##### Setup.

We strive for simplicity and thus adopt a unified recipe: image and text encoders are trained separately , each with the same LeJEPA objective [ 5 ] (invariance to the mean of two global views, plus SIGReg with $\lambda=0.02$ ), projector, optimizer and schedule, using stable-pretraining. Data is Flickr30k [ 70 ] with the Karpathy split [ 25 ] : 29k training images with five captions each. The image encoder is a ViT-S/16 [ 15 ] trained on 2 global ( $224^{2}$ ) and 6 local ( $96^{2}$ ) crops. The text encoder is a 12-layer BERT of the same width (384, 33M parameters; BERT-S below, with BERT-T, -B and -L of 11M, 109M and 335M parameters for scaling) [ 13 ] , trained from scratch in three variants that differ only in their positives: masked spans (bad-view JEPA): 8 views of one caption, each trimmed to a random contiguous span, with 15% of tokens masked; caption views (good-view JEPA): each of the 8 views is drawn from a different caption of the same image, then trimmed and masked the same way; next-token prediction : the same BERT made causal, with a tied softmax head, on one caption per image per epoch; as a control, we also train it on pairs of captions of the same image. No encoder ever sees the other modality. The runs of Table 1 use 200 epochs, batch 256 and AdamW (lr $5\!\cdot\!10^{-4}$ , weight decay 0.05); longer and larger runs use a lower learning rate with gradient clipping. We train three seeds of every comparison and report their mean unless stated otherwise. Details are in Appendix B .


##### Metrics.

We embed the 1,014 validation and 1,000 test image–caption pairs (first caption). Mutual 10-NN [ 22 ] measures the overlap of the 10-nearest-neighbor sets in the two spaces without fitting anything; chance is $10/N=.01$ . To test equality up to rotation directly, we fit one orthogonal map on the validation pairs [ 55 ] and report image $\to$ caption retrieval R@1 and R@5 among the 1,000 test captions (chance 0.1%).

#### 3.1 Good Views Align Across Modalities

Figures 5 and 1 report the alignment of a separately trained image JEPA with each text encoder, and we obtain a few key observations. First, cross-modal alignment rises from chance throughout training for every text encoder, although none ever sees the other modality. Second, with caption views the true caption is retrieved first for 4.4% of test images through one rotation, 44 times chance, while masked spans and next-token prediction, trained on the same captions with the same encoder, nearly coincide and stay well below–a ranking that holds in every seed, with gaps several times the seed spread. Third, the gain comes from the objective and not from the information that two captions describe the same image: next-token prediction trained on two captions of the same image, [CLS] $a$ [SEP] $b$ [SEP] , barely moves (mutual 10-NN .042 to .043, R@1 .021 to .023), i.e., the same positives help next-token prediction far less than they help as JEPA views, as per Section 2.4 . Fourth, alignment concentrates in the leading spectral directions, as the block version of Section 2.2 suggests: after the best rotation, the correlation between image and text embeddings is 0.70 on the first principal direction of the text space and decays to about 0.1 by the 60th, while it is near zero everywhere at initialization ( Figure 6 ). Finally, the nuisance mechanism of Section 2.3 can be measured directly, since every Flickr image (a world state) has five captions (five nuisances): from all captions of the 2,014 validation and test images, we estimate $\boldsymbol{C}_{f}$ for each text encoder, restricted to its top- $k$ principal directions and whitened, with the mean of five captions corrected for its within-image spread. For $k=32$ , the scene explains a fraction $\rho$ of .804 of the good-view JEPA’s embedding, against .655 for the bad-view JEPA and .608 for next-token prediction (each within $\pm$ .005 across seeds), so Section 2.3 bounds their best-rotation error against DINOv2 below by 12.6, 22.1 and 25.1 (from the unrounded fractions), which the measured errors (33.5, 39.4 and 40.3) respect and follow in order; $k=128$ gives the same ordering ( Table 6 ). That is, bad views and next-token prediction spend more of their embedding on the wording of a particular caption, as Section 2.3 predicts, and align less .

#### 3.2 Trimming Makes Good Views, the Rest Barely Matters

Recall that a good view should keep only what two observations share. To identify which knob of the good-view JEPA matters, Figure 7 changes one setting at a time and scores the text JEPA against frozen DINOv2 (R@1 through a rotation fitted on validation pairs, last epoch, no selection on test pairs). With three seeds for every setting except the SIGReg weights, the larger learning rate and the 1,000-epoch run, every run that does not collapse lands between .18 and .28, far above the bad-view JEPA (.13) and next-token prediction (.12), which use the same recipe and were not tuned. Trimming matters most: whole captions as views lose a quarter of the alignment (.185 against .246), as trimming is what makes two descriptions of a scene share only their common content. Masking, the number of views, batch size, width and schedule move the mean by at most .016, about the spread of the default’s own seeds (.230–.260). Stability is another matter. Without gradient clipping, 3 of the 36 runs with the default SIGReg weight collapse abruptly between epochs 20 and 60 of 200 (two of three seeds with 2 + 2 views, one with mask 30%), and so does every other SIGReg weight we tried. With clipping, 2 + 2 views trains for all three seeds (.262) and so do $\lambda=0.005$ and $0.1$ , while a larger learning rate and $\lambda=0.05$ still collapse; none of our longer runs, all trained with clipping and a lower learning rate, collapses ( Table 8 ). We thus recommend views drawn from different observations of the same world state, each trimmed (cropped, for images) and lightly masked, trained with gradient clipping, as the starting point for the modalities we tested.

#### 3.3 Good Views Help on Both Sides

To test the image half of the prediction we need a world state observed several times in both modalities, which video provides. On MSR-VTT [ 68 ] (7k training clips, 20 captions each) we train the same image and text JEPAs with views from different frames (images) or captions (text) of a clip (good), or from a single frame or caption (bad), and evaluate on the 1,000 test clips (middle frame and one caption; Appendix B ). Good views raise mutual 10-NN on each side, in every seed, the text side much more than the image side: .067 when both sides have good views, .059 with good text views only, .043 with good image views only and .039 with neither (same ordering in each seed; chance .01; Figures 8 and 13 ). Conversely, frozen DINOv2, whose positives are crops, agrees more with the image JEPA trained on crops than with the one trained on frames (.144 vs. .132, in every seed). That is, alignment follows agreement of positives , as per Section 2.2 .

#### 3.4 Good-View JEPAs Beat a Pretrained Language Model and Approach Pair-Trained Encoders

Our absolute numbers so far are limited by the image encoder, a ViT-S trained from scratch on 29k images: training it five times longer raises its mutual 10-NN with the good-view text JEPA from .065 to .077 (seed 0), and with DINOv2 from .101 to .121. To isolate the text side, we pair each text encoder with a frozen DINOv2 ViT-B/14 [ 44 ] under the same protocol ( Table 2 ). With final checkpoints and no selection on test data, the good-view JEPA retrieves the true caption first for 24.6% of test images, about twice the bad-view JEPA (.133), our next-token model (.118, or .137 with caption pairs) and the pretrained Qwen2.5-0.5B language model [ 49 ] (.135, last layer). Text encoders trained on vastly more data reach .300 (all-mpnet-base-v2 [ 51 , 57 ] , a billion sentence pairs, Flickr and COCO captions among them) and .320 (CLIP’s text encoder, 400M image–text pairs, 50 ), but our good-view JEPAs have the neighborhoods that agree most with DINOv2’s (mutual 10-NN .204–.257 vs. .189 and .167), and the one trained on the texts of 940M web images also retrieves better than both (.349, seed 0). Retrieval is limited by how well the rotation is estimated: fitting the rotation on all 30k Flickr train and validation pairs instead of 1k, still the only part fitted on pairs, raises R@1 to .346 for the good-view JEPA (.363 with BERT-B and .400 for a BERT-B trained on Flickr and COCO captions, one seed each), against .21–.25 for the bad-view JEPA and next-token prediction and .48–.49 for the two reference encoders. Crucially, a good-view JEPA trained on web text alone closes most of that last gap (.464, Section 4 ).

### 4 Next-Token Prediction Scales Poorly Compared to Good-View JEPAs

Language models align with vision through scale [ 22 ] . We now show that next-token prediction spends that scale poorly: on the same corpus, good views reach its alignment with 42 times fewer samples, and a 109M good-view JEPA that read 116B text tokens beats language models with up to 660 times more parameters, trained on 15–18T tokens, over 100 times more.


#### 4.1 Good Views Scale on Web Text

Trained on more text, a text JEPA leaves the caption domain. We train the same 33M-parameter encoder on the texts of Recap-DataComp web images [ 34 ] : each image’s alt-text and two recaptions, written by an image captioner (LLaVA built on LLaMA-3-8B), are its views. We obtain three key observations ( Figure 9 ). First, on the WIT-1024 benchmark of Huh et al. [22] , on which none of our models is trained, its alignment with DINOv2 ViT-B rises from .034 when trained on Flickr captions to .108, .111 and .125 when trained on the texts of 2.16M, 10M and 50M images and stays at .125 with 100M (seeds differ by at most $\pm$ .009). At 50M and 100M images this 33M-parameter encoder, trained without next-token or masked-token prediction, is level with Qwen2.5-3B and -7B (.125 and .118; intervals in Appendix C ). Second, views matter at both scales we tested: with the same encoder, texts and steps, the bad-view JEPA reaches .053 and next-token prediction .071, against .108 for the good-view JEPA, and with 23 times more images (1.9 times more steps) .063 and .079 against .125. Third, Flickr retrieval keeps rising with data, to .348, .376 and .402 with the texts of 10M, 50M and 100M images and .408 with BERT-B on 50M, level with our best in-domain model (.400) without a single Flickr or COCO caption: good views on web text carry most of the in-domain alignment. Width helps Flickr retrieval but not yet WIT at these scales: BERT-B reaches R@1 .388 and .408 with the texts of 2.16M and 50M images, but WIT .109 and .116, at or below BERT-S (.108 and .125), and with Flickr and COCO captions fixed, R@1 grows with the size of the text encoder up to BERT-B and then drops (.335, .367, .400 and .376 for 11M, 33M, 109M and 335M parameters; one seed).


#### 4.2 Good Views Need 42 Times Fewer Samples

With the same 109M encoder and texts from the same corpus, the good-view JEPA reaches .109 on WIT after 21.6M training samples (the texts of 2.16M images), the alignment that next-token prediction reaches after 900M samples (.111, the texts of 940M images; we scored no earlier next-token checkpoint), and a higher Flickr R@1 (.388 against .335); each run uses the recipe of its scale (learning rate $10^{-4}$ and batch 256 against $2\cdot 10^{-4}$ and 2,048). That is 42 times fewer samples, 14 times fewer text tokens since a good-view sample reads three texts, and 435 times fewer unique images ( Figure 1 , right). At matched samples the gap is larger still: .108 against .071 at 21.6M samples for the 33M encoder.


#### 4.3 A 109M Text JEPA Beats Language Models up to 72B Parameters

Scaling data and model together changes the picture. We train the 109M-parameter BERT-B text JEPA with 900M samples from the texts of all 940M Recap-DataComp images (8 or 16 GPUs), and as controls next-token prediction and the bad-view JEPA (two masked copies of one text) with the same encoder, texts and samples. Table 3 gathers the comparison, from which we draw four conclusions.

Good views beat every language model we evaluated. On the PRH paper’s benchmark, the good-view JEPA reaches .184 with DINOv2 ViT-B, above Qwen2.5 from 0.5B to 72B, Llama-3-8B and -70B and Mistral-7B, and it stays ahead with every DINOv2 size, with linear CKA in place of mutual 10-NN, and on the five times larger COCO benchmark ( Tables 16 and 17 ).

The views make the gain, not the texts. Next-token prediction and the bad-view JEPA trained on the same texts stay far below, and, as on Flickr captions, the scene explains more of the good-view embedding than of theirs (measured on the texts of 50M images, Table 6 ).

The lead holds with every vision family. Language models align best with CLIP’s image encoders [ 22 ] , yet with CLIP ViT-H/14 the good-view JEPA is ahead of Qwen2.5-72B and Llama-3-70B, and it leads every language model with MAE and I-JEPA as well ( Table 18 ).

It rivals text encoders trained on pairs. It agrees with DINOv2 more than CLIP’s text encoder (400M image–text pairs) on both benchmarks, more than all-mpnet-base-v2 (a billion sentence pairs, COCO captions among them) on WIT and as much on COCO, and it retrieves Flickr captions through one rotation almost as well as both. CLIP’s text encoder is trained to agree with CLIP’s own image encoder, not with DINOv2, so this compares agreement with an independently trained vision model.

Three caveats qualify these numbers. A fourth seed, trained with twice the effective SIGReg weight, reaches only .121 on WIT, after which we kept the first seed’s weight for the others; all four seeds average .168, still above every language model ( Appendix C ). The recaptioner that wrote most of these texts sees images through CLIP ViT-L/14 ( Section 7 ). And at least 43 of the 1,024 WIT captions appear among the Recap-DataComp alt-texts, although removing them changes none of the nine text models we checked by more than .002. Still, our model has over 600 times fewer parameters than Qwen2.5-72B and never saw pixels: good views turn scale into alignment far more efficiently than next-token prediction .

The image side matters as well. Image JEPAs trained from scratch without texts, on more images (all bad-view JEPAs: their positives are crops of one image), rank the text side the same way: with a ViT-S on the 2.16M Recap images, a ViT-B on 10M and a ViT-S on ImageNet-1k, the good-view text JEPA aligns best, next-token prediction second and the bad-view JEPA last ( Figure 9 , right). The ImageNet ViT-S, which sees the most samples, agrees most with DINOv2 and with our text JEPA, and with it two JEPAs trained separately from scratch agree about as much as the image JEPA agrees with Mistral-7B and Qwen2.5-7B ( Appendix C ).

#### 4.4 A Scaling Law for the PRH

Scaling laws turned the training of language models from trial and error into prediction; we now show that alignment admits one too. With one recipe (BERT-S on the texts of Recap-DataComp images; Appendix C ), each training item draws good views (two texts of its image) with probability $q$ and bad views (two corruptions of one text) otherwise, on the texts of $N$ images for $M$ samples. A bad-view floor rising with $\log M$ plus a good-view term,

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{R@1}=a+d\log M+b\log\big(1+q^{\alpha}M^{\beta}N^{\gamma}/c\big),$$ (7)

fits the 45 cells of a grid up to 80M samples with RMSE .007 ( Figure 11 ). We then predicted 85 runs at random $(q,M)$ on 10.2M images, with $M$ from 3M to 133M samples, before training them: it predicts their Flickr R@1 with RMSE .0093, while two seeds of one run differ by .0096 (pooled over six cells and three seeds each). That is, the law predicts a run it has never seen as well as rerunning that run with another seed . Along its iso-alignment lines, more samples substitute for good views sub-linearly ( $q\propto M^{-0.30}$ ), and unique images enter weakly ( $\gamma=.19$ ): without good views, WIT stays within .054–.057 from the texts of 0.41M to 50.9M images, while with them it rises from .090 to .115. The law fails further out, for good views only: at 320M samples, four times its longest fitted run and 31 passes over the images, it overestimates the runs with good views (R@1 .418 against .455 predicted at $q=1$ , .348 against .389 at $q=.1$ ), while the run without matches (.338 against .337); it has no term for repeated data. Its exponents are poorly identified, and it is an empirical summary of one recipe, not a consequence of Section 2.3 .


### 5 Platonic Representations as a Tool

Alignment up to one rotation lets separately trained encoders be composed. We show two uses: a captioner whose only part fitted on pairs is one rotation, and alignment with no pair at all.


#### 5.1 Captioning Without Training a Network on Image–Caption Pairs

Alignment up to one rotation makes a new kind of captioner possible. We train a caption decoder on text alone: a causal transformer reconstructs each training caption from the frozen good-view JEPA embedding of that caption, with Gaussian noise added to the embedding so that it tolerates alignment error [ 43 ] . To caption an image, we embed it with the frozen DINOv2, apply the rotation and decode. That is, the rotation is the only part fitted on image–caption pairs, and no network is trained on them. Crucially, the noise level is matched to the alignment error rather than tuned: after the rotation, an image and its caption have mean cosine $c\approx.3$ (.29 on held-out validation pairs, .33 on test pairs), and Gaussian noise whose norm is $\sigma$ times the embedding’s leaves an expected cosine of $(1+\sigma^{2})^{-1/2}$ with it, so matching the two gives $\sigma=(c^{-2}-1)^{1/2}\approx 3$ ; we use $\sigma=3$ , and transfer peaks there ( Figure 13 ). We obtain a few key observations. First, on the 1,000 Flickr test images the captioner reaches CIDEr .396 (on a 0–1 scale throughout; most prior work reports it times 100) against five human captions, nine times a random rotation and 2.5 times retrieving the nearest caption through the same rotation ( Table 9 ), and .431 with the rotation fitted on all 30k Flickr pairs. Second, rotations fitted on fewer pairs than dimensions are underdetermined ( Section 2.2 (ii)) but improve when refined on unpaired images and captions ( Figure 13 ): from 30 pairs, the captioner (.169) matches caption retrieval with 1,014 pairs (.158). Third, the captioner transfers across datasets: on the 5,000 COCO Karpathy test images, with the rotation fitted on Flickr pairs only and the decoder trained on Flickr and COCO training captions, it reaches CIDEr .668 and BLEU-4 .199 with a beam of three (.630 greedy), against .038 for a random rotation and .169 for retrieving the nearest Flickr caption. Captioners built on CLIP’s jointly trained encoders, and hence on 400M image–text pairs, report .345 (ZeroCap, 60 , no training), .493 (MAGIC, 59 ) and .918 and .912 (CapDec and DeCap, 43 , 33 ); unlike these, ours uses no encoder trained on pairs but fits its rotation on in-domain Flickr pairs, which they do not use. The rotation is also what makes the captioner transfer: replacing it by maps trained on the same 30k Flickr pairs ( Table 10 ), an unconstrained linear map gains a little on Flickr (CIDEr .531 against .504, web-pretrained decoder, beam 3) but loses a fifth on COCO (.635 against .802), and a two-layer MLP loses on both (.498 and .585); with consensus decoding, the same maps reach .561 and .511 on Flickr but .652 and .597 on COCO, against .538 and .832 for the rotation. That is, a map free to fit the pairs fits Flickr, while a rotation, which can only turn one space onto the other, carries over to new images , as expected if the two spaces agree up to a rotation.

Lastly, the captioner improves further without pairs ( Table 10 ): with a pretrained language model fine-tuned on text alone as the decoder (Qwen2.5-1.5B, 49 ), the text JEPA trained on the texts of 100M web images, DINOv2 ViT-g and DINOv3 ViT-L [ 56 ] as frozen image encoders, and the consensus of 32 sampled captions [ 27 ] , a single map fitted on the same 30k Flickr pairs gives CIDEr .519 on Flickr and .838 on COCO, and .538 and .832 with the decoder of our demo, first trained on web sentences ( Figure 10 ). We thus recommend matching the decoder’s noise to the residual cosine of the rotation, $\sigma=(c^{-2}-1)^{1/2}$ , rather than tuning it.

From photos to videos. Neither the encoders nor the decoder ever saw a video, yet the same captioner describes one from the mean of its frames’ rotated embeddings. On the 2,990 MSR-VTT test clips [ 68 ] , with the rotation fitted on the 30k Flickr pairs and the mean of eight frames, it reaches CIDEr .246 against the 20 human captions of each clip, above the zero-shot captioners whose decoder is trained on image captions or general text alone, all built on CLIP’s jointly trained encoders (.058 for ZeroCap, .074 for MAGIC and .150–.186 for DeCap, 33 ), though below video–language systems such as VideoCoCa (.271, 144M video–text pairs, 69 ) and TOPA (.329, CLIP features and LLM-written textual videos, 32 ), and below decoders trained on MSR-VTT captions (.348). Averaging frames helps both captions and alignment, as Section 2.3 suggests: the frames of a clip are several observations of one scene, and their mean averages out what is particular to each frame. On the 1,000 clips of the 1k-A split, it raises the fit-free mutual 10-NN between clips and captions from .148 (middle frame) to .167, and the same rotation, fitted on photos, retrieves the right clip first for 16.5% of captions, against 31.2% for CLIP ViT-B/32 trained on 400M image–text pairs [ 35 ] ( Table 11 ). That is, the rotation fitted on photos carries over to video .


#### 5.2 Alignment Without Any Pair

Section 2.2 says that the first two moments of unpaired data cannot find the rotation, but the geometry of a finite world can: Schnaus et al. [54] recover it with no pair at all, by matching clusters of the two clouds and refining an orthogonal map by optimal transport. The theory predicts that this works best when the two encoders’ geometries agree, i.e., with good views. We test it with their method, code, hyperparameters and five seeds unchanged, on their protocol: DINOv2 ViT-B/14 against a text encoder, the map fitted on disjoint unpaired halves of COCO train2014 (images from one half, captions from the other), and scored on the 40,504 val2014 pairs by FOSCTTM (the fraction of samples closer than the true match; 0 is perfect, .5 chance) and by zero-shot classification ( Table 4 ). Our setup reproduces their numbers for all-mpnet-base-v2. Our 109M good-view JEPA (three training seeds, five method seeds each) reaches FOSCTTM .018 and zero-shot accuracies of 86.0% (CIFAR-10 top-1), 67.2% (CIFAR-100 top-5) and 64.4% (ImageNet-100 top-5): above every zero-shot accuracy of their paper, over 21 pairs of seven vision and three language models, up to DINOv3-7B and Qwen3-8B, and five settings (at most 82%, 62% and 56%), and level with their best FOSCTTM on these data (.02; .01 with captions from another dataset). Rerun under identical conditions, their strongest text encoder, Qwen3-8B, reaches .018, 83.4%, 61.7% and 47.6%: our 70 times smaller JEPA matches it on FOSCTTM and CIFAR-10 and beats it by 5.5 points on CIFAR-100 and 16.8 on ImageNet-100 (Mann–Whitney $p=.003$ and $p<.001$ ). It even matches CLIP’s text encoder, trained on 400M image–text pairs, on both CIFARs, though not on FOSCTTM (.006) or ImageNet-100 (68.7%). Next-token prediction on the same texts fails in half of its runs (FOSCTTM .287, 25.5% on CIFAR-10), while our bad-view JEPA aligns well too (.026), so on COCO the contrast is between JEPAs and next-token prediction. On Flickr, whose unpaired pools are smaller (15,007 images and the captions of 15,007 others), only good-view JEPAs leave chance: with no pair, the 109M good-view JEPA retrieves the right caption first for 16.4% $\pm$ 10.4% of test images (median 16.1%; chance 0.1%), against at most 0.3% for CLIP’s text encoder, all-mpnet-base-v2, BERT, Qwen2.5-0.5B, and our bad-view JEPA and next-token models trained on the same texts ( Appendix C ). The method’s own objective on the unpaired pools, which uses no test data, picks the best of its five seeds in all six good-view JEPA cases (R@1 13.5–36.2%). Through such a map, a captioner fitted with zero pairs reaches CIDEr .158 $\pm$ .042 on Flickr (.208 for the map the objective selects), against .430 with the 30k-pair rotation and .028 for a random map. That is, good views make two modalities alignable from their geometry alone , which next-token prediction does not.

### 6 Related Work

The PRH was formulated by Huh et al. [22] , whose contrastive argument assumes bijective observations and co-occurrence positives. They note that it fails for lossy observations, which remain open; Section 2.2 addresses a different departure, mismatched positives. Ziyin and Chuang [75] prove perfect alignment for deep linear networks trained by SGD, and Doshi et al. [14] explain why translations align inside multilingual language models by a hierarchical structure that languages share beneath surface features of their own, a within-modality analogue of our world states and nuisances. Identifiability results show that contrastive and augmentation-based learning recover latent variables up to simple transformations [ 74 , 64 ] , including across modalities when trained jointly [ 12 ] ; we instead train modalities separately and characterize when they agree. The spectral view of self-supervised learning [ 19 , 4 , 8 ] underlies Section 2.1 . How views and data shape what one encoder learns has been analyzed through augmentation graphs and their overlaps [ 19 , 66 ] , the inductive bias of the function class [ 52 , 26 , 9 ] , the eigenfunctions of the positive-pair kernel [ 24 ] and multi-view redundancy [ 61 ] ; Section 2.3 is an analogue with world states in place of classes, and its part (ii) the cross-modal consequence. The density view of JEPA anti-collapse [ 3 , 5 ] underlies our reading of next-token prediction. Aligning separately trained embedding spaces with few or no pairs goes back to word translation, through orthogonal Procrustes and unsupervised matching [ 11 , 1 ] , and extends to relative representations [ 41 ] , training-free multimodal coupling from a few anchor pairs [ 42 ] , translation between text-embedding spaces without pairs [ 23 ] , vision–language matching without parallel data [ 53 ] and, most recently, cross-modal alignment with no pairs at all from shared geometry [ 54 ] ; unimodal vision and language encoders can be matched with little or no paired data [ 36 ] . These works align given encoders; we ask which training objective makes encoders alignable, and our Procrustes map is the simplest of these tools. Text-only captioners decode the embeddings of jointly trained encoders [ 43 , 33 , 60 , 59 ] , a linear map trained on pairs can feed image features to a frozen language model [ 38 ] , and unpaired captioning trains adversarially or through shared detector concepts [ 18 , 28 ] ; ours needs no joint training and uses pairs only to fit one map with orthonormal rows.


### 7 Conclusion

We have turned the Platonic Representation Hypothesis into a Platonic Representation Theorem for JEPAs which, for population optima on injective renderings of one world and objectives whose minimizers are unique up to rotation, reduces it to one requirement for any number of modalities: they must agree on which world states are positives. We further proved that positives which corrupt a single observation make an encoder spend dimensions on its modality’s nuisance, which no rotation can align, and showed that next-token prediction has the form of such a bad-view JEPA. Empirically, good views align best across data, encoders and modalities, follow a scaling law that predicts unseen runs within seed noise, need 42 times fewer samples than next-token prediction on the same corpus, and give a 109M text JEPA that beats every language model we evaluated, up to 72B parameters, on the PRH paper’s own benchmark, and a captioner whose only parameters fitted on image–caption pairs are those of one rotation, which unpaired data alone can find well enough to beat every pair-free zero-shot classification reported with language models up to 8B parameters.

Known limitations. Our theorems are population statements over a finite world with injective renderings and matched marginals; real modalities are lossy, and finite-sample training relies on the network’s inductive bias. Uniqueness up to rotation is proved for second-order anti-collapse, not for LeJEPA’s full-distribution constraint, which a finite world cannot meet exactly; Section 2.2 is proved for spectral solutions only, and no experiment meets the hypotheses of Section 2.1 exactly, since our runs use LeJEPA and the image side mostly uses crops as positives. The main caveat is where good views come from: they require knowing which texts describe the same scene, the grouping of human captions by image on Flickr and COCO, while on Recap-DataComp two of each image’s three texts are generated by an image captioner (LLaVA built on LLaMA-3-8B, whose vision encoder is CLIP ViT-L/14, 34 ) and carry information distilled from image–text training. Without any image captioner, good views on the alt-text and a text-only paraphrase of it reach .060 on WIT against .107 with the recaptions ( Table 7 ), so the comparisons with language models and CLIP’s text encoder at scale rely on these texts, while the comparisons between views, on identical texts, do not. Paired information thus enters through the definition of positives, one fitted linear map and, on Recap, captioner-generated texts, never through a cross-modal loss. The bad-view JEPA and next-token prediction see exactly the same texts as the good-view JEPA. Outside MSR-VTT our image encoders are bad-view JEPAs by our own definition, and hyperparameters were tuned only for the good-view JEPA.

Critically, our study provides what the PRH has long needed: a provable sufficient condition that directly informs how to train for it, and evidence that next-token prediction, the objective behind today’s largest models, is an inefficient way to get there. We hope that our study will establish positive views, and not scale alone, as the lever to build platonic representations, and open new avenues for modalities beyond images and text, e.g., audio, video and actions.

#### A.1 Notation and Objectives


##### Notation.

On a finite world, the positive-pair distribution $\pi$ defines the Markov operator $(T_{\pi}h)(z)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[h(z^{\prime})\mid z]$ , i.e., the matrix $\boldsymbol{D}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Pi}$ acting on functions $h:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}$ . It is self-adjoint in $L^{2}(p)$ , with $\langle h,h^{\prime}\rangle_{p}=\sum_{z}p(z)h(z)h^{\prime}(z)$ , and $h\mapsto\boldsymbol{D}^{1/2}h$ maps it to $\boldsymbol{K}_{\pi}=\boldsymbol{D}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\Pi}\boldsymbol{D}^{-1/2}$ : the two share the eigenvalues $\mu_{i}$ , and the eigenfunctions $\phi_{i}=\boldsymbol{D}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}$ of $T_{\pi}$ are orthonormal in $L^{2}(p)$ , with $\phi_{0}=\mathbf{1}$ , so that the spectral embedding of Section 2.1 is $\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\pi}=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{k})^{\top}$ . The operator and Hilbert–Schmidt norms of $T_{\pi_{1}}-T_{\pi_{2}}$ on $L^{2}(p)$ are the spectral and Frobenius norms of $\boldsymbol{K}_{\pi_{1}}-\boldsymbol{K}_{\pi_{2}}$ , with $\|\boldsymbol{K}_{\pi_{1}}-\boldsymbol{K}_{\pi_{2}}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{z,z^{\prime}}\big(\pi_{1}(z,z^{\prime})-\pi_{2}(z,z^{\prime})\big)^{2}/\big(p(z)p(z^{\prime})\big)$ . On world states, the objective of ( 1 ) is $\mathcal{L}_{\pi}(h)=\mathbb{E}_{(z,z^{\prime})\sim\pi}\ell\big(h(z),h(z^{\prime})\big)+\Omega(h)$ , where $\Omega(h)$ depends on $h$ only through the distribution of $h(z)$ , $z\sim p$ .


##### Objectives of the form ( 1 ).

Spectral contrastive learning, whose minimizers are rescaled eigenfunctions, and whitened MSE fit this form [ 19 , 16 ] ; VICReg (with the mean fixed) and Barlow Twins (with $\lambda=1$ ) do only in a whitened hard-constraint limit, where their minimizers are those of Section 2.1 and, both being translation invariant, agree only after centering [ 6 , 72 , 4 ] ; LeJEPA’s SIGReg term is rotation invariant in expectation over its projection directions [ 5 ] .


#### A.2 The Platonic Representation Theorem ( Section 2.1 )

Since $g_{m}$ is injective it has a left inverse $g_{m}^{-1}$ on $g_{m}(\mathcal{Z})$ , and $h\mapsto h\circ g_{m}^{-1}$ inverts $f\mapsto f\circ g_{m}$ . For the objective, the pair $(g_{m}(z),g_{m}(z^{\prime}))$ with $(z,z^{\prime})\sim\pi_{m}$ is mapped by $f$ to $(h(z),h(z^{\prime}))$ with $h=f\circ g_{m}$ , and the embeddings $f(x)$ of the data $x=g_{m}(z)$ , $z\sim p$ , have the distribution of $h(z)$ . Hence $\mathcal{L}_{m}(f)=\mathcal{L}_{\pi_{m}}(f\circ g_{m})$ . ∎

By Section A.2 , $f$ minimizes $\mathcal{L}_{m}$ if and only if $f\circ g_{m}$ minimizes $\mathcal{L}_{\pi}$ , for every modality $m$ . The minimizers of $\mathcal{L}_{\pi}$ lie in one rotation orbit $\{\boldsymbol{Q}h^{\star}:\boldsymbol{Q}\in\mathrm{O}(k)\}$ ; hence $f_{m}^{\star}\circ g_{m}=\boldsymbol{Q}_{m}h^{\star}$ for some $\boldsymbol{Q}_{m}\in\mathrm{O}(k)$ , for every $m$ , and $f_{m}^{\star}(g_{m}(z))=\boldsymbol{Q}_{m}\boldsymbol{Q}_{m^{\prime}}^{\top}f_{m^{\prime}}^{\star}(g_{m^{\prime}}(z))$ for every $z$ and every two modalities $m,m^{\prime}$ . ∎

Write $h=(h_{1},\dots,h_{k})$ . The constraints say $\langle h_{i},h_{j}\rangle_{p}=\delta_{ij}$ and $\langle h_{i},\mathbf{1}\rangle_{p}=0$ . Expanding, and using that both marginals of $\pi$ equal $p$ ,

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\|h(z)-h(z^{\prime})\|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(2\mathbb{E}_{p}h_{j}^{2}-2\mathbb{E}_{\pi}h_{j}(z)h_{j}(z^{\prime})\big)=2k-2\sum_{j=1}^{k}\langle h_{j},T_{\pi}h_{j}\rangle_{p}.$$

$T_{\pi}$ is self-adjoint on $L^{2}(p)$ and leaves $\mathbf{1}^{\perp}$ invariant, with eigenpairs $(\mu_{i},\phi_{i})_{i\geq 1}$ there. By Ky Fan’s maximum principle [ 17 ] , $\sum_{j}\langle h_{j},T_{\pi}h_{j}\rangle_{p}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i}$ over orthonormal families in $\mathbf{1}^{\perp}$ , with equality if and only if $\mathrm{span}(h_{1},\dots,h_{k})$ is spanned by eigenfunctions for the $k$ largest eigenvalues. When $\mu_{k}>\mu_{k+1}$ that space is $\mathrm{span}(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{k})$ , and two orthonormal bases of the same $k$ -dimensional space differ by an orthogonal matrix, so $h=\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\pi}$ . Conversely every $\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\pi}$ is feasible and attains $2\sum_{i\leq k}(1-\mu_{i})$ . ∎


#### A.3 When Positives Differ ( Section 2.2 )

Write $\boldsymbol{K}_{m}=\boldsymbol{K}_{\pi_{m}}$ . The map $h\mapsto\boldsymbol{D}^{1/2}h$ is an isometry from $L^{2}(p)$ to $\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Z}|}$ under which $T_{\pi_{m}}$ becomes $\boldsymbol{K}_{m}$ . Both matrices have the same top eigenvector $\boldsymbol{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{1}$ with eigenvalue 1, because the marginals coincide, and both leave its orthogonal complement $H$ invariant. On $H$ , let $\boldsymbol{V}_{m}$ be the matrix whose columns are orthonormal eigenvectors of $\boldsymbol{K}_{m}$ for its $k$ largest eigenvalues; then the row of $\boldsymbol{V}_{m}$ at $z$ is $\sqrt{p(z)}\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{m}(z)^{\top}$ , and $\mathbb{E}_{p}\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{1}(z)-\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{2}(z)\|^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{V}_{1}-\boldsymbol{V}_{2}\boldsymbol{Q}^{\top}\|_{F}^{2}$ . The variant of the Davis–Kahan theorem of Yu et al. [71, Theorem 2] , applied on $H$ with $\Sigma=\boldsymbol{K}_{2}|_{H}$ , $\hat{\Sigma}=\boldsymbol{K}_{1}|_{H}$ , $r=1$ , $s=k$ (so the gap is $\delta=\mu_{k}-\mu_{k+1}$ of $\boldsymbol{K}_{2}$ ), gives an orthogonal $\hat{\boldsymbol{O}}$ with

[Tableau] $$\|\boldsymbol{V}_{1}\hat{\boldsymbol{O}}-\boldsymbol{V}_{2}\|_{F}\leq\frac{2^{3/2}\min\big(\sqrt{k}\,\|\boldsymbol{K}_{1}-\boldsymbol{K}_{2}\|_{2},\|\boldsymbol{K}_{1}-\boldsymbol{K}_{2}\|_{F}\big)}{\delta},$$

which is the bound with $\boldsymbol{Q}=\hat{\boldsymbol{O}}$ , since $\|\boldsymbol{V}_{1}\hat{\boldsymbol{O}}-\boldsymbol{V}_{2}\|_{F}=\|\boldsymbol{V}_{1}-\boldsymbol{V}_{2}\hat{\boldsymbol{O}}^{\top}\|_{F}$ . The restriction to $H$ leaves both norms unchanged, because $(\boldsymbol{K}_{1}-\boldsymbol{K}_{2})\boldsymbol{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{1}=0$ . ∎

Let $u=f_{2}^{\star}\circ g_{2}$ , so that $f_{1}^{\star}\circ g_{1}=\boldsymbol{Q}u$ and $\mathbb{E}_{p}uu^{\top}=\boldsymbol{I}_{k}$ . (i) $\mathbb{E}_{p}(\boldsymbol{R}u)(\boldsymbol{R}u)^{\top}=\boldsymbol{R}\,(\mathbb{E}_{p}uu^{\top})\boldsymbol{R}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{I}_{k}$ for every $\boldsymbol{R}\in\mathrm{O}(k)$ , and the first moments are zero for every $\boldsymbol{R}$ . Beyond the finite world: if the embedding distribution $\kappa$ is rotation invariant, as SIGReg’s target $\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{I}_{k})$ in the limit of a continuum of world states, then $\boldsymbol{R}_{\#}\kappa=\kappa$ for every $\boldsymbol{R}$ and no statistic of unpaired samples identifies $\boldsymbol{Q}$ ; on a finite world, the distribution of $u(z)$ is finitely supported and cannot be rotation invariant for $k\geq 2$ , which is why unpaired data can refine a map in practice, and why the shared geometry of a finite world can reveal it [ 54 ] . (ii) Write $\boldsymbol{X}=[u(z_{1}),\dots,u(z_{n})]$ and $\boldsymbol{Y}=\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{X}$ . The objective is $\|\boldsymbol{Y}-\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{X}\|_{F}^{2}\geq 0$ , with equality at $\boldsymbol{R}=\boldsymbol{Q}$ . If $\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{X}$ then $(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{Q})\boldsymbol{X}=0$ , and since $\boldsymbol{X}$ has rank $k$ , $\boldsymbol{R}=\boldsymbol{Q}$ . ∎


#### A.4 Good-View and Bad-View JEPAs ( Section 2.3 )

Write $x_{1}=g_{1}(z,\nu_{1})$ and $x_{2}=g_{2}(z,\nu_{2})$ for a paired observation, with $\nu_{1},\nu_{2}$ independent of each other and of $z$ . Since $f_{1}$ and $f_{2}$ are centered and whitened, for every $\boldsymbol{Q}\in\mathrm{O}(k)$ , $\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{Q}f_{1}(x_{1})-f_{2}(x_{2})\|^{2}=2k-2\,\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\Sigma})$ with $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\mathbb{E}[f_{1}(x_{1})f_{2}(x_{2})^{\top}]$ , and the minimum over $\boldsymbol{Q}$ is $2k-2\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\|_{*}$ (von Neumann’s trace inequality, attained by orthogonal Procrustes). Given $z$ , $x_{1}$ and $x_{2}$ are independent, so $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\mathbb{E}[u(z)w(z)^{\top}]$ with $u=\mathbb{E}[f_{1}(x_{1})\mid z]$ and $w=\mathbb{E}[f_{2}(x_{2})\mid z]$ , both centered, with covariances $\boldsymbol{C}_{1}=\boldsymbol{C}_{f_{1}}$ and $\boldsymbol{C}_{2}=\boldsymbol{C}_{f_{2}}$ . By the law of total covariance $\boldsymbol{C}_{2}=\boldsymbol{I}_{k}-\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Cov}(f_{2}(x_{2})\mid z)]\preceq\boldsymbol{I}_{k}$ , and likewise $\boldsymbol{C}_{1}\preceq\boldsymbol{I}_{k}$ . Let $\tilde{u}=(\boldsymbol{C}_{1}^{1/2})^{+}u$ and $\tilde{w}=(\boldsymbol{C}_{2}^{1/2})^{+}w$ (pseudo-inverses). Since $\mathbb{E}(a^{\top}u)^{2}=a^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{1}a=0$ for $a\in\ker\boldsymbol{C}_{1}$ , $u$ lies in the range of $\boldsymbol{C}_{1}$ almost surely, so $u=\boldsymbol{C}_{1}^{1/2}\tilde{u}$ , and likewise $w=\boldsymbol{C}_{2}^{1/2}\tilde{w}$ , with $\mathbb{E}\tilde{u}\tilde{u}^{\top}$ and $\mathbb{E}\tilde{w}\tilde{w}^{\top}$ the orthogonal projections onto the ranges of $\boldsymbol{C}_{1}$ and $\boldsymbol{C}_{2}$ ; then $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\boldsymbol{C}_{1}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{R}\,\boldsymbol{C}_{2}^{1/2}$ with $\boldsymbol{R}=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{u}\tilde{w}^{\top}]$ , and $\|\boldsymbol{R}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1$ by Cauchy–Schwarz. By Hölder’s inequality for Schatten norms, $\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\|_{*}\leq\|\boldsymbol{C}_{1}^{1/2}\|_{*}\,\|\boldsymbol{R}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,\|\boldsymbol{C}_{2}^{1/2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\mathrm{tr}\,\boldsymbol{C}_{1}^{1/2}=k\rho(f_{1})$ , which bounds the error below by $2k(1-\rho(f_{1}))$ ; exchanging the roles of $f_{1}$ and $f_{2}$ bounds it below by $2k(1-\rho(f_{2}))$ as well, hence by $2k(1-\min_{m}\rho(f_{m}))$ . Since $0\preceq\boldsymbol{C}_{1}\preceq\boldsymbol{I}_{k}$ , $\rho(f_{1})=1$ if and only if $\boldsymbol{C}_{1}=\boldsymbol{I}_{k}$ , i.e., $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Cov}(f_{1}(x_{1})\mid z)]=0$ , i.e., $f_{1}(x_{1})$ is almost surely a function of $z$ . ∎

(i) The good kernel $\pi(z,z^{\prime})q_{m}(\nu)q_{m}(\nu^{\prime})$ has marginal $p\otimes q_{m}$ , and its Markov operator on $L^{2}(p\otimes q_{m})$ is $T_{\pi}\otimes J_{m}$ , where $J_{m}$ projects onto the constants of $L^{2}(q_{m})$ . Its eigenpairs are $(\mu_{i},\phi_{i}\otimes\mathbf{1})$ and the eigenvalue $0$ on $L^{2}(p)\otimes\mathbf{1}^{\perp}$ . If $\mu_{k}>\max(\mu_{k+1},0)$ , the $k$ largest eigenvalues on $\mathbf{1}^{\perp}$ are $\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}$ , separated from the rest, and Section 2.1 gives the minimizers $\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\pi}\otimes\mathbf{1}$ : $f_{m}^{\star}(g_{m}(z,\nu))=\boldsymbol{Q}_{m}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\pi}(z)$ . For two modalities with the same $\pi$ , $f_{m}^{\star}(g_{m}(z,\nu_{m}))=\boldsymbol{Q}_{m}\boldsymbol{Q}_{m^{\prime}}^{\top}f_{m^{\prime}}^{\star}(g_{m^{\prime}}(z,\nu_{m^{\prime}}))$ for all $z,\nu_{m},\nu_{m^{\prime}}$ . (ii) Two corruptions of one observation, drawn independently given it, have the joint law $\Gamma_{m}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{D}_{p}\otimes\boldsymbol{D}_{q_{m}})\Gamma_{m}$ with $\Gamma_{m}=\boldsymbol{A}\otimes\boldsymbol{B}_{m}$ , which factorizes as $(\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{D}_{p}\boldsymbol{A})\otimes(\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{D}_{q_{m}}\boldsymbol{B}_{m})$ . By stationarity ( $p^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}=p^{\top}$ , $q_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{m}=q_{m}^{\top}$ ) its marginal is $p\otimes q_{m}$ , the law of clean observations, so the minimizer is centered and whitened on clean observations and Section 2.3 applies to it; its Markov operator is $(\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{A})\otimes(\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{*}\boldsymbol{B}_{m})$ , with $\boldsymbol{A}^{*}$ the adjoint of $\boldsymbol{A}$ in $L^{2}(p)$ , and $\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{A}$ and $\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{*}\boldsymbol{B}_{m}$ are positive semidefinite contractions fixing $\mathbf{1}$ , so its eigenpairs are $(\alpha_{i}\beta_{j},\psi_{i}\otimes\chi_{j})$ with $\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}\in[0,1]$ and $\psi_{0}=\chi_{0}=\mathbf{1}$ . Under the separation assumption, Section 2.1 gives the minimizers $\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\Psi}$ , where $\boldsymbol{\Psi}$ stacks the $k$ top eigenfunctions after $\psi_{0}\otimes\chi_{0}$ . Since $\mathbb{E}[\psi_{i}(z)\chi_{j}(\nu)\mid z]=\psi_{i}(z)\,\mathbb{E}_{q_{m}}\chi_{j}$ , which is $0$ for $j\geq 1$ and $\psi_{i}(z)$ for $j=0$ , $\boldsymbol{C}_{f}=\boldsymbol{Q}\,\mathrm{diag}(c)\,\boldsymbol{Q}^{\top}$ with $c$ equal to $0$ on the $r$ eigenfunctions with $j\geq 1$ and $1$ on the others, so $\mathrm{tr}\,\boldsymbol{C}_{f}^{1/2}=k-r$ , $\rho(f)=1-r/k$ , and Section 2.3 gives the error bound $2k(1-\rho(f))=2r$ . Finally, every eigenvalue with $j\geq 1$ satisfies $\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}\leq\beta_{j}\leq\beta_{1}=\alpha_{0}\beta_{1}$ , so $r\geq 1$ if and only if $\beta_{1}$ is among $\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{k}$ ; the eigenvalues with $j=0$ that exceed $\beta_{1}$ are the $\alpha_{i}>\beta_{1}$ , $i\geq 1$ , and if $\beta_{1}>\alpha_{k}$ there are at most $k-1$ of them, so $\beta_{1}\geq\xi_{k}>\xi_{k+1}$ and $r\geq 1$ ; conversely, if $\beta_{1}\leq\alpha_{k}$ , then $\xi_{i}=\alpha_{i}$ for $i\leq k$ and separation leaves $r=0$ . A corruption that keeps the nuisance exactly ( $\boldsymbol{B}_{m}=\boldsymbol{I}$ , $\beta_{j}=1$ for every $j$ ) violates separation as soon as $|\mathcal{N}_{m}|>k+1$ , but there every minimizer lies in the nuisance-only eigenspace of eigenvalue 1, and $\boldsymbol{C}_{f}=0$ when $\alpha_{1}<1$ . ∎

### Appendix B Experimental details


##### Code.

Everything is built on stable-pretraining: its LeJEPA class (loss, SIGReg with 1024 slices, projector $384\!\to\!512\!\to\!2048\!\to\!2048\!\to\!512$ with batch normalization), Module , Manager (training loop, checkpointing, SLURM requeue), and GPU augmentation ops. The experiment-specific code is a text view generator, a text encoder wrapper, a next-token loss, and the alignment metrics, a few hundred lines.


##### Image encoder.

ViT-S/16 (timm vit_small_patch16_224 , drop path 0.1), CLS embedding. Views: 2 global crops ( $224^{2}$ , scale 0.3–1.0) and 6 local crops ( $96^{2}$ , scale 0.05–0.3), each with horizontal flip, color jitter (0.4, 0.4, 0.2, 0.1; $p=0.8$ ), grayscale ( $p=0.2$ ), Gaussian blur ( $p=0.5$ ) and solarization ( $p=0.2$ ). Crops are made on CPU workers and photometric operations on GPU.


##### Text encoders.

BERT with hidden size 384, 12 layers, 6 heads, MLP 1536, random initialization, bert-base-uncased tokenizer, maximum length 64. The JEPAs use the CLS token. Views are trimmed: global views keep a contiguous span of 50–100% of the caption, local views 15–50%; 15% of kept tokens are replaced by [MASK] . The next-token model uses causal attention, a softmax head tied to the input embeddings, and mean-pooled final hidden states as its embedding; the last token’s hidden state, the other usual choice for a causal model, aligns worse with DINOv2 ViT-B (Flickr R@1 .061 against .119 for mean pooling, three seeds; evaluate.py embed --last-token ).


##### Optimization.

AdamW, learning rate $5\cdot 10^{-4}$ with 10 epochs of linear warmup and cosine decay, weight decay 0.05, batch 256, bf16, 200 epochs, seeds 0, 1 and 2 (image and text reseeded together), for the Flickr runs. Longer and larger text runs (BERT-B on Flickr, the Flickr+COCO runs, every Recap run) use learning rate $10^{-4}$ with gradient clipping at 1.0 and one seed unless stated; the MSR-VTT runs use 800 epochs. One GPU per run, except eight for the ImageNet and ViT-B image JEPAs and for the 940M-image BERT-B runs (16 for its first seed), and two for the 200M-image BERT-B.


##### MSR-VTT.

The 7,010 clips of the 7k split train, the other 1,990 clips of the 9k split (official test clips outside the JSFUSION split, as is usual with the 9k split) fit the means and the rotation, and the 1,000 clips of the JSFUSION split test. We keep 8 evenly spaced frames per clip ( prh.data.extract_frames ). Good image views draw each of the 2 global and 6 local crops from its own frame of the clip; bad image views crop one frame, drawn once per clip and epoch. Text views are as on Flickr, with 20 captions per clip. All runs use the Flickr recipe for 800 epochs (21.6k steps, about as many as on Flickr), seeds 0, 1 and 2; evaluation uses the middle frame and, for test clips, the JSFUSION caption.


##### PRH protocol.

For every WIT-1024 score we follow the released code of Huh et al. [22] : WIT-1024 pairs, first caption, per-layer features (vision: CLS after each block; language: mask-weighted mean of each hidden state), elementwise clipping at the 0.95 quantile of absolute values, $\ell_{2}$ normalization, mutual 10-NN, maximum over layer pairs.

##### The scaling law in detail.

Figure 11 ( Section 4.4 ) varies the positives and the data with one recipe (BERT-S, learning rate $10^{-4}$ , clipping 1, 64 tokens, 1M samples per epoch, seed 0): each training item draws good views (two texts of its image) with probability $q\in\{0,.001,.003,.01,.03,.1,.3,1\}$ and bad views (two corruptions of one text) otherwise, on the texts of $N\in\{0.41,2.24,10.2,50.9\}$ M images for $M=20$ M samples, and on 10.2M images for $M\in\{5,40,80\}$ M at $q\in\{0,.01,.1,.3,1\}$ ( tables.py ). Bad views gain nothing from more images: at $q=0$ , WIT stays within .054–.057 from 0.41M to 50.9M images, while good views rise from .090 to .115. A fraction of good views helps Flickr retrieval before WIT (R@1 .26 at $q=0$ , .28 at $q=.1$ , .31–.33 at $q=.3$ ; WIT only from $q=.3$ ), and training longer raises both views (R@1 .221 to .310 from 5M to 80M samples with bad views, .292 to .409 with good views; WIT with good views .078, .099, .124 and .132 at 5M, 20M, 40M and 80M samples). ##### Few pairs and no pairs.

The unpaired refinement alternates optimal transport between the rotated image cloud and the text cloud (entropic, Sinkhorn) and Procrustes on the transport plan, on the 1,014 validation images and captions with their pairing hidden. From 100 pairs, CIDEr rises from .244 to .294; from 30 pairs, the refined captioner reaches .169 ( Table 9 ). With no pairs at all, matching these two small clouds by their internal geometry (Gromov–Wasserstein [ 37 , 47 ] , then the same refinement) stays at chance (test R@1 $\approx$ .001); so does the method of Schnaus et al. [54] with this Flickr-caption JEPA (R@1 .012), whose geometry is learned from 145k short captions. With the good-view JEPAs trained on web text, that method aligns the two clouds far above chance ( Section 5.2 ).

##### Decoder noise.

The captioner’s training noise is set by matching cosines ( Section 5.1 ), $\sigma=(c^{-2}-1)^{1/2}$ with $c$ the mean cosine between a rotated image embedding and its caption’s embedding. We first measured it on test pairs; held-out validation pairs (a rotation fitted on one half of the 1,014 validation pairs, the cosine taken on the other half) give nearly the same noise (column cos of evaluate.py align , with --val-only --split 507 for held-out validation pairs). With the rotation fitted on 1,014 pairs ( $c=.29$ held out, $\sigma\approx 3.3$ ; $c=.33$ on test pairs, $\sigma\approx 2.9$ ), Flickr test CIDEr is .225, .304, .368, .396 and .377 for $\sigma=0.5,1,2,3,4$ : lower noise decodes the true caption embedding better but transfers worse, and transfer peaks where predicted. The 30k-pair rotation leaves a smaller residual ( $\sigma\approx 2.1$ held out, 2.3 on test pairs) and gives .438 at $\sigma=2$ against .431 at $\sigma=3$ . Differences below about .01 CIDEr are within the variation of greedy decoding across hardware (floating-point ties), and we do not interpret them. For COCO, the decoder is trained with $\sigma=2$ on the embeddings of a text JEPA trained on Flickr and COCO training captions (Karpathy train and restval splits, disjoint from its test split), and the rotation is fitted on the 30k Flickr pairs.


##### Scaling the captioner.

The language-model decoder maps the centered, unit-norm text-JEPA embedding to 10 input embeddings of Qwen2.5 through a two-layer MLP (a soft prompt, as in ClipCap, 40 ) and is fine-tuned entirely (AdamW, learning rate $3\cdot 10^{-5}$ , batch 256, gradient clipping 1.0, bf16) on 1.4M draws of Flickr and COCO training captions (Karpathy train and restval), each embedded by the frozen text JEPA with noise $\sigma=1.9$ , matched to the cosine $c=.47$ between rotated DINOv2-g embeddings and their captions. The target is the caption as written, at most 64 tokens. Two image encoders are concatenated after rescaling each embedding to unit root-mean-square per coordinate ( Table 12 ); otherwise the encoder with larger features dominates. Consensus decoding (minimum Bayes risk, 27 ) samples 32 captions besides the greedy one (temperature 0.7, top- $p$ 0.95) and keeps, of these 33, the one most similar to the other 32 under CIDEr’s n-gram similarity without document frequencies, which one image’s candidates cannot estimate; it uses no reference caption and no other image. With 16 samples it gains little, and 64 samples gain no more than 32 on Flickr. Keeping instead the beam candidate whose text-JEPA embedding is closest to the rotated image embedding does not help. Pretraining the decoder on 4M first sentences of Recap recaptions before the captions lets it decode true caption embeddings better (.743 and 1.369) but transfers no better on Flickr and COCO; on photos of other kinds its captions are more often right (coats of arms, a cake shaped like a sheep), and the demo uses it. Two other changes do not help: a higher input resolution raises retrieval (R@1 .440 to .462 with DINOv2-g at 448 px) but not the cosine the decoder sees, and a per-direction gain after the rotation, fitted on the same pairs, at best raises Flickr CIDEr by .017 and lowers COCO’s by .053 (web-pretrained decoder, beam 3).


##### Projector outputs.

On projector outputs, where SIGReg acts, whitening each side before the rotation barely changes retrieval (R@1 .030 vs. .034), as expected once the embedding is nearly isotropic.

The rotation fitted on the 30k Flickr pairs (text JEPA trained on Flickr and COCO captions, BERT-S) ranks the true caption first for 11.3% of the 5,000 COCO Karpathy test images (chance 0.02%; mutual 10-NN .185), with each side centered by its own unpaired COCO mean; a rotation fitted on 2,500 COCO pairs and tested on the other 2,500 reaches 15.8% (chance 0.04%).

##### Text-only quality.

Good views also make better text encoders on their own. On the STS-Benchmark test set [ 10 ] (Spearman correlation between the cosine similarity of two sentences’ embeddings and human similarity, no fine-tuning), the good-view JEPA, the bad-view JEPA and next-token prediction score .579 / .528 / .430 when trained on Flickr captions and .612 / .501 / .377 when trained on 2M Recap images, and the 50M-image good-view JEPA reaches .632, above BERT-base (.551) and Qwen2.5-0.5B (.352, mean-pooled) though below all-mpnet-base-v2 (.834), which is trained on a billion supervised sentence pairs. The ordering good $>$ bad $>$ next-token is the same as for alignment on Flickr (on WIT, next-token prediction is above the bad-view JEPA).


##### Uncertainty on WIT.

WIT-1024 is small. We compute each score on 200 random 800-pair subsets drawn without replacement (the same subsets for every model; each model at its best layers on all pairs; DINOv2 ViT-B) and turn their spread into a 95% interval for the 1,024-pair score: for average-like statistics, the spread of 800-pair subsets around the full score is $\sqrt{224/800}$ times the full score’s standard error, so we rescale it by $\sqrt{800/224}$ ( prh.metrics.subsample_interval ). Against language models our text JEPAs are level: the 50M-image JEPA minus Qwen2.5-7B is $+.010$ ( $[-.005,+.025]$ ) and minus Qwen2.5-3B $+.003$ ( $[-.011,+.018]$ ), the 100M-image JEPA minus Mistral-7B $-.011$ ( $[-.026,+.004]$ ), and the 2M-image JEPA minus Qwen2.5-0.5B $+.005$ ( $[-.008,+.018]$ ). Between views the differences are clear: on 2M images, the good-view JEPA minus next-token prediction is $+.037$ ( $[+.025,+.049]$ ) and minus the bad-view JEPA $+.054$ ( $[+.041,+.067]$ ); on 50M images, $+.050$ ( $[+.037,+.062]$ ) and $+.063$ ( $[+.050,+.075]$ ). Training seeds matter as much as test sampling: the three seeds of the 50M-image JEPA score .129, .118 and .127 with DINOv2 ViT-B (seed 0 is used above), so the comparisons with language models are within noise, while the gaps between views, at least $+.040$ over next-token prediction and $+.056$ over the bad-view JEPA for every seed, are far outside it.

Behind Figure 9 (right): a ViT-S on the 2.16M Recap images (10 epochs), a ViT-B on 10M Recap images (8 epochs) and a ViT-S on ImageNet-1k (300 epochs), all trained without texts. The ImageNet ViT-S, which sees the most samples, agrees most with DINOv2 (.407, against .315 and .317) and with the 50M-image text JEPA (.095 over three text seeds, against .079 and .080), and its alignment with the 109M good-view JEPA reaches .130 ( Table 15 ); two more ImageNet ViT-S trained from other seeds reproduce it (.414 and .405 with DINOv2; with the good-view, next-token and bad-view text JEPAs .098, .061 and .055, and .100, .062 and .053, against .098, .062 and .055). ##### Scaling to the texts of 940M images.

Table 14 follows our largest good-view JEPAs along training. With the same BERT-B encoder, the texts of 200M images and a global batch of 256 reach .125–.126 on WIT after 89M–160M samples (the end of training), while the texts of all 940M images and a global batch of 2,048 reach .182 after 430M samples and .191 after 850M and at the end of training (900M). These runs change unique texts, samples seen and batch size together, and we do not separate their effects. The SIGReg weight also differs: in stable-pretraining as we ran it, the Epps–Pulley statistic is computed on each GPU’s batch and scaled by its size, so its effective weight grows with the per-GPU batch, and the first 940M run (16 GPUs of 128) trains with the equivalent of $\lambda=.01$ on a single GPU, half the weight of our single-GPU runs, as do the 200M-image BERT-B (2 GPUs of 128) and the ImageNet and ViT-B image JEPAs (8 GPUs of 128). A second seed on 8 GPUs of 256, i.e., at $\lambda=.02$ , reaches .121 on WIT and .477 Flickr R@1: at this scale WIT is sensitive to the SIGReg weight, which our ablations on Flickr captions did not reveal. We chose the weight of the first run for every other 940M run after seeing this result, so that weight was selected on WIT: at $\lambda=.01$ on 8 GPUs of 256, the effective weight of the first run, two more seeds reach .181 and .179 on WIT and .484 and .485 Flickr R@1 (three seeds: .184 and .478), the bad-view control .088 and .318, and three next-token seeds .109, .111 and .112 on WIT and .346, .332 and .328 Flickr R@1. Across DINOv2 sizes, the three good-view seeds average .166–.197 ( Table 15 ), the bad-view control .082–.097 and next-token prediction .101–.122. Averaged over all four good-view seeds, WIT is .168 with DINOv2 ViT-B, above every language model, and .198, .202 and .212 with CLIP ViT-B, -L and -H, level with the best language model (.193, .203 and .210).

##### WIT captions in the training texts.

DataComp is crawled from the web, so some WIT captions may appear among the Recap-DataComp alt-texts. We lower-case each WIT caption and each of the 940M alt-texts, keep letters, digits and spaces, and match them exactly or by their first 40 characters: 26 WIT captions match exactly and 17 more by prefix, at least 43 of 1,024 in all (captions shorter than 20 characters are not checked; 16 within the texts of the first 100M images, 20 within the first 200M); 3.3M of the 940M images are hosted on Wikimedia. Removing the 43 pairs changes none of the nine text models we checked with DINOv2 ViT-B by more than .002 ( Section 4 ), and the 109M model scores .193 on the 981 remaining pairs. The WIT-1024 release does not identify its images, so we cannot match them to DataComp images. Flickr30k images can be: their file names are Flickr photo ids, which Flickr-hosted DataComp URLs contain, and at least 178 of the 31,014 Flickr30k photos appear among the 940M Recap-DataComp images (166 train, 6 validation and 6 of the 1,000 test photos, each with its web alt-text and recaptions, never its Flickr captions), so these can move Flickr test retrieval by at most .006 ( python -m prh.data overlap ). COCO photos, also hosted on Flickr, match the same way: at least 1,207 COCO 2014 photos are among the 940M images, 393 of them from val2014, where Table 4 scores, and 50 of the 5,000 COCO test photos of Table 17 , again with their web texts and never their COCO captions; removing these 50 changes the COCO score of the 109M JEPA, and of every baseline we checked, by at most .001 ( evaluate.py wit --bench coco --drop ).
