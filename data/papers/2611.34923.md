## Résumé

Looped Transformers boost expressivity by reusing shared layers across loops, yet at the cost of efficiency. Pushing their expressivity-efficiency frontier requires rethinking the core mechanism connecting adjacent loops: What should be passed as a Transformer loops? We introduce Mixture-of-Loops Transformer (MoLT), whose Mixture-of-Loops Attention (MoLA) uses keys and values (KV) as memory for selective reuse across depths and loops. Under loop residual, MoLT-R retains the residual stream and reads local KV across loops for expressivity. Under loop KV, MoLT-KV instead restarts from embeddings and reads current-loop local KV with preceding-loop memory KV at earlier positions for efficiency. MoLT-KV eliminates same-position cross-loop dependence and, paired with loop forcing for parallel training, unlocks single-traversal Mixture-of-Loops (MoL) decoding beyond the training loop horizon. To balance efficiency and quality, we introduce U-shaped MoLT (U-MoLT), which separates memory construction from prediction readout and skips unused decoder traversals without changing predictions. 1 Introduction
Model architecture design is fundamentally a tradeoff between expressivity and efficiency. Recent frontier
architectures have largely advanced this tradeoff through sparser computation, linear attention, and richer
cross-layer connectivity [14, 27, 28, 55, 65, 70]. More recently, looped Transformers have risen to the forefront
of architecture design and achieved remarkable success [9, 15, 19, 57, 69, 71]. This stems from reusing
parameters across repeated loops for stronger expressivity, though at the cost of efficiency. Building on this
paradigm, we pursue next-generation foundation model architectures that combine stronger expressivity with
high computational efficiency. Achieving this requires rethinking the core mechanism connecting adjacent
loops: What should be passed as a Transformer loops?
The standard answer is loop residual, which passes each loop’s output directly into the next loop. This
choice bottlenecks both expressivity and efficiency. Repeatedly compressing computation into a single residual
stream causes information dilution across layers and loops, leading to representational degradation. Moreover,
each token depends on its own preceding-loop output, forcing inference to run every loop sequentially. Instead
of relying solely on a residual stream, our answer is to pass computed keys and values (KV) as memory for
selective reuse across depths and loops.
We introduce Mixture-of-Loops Transformer (MoLT), whose Mixture-of-Loops Attention (MoLA) lets each
layer jointly attend to KV from different depths and loops (Fig. 1). Under loop residual, MoLT-R keeps the
residual stream across loops for maximum expressivity. MoLA directly reads current-loop and preceding-loop
local KV at the same token position, mitigating information dilution through selective reuse. When decoding
2

efficiency is the priority, keeping the residual stream still requires sequential loop execution. To remove this
bottleneck, we ask whether each loop can start afresh and rely purely on KV memory.
This motivates the loop KV paradigm, in which each loop restarts from embeddings. In MoLT-KV, MoLA
jointly reads memory KV projected from the preceding loop’s outputs at strictly earlier positions and current-
loop local KV at the current token. We introduce loop forcing to train this paradigm, supervising every loop
while training each feedback loop in parallel from preceding-loop memory KV rather than sequentially decoded
prefixes. Because each loop restarts from embeddings and reads memory KV only from earlier positions, a
token no longer depends on its own preceding-loop output. This unlocks Mixture-of-Loops (MoL) decoding,
where each new token skips the preceding loops and traverses the shared stack once. It reads cached prefix
memory KV alongside current-loop local KV and appends new memory KV for subsequent tokens, extending
looped computation beyond the training loop horizon. For fast prompt processing, MoL-Hybrid decoding
further pairs parallel fixed-loop prefill with MoL decoding.
MoLT-KV brings single-traversal decoding to a plain stack while looping all layers. To further lower prefill
cost and balance efficiency with quality, we introduce U-shaped MoLT (U-MoLT), inspired by encoder-source
reuse in YOCO, Universal YOCO, and DeepSeek’s CED architecture [14, 55, 56]. U-MoLT separates memory
construction from prediction readout. It pairs a loop KV MoLT Encoder with a cross-only MoLT Decoder that
reads the normalized encoder output through independent per-layer decoder KV projections. The decoder
stays outside the encoder loop and does not depend on decoder states at earlier positions. At inference,
U-MoLT therefore skips decoder traversals at intermediate loops and non-final prompt positions without
changing the next-token prediction.
Across scales and architectures, MoLT pushes the expressivity and efficiency frontier of looped models (Fig. 2).
In 400B-token from-scratch pretraining against OLMo3, U-MoLT improves mean validation perplexity and
downstream accuracy on 700M dense, 1.5B dense, and 3B mixture-of-experts (MoE) models. At 700M
scale, MoLT-R and U-MoLT raise 10-task accuracy by 2.6 and 2.5 percentage points, and U-MoLT matches
OLMo3’s final mean validation cross-entropy with 1.7× token efficiency. On 3B MoE models with 660M active
parameters, U-MoLT lowers 10-domain perplexity by 0.75 and raises 10-task accuracy by 2.1 percentage points.
Beyond pretraining from scratch, we validate cross-architecture continued pretraining on Qwen3.5-0.8B-Base,
where U-MoLT reduces validation cross-entropy on all 11 domains over the matched 100B-token baseline with
1.4× mean token efficiency. At inference, MoL-Hybrid decoding eliminates the latency penalty of fixed-loop
decoding while preserving prediction quality. On S-size (∼113M) models with 4,096-token prompts and 64
decoding steps, U-MoLT with MoL-Hybrid-2 runs in 996.6ms, matching non-looped OLMo3 at 1,000.6ms.
The contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
• We address the expressivity–efficiency bottleneck of looped Transformers and propose a new family of
model architectures, MoLT, spanning MoLT-R (loop residual), MoLT-KV (loop KV), and U-MoLT.
Through MoLA, MoLT passes computed KV as memory for selective reuse across depths and loops,
while U-MoLT separates memory construction from prediction readout.
• We introduce loop-forcing training and MoL decoding. To our knowledge, MoL decoding is the first
single-traversal scheme that runs looped Transformers beyond the training loop horizon. By reading
cached prefix memory KV alongside current-loop local KV, it halves shared-stack decoding traversals
relative to fixed 2-loop decoding. With parallel prefill, MoL-Hybrid decoding cuts fixed 2-loop latency
by 32–50% on S-size MoLT-KV and U-MoLT for 4,096-token prompts and 64 decoding steps, matching
non-looped OLMo3 latency.
• We provide extensive empirical evidence and comprehensive ablations showing that MoLT consistently
outperforms non-looped baselines across scales and architectures. In 400B-token pretraining against
OLMo3 on 700M and 1.5B dense and 3B MoE models, U-MoLT reaches 1.7× token efficiency at 700M,
while lowering 10-domain perplexity by 0.75 and raising 10-task accuracy by 2.1 percentage points on 3B
MoE. In addition, in 100B-token continued pretraining of Qwen3.5-0.8B-Base, our U-MoLT adaptation
reduces validation cross-entropy on all 11 domains over the matched baseline with 1.4× mean token
efficiency.

2 Related Work
Looped Transformers Sharing weights across depth goes back to Universal Transformers and ALBERT [15, 30].
Recent work scales looped language models across dense and MoE architectures [9, 19, 57, 71], varies loop
depth through adaptive token routing or elastic compute budgets [2, 24], stabilizes deep unrolls [32, 44], and
adapts pretrained models into looped backbones [8, 26, 35]. These approaches primarily carry computation
across loops through the residual state. MoLT instead uses KV as memory for selective reuse across depths
and loops. As summarized in Table 1, it spans MoLT-R under loop residual, MoLT-KV under loop KV, and
the encoder–decoder variant U-MoLT.
Attention across depth Beyond standard residual connections, DenseFormer [42] aggregates earlier layer
outputs with static weights, whereas Mixture-of-Depths Attention (MoDA) [70] and Attention Residuals [27]
use input-dependent weights. In particular, MoDA jointly reads sequence KV and same-token depth KV
within a single pass. In looped architectures, CoTFormer, Dreamer, and RecurTrace expose intermediate
representations across repeated computation [29, 38, 59]. In contrast, MoLA unifies cross-loop KV reuse
and same-token depth attention within one attention distribution. Under loop residual, MoLT-R augments
current-loop local KV with preceding-loop local KV at the same token position, allowing each layer to directly
read intermediate representations from earlier layers in the current loop and all layers of the preceding loop.
Under loop KV, MoLT-KV pairs current-loop local KV with strict-prefix memory KV from the preceding
loop, separating same-token depth attention from cross-loop contextual feedback. Here, feedback refers to
passing computed representations from one loop into the next.
Prefix feedback and loop forcing Caching earlier hidden states across segments begins with same-level
recurrence in Transformer-XL [13], whereas Staircase Attention and Feedback Transformer expose higher-level
representations from earlier chunks or a weighted layer mixture as memory for subsequent tokens [17, 25].
In non-looped Transformers, Layer-Condensed KV Cache (LCKV) shares top-layer KV across condensed
layers under a strict-prefix mask [62], and Recurrent Transformer attends to layer-output prefix KV alongside
own-input KV within each layer [41]. Parallel iterative refinement has also been studied for latent thought
tokens and hidden-state feedback [7, 16, 23, 58, 63, 67], whereas Deep Equilibrium Models solve for fixed
points via root finding [3]. MoLT-KV introduces the loop KV paradigm for looped Transformers, pairing
strict-prefix memory KV projected per layer from the preceding loop’s output with current-loop local KV at
the current token. Through loop forcing, MoLT supervises next-token prediction at every loop from the initial
memory-building pass onward, aligning cross-loop representations for recursive reuse beyond the training
loop horizon. Combined with embedding restart and strict-prefix masking, this interface yields finite-prefix
stabilization in token order without contraction assumptions or iterative root finding (Section B.1).
KV sharing and efficient looped decoding Sharing KV caches reduces memory and computation in deep
and looped Transformers. Cross-Layer Attention shares projected KV across adjacent layers [5], and recent
looped models reduce KV overhead by updating a shared cache, replaying selected loops, or sharing final-loop
KV [12, 33, 45]. Looped Prediction Transformer (LPT) jointly attends to a layer’s first-loop KV cache and
a current-loop sliding window, yet still relies on residual feedback and executes all loops sequentially per
token [39]. Parallel Loop Transformer (PLT) reduces decoding latency by overlapping loops across token
positions via shifted states [61], whereas LoopFold batches multiple loop depths for the same token prefix
using predicted loop inputs and a corrector [53]. Rather than executing or overlapping multiple loops per
token, MoLT-KV and U-MoLT eliminate same-position cross-loop dependence through embedding restart and
strict-prefix memory KV, unlocking single-traversal MoL decoding beyond the training loop horizon. Each
new token reads cached prefix memory KV and appends its own memory KV in one traversal. MoL-Hybrid
decoding further combines parallel fixed-loop prefill with this single-traversal decoding update, retaining
prompt parallelism alongside reduced decoding cost and a single persistent KV history for the shared stack.
Decoder with encoder-sourced KV Separating memory construction from prediction readout reduces both
KV storage and prefill computation. YOCO builds a shared global KV cache in a self-decoder and reuses
it across a cross-decoder [55], and Universal YOCO loops the self-decoder that constructs this cache [56].

3 Mixture-of-Loops Transformer
Given tokens x0, . . . , xT −1, a looped Transformer repeatedly applies a shared network to produce repre-
sentations for predicting xt+1 from x≤t. Each pass through the shared network is called a loop. Passing
computation from one loop into the next is referred to as cross-loop feedback, and we call subsequent loops
after the first feedback loops. In the residual-feedback Loop Model, each loop passes its final state to the
next. Its intermediate representations are not retained for direct reads in later loops, and each token remains
dependent on its own preceding-loop state.
We introduce Mixture-of-Loops Transformer (MoLT), which uses KV as memory for selective reuse across
depths and loops through Mixture-of-Loops Attention (MoLA) (Figs. 1 and 3). MoLT-R carries the preceding
residual state forward and exposes retained local KV, while MoLT-KV restarts from embeddings and receives
earlier-loop context through strict-prefix memory KV. Both are plain MoLT models: their shared loop stack
has MoLA at every layer and feeds directly into the prediction head. U-shaped MoLT (U-MoLT) separates
5

memory construction from prediction readout. Its MoLT Encoder uses loop KV to build feedback memory
across loops, while its separate MoLT Decoder reads the encoder output to predict the next token. MoLT-R
prioritizes expressivity, MoLT-KV brings single-traversal decoding to a plain stack, and U-MoLT balances
efficiency and quality through an encoder–decoder split, as compared in Section 5. We first define the feedback
interfaces and the attention computation they support (Sections 3.1 and 3.2), separate memory construction
from prediction readout (Section 3.3), and then present loop-forcing training and MoL decoding (Sections 3.4
and 3.5).

3.1 Loop Residual and Loop KV
A feedback interface determines what a loop retains and how the next loop receives it. Each loop initializes
a residual stream, applies L blocks of MoLA and feed-forward, and produces an output for prediction and
subsequent feedback. We index loops by r ≥ 1 and layers by ℓ ∈ {0, . . . , L − 1}. Layers have distinct
parameters within a loop and share those parameters across loops. We write X r,ℓ for the residual stream
entering layer ℓ and zr for the normalized loop output. We write MoLAℓ(X, S, D) with three arguments: the
layer input X, the sequence KV sources S, and the depth KV sources D. MoLA projects its query from X and
jointly reads the KV supplied by S and D. These collections contain the selected sources’ projected KV pairs,
with key normalization and RoPE already applied. We omit the query indices (r, ℓ, t) on the source collections
below, and ⊔ concatenates their KV pairs. MoLA returns the attention update after head concatenation and
output projection. FFN denotes the feed-forward sublayer. For clarity, the layer updates and projections
6

below omit standard block normalization. The two feedback forms differ in how the retained computation
enters this shared network.

We train the MoLT family by supervising next-token predictions at successive loops of the shared network.

Loop forcing For MoLT-KV and U-MoLT, applying Eq. (8) with positive weight on every loop defines
loop forcing. Analogous to teacher forcing, each feedback loop trains in parallel across tokens from the
preceding loop’s strict-prefix memory KV rather than from sequentially decoded prefixes, reducing training
from T sequential steps to N parallel passes. Allowing gradients through cross-loop feedback additionally lets
later-loop losses refine the earlier computation that produces the feedback sources, while per-loop supervision
remains applicable when cross-loop gradients are detached. After 2-loop training, single-traversal decoding
achieves similar mean continuation CE to fixed 2-loop decoding on the evaluated windows (Section 4.4). Three
design choices help support this shift to recursively generated prefix memory KV (Section B.2). Shared-readout
supervision encourages the supervised loop outputs z1 and z2 to align. Embedding restart anchors each
token’s residual stream to e(xt), while output and QK normalization keep activations and attention logits
bounded.
3.5 Mixture-of-Loops Decoding
We consider three decoding schedules: fixed-loop decoding, Mixture-of-Loops (MoL) decoding, and MoL-
Hybrid decoding. All MoLT variants support fixed-loop decoding, which preserves the chosen finite-loop
computation. MoL decoding combines current-loop local KV with cached prefix memory KV and skips the
preceding loops at each new token. In MoLT-KV and U-MoLT, embedding restart and strict-prefix loop KV
feedback remove same-position cross-loop dependencies, enabling one traversal per decoding step. Both MoL
decoding and MoL-Hybrid decoding use this single-traversal update, differing in how they initialize prompt
memory KV. A traversal executes the full Transformer backbone in plain MoLT or the encoder in U-MoLT
once, over either the prompt or one token position, while a decoder traversal executes the MoLT Decoder
once over the requested positions. Prefill supplies the first continuation prediction, and each decoding step
consumes one token to predict the next.
Fixed-loop decoding Fixed-loop prefill executes N traversals of the shared network, processing prompt
positions in parallel within each loop. Fixed 2-loop decoding sets N = 2. Each loop retains its own prefix
sequence KV cache. During decoding, each new input token passes through all N loops, reading and extending
their respective caches before computing the final-loop prediction. MoLT-R also passes the current token’s
normalized output and selected local KV from each loop to the next. These same-position sources require the
token’s loops to execute in order. This preserves the chosen finite-loop computation and requires N network
traversals per decoding step.
Mixture-of-Loops decoding Applied from empty caches, MoL decoding processes the prompt one token at a
time. At each position, one traversal starts from e(xt), jointly reads completed prefix memory KV at j < t
and current-loop local KV through MoLA, and produces a new output. Memory KV projected from the
normalized output are appended to the cache for later tokens. Decoding uses the same update, so both phases
require one traversal per token.
MoL-Hybrid decoding MoL-Hybrid decoding combines fixed-loop parallel prefill with the MoL decoding
update. MoL-Hybrid-1 and MoL-Hybrid-2 use 1-loop and 2-loop prefill, respectively, initializing prompt
memory KV by projecting z1 and z2. In U-MoLT, decoder KV come from the final prefill loop. The prediction
10

at the final prefill position supplies the distribution for the first continuation token. Each decoding step uses
one traversal and extends the retained caches, including memory KV projected from each new output. The
history therefore combines finite-loop prompt caches with KV recursively appended during decoding, trading
exact equivalence to an empty-cache sweep for parallel prompt processing.
Key properties and inference costs Because embedding restart and strict-prefix memory reads at j < t
remove same-position cross-loop dependence, every feedback loop reduces to a strictly triangular recurrence
across token positions. Position 0 has an empty prefix and stabilizes by loop 2 from any initial state, and each
subsequent position stabilizes one loop after its prefix (Section B.1). Empty-cache MoL decoding therefore
reaches the unique stabilized solution matched by every unroll of depth r ≥ t + 2. After a length-P prompt,
MoL-Hybrid decoding preserves the same cross-token recursion over continuation positions m = t − P ≥ 0,
matching any multi-loop unroll that holds the prompt fixed and updates only the continuation for s ≥ m + 2
loops. Although each token traverses the shared stack once during decoding, recursive prefix memory
propagation, current-loop depth KV, and U-MoLT’s separate decoder preserve multi-loop prefix computation
rather than collapsing to single-loop depth. Even with an equal split LE = LD = L/2, U-MoLT reaches at
least 3L/2 blocks of causal path depth over earlier tokens, compared with at most L in a non-looped L-layer
Transformer.
Together, U-MoLT and loop KV close the inference efficiency gap in two steps. First, even under fixed 2-loop
execution, omitting unused decoder traversals lets equal-split U-MoLT match a non-looped L-layer Transformer
in full-prompt prefill block depth (2LE = L) and quadratic prefill attention, while keeping decoding traversals
and persistent KV storage at 1.5× instead of 2×. Second, switching to MoL or MoL-Hybrid decoding lowers
both an L-layer MoLT-KV stack and any U-MoLT partition with LE + LD = L to L block traversals per
decoding step and a single persistent prefix KV history for the shared stack, matching a non-looped L-layer
Transformer in total persistent KV storage. Under MoL-Hybrid-2, equal-split U-MoLT therefore matches a
non-looped backbone across all four dominant inference costs while retaining parallel prefill. Its remaining
decoding overhead consists of LE extra memory KV projections and small depth attention, which scales
as an O(LE /T ) fraction of sequence attention under full history. During 2-loop prefill, it also adds the
single-position decoder readout at P − 1, prompt decoder KV projections, and extra memory KV projections.
Section B provides the formal proofs, loop-forcing alignment analysis, and complete traversal, KV-storage,
and computational cost breakdowns for the all-layer loop KV architectures, and Section 4.4 evaluates these
schedules empirically.

4.5 Adapting Qwen through Continued Pretraining
We test whether the MoLT feedback interface improves an existing pretrained backbone through matched
continued pretraining (CT). Starting from Qwen3.5-0.8B-Base, we train Qwen3.5-CT, U-MoLT-CT1, and
U-MoLT-CT2 on the same 100B-token stream with the same tokenizer and AdamW schedule. Training uses a
1.05M-token global batch and a peak learning rate of 10−4, with a 1.05B-token warmup and cosine decay
to 10−5. Qwen3.5-CT retains the original architecture, while U-MoLT-CT1 and U-MoLT-CT2 apply two
MoLT adaptations. Evaluation uses fixed validation blocks from 11 domains and their mean CE, with final
second-loop predictions for the adaptations. Table 8 compares the three architectures after 100B tokens of
continued pretraining.
U-MoLT-CT1 and U-MoLT-CT2 differ in the source used to construct decoder Gated DeltaNet (GDN) [65]
memory. Qwen3.5-0.8B-Base has 24 layers, comprising 18 GDN layers and 6 full-attention layers. Both
adaptations split this backbone into a 12-layer encoder and a 12-layer decoder, with 9 GDN layers and 3
full-attention layers in each half. In the encoder, the 3 full-attention layers act as MoLA feedback layers
reading strict-prefix memory KV from the preceding loop, while the 9 GDN layers remain recurrent sequence
layers whose states reset between loops. Both use the re-proj. decoder-attention design in Table 2, with
independent decoder KV projections at each attention layer. U-MoLT-CT1 constructs memory in the 9
decoder GDN layers from each layer’s own input, following the self source choice. Because these decoder GDN
layers retain dependence on earlier decoder states, U-MoLT-CT1 is not strictly cross-only like pure-attention
U-MoLT. U-MoLT-CT2 instead projects decoder GDN KV, decay gates, and update strengths from the
normalized encoder output, while keeping queries and output gates dependent on the decoder state. Because

each Qwen3.5 GDN layer applies a length-4 causal convolution to its queries, U-MoLT-CT2 still retains

MoLT improves all 11 domains over the matched baseline Both adaptations achieve lower CE than Qwen3.5-

to an existing pretrained backbone under the same input-token budget, using additional shared-weight loop

U-MoLT-CT2 in all 11 domains, with a mean CE advantage of 0.003.
U-MoLT-CT1 reaches the baseline endpoint with fewer input tokens U-MoLT-CT1 matches Qwen3.5-CT’s
final domain-level CE after 66.3–78.3B input tokens along the recorded training trajectories (Fig. 5). Across

measured along the full 100B-token learning-rate schedule.

5 Discussion
Choosing among MoLT family architectures MoLT-R, MoLT-KV, and U-MoLT target distinct regimes across
expressivity, efficiency, and backbone structure. Table 9 summarizes their mechanisms, inference profiles, and
intended use cases.
MoLT-R prioritizes expressivity over single-traversal inference. It keeps the residual stream across loops
instead of passing earlier loops only through the normalized loop output. Without adding parameters, MoLA
directly reads current-loop and preceding-loop local KV at the same token position. In Tables 4 and 5, MoLT-R
reaches the highest 700M aggregate scores, with 59.2% mean 10-task accuracy and 14.84 mean 10-domain
PPL. It also achieves the lowest validation CE among plain 12-layer stacks in Table 2. Our preliminary
observations further indicate that its gain over plain residual feedback grows with effective computational
depth, where information dilution in a single residual stream becomes more severe. MoLT-R therefore fits
expressivity-critical settings that can afford fixed N-loop decoding and N sequence KV caches.
MoLT-KV prioritizes decoding efficiency with minimal architectural inductive bias. Embedding restart and
strict-prefix memory KV remove same-position cross-loop dependence. Each decoding token can then skip
preceding-loop traversals and run one traversal of the shared stack under MoL decoding. Relative to fixed
N -loop decoding, this cuts per-step decoding traversals and persistent KV storage by a factor of N . At N = 2,
MoL-Hybrid decoding reduces total S-size MoLT-KV latency by nearly half in Tables 6 and 7. Meanwhile,
reading cached prefix memory KV across the shared L-layer stack preserves multi-loop computation over the
prefix, as analyzed in Section B.1. Because MoL-Hybrid-2 still runs 2L full-prompt prefill blocks in MoLT-KV,
this variant fits generation-heavy workloads where decoding dominates latency, matching U-MoLT’s decoding
speed. It also fits standard or hybrid backbones where additional architectural inductive bias is undesired.
U-MoLT builds on loop KV by adding an architectural inductive bias tailored to autoregressive language
modeling. It separates memory construction in a looped MoLT Encoder from prediction readout in a causal,
cross-only MoLT Decoder. By Proposition 2, U-MoLT omits unused decoder traversals during prefill and
intermediate loops. With an equal split LE = LD = L/2, MoL-Hybrid-2 removes the 2× prefill block depth
of MoLT-KV. As shown in Section B.3, it matches a non-looped L-layer backbone in full-prompt prefill
block depth, quadratic prefill attention, decoding traversals, and persistent KV storage. With independent
decoder KV projections, U-MoLT also lowers validation CE relative to MoLT-KV across all matched settings
in Table 2. U-MoLT is therefore our default choice for general-purpose pure-attention language models.

Representational decoupling in U-MoLT A natural question is why U-MoLT outperforms MoLT-KV in
validation CE even though it loops only half the layers. We attribute this gain to decoupling two conflicting
roles in deep autoregressive layers. In a standard stack, deep layers near the prediction head face two
competing pressures. First, for prediction, hidden states near the output must progressively collapse toward
the next-token distribution p(xt+1 | x≤t). This process discards or rewrites fine-grained semantic details of
the current token. Second, for memory, self-attention KV are projected directly from each layer’s hidden
states. Those same deep layers must therefore preserve clean historical context for future tokens at t′ > t.
MoLT-KV sharpens this conflict because its normalized loop output zr feeds the vocabulary projection while
also supplying memory KV to the next loop.
U-MoLT physically isolates these two responsibilities. The normalized encoder output zr sits midway through
the network rather than directly beneath the prediction head. It can therefore act as a high-fidelity shared
memory without collapsing toward next-token logits. In contrast, hidden states in the cross-only MoLT
Decoder never supply KV for future tokens and never feed back into the encoder. The decoder residual stream
can therefore specialize toward predicting xt+1 without contaminating the memory read by later tokens. Even
at the deepest decoder layers, independent decoder KV projections can still read clean context from zr right
before the final prediction. Our ablations in Table 2 are consistent with this view. Across all 6 matched
U-MoLT triplets, decoder self-attention under the self setting raises CE by 0.014–0.021 relative to independent
per-layer projections under re-proj. Independent projections also beat shared projections by 0.009–0.018
CE, showing that decoder layers benefit from separate views of the same normalized encoder output. In
Table 8, however, this cross-only decoder design also depends on the backbone’s sequence layers. On Qwen3.5,
forcing decoder GDN layers to read only encoder-projected memory in U-MoLT-CT2 lags U-MoLT-CT1
by 0.003 mean CE across the 11 domains, while still not achieving exact single-position prefill omission
(Section 4.5). On hybrid backbones with non-attention sequence mixers, retaining decoder-state recurrence in
hybrid U-MoLT adaptations or using plain MoLT stacks therefore remains an important alternative.
Beyond two stages: Encoder–Neck–Decoder architectures U-MoLT splits a looped Transformer into two
stages, using a looped encoder for memory construction and a cross-only decoder for prediction readout. A
natural generalization is a three-stage Encoder–Neck–Decoder architecture. In this layout, a non-looped
encoder extracts foundational context, a looped middle neck performs iterative cross-loop refinement, and
a cross-only decoder handles prediction readout. Informally, this forms a “giraffe” network whose recurrent
depth is elongated in the middle neck while keeping the encoder and decoder non-looped. In our preliminary
experiments extending U-MoLT to this three-stage layout, looping the middle neck achieved the strongest
validation results. This points toward an even cleaner separation among input encoding, iterative refinement,
and prediction readout. We leave a systematic study of this “giraffe” architecture, including stage partitioning
and inference schedules, to future work.

Scaling to larger models Computational constraints have limited our scaling experiments to 700M and 1.5B
dense models and 3B MoE models. How the gains from loop reuse evolve with larger model capacity and
training budgets remains an open question. A broader scaling study could compare allocating additional
computation to larger models or to additional loops, measuring prediction quality alongside training and
inference cost. Such experiments would help determine how to balance parameter capacity and shared-weight
loop computation as MoLT scales up.
6 Conclusion
In this paper, we present Mixture-of-Loops Transformer (MoLT), which uses keys and values (KV) as memory
to selectively reuse computation across depths and loops. Through Mixture-of-Loops Attention (MoLA), MoLT
spans MoLT-R under loop residual for expressivity and MoLT-KV under loop KV for plain-stack efficiency.
Under loop KV, embedding restart and strict-prefix memory KV remove same-position cross-loop dependence.
Trained via loop forcing, this enables single-traversal MoL decoding beyond the training loop horizon, while
MoL-Hybrid decoding adds parallel prefill. To balance efficiency and quality, U-MoLT further separates
memory construction from prediction readout and omits unused decoder traversals at inference. Experiments
on 700M and 1.5B dense models and 3B MoE models trained with the OLMo3 recipe show consistent gains in
perplexity and downstream accuracy. Continued pretraining on Qwen3.5-0.8B-Base confirms effective transfer
to pretrained backbones, and MoL-Hybrid decoding achieves substantial latency reductions over fixed-loop
decoding. These results suggest that explicit KV reuse across depths and loops is a practical and effective
primitive for scaling computational depth in looped Transformers. We will release the full implementation of
MoLT, and we hope it will serve as a foundation for building stronger looped large language models in the
open-source community. MoLT integrates naturally with dense, MoE, and hybrid backbones. Beyond language
modeling, it can be readily extended to multimodal intelligence, visual understanding, and world models,
where iterative Transformer computation is increasingly adopted. We believe that principled information
reuse across depths and loops will bring broad and lasting benefits across these diverse domains.

B Analyzing Loop KV: Recurrence, Loop Forcing, and Efficiency
Why can loop KV break the sequential-loop decoding bottleneck while preserving multi-loop computation
over the prefix, whereas loop residual cannot? We answer this question for the all-layer MoLA stacks of
MoLT-KV and U-MoLT in three steps. First, we derive the strictly triangular recurrence of loop KV and verify
autoregressive causality (Section B.1). This establishes single-traversal equivalence for MoL and MoL-Hybrid
decoding and quantifies the resulting multi-loop path depth over the prefix. Second, we explain how loop
forcing trains this token-ordered recurrence in parallel and how 2-loop training prepares the model for
recursively generated prefix memory KV (Section B.2). Third, we prove decoder omission for U-MoLT and
quantify traversal, KV-storage, and per-traversal computational costs (Section B.3).
B.1 Strictly Triangular Recurrence and Single-Traversal Decoding
Strictly triangular recurrence Under loop residual, token t in feedback loop r > 1 depends on its own
preceding-loop output zr−1 t . In MoLT-R, it also reads preceding-loop local KV Dprev at position t. This
same-position cross-loop dependence forces inference to run all N loops sequentially before producing a token’s
final output.
Under loop KV, embedding restart fixes the layer-0 input at X r,0 t = e(xt), and strict-prefix memory reads
restrict Smem to zr−1 <t . Current-loop depth KV Dcur come only from layers k ≤ ℓ at position t, so they
introduce no cross-loop dependence. Layer projections, RoPE, normalization, and feed-forward updates are
also position-wise. For fixed inputs and deterministic evaluation, every feedback loop therefore reduces to the
strictly triangular map

B.2 Parallel Training with Loop Forcing and Cross-Loop Alignment
The preceding results identify the unroll computed by single-traversal decoding, leaving two practical questions.
First, how can we train Ft without running a sequential recurrence across all T token positions? Second, how
does training on 2 loops prepare the model to use recursively generated prefix memory KV, and how does this
change in memory source affect continuation quality?
30

Parallelizing feedback training with loop forcing Under loop residual, training and fixed-loop inference execute
the same N -loop computation. Under loop KV, single-traversal decoding instead evaluates the token-ordered
recurrence z⋆ t = Ft(xt, z⋆ <t). Directly training this recurrence requires T sequential steps across the sequence,
which is prohibitive at long context lengths. Loop forcing restores sequence parallelism in the same way
teacher forcing parallelizes autoregressive training. For each feedback loop r ≥ 2, loop forcing conditions Ft on
the preceding loop’s normalized outputs zr−1 <t rather than on the sequentially constructed prefix z⋆ <t. Loop 1
computes z1 in parallel using current-loop sequence KV. Each subsequent loop evaluates Ft in parallel across
all positions. The multi-loop loss in Eq. (8) supervises every loop with positive weight. Training therefore
runs in N parallel passes instead of T sequential steps. Loop forcing works with either open or detached
cross-loop gradients. Open gradients let later-loop losses also refine earlier loop outputs through memory KV.
Cross-loop alignment and continuation quality The hardest extrapolation setting is N = 2, where Ft never
sees its own outputs during training. Analogous to exposure bias in autoregressive decoding, 2-loop training
evaluates Ft only on memory KV projected from loop-1 outputs z1 <t. Those outputs are produced with
current-loop sequence KV rather than by Ft itself. During MoL decoding, Ft instead reads prefix memory
KV projected from its own earlier outputs. Under MoL-Hybrid decoding with k ∈ {1, 2} prefill loops, Ft
reads frozen prompt memory KV projected from zk <P together with continuation memory KV recursively
appended from its own earlier outputs. Without alignment across loops, recursive extrapolation over hundreds
of continuation tokens could cause representation drift. Three training and architectural choices help support
this shift in memory source.
First, supervising next-token prediction at every loop through the shared prediction readout encourages z1
and z2 to align under 2-loop training. In MoLT-KV, the shared vocabulary projection maps each supervised
zr directly to next-token logits. In U-MoLT, the shared cross-only decoder starts from X r,LE and reads zr
through independent decoder KV projections across all LD layers. Both loops also run the same shared stack
from e(xt). Together, these shared components encourage z1 and z2 to remain compatible when memory
KV projections trained on z1 are applied to z2 and later recursive outputs. With detached gradients, loop-1
loss is the only loss that backpropagates into z1, directly aligning it with the shared readout. With open
gradients, later-loop losses also backpropagate into z1 through memory KV. Positive loop-1 weight then
prevents later-loop losses from reshaping z1 solely as an internal feedback source. In Table 3, raising the
relative weight on loop 2 lowers loop-2 CE but raises loop-1 CE. Loop-loss weights therefore trade loop-1 CE
against loop-2 CE.
Second, embedding restart anchors each token’s residual stream to e(xt), and single-traversal decoding runs
the shared stack only once per token. Even when memory propagates recursively across hundreds of earlier
positions, earlier normalized loop outputs never overwrite the current token’s residual stream. Instead, they
enter only through softmax attention over prefix memory KV, competing with current-loop depth KV along
the current token’s residual stream. Unlike unrolling loop residual to depth r ≥ t + 2 at the same token, long
continuations under MoL decoding therefore avoid compounding same-token residual drift away from e(xt).
Third, even without a contraction assumption, normalization keeps activations and attention logits uniformly
bounded across arbitrarily long recursive histories. For fixed parameters, output normalization bounds every
normalized loop output and its projected memory values independently of position and unroll depth. Starting
from a bounded token embedding e(xt), each layer forms a softmax convex combination of sequence and
depth values before its residual and feed-forward updates. Across a single finite traversal, layer induction
therefore keeps every layer state and its current-loop local values bounded regardless of sequence length. In
U-MoLT, the same finite-layer induction bounds the LD decoder layers over projected decoder values. With
norm-preserving RoPE, per-head affine RMS QK normalization further bounds attention logits.
At t > 0 under MoL decoding and across MoL-Hybrid continuation steps, each token still evaluates the feedback
map Ft over prefix memory KV rather than running the loop-1 path. At the same time, 2-loop training under
loop forcing supervises only one feedback step from z1 to z2, so it does not train the recursion for extra
gains over longer chains. Together with the three mechanisms above, these properties motivate the empirical
evaluation in Section 4.4. In Table 6, MoL and MoL-Hybrid decoding achieve similar mean continuation CE

We now show how single-traversal decoding and U-MoLT’s cross-only decoder remove repeated per-step

Omitting unused decoder traversals In U-MoLT, the cross-only decoder reads only encoder-sourced decoder
KV and stays outside the encoder feedback path.
Proposition 2 (Decoder omission). Fix the encoder schedule and its outputs, and consider the deterministic
cross-only decoder and prediction head in Eqs. (6) and (7). Every requested next-token distribution at position
t is unchanged when all decoder work is omitted at unrequested loops. At requested loops, omitting decoder
query, cross-attention, and feed-forward updates at other positions also leaves that distribution unchanged,
provided decoder KV remain available at j ≤ t.
Proof. Omitting the decoder at an unrequested loop leaves subsequent encoder loops unchanged because
decoder outputs are not fed back. At any requested position t, the decoder starts from the raw encoder output
at t. Each decoder layer depends only on the preceding decoder state at t and on decoder KV projected from
normalized encoder outputs at j ≤ t. Induction over decoder layers makes the next-token distribution at t
independent of decoder states at all other positions.
For next-token inference, the decoder runs its query, cross-attention, and feed-forward updates only at the
final prefill position and once per decoding step. It projects decoder KV only from the final normalized
encoder outputs of prefill and of each decoding step. In multi-loop prefill or decoding, all decoder work is
skipped at intermediate loops. Because Proposition 2 holds for any fixed encoder schedule, this omission
applies to fixed-loop, MoL, and MoL-Hybrid decoding alike.
