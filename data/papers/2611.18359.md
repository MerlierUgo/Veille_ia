## Envisioning Agentic Robotics: A New Era of Robotic Systems

The release of GPT-6 Astra marks the emergence of a new paradigm in robotics: agentic robotics . Within weeks, frontier models with agentic harnesses matched or exceeded task-specific policies on many embodied benchmarks. Instead of training a dedicated policy for each family of tasks, an agentic robotic system applies a foundation model to open-world embodied tasks. It operates in a closed loop: observing the environment, interpreting user intent, reasoning about the task, invoking skills and tools, verifying outcomes, and adapting its strategy. Despite these advances, the community has yet to converge on a shared definition, a reliable evaluation framework, or a research roadmap. We argue that Embodied System 2 , realized by a foundation model together with an embodied harness, forms the decision-making core of agentic robotics. Its central challenge is to translate general-purpose reasoning into effective and verifiable actions under physical constraints such as limited reaction time, mechanical limits, unpredictable environment changes, and irreversible consequences. Motivated by this perspective, we propose an evaluation framework that measures both model capabilities and closed-loop system performance. Furthermore, we identify the principles that carry over from digital to physical agents and the challenges that must be addressed to achieve reliable physical autonomy. On top of the Embodied System 2 and the evaluation framework, we envision a research roadmap toward sustained autonomy: the ability to perform work safely and reliably with progressively broader responsibility, from executing individual primitives to fulfilling standing roles.

### 1 Introduction

A central goal of robotics is to enable robots to translate human intent into desired outcomes in the physical world. Early autonomous robots often integrated hand-engineered components for perception, planning, and control within modular or layered architectures ( Nilsson, 1984 ; Gat et al., 1998 ) . Since then, advances in deep learning have allowed robots to learn increasingly complex behaviors. Policies learned in simulation, mostly with deep reinforcement learning, have produced agile locomotion ( Hwangbo et al., 2019 ) and versatile humanoid whole-body control by tracking human motion data ( Liao et al., 2025 ; Luo et al., 2026c ; Liao et al., 2026 ) . However, these simulation-trained policies remain specialized to specific tasks and embodiments, limiting their ability to generalize across diverse and open-ended environments. To move beyond task-specific controllers and enable robots to follow human instructions, the field has increasingly turned toward models that unify perception, language understanding, and action generation within a single framework. Among them, vision-language-action (VLA) models, exemplified by the $\pi$ series, combine vision-language pretraining with diverse robot data to learn general policies that map visual observations and language instructions to actions ( Black et al., 2025a ; Black et al., 2025b ) . More recently, world-action models (WAM) integrate video-based modeling of physical dynamics with robot policy learning ( Ye et al., 2026a ; Yuan et al., 2026b ; Li et al., 2026c ) . Together, these advances have broadened robot capabilities. Yet reliable generalization remains a central challenge, as policy performance can still deteriorate when scenes or instructions change ( Zhou et al., 2025c ) .

To address this generalization challenge, a major line of research focuses on scaling and diversifying training data for VLA and WAM policies ( Zheng et al., 2026 ; Dyna Robotics, 2026a ) . These efforts draw on diverse data sources, including real-robot data ( Black et al., 2025a ) , UMI demonstrations ( Chi et al., 2024 ) , egocentric human data ( Zheng et al., 2026 ) , and multimodal web data ( Brohan et al., 2023 ) . The release of GPT-6 Astra ( OpenAI, 2026a ; OpenAI, 2026b ) and its early applications to robotics offered a different perspective on this challenge. In an early evaluation, the model outperformed trained policies on many robotic benchmarks without task-specific fine-tuning ( Zhang et al., 2026c ) . Subsequent work explored its use with learned policies and robotic tools ( Galbot Team et al., 2026 ; Gao et al., 2026b ) . These results suggest that generalization can also be achieved by relying on a general-purpose model’s knowledge and reasoning to organize and adapt behavior during execution. This perspective motivates a paradigm shift from policy-centric robotics to agentic robotics , by shifting the focus from learned action policies (Figure 2 a) to an agent that pursues goals by coordinating skills and tools, evaluating outcomes, and adapting its decisions (Figure 2 b).

> Figure 2: Two paradigms. (a) An action policy maps the task instruction and observation to action targets. It might include reasoning, subtask prediction, or in-context adaptation. (b) S2 combines a foundation model with an embodied harness. Together, they interpret intent, orchestrate skills and tools, verify outcomes, and adapt subsequent decisions. S1 provides callable skills and tools. S0 performs body control. The S1 path is illustrative; S2 may also call S0 or external tools.

Despite this progress, the community still lacks a shared understanding of agentic robotics or a reliable evaluation framework. The scope of the paradigm, its relationship to existing forms of robot autonomy, and the organization of its constituent functions remain insufficiently clarified ( Qin et al., 2026a ) . Furthermore, differences in experimental conditions and system configurations also make it difficult to compare results across studies. For example, RoboDojo evaluations of Astra vary in the task-knowledge sources they use and the action budgets they permit ( Zhang et al., 2026c ; SenseNova et al., 2026 ) ; separately, Harness VLA shows substantial performance gains from execution memory when the planner and VLA are held fixed ( Zhang et al., 2026f ) . All of these results underscore the need to establish what constitutes the agentic system, which capabilities are being assessed, and under what conditions comparisons are meaningful. These conceptual and methodological challenges call for a unified framework that clarifies the agentic workflow by specifying the functional roles of its components and how they interact. Such a framework can provide a shared conceptual foundation for characterizing agentic robotics, developing valid and comparable evaluations at the model and system levels, and formulating a research roadmap.

To address this, we propose a framework of agentic robotics that distinguishes three functional roles (Figure 3 ). System 2 (S2) interprets intent, reasons about goals and constraints, orchestrates skills and tools, and verifies outcomes to guide subsequent decisions. System 1 (S1) provides skills and callable modules, while System 0 (S0) supplies the body interface and real-time control. Specifically, S2 is the decision-making core of agentic robotics, realized by a foundation model together with an embodied harness . The foundation model supplies knowledge, understanding, and reasoning, while the harness organizes observations, maintains plans and memory, exposes skills and tools, and supports execution and verification. Learned policies, perception layers, motion planning modules, and generated programs are included in S1. S2 may orchestrate these capabilities or send action targets directly to S0, while retaining responsibility for verifying outcomes and adapting its decisions. With these roles defined, we investigate how the foundation model and harness should work together under physical constraints, and how to evaluate their contributions to closed-loop system performance.

Building on this division of responsibilities, we first explain how S2, S1, and S0 work together (Section 2 ). We then articulate S2 and examine its foundation model, its harness, and the physical constraints under which they operate (Section 3 ). Next, we develop an evaluation framework across nine dimensions that distinguishes model capabilities from whole system performance, and use it to assess the available evidence (Section 4 ). For a closed-loop system, the framework emphasizes independent outcome verification, transparent reporting, and evaluation while the environment continues to evolve. Furthermore, we examine which design principles carry over from digital agents to physical systems and which assumptions must be reconsidered in the physical world (Section 5 ). These analyses inform a roadmap organized around four levels of delegable work, from executing primitives (L1), completing tasks (L2), coordinating workflows (L3), to fulfilling standing roles (L4) (Figure 1 ; Section 6 ). The roadmap connects this progression to four research directions, including continual improvement from physical experience. Finally, we conclude with several guidelines to verify and evaluate the progress of agentic robotics.


### 2 Overview of Agentic Robotics

We adopt a system-level view of agentic robotics: an agent interprets the user’s intent, decides how to proceed, orchestrates skills and tools at run time, and verifies their physical effects against the intended goal. Feedback from verification guides subsequent decisions, enabling the system to adapt its actions, recover from failures, and seek clarification or assistance when needed. We first present a reference architecture that defines the responsibilities and interfaces of S2, S1, and S0. We then go through the agentic loop and examine three system-level properties: operation across multiple timescales, responsiveness during deliberation, and state persistence across tasks.


#### 2.1 Reference Architecture

In general, an agentic robot requires three functional roles: S2 interprets intent, reasons about goals and constraints, orchestrates capabilities, and verifies outcomes; S1 provides skills and callable modules; and S0 provides the body interface and local real-time control. Note that this decomposition is functional: one model may serve several roles, and one role may span several models or modules. Figure 3 shows the three systems and their interfaces, together with the user, the robot body, the physical world, and a real-time event monitor. The systems typically run on different timescales, but timing alone does not distinguish them. We describe them from S2 downward, focusing on their responsibilities and interactions.

> Figure 3: A reference framework for agentic robotics. An Embodied S2 consists of a foundation model and an embodied harness, whose five core parts manage context, tools, planning, memory, and verification. S2 runs on demand or in response to events, whereas S1 and S0 execute at their own rates. A real-time event monitor outside the three systems watches the user, the robot body, and the physical world at a high rate. It interrupts S2 when an event may change the task and, under predefined safety rules, triggers an immediate reaction in S0 without waiting for S2. S2 may call S0 directly through the tool interface; S0 keeps its own real-time loop.

S2 is the functional layer that interprets intent, reasons about goals and constraints to make decisions, orchestrates skills and tools accordingly, and verifies outcomes to adapt subsequent decisions toward the intended goals. It decides whether to act, gather information, ask for clarification, continue, retry, replan, or stop. When acting, it chooses a capability and specifies the call, which may invoke an S1 skill or a tool, or send an action target directly to S0. It checks the returned evidence against the intended goal before deciding on the next subtask. In hierarchical systems such as Hi Robot ( Shi et al., 2025 ) and Gemini Robotics ( Gemini Robotics Team, 2025a ; Gemini Robotics Team, 2025b ; Gemini Robotics Team, 2026b ) , a high-level vision-language model contributes to the orchestration: it interprets instructions and feedback before delegating the next subtask to an action model.

In our framework, S2 is realized by a foundation model and an embodied harness. The S2 foundation model supplies knowledge, understanding, and reasoning, and proposes the next decision from the context it receives. The embodied harness supplies the runtime that assembles the model’s context and turns its decisions into constrained, observable, and verifiable execution. Concretely, it has five core parts. A context manager assembles current observations, task progress, interaction history, and the state of the world, the user, and the body. A tool interface exposes S1 skills, permitted S0 calls, code execution, and other tools to S2. A planner maintains and revises task plans given the foundation model’s proposals or dedicated planning routines. A memory stores, retrieves, updates, and removes information beyond the current context. A verifier judges from evidence whether a step achieved its subgoal and whether the task is complete. Existing systems implement some of these parts: Inner Monologue ( Huang et al., 2023b ) feeds scene and skill-success signals back into planning; SCOPER ( Wei et al., 2026a ) updates an evidence memory and verifies progress to guide recovery.

S1 provides skills and callable modules that perform the functions S2 delegates, including action execution, perception, and motion planning. Specifically, action execution modules map observations to actions within the scope of a subtask, such as VLA policies ( Black et al., 2025b ; Black et al., 2025a ; NVIDIA et al., 2025c ) , world-action models (WAMs) ( Dyna Robotics, 2026b ) , locomotion policies, and navigation policies. Perception modules extract task-relevant estimates from sensor data through detection ( Minderer et al., 2022 ) , segmentation ( Carion et al., 2025 ) , pointing ( Deitke et al., 2024 ) , and pose estimation ( Wen et al., 2024 ) . Motion planning modules turn a goal into a feasible motion ( Sundaralingam et al., 2023 ; Sundermeyer et al., 2021 ; Kim et al., 2025 ; Huang et al., 2025 ) . A single call may combine several of these categories. For example, a grasp call may segment the object, select a grasp function, plan a path, and execute it. Programs written by S2 likewise serve as S1 skills when they execute delegated behavior ( Yang et al., 2026j ) .

S0 is the embodiment-specific layer that executes control targets in a real-time loop. Examples are whole-body tracking and balance for a humanoid, gait tracking and stabilization for a quadruped, inverse kinematics and trajectory tracking for an arm, and chassis control for a mobile base. For humanoids, whole-body tracking is increasingly handled by general-purpose controllers—often called behavior foundation models—that are pretrained at scale ( Yuan et al., 2025a ) , for example by learning to track large human-motion datasets ( Luo et al., 2026c ; Liao et al., 2026 ) . S0 receives control targets from S1, or from S2 through a permitted action interface, and reports state estimates such as joint positions, contact, and pose. It maintains stability and enforces the body’s control limits without waiting for S2 to deliberate.

A lightweight real-time event monitor runs alongside the three systems. At a high rate, it watches the user, the robot body, and the physical world for events that cannot wait for S2 to decide. Such events include a sudden command or interjection from the user, an unexpected body movement caused by a collision or fault, and a change in the scene such as a person entering the workspace. In practice, the monitor may use a small streaming vision-language model (VLM), rule-based detectors, or both. It interrupts S2 when an event may change the task. When an event demands an immediate reaction, it may apply its predefined safety rules and trigger a protective response in S0, such as stopping or slowing the current motion.

> Figure 4: Three typical execution paths. One system may use all three paths within a single task. For simplicity, the robot body is omitted. (a) Layered orchestration: S2 invokes S1 skills and tools, which produce control targets that S0 executes. (b) Direct control: S2 controls S0 directly through an action interface; S0 still runs its own real-time control loop. (c) Code as policy: S2 writes code that a runtime interpreter executes in the role of S1.

In general, commands flow downward and evidence flows upward across the three systems. In the downward direction, we divide the execution of a decision into three typical paths by how S2 reaches the body, as shown in Figure 4 . In layered orchestration , S2 invokes S1 skills and tools, which produce the actions that S0 executes. In direct control , S2 bypasses S1 and controls S0 through an action interface, as several systems built on frontier models do ( Zhang et al., 2026c ; Yang et al., 2026j ) . Direct control does not place the foundation model inside the real-time control loop: S0 still runs its own local loop, and the action interface defines what S2 may send to it. In code as policy , S2 writes code that plays the role of S1 at run time ( Liang et al., 2023 ) ; the code may execute several actions before S2 decides again. Note that these paths are not alternative architectures: one system may use all three within a single task. In the upward direction, S0 reports the state of the body. Each call should return the observations available at that point and an execution status, allowing S2 to assess the outcome. Outside this call chain, the event monitor signals S2 and S0 without being invoked.

The three systems also differ in how directly they depend on the body. S0 must account for the robot’s dynamics and control interface, whereas S1 skills depend on the actions and observations that the body provides. An S2 strategy of looking in the drawer before the cabinet may transfer across bodies, but only if it remains appropriate for the new robot’s capabilities, access constraints, and observations.


#### 2.2 Agentic Loop: From Intent to Verified Outcome

> Figure 5: The agentic loop. Top: one cycle comprises six steps. S2 interprets intent, reasons about goals and constraints to decide how to proceed, and invokes an S1 skill, an S0 interface, or an external tool as needed. The invoked capability executes under its contract. S2 then observes and verifies the outcome by answering four questions (whether the request was accepted, whether execution terminated, whether the physical effect holds, and whether the user’s intent is satisfied). Based on the verification, it continues, recovers, asks, or reports. The real-time event monitor, which watches the user, the body, and the scene, can interrupt S2 at any point. Bottom: the same steps on a schematic time axis, labeled with their step numbers and colors. S2 deliberates on demand, S1 runs while a tool executes, and S0 runs continuously; after an interruption, S2 cancels the running tool call and replans while S0 keeps the body stable; the hatched segment marks the canceled execution.

Consider the request, “Find the red cup and bring it to the dining table.” Given this request, S2 interprets it and plans four subtasks: locate, grasp, carry, and place the cup. It first invokes perception, and the harness records the cup’s identity, location, and observation time. Then S2 invokes a grasp skill and checks the returned observation to make sure that the cup is held securely for transport. During transport, S1 and S0 maintain control while new observations inform S2’s subsequent decisions. After placement, S2 verifies that the cup is supported by the table at the requested location. Each stage forwards state to the next, and the harness distinguishes a past event, “the cup was grasped”, from a current state, “the cup is still held”.

Suppose instead that the user interjects while the robot is grasping the red cup: “Not the red cup, the blue one.” The event monitor detects the interjection and interrupts S2. S2 recognizes that the revised goal conflicts with the current action and requests that the grasp be paused or canceled. A cancellation request alone is insufficient: before dispatching a conflicting action, the harness must confirm that execution has stopped and that the body is in a suitable holding state. S2 then refreshes its observations and replans to retrieve the blue cup. Sometimes a skill reports success even though the action did not succeed. In this case, verification also catches unreported failures. For example, the grasping skill reports success on the blue cup, but the wrist camera shows that the cup is still on the shelf: the grasp was executed as commanded but missed. S2 attributes the miss to a grasp point that was occluded by the cup’s handle. It therefore observes the cup from another side and retries with a different grasp rather than repeating the same call.

The example above traces six logical steps, which form a feedback cycle.

- Step 1: Interpret Intent. The user expresses intent, which may be explicit (“put the mugs in the dishwasher”) or implicit in preferences and context. S2 combines the request with the context assembled by the harness, such as a known preference that determines where a cup belongs. When the intent or the evidence is insufficient, a valid decision should be asking the user or observing again.

- Step 2: Decide. Given the current context and memory, S2 reasons about goals and constraints to decide how to proceed. It may select a subgoal or capability, specify a permitted control target, or supply a program.

- Step 3: Invoke. S2 calls an S1 skill, a permitted S0 interface, or an external tool, with arguments that respect the tool’s contract, such as reference frame, units, object identifiers, and preconditions.

- Step 4: Execute. The invoked skill or tool carries out the call under its execution contract. Local control continues while S2 deliberates; a skill proceeds until it finishes, is paused or canceled, or a safety condition requires intervention. The event monitor continues to watch the user, the body, and the scene.

- Step 5: Verify. S2 distinguishes four questions when assessing the outcome: was the request accepted , has the execution terminated , is there evidence that the intended physical effect holds, and does that effect satisfy the user’s intent ? Termination may indicate completion, failure, or cancellation; it does not by itself establish success. A successful tool return is only intermediate evidence: a grasp can be executed exactly as commanded and still miss the object.

- Step 6: Continue, Replan, Ask, or Report. Completion is judged against the user’s intent. If the task has not completed, S2 proceeds to the next subgoal or replans after a failure. If the task is complete, S2 reports; if the evidence is inconclusive, it observes again or asks the user.

Across the whole system, three properties should hold.

The components typically operate on different timescales. S0 typically runs locally at roughly 50 Hz to 1 kHz ( Luo et al., 2026c ; Dyna Robotics, 2026b ; Figure AI, 2026a ) . S1 control skills run continuously while a subtask executes, at rates that depend on the platform and the task. Some commercial stacks report rates from about 5 Hz to 200 Hz ( Dyna Robotics, 2026b ; Figure AI, 2025b ; NVIDIA et al., 2025c ) . By contrast, perception and motion planning modules are invoked on demand and return results after their computation finishes. S2 does not run at a fixed rate: it could be invoked when a skill finishes or fails, when new observations arrive, when the event monitor reports an event, or when the user speaks. A frontier model in this role may take from several seconds to tens of seconds per decision ( Xu et al., 2026d ; Galbot Team et al., 2026 ) , although this latency is an implementation property, not a defining feature of S2.

While S2 is deliberating or a skill is executing, the physical world or the body state may change, and the user may revise an instruction. The event monitor should watch for these changes and remain responsive throughout deliberation and execution. Current systems achieve this responsiveness in different ways. Gemini Robotics-ER 2 treats a new prompt received during generation as an interruption of model reasoning and of pending tool calls ( Google, 2026 ) . MIRA combines streaming speech understanding with interruptible motion ( Lin et al., 2026 ) . ProAgent pairs a fast model that decides whether and when to intervene with a slower model that decides what to do ( Yang et al., 2026g ) . In our architecture, the safety rules reside in the event monitor, so a protective stop can be requested in time.

The harness should keep a persistent state. We call one task an episode and a continuous period of use a session , which may contain many episodes. Within an episode, the harness tracks progress, object identities, and previous attempts. Across episodes and sessions, it maintains what the robot has learned about the environment, the user’s preferences, and the body state. The record of the physical world must be reconstructed from observations and could become stale without notice, as when an object is moved. Persistent memory should therefore record when and how each item was obtained and allow revision, rather than treat a stored observation as a permanent fact.


### 3 Embodied System 2: Foundation of Agentic Robotics

The previous section described how S2 interacts with S1 and S0. In this section, we examine S2 itself. We first define S2 and give a criterion for identifying it in a robotic system. We then examine how the foundation model and embodied harness contribute to its eight capabilities and how physical constraints shape its operation.


#### 3.1 Definition

We formally define Embodied System 2 as follows.

In this paper, Embodied S2 refers specifically to an implementation in which a foundation model and an embodied harness jointly fulfill that role. Hereafter, we use Embodied S2 and S2 interchangeably and reserve S2 foundation model for the model inside Embodied S2 alone.

The above definition has three notable features. First, it is anchored in intent rather than in a fixed task specification: the objective is a world state that satisfies the user. Reaching it may require clarifying an ambiguous request, inferring an unstated preference, or accommodating a change of mind. Second, it rests on responsibility : S2 makes high-level decisions, whereas S1 and S0 make action-level decisions within the scope that S2 delegates to them. Third, it includes the harness , because the state on which S2’s decisions depend, namely that of the world, the user, and the body, is usually not supplied by the environment directly. This state must be assembled by the harness from partial, noisy, and aging observations.

Labels such as System 2, embodied brain, planner, and orchestrator are applied inconsistently, so we identify S2 by functionality, not by name. A component, or a set of components, acts as S2 if and only if it closes the loop of the definition, which requires two kinds of decision:

- (i) decisions on how to proceed : interpreting the intent and, given the goals and constraints, either choosing the next subgoal and the skill or tool that should achieve it, together with the arguments of the call, or choosing to continue, retry, replan, ask, or stop; and

- (ii) decisions on outcomes : judging from evidence whether the previous step, or the task as a whole, achieved its goal, so that the next decision can adapt accordingly.

Both kinds of decision must also be identifiable from outside : either the component’s output interface exposes them, or a controlled replacement experiment reveals them. A component that makes only one kind of decision implements part of S2 but does not act as S2 on its own. For example, a planner that chooses and sequences skills without checking their effects orchestrates but never verifies. By contrast, a success detector makes neither kind of decision: it scores an observation, but whether that score settles the outcome of a step or of the task, and what follows from it, is decided elsewhere. State estimators in S0, perception modules in S1, world models, and the real-time event monitor likewise supply evidence without deciding what it means for the task: they inform S2 but do not act as S2. Because the criterion concerns function, it applies to any implementation, including classical planners and behavior trees.

In dual-system VLAs whose upper module emits only a latent representation, such as Helix ( Figure AI, 2025b ) and GR00T N1 ( NVIDIA et al., 2025c ) , high-level decisions cannot be read from the interface. We therefore do not classify the upper module as S2 unless replacement experiments show otherwise. Conversely, a model that first emits a subtask and then the actions to achieve it, such as $\pi_{0.5}$ ( Black et al., 2025a ) , or G0.5 with its chain-of-thought stream enabled ( Liu et al., 2026e ) , makes an identifiable decision on how to proceed. Its subtask stage therefore implements part of S2, even though it shares weights with S1. As the authors of Hi Robot note, “the separation of roles at the model level is not fundamental to this design” ( Shi et al., 2025 ) .

Decision frequency is not part of the criterion. A model trained for the S2 role may decide about once per second ( Shi et al., 2025 ) , whereas a frontier model queried over a network may take tens of seconds per decision ( Galbot Team et al., 2026 ) . Either acts as S2 if it makes both kinds of decision. Speed determines whether an S2 is useful in a given setting, so we treat it as a physical constraint rather than as part of the definition. Nor is the granularity of the outputs part of the criterion. A component that emits end-effector targets or programs acts as S2 only if it also makes both kinds of decision, and an S2 that orchestrates skills remains S2 when it emits a pose directly (Figure 4 ).

Our definition builds on recent proposals that describe S2 as the embodied “brain” that selects skills and checks their effects ( Luo et al., 2026b ; Wei et al., 2026a ) , and on work that distinguishes a model from the runtime around it ( Liang et al., 2026b ; Huang et al., 2026c ; Wang et al., 2026e ) . Our definition differs from these works in two respects: it provides an explicit, testable criterion, and it counts the harness as part of Embodied S2.


#### 3.2 S2 Foundation Model

The S2 foundation model turns the context assembled by the harness into decisions. It may be a general frontier model used without embodied training, an embodied foundation model post-trained for embodied tasks, or the reasoning stage of a larger model that also produces actions. In each case, it inherits broad knowledge and reasoning from large-scale pretraining. However, its role differs from that of a chatbot or coding agent, since its decisions are carried out by a body in the physical world.

Doing the work that the definition assigns to S2 in the physical world requires eight abilities (Figure 6 ). Knowledge, understanding, and reasoning reside mainly in the foundation model; tool use, memory, cross-embodiment, safety, and efficiency arise from the foundation model and the embodied harness together. For each ability, we describe what it is, why S2 needs it, and how the foundation model’s share of it can be cultivated.

> Figure 6: Core abilities of Embodied S2. These abilities are necessary for Embodied S2 to perform successfully and reliably in the physical world; some reside in the foundation model, while others emerge from its integration with the embodied harness.

Knowledge comprises the general facts, concepts, and procedures on which S2 draws. It matters because S2 must fill the gaps that a request leaves open, such as where a dish belongs or that a heavy box goes below a light one. World knowledge covers objects, everyday relations, and culture. Embodied knowledge covers materials, physical laws, tools and mechanisms, the preconditions and effects of actions, and the structure, sensing, and kinematics of robots. Programming knowledge covers languages, algorithms, and software interfaces, which empowers an S2 model to compose skills with code ( Liang et al., 2023 ) . Knowledge requires broad coverage, which only pretraining-scale data provides. World and programming knowledge are therefore inherited largely from pretraining on web text, images, and code. Embodied knowledge is sparser in these sources but needs the same breadth. It should therefore also be acquired during pretraining, by adding embodied data to the corpus at scale rather than leaving it to the smaller and narrower data of post-training. Suitable sources include egocentric and instructional videos that show how people handle objects and what their actions achieve, and robot trajectories paired with language, which tie actions to their preconditions and effects. For example, Magma includes human videos and robot data in its pretraining ( Yang et al., 2025a ) . Others are technical documentation, such as robot specifications, kinematic models, and controller interfaces, and simulated data on physical properties and dynamics, such as mass, friction, stability, and deformation.

Understanding is S2’s grasp of the current situation: the state of the world, what the user wants, the state of the robot, and what cannot be inferred from the available observations. Understanding of the robot extends beyond its body to the capabilities beneath S2: which S1 skills exist, what their preconditions and failure modes are, and which S0 interfaces, reference frames, and limits apply. It differs from embodied knowledge: knowing how inverse kinematics works is knowledge, whereas judging whether this robot, in its current state, can reach a given pose is understanding. Understanding matters because every decision rests on it: an understanding error could propagate into planning and execution. Understanding of the robot lets S2 choose the level at which to act: whether to orchestrate a skill, control S0 directly, ask, or decline. Understanding of progress and completion, in turn, makes verification possible.

Reasoning is how S2 decides what to do next and revises that decision as evidence arrives. It encompasses inferring the intent behind an underspecified request, deciding when to ask for clarification, planning over long horizons, diagnosing failures, and handling interruptions. Reasoning is therefore central to S2’s role. Two of these sub-abilities are particularly relevant to the execution paths in Figure 4 : skill selection and orchestration , which decides which existing skills and tools to use and in what order (the layered-orchestration path), and executable action inference , which decides which action target to send directly when no suitable skill exists, without placing the model inside the real-time control loop (the direct-control path). Over a long task, diagnosis and recovery further limit the compounding of small failure rates (Section 2.2 ): S2 should change the conditions that caused a failure, such as the grasp point after a slip, rather than repeat the same call. Diagnosis can even drive learning without updating the model: by tracing each failure to its originating step, GPT-6 Astra learned manipulation skills on a real arm without demonstrations ( Yu et al., 2026b ) .

Tool use is the ability to choose, parameterize, and combine the capabilities available to S2, namely perception, planning, action, and code, and to handle their failures. Each call involves three decisions: whether a call is needed at all; which arguments, in which reference frame and units, denote the intended physical target; and how far to rely on the result. Tool use matters because S2 acts on the world through calls. A sound decision executed with the wrong arguments still fails in execution, and sometimes a result can promise more than it delivers: a successful return from an inverse-kinematics solver does not guarantee that the robot can reach the solution without collision.

Memory is the ability to retain what the current observation no longer shows, to use it in a later decision, and to replace it when it becomes stale. A robot must remember objects and their attributes, locations, the order of completed steps, procedures demonstrated earlier, world state revealed by interaction, and the preferences of the people it serves. Memory matters because physical work extends beyond what a camera sees at any moment and acting on an outdated memory can cause failures ( Liang et al., 2026a ) . The model’s role is to decide what is worth recording and whether a memory still applies to the present situation; storing and retrieving information beyond the context window is the harness’s role. The model learns these judgments from tasks in which earlier observations must be recalled and revised as the world changes.

Cross-embodiment is the ability to achieve a given objective when the body changes in appearance, morphology, end effector, action space, or dynamics. The same goal may require different paths or motions on different platforms. For example, a wheeled base may need a longer route around obstacles that a legged robot can cross. It matters because the value of a general S2 lies in serving many bodies without being rebuilt for each. To serve a new body, the model must understand it, adapt the plans and action parameters to it, and reuse experience gained on other bodies without letting that experience interfere.

Safety is the ability to avoid harm along the entire action chain: recognizing a risk, predicting its consequences, choosing a cautious response, preventing harm before acting, monitoring during execution, recovering afterward, responding in time, and coordinating a stop. Specifically, risk recognition identifies hazards in the scene and in the request, such as hot liquid near a child’s hand or a knife handed over blade first. Consequence prediction anticipates what an action would do in this particular scene. For example, pulling one box from a stack may topple the rest. A prudent decision chooses whether to proceed, proceed more cautiously, gather information, ask, or refuse, within the robot’s validated capability limits, and prefers reversible or information-gathering actions under high uncertainty. Prevention before action checks a planned call against these predictions before it is dispatched. During execution, S2 monitors whether the situation still matches its assumptions and replans when conditions change–for instance, when a person enters the workspace. After a failure, safe recovery first returns the robot and the scene to a safe state and only then retries. A stop decided by S2 must be coordinated with S1 and S0 so that the running skill halts in a safe state. Safety is thus a property of decisions in the action loop rather than of answers about hazards. The model does not carry this responsibility alone. Hard limits on force and speed, protective stops, and permissions are enforced by the harness, S1, and S0 below the model, so that safety never rests on the model’s judgment only.

Efficiency is the ability to make a good decision with low latency and with few tokens per decision. It matters because the physical world does not wait. A decision that arrives after the situation has changed is useless or even dangerous, and every token adds both latency and cost, which accumulate over hours of operation. Two ways to improve decision efficiency are faster decoding and distilling capable models into smaller ones that run on or near the robot.

A growing family of embodied foundation models, often called embodied reasoning models, develops the abilities described above by post-training a general vision-language backbone on embodied data. Examples include Gemini Robotics-ER ( Gemini Robotics Team, 2025a ; Gemini Robotics Team, 2025b ) , Cosmos-Reason1 ( NVIDIA et al., 2025b ) , Embodied-R1 and R1.5 ( Yuan et al., 2026c ; Yuan et al., 2026d ) , RynnBrain ( Dang et al., 2026 ; Li et al., 2026b ) , MiMo-Embodied ( Hao et al., 2025 ) , HY-Embodied ( Tencent Robotics X et al., 2026 ) , Vesta ( Bjorck et al., 2026 ) , ACE-Brain ( ACE-Brain Team et al., 2026 ) , and PhysBrain ( DeepCybo Team et al., 2026 ) . Whatever model is chosen, it decides from the context it is given and acts through the interfaces it is offered. Both are provided by the embodied harness, which we examine next.


#### 3.3 Embodied Harness

Section 2.1 introduced the embodied harness through its five core parts; here we define its boundary. The harness comprises the components outside the foundation model that assemble the context for S2 and execute the decisions from S2. A recent survey similarly describes the harness as the “runtime control plane” that grounds observations, coordinates executable capabilities, monitors outcomes, and manages intervention when execution diverges from the plan ( Huang et al., 2026c ) . A context manager assembles the model’s current context, turning a heterogeneous, continuously changing physical situation into a bounded and structured input for the model. This context should cover at least three kinds of state. World context records objects, places, and their relations, together with when and how each fact was observed. User context records preferences, habits, and commitments; it changes at a different timescale and raises privacy questions that world context does not. Body context records the robot’s geometry, kinematics, calibration, and current condition, together with descriptions of the S1 skills and S0 interfaces available to it. The model’s understanding of the situation rests on these records. When the body changes, an explicit description of the new body, with its frames, kinematics, sensors, tools, and validated limits, lets S2 plan for a robot that it has not used before.

The entire interaction history of an agentic robot cannot simply reside in the model’s context window due to limited capacity. For instance, a 128K-token context can hold only roughly three minutes of dense multimodal memory ( Gopalakrishnan and Labenz, 2026 ) . Within an episode, long context can nonetheless substitute for engineered memory, at a high cost in latency: a frozen GPT-6 Astra that kept an entire episode in context succeeded in 92.1% of the 800 test episodes of RoboMME ( Dai et al., 2026 ) in a single run, averaging 18.3 s per model call with the simulator paused during each call ( Jedlicki et al., 2026 ) . This result shows that in-context memory can be viable within an episode, but its latency in this setting makes it unattractive as the sole memory mechanism for time-sensitive tasks. Across sessions, therefore, a memory module must store, retrieve, update, and invalidate information beyond the context window. Several harnesses make this module explicit: Thea maintains a persistent scene graph ( Wang et al., 2026e ) , HarnessVLN combines hierarchical event memory with a persistent spatiotemporal graph ( Chen et al., 2026c ) , and KnowBody maintains a revisable model of action-relevant body relations ( Lou et al., 2026 ) .

Since physical actions have side effects, the harness must know precisely what each call promises. Its tool interface exposes every tool with a schema, units, reference frames, and failure semantics, so that the model’s choices of tool and arguments refer to well-defined physical quantities. Recent systems point to four contracts ( Wang et al., 2026c ; Deng et al., 2026 ; Huang et al., 2026c ) . An observation snapshot records when, where, and in which reference frame each observation was captured, and which calibration was in force. A capability contract specifies a skill’s inputs and units, preconditions, expected effects, resources, time limits, behavior on cancellation, and the state it leaves for the next skill. An invocation record links each call to its task, subgoal, attempt, inputs, and final status, so that retries and late results can be traced. An evidence bundle records the observations, controller states, and verifier outputs on which a judgment of success was based. Together, the contracts define a minimal lifecycle for every physical call: propose $\rightarrow$ admit $\rightarrow$ dispatch $\rightarrow$ execute $\rightarrow$ confirm the terminal state $\rightarrow$ verify the effect $\rightarrow$ commit progress . The lifecycle has explicit branches for rejection, failure, cancellation, and unknown outcomes. The branch for unknown outcomes is necessary since a timeout is not evidence that an action did not run. Before retrying, the harness should query the execution handle and observe the scene again; repeating a command that has in fact completed can execute a physical action twice. Cancellation passes through three distinct states as well: requested, accepted, and completed; the return of a cancel call does not show that the robot has stopped. Two systems illustrate this discipline: RegenHarness separates a model’s proposal from a controller’s termination and verified completion ( Wang et al., 2026c ) , and DynaHarness records evidence under a shared physical execution contract ( Deng et al., 2026 ) . Skill boundaries require the same care, because a grasp can succeed in a way that obstructs the subsequent insertion. Handoffs can be made more reliable in two ways: by specifying explicit entry and exit conditions between skills ( Xu et al., 2026c ) , and by restricting handoffs to configurations that the next policy is known to support ( Huang et al., 2026b ) .

Safety checks are required before execution. A dedicated safety gate is one possible implementation; the checks may instead be integrated into the tool interface or enforced by an independent execution layer. This check is the admit step of the lifecycle above. Hard limits on force and speed are enforced below the harness, by S1 and S0, and the real-time event monitor can trigger a protective stop in S0 according to predefined safety rules. A stop thus takes effect even if the model or the harness fails.

A harness supports three feedback loops. The sensorimotor loop determines how actions respond to physical state; it resides in S1 and S0. The planning loop determines how the next subgoal is chosen after each result; it is the main loop of S2. The evolution loop determines which capabilities the next run will use, such as skills, prompts, context rules, tools, or model weights revised from experience. A frozen model can still improve through the evolution loop, and a fixed program can still react through the sensorimotor loop. Conflating the three therefore obscures where a reaction or an improvement originates. Because the harness consists of code and data, S2 can also modify it: several recent self-improving robot agents revise tools, skills, context rules, or recovery policies rather than model weights ( Tian et al., 2026 ; Deng et al., 2026 ; Lee et al., 2026a ; Wang et al., 2026c ; Lee et al., 2026b ) . Such changes require the same discipline as model training, namely held-out tasks, regression checks, and versioned rollback.

Many hard problems reside in the harness: multi-rate scheduling, cancellation that actually stops motion, consistent state across asynchronous components, and accountable records of what the robot did and why. Existing robotics infrastructure provides many building blocks for these problems, including action protocols with feedback and cancellation, middleware for real-time data flow ( Macenski et al., 2022 ) , and behavior trees with bounded recovery ( Colledanchise and Ögren, 2018 ) . Runtime governance layers add policy checks, capability admission, monitoring, rollback, and human override around the agent ( Qin et al., 2026b ; Liu et al., 2026d ) . The harness also carries the system’s share of efficiency: it calls the model only when a decision is needed, compiles stable sub-processes into programs that run without the model, and delegates fast loops to S1 and S0. What remains is to connect these mechanisms to a model and to measure what each mechanism contributes.

Existing measurements already show that the harness substantially influences overall performance. For example, interfaces that let the model check and retry locally outperformed plain tool calling while using fewer model calls ( Si et al., 2026 ) . Verification can be decisive: in one ablation, with the model fixed, removing HumanCLAW’s verifier nearly eliminated navigation success ( Siyao et al., 2026 ) . The best level of abstraction for the interface also depends on the model: a skill-only interface raised GPT-5.6-Sol from 11% to 63% but lowered GPT-6 Astra from 92% to 61%; letting the model decide when to use skills recovered most of the loss ( Han et al., 2026 ) .


#### 3.4 Constraints Imposed by the Physical World

Unlike digital environments, the physical world involves embodiment, continuous dynamics, and irreversible consequences (Section 5 ). Observations are partial and noisy, actions are delayed and constrained by actuator limits, and contact-rich interactions are governed by friction, compliance, and wear that are difficult to model exactly. Whatever model fills the S2 role, the physical world constrains how it may decide: decisions face deadlines, can be interrupted at any moment, must fit the robot’s compute and cost budgets, and rest on aging, partial observations. Answer accuracy captures none of these constraints, which the following paragraphs discuss in turn.

> Figure 7: Capped scaling. Frontier models scale monotonically: greater capability comes with longer latency. Embodied S2 must instead become more capable within a fixed decision deadline. A decision that arrives after the deadline is usually too late, even if it is correct: the situation has already changed, so acting on it could fail the task or create a hazard. Note that the curves are schematic: they illustrate the qualitative relationship, not measured data.

For frontier models, capability has grown with the computation: more reasoning tokens generally yield better answers at the cost of longer latency ( OpenAI, 2024 ) . We call this regime monotonic scaling . A robot cannot follow it since the physical world imposes deadlines. A decision is useful primarily when it arrives before the situation that it addresses has changed, for instance before an object rolls off the table or a grasp slips. Embodied S2 therefore requires a different regime, which we call capped scaling : becoming more capable within a fixed decision deadline (Figure 7 ). What matters is not how long the model deliberates but how well the system decides within the time available. Humans meet physical deadlines through layered control ( Todorov, 2004 ) : grip-force corrections begin about 74 ms after an object starts to slip ( Johansson and Westling, 1987 ) , and the reflex responses that support recovery from a trip begin 60 to 140 ms after it ( Eng et al., 1994 ) . Deliberation handles only what cannot be handled faster. An S2 should likewise match its reasoning effort to the time available and delegate fast loops to S1 and S0. For the model, capped scaling means gaining capability within a fixed budget of tokens and time rather than by generating more tokens.

In terms of the S2 foundation model, capped scaling calls for concise reasoning . Much of the reasoning that people perform when acting physically is never verbalized, such as where to put a heavy box when packing or how to approach a fragile glass. We believe that such reasoning is under-represented in the text and chain-of-thought data on which frontier models are trained, and that it does not require thousands of tokens. An S2 should therefore reason briefly and in grounded terms, using points, regions, relations, and short plans rather than long verbal deliberation. Concise reasoning helps meet deadlines; whether it also improves correctness on physical tasks remains an open question.

S0 loops run at about 50 Hz to 1 kHz, that is, every 1 to 20 ms ( Luo et al., 2026c ; Figure AI, 2026a ) . The control skills of S1 run at about 5 to 200 Hz, depending on the platform. The Dyna-2 policy updates its whole-body targets at about 5 Hz ( Dyna Robotics, 2026b ) , $\pi_{0.5}$ produces actions at 50 Hz ( Black et al., 2025a ) , and Helix’s S1 runs at 200 Hz ( Figure AI, 2025b ; Figure AI, 2026a ) . For $\pi_{0.5}$ , a median inference time of about 47 ms has been reported (as quoted in Yang et al., 2026d ). The perception and motion planning modules of S1 have no fixed rate, because S2 invokes them on demand. Used directly as the S2 foundation model, GPT-6 Astra took roughly 40 to 8,000 times as long per decision as S1 control skills take per update (Figure 8 ). When issuing actions directly, it took 18.56 s per inference call ( Yang et al., 2026d ) ; when generating locomotion references, it took 39.86 s per call on average, and a 30-second walk required 250 calls with a paused simulator ( Galbot Team et al., 2026 ) . When the world does not pause, latency becomes a safety variable. RT-Safe ( Xu et al., 2026d ) , a simulated urban navigation benchmark, keeps running while agents deliberate. On its hardest routes, Astra at its default reasoning effort took 7.7 s per decision, and models with longer response latency collided more often.

Language models typically complete a response before reading the next input. However, an S2 must be interactive: when the user says “stop” or the scene changes, it must abandon or revise its current line of reasoning. This requires a model that accepts new input during generation and resumes from partial state, and a harness that cancels in-flight tool calls. At least one commercial interface already supports such interruption: the streaming preview endpoint of Gemini Robotics-ER 2 cancels in-flight reasoning and tool calls when the user interjects ( Google, 2026 ) . Real-time chunking addresses a related problem in S1 by generating the next action chunk while the current one executes ( Black et al., 2025c ) .

Robots often operate with limited connectivity, and a single task may involve hundreds of decisions. Therefore S2 must run within the robot’s compute and power budget or tolerate network latency and outages. The cost per task should be acceptable as well. Several open embodied foundation models intended for the S2 role have 2B to 30B parameters, and some include variants designed for edge deployment ( Tencent Robotics X et al., 2026 ; Dang et al., 2026 ; Yuan et al., 2026d ) . Such smaller models are attractive in part because frontier models can be expensive in agentic settings: on one simulated benchmark, an agentic Astra policy incurred nearly 19 times the API fees of direct prompting ( Yang et al., 2026d ) .

While S2 deliberates, S1 and S0 keep acting and the world keeps changing, so the observation behind a decision has aged by the time the decision takes effect. S2 must therefore reason about the age of its information, request fresh observations when necessary, and express decisions in forms that remain valid under small changes. For instance, it should issue “place the cup on the shelf” rather than a fixed joint configuration. Partial observability compounds the problem: S2 must distinguish what it observes, what it infers, and what it does not know, and act on this uncertainty by looking again, asking, or abstaining.

These constraints do not imply that frontier models cannot serve as S2 foundation models; used with a harness, GPT-6 Astra already matches or exceeds trained policies on several simulated benchmarks (Section 4.3 ). Instead, the constraints imply that S2 must be judged not only by answer accuracy but also by the quality of its decisions under the deadlines, interruptions, and budgets that the physical world imposes. This section has examined three aspects of Embodied S2: the foundation model’s share of its abilities, the harness that supplies the rest, and the physical constraints on both. An evaluation must address all three: it must test the model on its own and the complete system in closed loop, and run the closed-loop tests on the world’s clock rather than with the world paused. The next section develops such an evaluation framework.


### 4 Evaluation Framework

Two months after the release of GPT-6 Astra ( OpenAI, 2026a ) , results in agentic robotics have become difficult to interpret. On RoboDojo alone, success rates reported for Astra across all 42 tasks range from 22% to 85%, depending on harness and protocol ( Zhang et al., 2026c ; SenseNova et al., 2026 ; Gao et al., 2026a ) . The difficulty has three sources. First, evaluations often do not state whether they measure a foundation model or a complete system. Second, they rarely disclose the full protocol, including observations, budgets, and demonstrations. Third, many pause the world while the model deliberates. This section asks how Embodied S2, a foundation model acting through an embodied harness, should be evaluated. It addresses what to evaluate, along the nine dimensions of Figure 9 ; how to evaluate; what current results show; and where current evaluation falls short.

#### 4.1 What to Evaluate

We evaluate Embodied S2 at two levels. At the model level , the foundation model answers on its own, typically in a single turn without tools; these tests isolate knowledge, understanding, and the model-side components of reasoning and safety. At the system level , the model acts through a harness and, where needed, invokes S1 skills and S0 interfaces; these tests measure closed-loop reasoning, tool use, memory, cross-embodiment transfer, safe execution, efficiency, and cost. The two levels answer different questions and provide complementary evidence about failure. A failure at the model level points to the model, whereas a failure that appears only at the system level points to the model, the harness, the interfaces, or their interaction. Comparing model-level and system-level results can help narrow down possible causes of failures. For example, replaying the observations and skill feedback that were available to the model at the point of failure can reveal whether the model would make the same decision again under controlled conditions. Within system-level evaluation, it also matters whether the harness is fixed across the models compared, supplied by each model’s developer, or itself the object of study, because harness changes alone can move success a lot.

Across these two levels, Figure 9 organizes the evaluation into nine dimensions with 53 sub-dimensions: the eight abilities of Embodied S2 (Figure 6 ) and cost, which is not an ability but a measure of the resources the system consumes. The 29 embodiment-related sub-dimensions are specific to embodiment or depend strongly on it, and standard evaluations of general language models rarely cover them. The reasoning budget appears under efficiency rather than reasoning because it is a variable to be controlled and reported, not an ability in itself. Fulfillment of user intent is not a separate dimension but the end-to-end outcome that all dimensions serve. The dimensions play different roles and are not orthogonal. Knowledge, understanding, and reasoning describe what the foundation model can do. Tool use, memory, and cross-embodiment describe what the system achieves through its harness and across bodies. Safety constrains all of them. Efficiency describes how quickly and with how few tokens the system decides, and cost the resources it consumes. Results should therefore be reported as a profile across dimensions rather than summed into a single score.

Knowledge tests probe the model’s facts, concepts, and procedures through self-contained, single-turn questions without tools. World knowledge spans objects and everyday relations, life science, matter, the Earth and the universe, and geography, history, and culture; CommonsenseQA ( Talmor et al., 2019 ) is a candidate source. Embodied knowledge spans materials and physical properties, basic physical laws, object functions, tools and mechanical mechanisms, action preconditions and effects, robot structure and actuation, sensing and feedback, and coordinate systems and kinematic fundamentals. PIQA, NEWTON, and RoboCSKBench ( Bisk et al., 2020 ; Wang et al., 2023 ; Töberg et al., 2025 ) cover parts of it. Programming knowledge spans language semantics, data structures and algorithms, software interfaces, and the writing of short functions from a specification. CodeMMLU ( Nguyen et al., 2025 ) tests the first three, and the EvalPlus versions of HumanEval and MBPP ( Liu et al., 2023c ) test the last. In HumanEval and MBPP, the model completes a small, self-contained function from its signature and description, and unit tests score the result. On a robot, the same skill produces helpers such as a function that converts a detected bounding box into a grasp point. Results should identify the categories tested and the scoring protocol. General physical-commonsense scores do not establish coverage of robot structure, sensing, or kinematics, which require explicit probes. Offline scores also characterize knowledge only under the chosen protocol; closed-loop competence requires a separate system-level evaluation.

Understanding tests ask whether an S2 foundation model can extract an accurate, task-relevant representation of the world, the user’s goal, and the robot’s own state from available observations. Unlike knowledge tests, they concern a particular physical situation. They cover multimodal perception, spatial and physical state estimation, affordance recognition, temporal understanding, task interpretation, recognition of human behavior, awareness of the robot’s body and of the skills and interfaces available to it, progress estimation, and uncertainty assessment. Table 2 lists these abilities with an evaluation target and candidate benchmarks for each. Two of them deserve particular weight because they matter for closed-loop control and are weak in current models. Understanding of the robot’s own body and capabilities tells S2 whether to delegate to a skill or to act directly. Understanding of task progress and completion makes verification possible, yet success detectors are biased toward reporting success ( Navasardyan et al., 2026 ) . Static question answering also misses the temporal side of understanding: because observations age while S2 deliberates, a model must distinguish what it observed, and when, from what still holds now. Finally, tests must respect the boundary with reasoning: understanding determines what is happening and what can be inferred , whereas reasoning determines what should be done next . For example, recognizing that an object has slipped from the gripper tests understanding; identifying the cause and selecting a corrective action tests reasoning.

Evaluating understanding therefore requires separating state inference from downstream decision-making and execution. At the model level, tests should specify the observations, task instructions, robot-state information, and interaction history available to the model. Such specification matters most for progress estimation and uncertainty assessment, where the correct answer may depend on earlier observations or on information that remains unobservable. At the system level, task outcomes provide complementary evidence, but they cannot by themselves attribute failures to understanding, because planning, tool use, and execution may also contribute.

Together, these tests characterize the information quality on which S2 decisions depend. Whether the agent can use that information to select effective actions is the subject of reasoning evaluation.

Reasoning tests ask whether the S2 foundation model can select and revise decisions that advance the user’s goal, given its understanding of the current state, the robot’s capabilities, and the constraints of the physical environment. They cover intent inference, causal and counterfactual prediction, hierarchical planning, spatial exploration, skill selection and orchestration, executable action inference, active perception and intervention decisions, failure recovery, and handling of interruptions and rescheduling. Executable action inference, which corresponds to the direct-control path of Figure 4 , needs a precise evaluation target. We treat it as a reasoning ability rather than as a separate policy dimension. The target is therefore whether the model produces physically executable, goal-consistent action decisions, not whether it can reliably control a robot as a complete policy. Reliable control also depends on continuous closed-loop operation, latency, controllers, and the harness, so it belongs to system-level evaluation.

Distinguishing reasoning competence from execution performance requires tests at both levels. At the model level, controlled decision-making tests assess whether the model selects feasible, goal-consistent actions under specified observations, capabilities, and constraints, without physical execution. At the system level, closed-loop tests additionally assess whether its decisions remain effective when actions are executed and their outcomes are observed. The distinction matters because task success depends not only on decision quality but also on the harness, tools, controllers, and physical environment. Evaluation gaps. Existing benchmarks provide useful measurements of reasoning-related capabilities, but several do not isolate the underlying decision. For instance, successful navigation does not necessarily establish effective exploration planning, and successful tool-based execution does not isolate skill selection and orchestration. Likewise, task success alone cannot show that one model generates better actions than another, because inverse kinematics, controllers, the action interface, and the observation contract also affect success. A more direct protocol would hold the state representation, available capabilities, and execution constraints fixed while scoring candidate decisions before execution. For failure recovery, existing benchmarks score detection, diagnosis, and correction guidance offline ( Zeng et al., 2026a ) ; controlled failure injection could measure the same steps during execution, together with recovery success and the additional time or actions required. Tool use should be evaluated with the experimental setup held fixed: the same robot, sensors, controllers, and scene configuration, prepared in advance for every model compared. The unit of an ablation is the actual callable interface rather than a tool family, because a single grasping API may bundle perception, planning, and execution. Three kinds of experiment answer different questions and should not be conflated. Freezing the model and varying the tools measures the gain from provisioning. Freezing the tools and varying the model measures how well models use them. Freezing both and varying prompts, memory, or feedback measures the harness’s effect.

The tools themselves fall into four types, each with its own evaluation target. Perception tools identify and segment targets, estimate spatial geometry, propose candidate grasps, and report contact; examples are SAM 3 ( Carion et al., 2025 ) , RGB-D projection and multi-view triangulation, Contact-GraspNet ( Sundermeyer et al., 2021 ) , and contact queries. Evaluation checks whether the model requests the estimate that the decision needs and uses the returned geometry in the correct reference frame. Motion-planning tools, such as planning backed by cuRobo ( Sundaralingam et al., 2023 ) and waypoint execution, turn target poses or waypoints into trajectories within kinematic limits and modeled collision constraints. Evaluation checks whether the model supplies feasible targets and interprets planning failures. Action tools include learned-policy calls and direct-control commands. Learned-policy calls delegate a subtask to a VLA or WAM, such as $\pi_{0.5}$ ( Black et al., 2025a ) , GR00T ( NVIDIA et al., 2025c ) , or Cosmos3-Nano-Policy ( NVIDIA, 2026 ) . Evaluation checks whether the model delegates suitable subtasks and monitors, accepts, corrects, or overrides the resulting actions where the interface allows. Direct-control commands for the end effector, the joints, and the gripper leave the target, the sequence, and the timing of observation to the model. Evaluation checks whether these commands respect the body’s units, frames, and limits. Code tools allow the model to compose perception, planning, and action calls into executable programs, as in Code as Policies ( Liang et al., 2023 ) . Evaluation checks whether the generated code uses the available APIs correctly, handles feedback and failures, and terminates appropriately. The underlying planners and controllers may be shared across types, so a test must name the interface that the agent actually called.

The ability to use tools has three aspects, each scored separately. Effectiveness and reliability concern whether the model uses tools correctly, verifies their actual results, and adjusts its actions after failures. Speed and efficiency concern how long a task takes, how many calls it needs, and how quickly the model corrects a problem once it is detected. They matter because each call to a frontier model can take seconds (Figure 8 ). They should be reported even when success does not change, together with how tools are organized and scheduled, for example whether the agent keeps reasoning while a tool runs. Learning and transfer concern whether the model distills what it has learned about tools into skills that improve later tasks, as when frontier models author skills that later run without them ( Bai et al., 2026 ) . Measuring these aspects apart from provisioning gains requires paired tests that change one condition at a time, with the correct behavior and an independent scoring rule fixed in advance. Memory evaluation asks whether a robot uses information that its current observation no longer contains. Inspired by MIKASA-Robo ( Cherepanov et al., 2026a ) , we divide memory-intensive tasks into six classes. Object memory keeps information about objects that become temporarily unobservable, and spatial memory keeps object locations and a map of the environment. Sequential memory keeps temporally ordered information, and memory capacity concerns keeping several pieces of information at once. Body memory keeps information about the robot’s own body, such as its kinematic configuration and action-relevant body relations; user memory keeps persistent information about the user, such as preferences and prior instructions. Table 5 lists five benchmarks that together cover tabletop manipulation, household interaction, and question answering. They score different outcomes: manipulation success (MIKASA-Robo ( Cherepanov et al., 2026a ) , RoboMME ( Dai et al., 2026 ) , and RoboMemArena ( Lei et al., 2026 ) ), success on interactive tasks that require building and updating memory (EMem-Bench ( Liang et al., 2026a ) ), or answers to questions about what the robot has seen (OpenEQA ( Majumdar et al., 2024 ) ). None of these benchmarks tests body or user memory.

Cross-embodiment evaluation asks whether a fixed objective remains achievable when the body changes. It has three aspects. Task transfer performance compares success rate, execution efficiency, and stability across the change of body, with the same objectives and matched scenes. Body understanding concerns whether S2 understands the new body’s perception, motion, and manipulation capabilities and adjusts its action parameters and execution plans to them. For example, when a task moves from a small arm to a larger one such as a Franka Panda, aligning the end-effector target pose is not enough. Longer links change the trajectory of the whole arm, so S2 must check again for collisions with the environment and with the robot itself. Experience reuse concerns whether S2 carries over what still applies from its existing skills, revises the parts specific to the old body, and keeps outdated experience from interfering with execution on the new one. A transfer protocol should fix the S2 foundation model, pair the scenes across bodies, and report each embodiment separately. It should also disclose all exposure to the target body (demonstrations, adaptation data, tools, and controller changes) and compare only tasks that are feasible for both bodies. Each robot should be described by a versioned capability card that lists its frames and units, kinematic and geometric parameters, sensors, tools with their preconditions and postconditions, controller version, and validated capability limits. Efficiency evaluation measures the latency from observation to action, the number of model calls, the freshness of the observations on which each decision rests (the observation age defined in Equation 1 ), and the rate of timeouts and missed deadlines, under a stated reasoning budget and response deadline. Output tokens per second measure decoding speed, not robot responsiveness. Latency should therefore include perception, context preparation, inference, network delay, tool execution, and controller response whenever they are on the critical path. Cost should be reported as tokens, API charges, total task time, and robot occupancy, per attempt and per success, including failures and retries. Reported costs differ by orders of magnitude, and cost rankings need not follow success rankings. In Astranomy, an agentic Astra session used 30.9M tokens ($36.40) per successful episode, against 160k tokens ($1.96) for direct prompting, although most of its input was served from cache ( Yang et al., 2026d ) . On RoboQuest, an average episode with Astra took 10.4 minutes of simulated time but 53.8 minutes of wall-clock time and cost $11.29 ( Liu et al., 2026c ) . On a single real six-axis arm, frontier coding agents took about 30 minutes per task that a person completes in 20 seconds ( Artificial Analysis, 2026 ) .

For user intent, explicit instructions are the easy case; harder cases include vague requests, unstated preferences, learned habits, and changes of mind during a task. Evaluation should therefore score the final state against the user’s intent rather than the literal instruction. It should also report the number of clarifying questions, adherence to stated and learned preferences, the handling of corrections and interruptions, and the number of human interventions. Existing benchmarks cover only parts of intent fulfillment. PRISM isolates implicit intent resolution, which was “a significant bottleneck for all model families” ( Lim et al., 2026 ) . TEACh requires acting on intent conveyed over several dialogue turns ( Padmakumar et al., 2022 ) , and Ask-to-Act scores clarification of ambiguous requests ( Ramrakhya et al., 2025 ) . MEMENTO finds that agents “can recall simple object semantics but struggle to apply sequential user patterns to planning” ( Kwon et al., 2026 ) . Two devices can be borrowed from digital agents: a hidden supervisor with a simulated user who gives multi-turn feedback “without leaking grading criteria” ( Chen et al., 2026e ) , and the pass k reliability metric ( Yao et al., 2024 ) . To our knowledge, no benchmark yet scores end-to-end intent fulfillment on real robots with real users across sessions.


#### 4.2 How to Evaluate

The dimensions above specify what to measure. Comparable results also require a common way of measuring, which the same benchmark name does not guarantee. We therefore propose the following principles, several of which are adapted from the evaluation of digital agents ( Yao et al., 2024 ; Kwa et al., 2025 ) .

- i. Separate the model from the system. Fix the harness when comparing foundation models, and fix the model when comparing harnesses. In both cases, match the embodiment, interfaces, observations, task instances, and budgets. Otherwise, label the comparison as one between complete systems, as CodeActionBench does ( Lyu et al., 2026 ) . What counts as “no tools” must be defined by the interface contract rather than by name: in EmbodiedSkillBench, for example, the “no skill” condition still acts through registered native end-effector and gripper tools ( Han et al., 2026 ) .

- ii. Treat the action interface as a controlled variable. The same foundation model may call skills, emit end-effector targets, or output joint targets (Figure 4 ). These interfaces do not form a scale from easy to hard: each brings different execution priors, control burdens, failure modes, and degrees of freedom. Model comparisons should therefore fix the action abstraction level and report it. Its effect can be measured directly by holding the model, tasks, robot, sensors, environment, and budgets fixed while varying the interface. The comparison should include a skill-only interface, a direct end-effector interface, and a hybrid in which the model decides when to call a skill and when to act directly. Such comparisons should report task success separately from action validity, collisions and constraint violations, the number of calls, physical execution time, inference latency, recovery, and total cost.

- iii. Run on a world clock. Many simulated evaluations pause the world while the model deliberates ( Jedlicki et al., 2026 ; Galbot Team et al., 2026 ; Yang et al., 2026j ) . Paused and live execution measure different abilities and must be reported separately. On matched hard routes of RT-Safe, live execution raised collisions by a factor of 12.3 but left task completion essentially unchanged. Safe success fell from 19.8% to 0.7%, and mean latency was positively associated with collisions across models ( Xu et al., 2026d ) . Embodied First Exam separates a Standard setting, in which the agent receives sensor observations and the simulation pauses while it deliberates, from a Realtime setting, in which the environment continues to advance during decision-making. On 54 dynamic core tasks evaluated in both settings, GPT-6 Astra solved 23 Standard tests and 17 Realtime tests ( Wang et al., 2026d ) . Live evaluations should report the rate of missed deadlines and the distribution of the observation age . The age is defined as $$\Delta t_{\mathrm{obs}}=t_{\mathrm{effect}}-t_{\mathrm{capture}},$$ (1) where $t_{\mathrm{capture}}$ is when the observation behind a decision was captured and $t_{\mathrm{effect}}$ when the resulting action takes effect. Because it includes perception, communication, queuing, and execution delays, observation age reflects the physical response more closely than model inference latency does. When sensors, computation, and actuators run on different devices, both times must be measured on synchronized clocks.

- iv. Disclose the observation contract. State what the model and its tools receive: cameras and their history, the source of depth (a sensor, a prediction, or triangulation), privileged object state, success or progress signals, and any demonstrations or scene assets. Give the agent only information that a real robot could also obtain ( OpenRUA Team, 2026 ) . Without this disclosure, numbers from different studies cannot be compared.

- v. Verify completion independently. Decide success with a checker that has access to ground truth that the system cannot observe, and never accept the model’s own claim or a successful tool return as proof. Report the rate of false completion claims, because agents often declare success prematurely ( Jedlicki et al., 2026 ; Liu et al., 2026c ) and VLM judges are biased toward success ( Navasardyan et al., 2026 ) . The checker must also be inaccessible to the agent. In one study, agents read static object files that a physical robot could not read and located a hidden success check within a single session ( Wang et al., 2026g ) . Hiding the verifier changes results substantially. When tool returns no longer revealed success, agents had to decide for themselves when an episode ended. Under this condition, the mean scores of two systems over 50 episodes on 10 tasks of RoboDojo fell from 83.2 to 63.5 and from 42.0 to 26.1 ( OpenRUA Team, 2026 ) .

- vi. Quantify uncertainty and reliability. Report per-task results, the number of repetitions, and confidence intervals. A difference of a few percentage points on a handful of real-robot trials rarely supports a conclusion ( TRI LBM Team et al., 2025 ; Kress-Gazit et al., 2024 ) . Reliability across repeated runs, such as the probability that all $k$ trials succeed, should complement the chance of at least one success ( Yao et al., 2024 ) . Rankings that aggregate benchmarks need the same care. The Robot Intelligence Index, for example, reports for each model the range within which its rank falls in 90% of resampled re-runs ( Playground Research, 2026 ) .

- vii. Control and sweep budgets. Fix and report the reasoning effort, the budgets for calls, steps, and wall-clock time, and any retry rules, and sweep them into success–cost and success–latency curves where possible. In Astranomy, raising the effort from medium to extra-high improved success only from 72.7% to 74.5% at 2.6 times the latency and 2.2 times the cost ( Yang et al., 2026d ) . Budgets must also match the benchmark’s own limits: recounted under RoboDojo’s native step limit, one system’s success rate fell from 47.1% to 1.0%, because 97 of its 99 successes came after that limit ( OpenRUA Team, 2026 ) . Improvement methods should be compared with retry baselines under the same budget, with development and test tasks kept apart ( Wang et al., 2026j ; Wang et al., 2026g ) . For example, a skill developed on one task with the benchmark’s verifier raised that task’s score from 0 to 80 yet left two of four other tasks at baseline ( OpenRUA Team, 2026 ) . Results that use context about the test tasks should not be compared with zero-shot results ( OpenRUA Team, 2026 ) .

- viii. Validate on real robots. Treat simulation as a screen rather than a verdict, and audit the shortcuts of shared simulated suites. In SIMPLER, simulated success tracked real success closely once control and visual gaps to the real setup were mitigated, and less closely when they were only partly mitigated ( Li et al., 2025a ) . Policies trained in simulation can also fail badly on real robots ( Ibarz et al., 2021 ) . Real-robot evaluations should pair seeds and scenes and report the human assistance and environment resets they required.

- ix. Fix the conditions of a level claim in advance. Some results are offered as evidence that a robot carries a level of delegated responsibility (Section 6.1 ). Such a result should fix beforehand the initial conditions, the permitted assistance and interruptions and how they are counted, and the evidence of completion. Completing a long script does not demonstrate delegation if a person must keep repairing the plan. The duration of the work should be measured in the time that a skilled person would need for it, as METR ( Kwa et al., 2025 ) does for software agents. The robot’s own wall-clock time and the human assistance it received should be reported separately, because a robot may need far longer than a person for the same work.

On several simulated benchmarks, a general model with a harness matches or exceeds trained policies, particularly on tasks that reward semantic understanding, memory, or flexible composition ( Gao et al., 2026b ; Yang et al., 2026d ; Si et al., 2026 ; Jedlicki et al., 2026 ; Petkov et al., 2026 ) . On LADDER, for example, orchestration recovers part of the success that learned policies lose as tasks lengthen ( Petkov et al., 2026 ) . In several comparisons, however, the model received information that the policies did not, such as a written task recipe ( Zhang et al., 2026c ; Li et al., 2026e ) . The result is also model-specific: under the same action interface, a previous-generation model reached 0.88% on RoboDojo, whereas Astra reached 22.48% ( Zhang et al., 2026c ) .

When tasks span domains or demand active exploration, success remains low: the best model reached 19.0% on RobotWorld ( Yang et al., 2026j ) , 23.2% on RoboQuest ( Liu et al., 2026c ) , and 16.8% on HumanCLAW ( Siyao et al., 2026 ) . The high scores in Table 8 come from narrower suites. Recognition also does not imply completion: on HumanCLAW, the best model found the target in 64.9% of episodes but completed only 16.8% ( Siyao et al., 2026 ) .

Failures cluster on two sides: physical action and perception, and the gathering and judging of evidence. On the physical side, major challenges remain in precise manipulation and contact-rich interaction ( Gao et al., 2026b ; Yu et al., 2026a ) , as well as dynamic control and locomotion; Astra, for example, solved none of the 11 locomotion tasks in RobotWorld ( Yang et al., 2026j ) . Spatial understanding has improved but remains below human level. In the evaluation of DeepCybo Team et al. (2026) , GPT-6 Astra at low reasoning effort scored 59.8 on VSI-Bench, against 45.4 for the best model in the original VSI-Bench evaluation and 79.2 for human evaluators on a 400-question subset ( Yang et al., 2025b ) . On MMSI-Bench, the strongest proprietary model at publication answered about 40% of multi-image spatial questions correctly, against 97% for humans ( Yang et al., 2026f ) . Body understanding is weak as well: in HumanCLAW, failures stem not from misrecognizing the target but from the model losing track of its own body ( Siyao et al., 2026 ) . On the evidential side, agents struggle to explore actively for task-relevant evidence ( Liu et al., 2026c ) , to recognize what cannot be answered from observation, and to judge completion. On AbstainEQA, for example, the best frontier model recognized 42.79% of unanswerable questions, against 91.17% for humans ( Wu et al., 2026 ) . Agents also declare success prematurely ( Jedlicki et al., 2026 ; Liu et al., 2026c ) ; this was the most common failure of a frozen Astra policy on memory-dependent manipulation ( Jedlicki et al., 2026 ) . Automatic judges share this bias toward reporting success ( Navasardyan et al., 2026 ) .

With the model held fixed, changes to verifiers, memory, interfaces, and post-call decision-making moved success rates by about 9 to 35 percentage points in four studies ( Siyao et al., 2026 ; Wei et al., 2026a ; Si et al., 2026 ; Ge et al., 2026 ) . In a fifth, restricting agents to skills moved them by up to 52 points ( Han et al., 2026 ) . Changing only the interface illustrates the effect: with the same Astra planner, primitives, and call budget, Python cells raised success over tool calling from 63.1% to 71.7% ( Si et al., 2026 ) . Changing the model can have effects of similar size, as the RoboDojo comparison above shows. Studies that cross models with harness conditions remain small, with at most four models and a few conditions on the same tasks ( Ge et al., 2026 ; Wei et al., 2026a ; Han et al., 2026 ) , so these figures show that both factors matter, but not which is generally larger. Combined differences in harness and protocol widen the spread further. For Astra on RoboDojo, one report gives 22.48% with one seed, a task recipe from the RoboDojo wiki, and direct end-effector control ( Zhang et al., 2026c ) . Another gives 37.82% with the same wiki recipes plus experience reused from practice scenes and a higher reasoning effort ( Li et al., 2026e ) . A third gives 46.9% in the official evaluation with a tool library built on the benchmark’s own tasks ( Gao et al., 2026b ) ; its later version, evaluated by its authors on public test layouts, gives 69.9% with a small set of general tools and no task-specific input, and 85.0% with about a page of guidance per task ( Gao et al., 2026a ) . A fourth gives 81.9% over five episodes per task, with in-context demonstrations and up to ten times the step budget ( SenseNova et al., 2026 ) . Embodied First Exam also finds substantial harness effects on a common set of 456 tasks: GPT-6 Astra solved 34% in Codex and 22% in Claude Code under its Standard setting ( Wang et al., 2026d ) . Without a harness card, such numbers cannot be compared.

More tools do not necessarily give better results. In EmbodiedSkillBench, restricting agents to skills selected for each task from a repository of 250 action, perception, and experience skills lifted GPT-5.6-Sol from 11% to 63% success but lowered GPT-6 Astra from 92% under native control to 61%. When Astra could decide for each skill whether to invoke, modify, or ignore it, it recovered to 85% ( Han et al., 2026 ) . Skills thus help an agent only as far as they exceed what it can already do. They can also reduce cost, even where they add no success. In the same benchmark, skill-only execution cut the token usage of the weaker agents by 58% to 85%. For Astra, which gained no success from skills, selective use still saved 14.1% of its tokens and 11.9% of its time relative to native control ( Han et al., 2026 ) . How tools are organized and scheduled matters as well. The motion, perception, skill, and episode tools of the uni-robot-agent harness share arguments, units, and feedback. In this harness, letting the agent keep reasoning while a tool runs shortened each of the five tasks tested, in one episode each, by 4.8% to 73.7%. Swapping two T-shaped blocks, for example, took 12.5 instead of 34.7 minutes, although the authors do not attribute the whole reduction to scheduling. Removing the harness’s predefined skill library, by contrast, left an open-weight Qwen model with 96.6% of its score, so effective execution did not depend mainly on that library ( Yuan et al., 2026a ) .

Frontier models take seconds to tens of seconds per decision (Figure 8 ), and many evaluations hide this delay by pausing the simulation. In a world that keeps running, latency is associated with collisions ( Xu et al., 2026d ) ; in one real-robot evaluation, deliberation dominated task time, which was about 100 times a person’s ( Artificial Analysis, 2026 ) . Several designs therefore move work out of the model loop: program-level control ( Si et al., 2026 ) , VLA-first execution that consults the S2 foundation model only when needed ( Sun et al., 2026b ) , and skills written offline by a frontier model that then run without it ( Bai et al., 2026 ) .

Pooled over the same six models, refusal fell from 72% of text requests to 18% of real-robot trials with end-effector control ( Sun et al., 2026a ) . This drop highlights a substantial text-to-action refusal gap under the tested conditions, and a second study corroborates it ( Grover, 2026 ) . Safety must therefore be measured in the action loop, along the full chain from recognizing a risk to stopping in time.

Current evidence does not support cross-paper rankings, because interfaces, privileged information, budgets, and time semantics differ. Nor does it support claims of reliability, because many headline results come from single runs or few seeds. Aggregate rankings have begun to address the first problem. The Robot Intelligence Index, launched with 16 public simulated and real-robot benchmarks and extended to 22 by November 4, 2026, combines them into one rating per model using a weighted Bradley–Terry model. In this model, a benchmark’s weight is the independence of its evaluators multiplied by the repeatability of its scores ( Playground Research, 2026 ) . The index itself shows, however, why a single rating cannot replace the profile we recommend: 13 of the 23 models that rank in the top three on some benchmark rank in the bottom half on another. Performance in the real world, in continuous time, and over long horizons also remains largely unmeasured, including memory across sessions and the fulfillment of user intent on real robots. Several further findings rest on a single study so far. One is that common embodied-reasoning benchmarks did not track closed-loop control ( Yang et al., 2026b ) ; another is that, when the world changes, memory fails mainly at replacing outdated state rather than at retaining history ( Liang et al., 2026a ) . Finally, benchmarks saturate quickly. VLAs that exceeded 90% on LIBERO ( Liu et al., 2023b ) dropped to around 50% on LIBERO-PRO ( Zhou et al., 2025c ) , yet an agentic system reached 94.0% on LIBERO-PRO’s perturbed suites within weeks of Astra’s release ( Si et al., 2026 ) . Evaluation must therefore move toward live execution, real robots, longer horizons, and the embodiment-specific sub-dimensions of Figure 9 .


#### 4.4 Limitations of Current Evaluation

Moving evaluation in these directions requires more than the principles above, because current evaluation remains limited even when they are followed. The most fundamental limitation is coverage: current evaluation spans too little responsibility, space, and time to test agentic robotics as we envision it. The others concern the validity of simulated results, comparability in continuous time, credible acceptance of complex tasks, and the cost of scaling real-world evaluation.

Table 9 lists the spatial scope and time span of benchmarks reviewed in this paper. Most of them test primitives and single tasks in a workcell or a room, within one episode. Building-scale scenes appear mainly in navigation and memory benchmarks, and site-scale settings in driving, aerial control, and city navigation. Evaluations beyond one episode use a continuous three-hour simulation, histories of earlier sessions, or learning across tasks. Responsibility is covered least: no benchmark we reviewed tests a workflow of interdependent tasks that share resources and deadlines, or a role sustained over days. How to construct benchmarks for workflows and roles at building and site scale, over days rather than minutes, remains open.

Simulation is useful for S2 only to the extent that it preserves what S2 must respond to: realistic observations, informative feedback, failures, and changes in the environment. Simulators may have limited fidelity in modeling complex physical interactions like deformation, gear backlash, wear and fluid dynamics ( Tobin et al., 2017 ) , and on real robots, physical parameters such as motor strength, latency, and battery voltage change over time ( Tan et al., 2018 ) . Real-to-sim evaluation can track real-world rankings for some policies ( Li et al., 2025a ) , but its effectiveness depends on the policy ( Zhou et al., 2025e ) , and we know of no comparable validation for Embodied S2. Whether rankings of S2 foundation models and harnesses obtained in simulation hold on real robots, and which properties of a simulator decide this, remain open questions.

Many agentic evaluations pause the world while the model deliberates ( SenseNova et al., 2026 ) . One report estimates that the budgets of RoboDojo ( Chen et al., 2026b ) correspond to 8 to 76 seconds of commanded motion, against an average of 55 minutes of computation. It summarizes the effect: “The model reasons as though the world waits for it” ( SenseNova et al., 2026 ) . A fair comparison in a world that keeps moving must count each system’s own reaction latency as part of its performance. Yet such a world does not repeat, so runs differ in timing as well as in decisions. How to control for timing variation across runs at a cost that real robots can bear is an open question.

A robot’s own judgment that a task is complete differs from external acceptance of the result. Automatic judges cannot yet provide such acceptance: VLM judges of robot task success have shown a systematic bias toward predicting success when the evidence is ambiguous ( Navasardyan et al., 2026 ) . False alarms are also costly: under a stop-and-reset model of intervention, even an idealized failure detector can afford a false-alarm rate of only 2.0% to 3.3% before unnecessary resets outweigh the savings from stopping failures early ( Ahmedt-Aristizabal et al., 2026 ) . Even an accepted result is hard to attribute to specific design choices. RobotWorld, for example, cautions that its five-model snapshot supports “matched-task observational comparisons, not controlled ablations” of history, code control, or reasoning budget ( Yang et al., 2026j ) . Workflows and roles make acceptance harder still, because their acceptance criteria span many subgoals and depend on the user’s intent. How to obtain acceptance judgments with known error rates for such work is open.

Because real-robot trials are usually slow and expensive, many reported real-robot results rest on few trials per task. This scarcity is especially consequential because results for the same task vary across runs and across the testers who prepare each trial ( Yakefu et al., 2025 ) . Distributed real-robot evaluation ( Atreya et al., 2025 ) , autonomous real-world evaluation ( Zhou et al., 2025e ) , remote access to standardized hardware ( Chen et al., 2026b ; Yakefu et al., 2025 ) , and third-party real-robot leaderboards ( Robocurve, 2026 ) are promising steps toward evaluation at scale. For evaluating Embodied S2, one reported protocol offers a reasonable minimum standard ( SenseNova et al., 2026 ) : it sets a fixed wall-clock budget, stations an operator at the emergency stop for the entire trial, and requires a human to judge success from the physical outcome rather than from the model’s own completion claim. The open question is how to obtain statistically reliable evidence as evaluation extends to larger spaces, longer time periods, and broader task responsibilities—settings in which each trial costs more.


### 5 Digital Agents versus Physical Agents

Agentic robotics inherits a central principle from digital agents: a foundation model can solve complex tasks by repeatedly observing an environment, invoking tools, and verifying the results. Its architecture, a foundation model in a loop with tools, context, and memory, draws on two lineages. One is digital agents, which have advanced rapidly on coding, computer use, and research tasks; the other is earlier work on robots driven by language models. The success of digital agents, however, rests on favorable properties of the digital world: interfaces that the model reads and writes directly, outcomes that can be checked automatically, abundant data, and environments that are cheap to copy and reset.

Physical environments weaken each of these properties and add constraints of time, irreversibility, and embodiment. The challenge is therefore not to give a digital agent cameras and robotic tools, but to determine which assumptions no longer hold, which principles still transfer, and what must be rebuilt for reliable physical execution. Our answer is that the principles of digital agents largely transfer, but the assumptions that make them reliable do not. We first review what digital agents can do and why, and then take up these three questions in turn.


#### 5.1 What Digital Agents Can Do and Why

The length of software tasks that frontier agents can complete has grown steadily. METR ( Kwa et al., 2025 ) measures this progress as a time horizon : the duration, in human working time, of tasks that an agent completes with a given probability. The horizon has doubled roughly every seven months since 2019 ( Kwa et al., 2025 ; METR, 2026a ) , and about every four months since 2023 on METR’s revised task suite ( METR, 2026a ) . By 2026, the best measured 50% horizons exceeded ten hours, and METR cautions that measurements above 16 hours are unreliable with its current task suite ( METR, 2026b ) . These results measure capability on particular distributions of software tasks with checkable specifications, not reliable long-horizon autonomy in general. Reliable completion is harder: time horizons at 80% success are several times shorter than at 50% ( Kwa et al., 2025 ; METR, 2026b ) .

A digital agent pairs a foundation model with a harness of tools, context management, memory, and an execution sandbox. Its results benefit from four conditions that software happens to satisfy. We call them enabling conditions : they recur across the systems behind much of the recent progress, and we treat them as design guidance. First, the interface is native to the model : state and actions take the form of the model’s own inputs and outputs. State is text that the agent reads, and actions are text or code that it emits directly through an agent–computer interface ( Yang et al., 2024 ) . Outcomes are generally verifiable : compilers, tests, and program execution return objective signals quickly and at scale, signals that support iteration at test time ( Yang et al., 2024 ) and reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards ( Lambert et al., 2024 ; Guo et al., 2025 ) . Data are abundant : public code, documentation, and discussion accumulated over decades give pretrained models broad knowledge of the domain; Codex, for example, was trained on code from 54 million public GitHub repositories ( Chen et al., 2021 ) . Environments are cheap : a sandbox with an executable runtime and unit tests can be copied, run in parallel, reset, and replayed, so that agents can be trained and evaluated on thousands of real tasks ( Pan et al., 2025 ) . The first two conditions shape every step of the agent’s loop; the last two determine how much the agent can learn before deployment and how widely it can be tested. Wei (2025) links verification to learning through a verifier’s rule : the ease of training AI to solve a task is proportional to how verifiable the task is.

> Figure 10: The four enabling conditions of digital agents, and how far each domain satisfies them. The domains illustrate how the conditions vary across task settings rather than forming a strict progression of capability or difficulty.

Because these conditions are properties of a task setting rather than of programming per se, capability has spread to settings in which they could be established. These settings include software engineering ( Jimenez et al., 2024 ) and professional work across many occupations ( Patwardhan et al., 2025 ) . They also include computer use, in which agents operate graphical interfaces in real operating systems ( Xie et al., 2024 ) , and visual coding, in which generated code is rendered and compared with a design or specification ( Si et al., 2025 ; Xu et al., 2026e ) . Most recently, they include 3D content, in which agents write programs for modeling software and game engines ( Gao et al., 2026c ; Ye et al., 2026b ) . Most of these extensions weaken some of the conditions, and capability lags where they weaken. Computer use weakens the native interface by replacing text with pixels and clicks. Its time horizons are 40 to 100 times shorter than those in software and reasoning domains, although they are increasing at similar rates ( Kwa and Cheng, 2025 ) . In 3D content, verification weakens, because a convincing render can hide incorrect spatial relations ( Ye et al., 2026b ) . Progress there depends on execution environments that return high-fidelity feedback and on more procedural data ( Gao et al., 2026c ) . Figure 10 summarizes our assessment for each domain. The domains differ in inputs, outputs, and verification requirements and do not form a strict progression of capability or difficulty. Physical robots weaken all four conditions at once, as the next subsection shows.


#### 5.2 Which Assumptions Fail in the Physical World

Each enabling condition enters digital-agent design as an assumption. This subsection asks which of these assumptions, and which further ones, fail in the physical world, and what problem each failure creates. Digital-agent design assumes that the agent reads the environment state, and acts on it, through documented interfaces. A coding sandbox offers such interfaces but a physical environment does not ( Wang et al., 2026e ) . Digital agents already falter when their environment is less transparent: they perform worse on tasks without clear feedback loops ( Kwa et al., 2025 ) . For a robot, partial observation is the usual condition: it observes only a local, momentary slice of the world through noisy sensors and must assemble and maintain a model of the rest ( Wang et al., 2026e ) . Physical outcomes also depend on quantities that cameras do not see, such as mass, friction, compliance, and contact force. Tasks such as insertion are difficult to complete robustly with vision alone ( Chen et al., 2026a ) . Touch carries much of this information: people correct their grip from touch within tens of milliseconds (Section 3.4 ), and in simulated tasks a frontier multimodal model interpreted vision-based tactile images when given them ( Yang et al., 2026a ) . Nor do actions take effect directly: they are skills, trajectories, and actuator commands whose meaning is set by controllers, reference frames, and units, and whose effects arrive with delay. The resulting problem is one of grounding: state must be estimated, together with its source, age, and uncertainty, and actions must carry explicit physical semantics.

Digital-agent design usually assumes that outcomes can be checked automatically, quickly, and reproducibly. Even with such checks, verification is imperfect: agents asked to evaluate their own work tend to praise it confidently ( Anthropic, 2026a ) , and benchmark oracles can themselves be flawed ( OpenAI, 2026c ) . Physical outcomes must instead be observed, judged, and sometimes re-measured, and both false successes and false failures are costly. A false success leaves a task unfinished or a hazard in place, and VLM-based judges are biased toward this error when the visual evidence is ambiguous ( Navasardyan et al., 2026 ) . By the verifier’s rule ( Wei, 2025 ) , this scarcity of credible verification is itself a central obstacle to training physical agents. The resulting problem is to verify outcomes using evidence: each verdict must state which of the four questions of the agentic loop it answers (acceptance, termination, physical effect, or intent) and come from a calibrated verifier.

Digital-agent design assumes that pretraining has already covered the domain. Unlike natural language and two-dimensional vision, dexterous manipulation has no Internet-scale corpus ( Hoque et al., 2025 ) . Robot data are produced by operating robots, at the pace of the physical world. The 76,000 trajectories of DROID, about 350 hours of interaction, took 50 data collectors twelve months on three continents ( Khazatsky et al., 2024 ) . Teleoperation of this kind lacks scale and diversity, and synthetic data bring a gap to reality ( Luo et al., 2025 ) . Human video scales more easily, but large collections such as Ego4D ( Grauman et al., 2022 ) lack native hand-pose annotations and action labels ( Hoque et al., 2025 ) , and video records no contact forces. Touch is scarcest: acquiring large-scale and reliable tactile data in the physical world remains costly ( Chen et al., 2026a ) . The resulting problem is one of supply: abundant data must be manufactured by a data engine that turns the operation of robots into training data.

Digital-agent design assumes that environments can be copied, reset, and run in parallel. Where simulation is faithful, the digital recipe returns: with thousands of simulated robots trained in parallel, a quadruped learns to walk on uneven terrain in about twenty minutes ( Rudin et al., 2022 ) . Where it is not, policies trained in simulation often fail to transfer to the real world ( Tan et al., 2018 ) , and simulation pipelines have only begun to cover complex physics such as deformable objects and fluids ( Qin et al., 2026c ) . The alternative, practice in the real world, requires the scene to be reset after each attempt; this is typically handled by automated reset skills, with manual reset as a fallback when automatic reset fails ( Li et al., 2026d ) . The cheap, parallel, and resettable environment of digital agents therefore exists only in part. The resulting problem is twofold: every physical experiment has a cost that must be budgeted, and results obtained in simulation must be validated against reality.

Three further assumptions of digital-agent design fail for reasons other than a missing condition: the physical world adds constraints of time, irreversibility, and embodiment. Web and computer-use agents meet milder forms of the first two, but the third has no close digital counterpart. Embodiment amplifies the first two and makes all three matters of safety and control.

- • Deliberation is not free. While a robot deliberates, the world around it continues to evolve ( Black et al., 2025c ) . A digital agent that takes a minute to respond inconveniences its user. A robot that takes a minute may act on a scene that no longer exists, with consequences for both performance and safety.

- • Errors cannot be undone. Coding agents undo errors with version control, snapshots, and sandboxes ( Anthropic, 2025b ) . Many physical actions cannot be undone ( Wang et al., 2026e ) , and the world offers no general counterpart to restoring a software snapshot ( Zhu and Cai, 2026 ) . Safety must therefore be enforced before an action is taken, and below the model.

- • Bodies do not stay fixed. Coding agents have no body to manage ( Wang et al., 2026e ) . A robot’s capabilities depend on its body, and that body’s condition changes with use. As Sagar and Senanayake (2026) note, over hours of operation motors gradually heat up, current saturates near contact, and voltage sags under load, so that the same command can produce a weaker, delayed, or noisier motion. Motor drivers reduce current near thermal limits and can leave closed-loop control beyond them ( ODrive Robotics, 2026 ) . Actuators can also switch off torque after overheating until they are rebooted ( ROBOTIS, 2026 ) . In simulated LIBERO ( Liu et al., 2023b ) tasks, one study perturbed only hardware and setup factors, such as camera mounting, calibration, actuation gain, control rate, and gripper pads. At the highest severity, the pooled success of the eight VLA models tested on all four suites fell from 93.2% to 50.9% ( Cui et al., 2026 ) .


#### 5.3 What Transfers

Beneath the practices that make digital agents work lie principles about how an agent organizes its work, not about what its environment provides. Because they do not depend on the enabling conditions, five such principles carry over to physical agents and already shape our architecture.

- • Close the loop on evidence. Agents are language models “using tools based on environmental feedback in a loop” and need ground truth from the environment at each step ( Anthropic, 2024a ) . For a robot, the principle requires the agent to observe and assess an action’s relevant effects before treating its intended outcome as established or making a decision that depends on that outcome. The agent may continue planning or monitoring while an action is in progress, provided that subsequent actions respect the execution and safety constraints.

- • Treat tools as contracts. Tools are contracts between deterministic systems and non-deterministic agents ( Anthropic, 2025d ) . Execution contracts extend this principle to physical calls. A capability contract, for example, states a skill’s inputs and units, preconditions, expected effects, time limits, and behavior on cancellation, so that S2 can reason about a call before making it and interpret its result afterward.

- • Manage context explicitly, and keep durable state outside it. Context is a finite resource with diminishing returns, managed through compaction, structured notes, and sub-agents ( Anthropic, 2025a ) . Long-running agents also keep progress files ( Anthropic, 2025b ) and a session log outside the model’s context window ( Anthropic, 2026b ) . The embodied harness applies the same discipline to the world, the user, and the body: it keeps task progress, maps, and memories outside the model and supplies them when a decision requires them.

- • Separate doing from judging. Long-running agents benefit from end-to-end tests, because models tend to mark work as complete without proper testing ( Anthropic, 2025b ) . Separating the agent that performs the work from the agent that judges it also helps ( Anthropic, 2026a ) . In the embodied harness, the verifier is the judge: it assesses each step and the task from evidence rather than from the model’s claim that it has finished.

- • Package capabilities as reusable skills. Digital agents load packaged skills when a task needs them ( Anthropic, 2025c ) . An agent in Minecraft accumulated a library of skills written as code ( Wang et al., 2024a ) . For robots, S1 skills and the programs that S2 writes play this role (Figure 4 ): a capability validated once can be invoked many times, and reuse also reduces the number of decisions that S2 must make at run time.

These principles transfer intact, but their digital implementations relied on the enabling conditions. The next subsection therefore turns to what physical agents must rebuild.


#### 5.4 What Must Be Rebuilt

Where an enabling condition fails, a physical agent must rebuild it. Where the physical world adds a constraint, the agent must operate within it. Success at either cannot be assumed, so claims about the agent must be validated in the running world. Figure 11 organizes these requirements in three layers: the foundations that the system must have, the constraints under which it must operate, and the evaluation that validates its claims.

> Figure 11: What physical agents must rebuild. Foundations replace the four enabling conditions of Figure 10 ; runtime constraints, which follow from time, irreversibility, and a changing body, govern how the system operates; and evaluation in the running world validates its claims.

Four foundations replace the four enabling conditions.

- • Grounded state and action interfaces in place of a native interface. On the state side, the harness maintains a belief state: a persistent and revisable estimate of world, user, and body state. The estimate is built from partial multimodal sensing, including touch and force when a task depends on contact. On the action side, each call passes through an execution contract. The contract states the call’s reference frame, units, preconditions, capability limits, and expected effects. It defines the feedback reported during execution and the behavior on cancellation, and it enforces safety checks, permissions, and postconditions that the model cannot bypass. Because a physical call can be executed exactly as commanded and still fail to produce its effect, the contract separates the termination of a call from the verification of its effect.

- • Calibrated verifiers in place of exit codes. Success detectors, progress estimators, and independent judges with known error rates make physical outcomes checkable. They serve both the loop at run time and the rewards for learning, so the quality of verification can become a bottleneck for how far a physical agent improves ( Wan et al., 2026 ; Zhou et al., 2026b ) .

- • Embodied data engines in place of Internet-scale corpora. Physical agents must produce their own data from teleoperation, human video, and simulation. Above all, they must produce it from deployment, in which autonomous attempts and human corrections across a fleet become training data ( Wang et al., 2026i ) .

- • Simulation calibrated against reality in place of sandboxes. Simulators and digital twins ( Todorov et al., 2012 ) cover part of the need but not the deployed world ( Wang et al., 2026e ) . They provide scale where their physics is faithful, and real-world experience corrects them where it is not. Autonomous resets make practice in the real world more affordable ( Li et al., 2026d ) .

The three constraints that the physical world adds govern how the system operates: time imposes deadlines and interruptions, irreversibility requires safety before execution, and a changing body requires adaptation.

- • Deadlines. The agent treats latency as a hazard, decides within deadlines rather than deliberating at length, and delegates fast loops to S1 and S0. This division of labor mirrors the separation of fast control from slow reasoning in Helix ( Figure AI, 2025b ) and of fast interaction from background reasoning in Thinking Machines’ interaction models ( Thinking Machines Lab, 2026 ) .

- • Interruptions. The agent accepts new input while it reasons or acts. Before dispatching a conflicting action, it confirms the state of the calls in flight and of the body, because a cancellation request alone does not establish that execution has stopped.

- • Safety before execution. Because many actions cannot be undone, the agent anticipates consequences, prefers reversible or information-gathering actions under uncertainty, and asks for help or refuses when needed.

- • Body-state adaptation. The agent tracks heat, wear, and power, and adapts to them, for example by conditioning its actions on actuator telemetry ( Sagar and Senanayake, 2026 ) . It re-validates its skills after maintenance or a change of embodiment.

Claims about a physical agent must be validated under the conditions in which it operates.

- • Live execution. Paused-world evaluations remain useful diagnostics because they separate the quality of a decision from its timing. Paused and live execution, however, measure different abilities, so live evaluation complements, and ultimately validates, paused-world results.

- • Independent outcome verification. Success should be decided by a checker with access to ground truth that the system cannot observe.

- • Intervention and cost. Each result should report the human assistance and interventions it required, the robot’s time, and the cost per completed task.

These requirements distinguish Embodied S2 from a digital agent: they reshape both the capabilities required of S2 and the conditions under which those capabilities must be evaluated. Progress on digital-agent benchmarks, however rapid, therefore does not translate directly into the ability to delegate physical work; that ability must be measured and built in the physical world.


### 6 Road Ahead

Agentic robotics broadens the question by which progress in robotics is judged: from what a robot can do on command to what it can be entrusted to do on its own. General foundation models already supply much of the knowledge and reasoning that such trust requires. Turning these capabilities into dependable physical work, however, raises problems of timing, persistent state, verification, adaptation, and learning from experience that no single model release will resolve. Where the field goes next will therefore depend on how it measures progress and which of these problems it addresses. This section proposes a ladder of delegable work as a measure of progress and places current systems on it. It then identifies research directions that climbing the ladder requires and closes with open questions about how agentic robotic systems should be built.


#### 6.1 Ladder of Delegable Work

> Figure 12: Ladder of delegable work. Progress is measured by the responsibility that a robot can be trusted to carry alone. Each of the four levels includes those below it; typical durations are illustrative.

Reaching embodied intelligence that is genuinely useful calls for a high-level ladder that guides progress toward it. We propose such a ladder for agentic robotics: it measures progress by the scope of responsibility that a robot can reliably carry alone, independently of its body, domain, or implementation. The ladder has four levels (Figure 12 ). At L1, the primitive level, the robot executes a local skill, such as picking up a cup, and checks whether the intended result has been achieved. At L2, the task level, the robot coordinates skills to deliver a complete task, such as making and serving coffee: Embodied S2 decomposes the goal, selects actions, monitors execution, and responds to failures until the acceptance criteria are met. At L3, the workflow level, the robot coordinates interdependent tasks toward an overall goal, such as preparing breakfast for four: Embodied S2 manages timing, priorities, and shared resources, and revises its plan as needed. At L4, the role level, the robot sustains an agreed responsibility across successive tasks and workflows, such as managing daily breakfast around household schedules and preferences. This responsibility continues after individual workflows end, until it expires or is withdrawn. Each level builds on those below it, extending the scope over which the robot must connect reasoning, execution, and verification.

Spatial scope and complexity complement the ladder: within each level, progress means reliably fulfilling the delegated responsibility across broader environments and more demanding activities. As the operating scope expands from a workcell to a room, a building, or a site (Figure 13 and Table 11 ), robots must integrate observations across locations, remember relevant objects and events, and seek information beyond their immediate view. These demands, like those of complex activities, fall on Embodied S2, which maintains goals and context while guiding action through the loop of observation, reasoning, execution, and verification. We therefore expect the value of agentic robotics to grow with scope and complexity.

> Figure 13: Spatial scope of delegated work, from a workcell within the robot’s reach to a site with several buildings and outdoor routes. The illustration is conceptual.

For current systems, they can execute primitives and complete tasks in unstructured environments, but have only begun to reach the workflow level, and have yet to reach the role level.

- • Primitives and tasks. Reliability on narrow tasks is rising. Sunday reports 99.1% success in folding laundry in unseen homes with a single model checkpoint and no adaptation per home ( Sunday Robotics, 2026 ) . Dyna Robotics reports that Dyna-2 passed customer acceptance criteria at a rate of 87% at deployment sites whose data it had not seen ( Dyna Robotics, 2026a ) . Longer tasks are now completed too: $\pi_{0.5}$ performs long-horizon cleaning in unseen homes ( Black et al., 2025a ) , and Figure AI reports a four-minute task of unloading and reloading a dishwasher ( Figure AI, 2026a ) .

- • Workflows. The first workflow-level results are single-company demonstrations, such as the hour-long laundry-room workflow that Dyna Robotics reports for Dyna-2.1 ( Dyna Robotics, 2026b ) . In simulation, a mobile manipulator with persistent memory has chained tasks for more than three hours ( Banwasi et al., 2026 ) .

- • Roles. To our knowledge, no robot system in an unstructured environment has been reported to hold a role without human repair. In structured settings, an autonomous materials laboratory operated for 17 days ( Szymanski et al., 2023 ) , although the novelty of its products was later disputed ( Leeman et al., 2024 ; Szymanski et al., 2026 ) ; sustained operation alone does not show that a system holds a role. Capabilities that a role relies on have begun to appear as harness prototypes that keep state outside the model. Some keep notes about a house that persist across tours and raise navigation success ( Zhao et al., 2026b ) or learn properties such as “locked” through interaction ( Banwasi et al., 2026 ) . Others carry user preferences across a program restart on a real robot ( Wang et al., 2026l ) or maintain a revisable model of the robot’s own body ( Lou et al., 2026 ) . Remembering such information, however, is not the same as organizing work around it. It remains to be shown that a robot can use these capabilities to manage goals across tasks and sessions and to sustain a role.

#### 6.2 Future Research Directions

Climbing the ladder, and doing so across larger spaces and longer periods, will require progress well beyond what current systems demonstrate. The evidence reviewed in this paper points to four directions that need further research. They concern embodied reasoning that meets physical deadlines, control whose state and outcomes remain trustworthy across tasks and spaces, adaptation to new environments and bodies at low cost, and continual improvement from physical experience. The list is not exhaustive.


##### 6.2.1 Embodied Reasoning Under Physical Constraints

S2 must understand the world, anticipate the consequences of its actions, and decide within limited time and computation, yet current S2 foundation models fall short in both speed and decision quality. With a frontier model as its S2 foundation model, S2 decides once every several to forty seconds ( Xu et al., 2026d ; Yang et al., 2026d ; Galbot Team et al., 2026 ) , far slower than the S1 skills it directs (Figure 8 ). More deliberation helps when the world waits ( Gemini Robotics Team, 2025b ) but hurts when it moves ( Xu et al., 2026d ) . Decisions remain weak where embodiment matters most: judging success from ambiguous evidence ( Navasardyan et al., 2026 ) , anticipating the consequences of actions ( Sun et al., 2026a ) , and deciding when to stop exploring ( Liu et al., 2026c ) .

We therefore advocate making capped scaling an explicit training and system objective: maximizing decision quality subject to a response deadline, evaluated without pausing the world and reported as success–latency–cost curves. Three questions follow. First, when is further reasoning worth its time, and when should the robot observe again or act at once? Second, which decisions must be made online, and which can be reused or turned into skills? Partial answers to both include routing each subtask to a planner of appropriate strength ( Dao et al., 2026 ) and launching chains of primitives with one call ( Xu et al., 2026a ) . A fast policy can also consult a frontier model only for recovery ( Sun et al., 2026b ) , and authoring skills offline removes the slow model from the loop ( Bai et al., 2026 ) . Third, how can the task benefit per unit of time and computation be raised? Distillation into small models that run locally is one candidate ( Izzo et al., 2026 ; Liu et al., 2026b ; Baumann et al., 2026 ; Foundation Model, Li Auto Inc, 2026 ) .


##### 6.2.2 Persistent and Verifiable Control Across Tasks and Spaces

As responsibility extends to workflows and roles, and the operating scope to buildings and sites, the system must keep its state trustworthy while organizing and verifying the work. However, current harnesses are built and evaluated almost entirely on single tasks in a workcell or a room (Section 4.4 ).

Three questions stand out. The first is how to represent and maintain state that spans rooms, floors, and days, so that the robot knows what it still knows. Candidates include augmenting spatially grounded scene graphs with outcomes learned through interaction ( Banwasi et al., 2026 ) and recording a subgoal as complete only after evidence of completion ( Zhao et al., 2026a ) . The second is how to coordinate the interdependent tasks of a workflow, which share information, resources, and deadlines. The third is how to divide judgment between the model and the execution system: which checks belong inside skills and verifiers, and which decisions must return to S2. Such a division can also keep a slow decision-maker compatible with continuous control. A precedent exists one level down, within S1: real-time chunking lets a VLA generate the next action chunk while the current one executes, remaining robust to inference delays of more than 300 ms ( Black et al., 2025c ) . S2 decisions, however, take seconds rather than milliseconds.


##### 6.2.3 Efficient Adaptation Across Environments and Embodiments

Deployment demands that capabilities transfer to new sites and embodiments with minimal configuration, data, and human effort. Yet current success remains highly dependent on the specific embodiment (Section 4.1 ). We advocate three lines of work. The first is reuse of knowledge and experience: in-context adaptation and skill libraries of validated repairs reduce what must be learned anew ( Lu et al., 2026 ) , and compiled skills can be frozen and carried to other agents ( Bai et al., 2026 ) . The second is capability interfaces, such as capability cards and device descriptions, that let S2 plan for a body it has not used before. The third is skill adaptation: in one study, a single policy, conditioned on examples of commanded actions and resulting motions, adapted in context to unseen controller modes and gains ( Yang et al., 2026e ) .

Fitting a system to a body also includes choosing loop rates and the placement of computation, as deployed stacks do. Helix 02 runs whole-body control at 1 kHz and a visuomotor policy at 200 Hz ( Figure AI, 2026a ) , beneath an upper module that ran at 7 to 9 Hz in the first Helix ( Figure AI, 2025b ) . An on-robot model avoids the network round trips and outages that a cloud model must tolerate. Conversely, skills, sensory feedback, and the body should be designed so that S2 can plan, execute, and verify more easily. Examples include skills that report their terminal states with evidence, and touch sensing: adding tactile observations to the in-context demonstrations of an embodied task agent raised its average success on four simulated tasks from 15.75% to 24.00% ( Wang et al., 2026k ) .


##### 6.2.4 Continual Improvement from Physical Experience

A robot holding a role for weeks keeps meeting new tasks and changes in its environment and body that no training run anticipates. It must therefore learn from physical experience which object properties, user preferences, causes of failure, and skills are worth keeping. We distinguish three kinds of change. Adaptation adjusts behavior to the current task, environment, or feedback. Continual improvement retains a change to memory, tools, skills, the harness, or a policy, and verifies that it improves later work. Recursive self-improvement (RSI) improves the process that produces such changes, making subsequent improvement more effective.

Robots have long improved through autonomous data collection, learned rewards, and reinforcement learning, and DrEureka used language models to design reward functions and domain randomization for sim-to-real transfer ( Ma et al., 2024b ) . What recent agentic systems add, mostly around frozen foundation models, is a loop that diagnoses failures from operating experience and revises the system, usually with a foundation model proposing the revisions ( Tian et al., 2026 ; Lee et al., 2026b ; Zhang et al., 2026e ; Deng et al., 2026 ) .
