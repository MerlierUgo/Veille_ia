## Shifter Quantization: Compact Representations for Native Integer Computation

Groupwise quantization is used in LLM quantization to improve accuracy at low bit widths, but grouping weights keeps standard integer MMA from spanning the full dot product for efficient GPU inference. We develop a new quantization method, Shifter Quantization (SQ), that reconstructs native-width integer operands by appending and shifting shared bits. To further extend the efficiency, we develop a special variant of SQ, Shifter Integer (SHI), to use INT4 kernels. We show that SQ and SHI formats perform better than standard groupwise quantization in quality, as measured by projection error and KL divergence, and in inference speed, as measured during decoding and prefill. For low precision like 2-bit quantization, SQ2 and SHI2 achieve $2.71$ – $3.77\times$ the 4,096-token prefill throughput of llama.cpp on a Q2_K checkpoint and reduce WikiText-2 KL by 6.6–7.8%. For mid precision like 4-bit quantization, SQ4 achieves $1.43$ – $2.47\times$ the prefill throughput of NInfer’s groupwise-integer baselines across the tested models and prompt lengths, with 24–40% lower KL than the NInfer baselines. For high precision like 8-bit quantization, SQ8 achieves 27.4% higher batch-one prefill throughput and 72.7% lower KL than vLLM’s online FP8.

### 1 Introduction

As large language models continue to grow, frontier models now exceed one trillion parameters [ 19 , 27 , 5 , 17 ] . Local inference typically uses smaller models, but consumer and workstation GPUs still face tight memory budgets. Model weights share that memory with the attention key-value (KV) cache, which grows with context length and the number of active requests [ 12 , 16 ] .

Quantizing weights and the KV cache helps fit larger models and longer contexts on local hardware [ 6 , 16 ] . At low bit widths, however, quantization must balance the range needed for large weights against the resolution needed for small ones. Groupwise scaling addresses this trade-off by assigning scales to groups of values. It is used in academic methods such as GPTQ and AWQ [ 8 , 14 ] , llama.cpp’s Qx_0, Qx_K, and IQx families [ 9 ] , and hardware-supported formats such as NVFP4 and MXFP4 on NVIDIA Blackwell and MXFP4 on AMD CDNA 4 [ 24 , 25 , 23 , 2 ] .

Groupwise quantization also affects inference speed. When a dot product spans groups with different scales, the kernel must preserve each group’s contribution separately so that it can apply the corresponding scale [ 34 , 15 ] . This prevents conventional integer matrix multiply–accumulate (MMA) instructions from accumulating across the full reduction dimension. A kernel can use MMA within each group, but the small groups limit how effectively it can use the hardware [ 34 , 15 ] . Recent GPUs provide block-scaled instructions for a limited set of formats and group sizes [ 23 , 2 ] . Supporting other groupwise representations efficiently remains a challenge.

To address this limitation, we introduce Shifter Quantization (SQ) to make low-bit local inference more efficient by enabling MMA across the full reduction dimension. Before multiplication, we expand weights stored at low precision in GPU registers using cheap bit shifting operations. We apply these operations to groups of four or eight weights (G4 or G8), smaller than the G16, G32, or G64 groups commonly used in conventional quantization. Smaller groups let the representation adapt to weight variation at a finer scale, which can reduce quantization error. SQ preserves the finer adaptation of small groups while allowing integer MMA to span the full reduction dimension.

Building on SQ, we develop Shifter Integer (SHI) to take advantage of faster INT4 kernels. SHI keeps the main product in INT4 and applies group corrections through shorter products when needed. Because of the special format, the group corrections only add a fraction of the W4A4 cost.

- • Shifter Quantization (SQ). SQ stores low-bit weight codes with shared information for each group. It reconstructs integer operands by appending shared bits, applying a group-specific shift, and truncating to the target width. The reconstructed operands can use integer MMA across the full reduction dimension, with channel scaling applied afterward.

- • Shifter Integer (SHI). SHI builds on SQ to use INT4 for the main matrix product. It represents group-level weight corrections with additional integer products over group sums. These products are shorter than the main product and can also account for activation corrections.

- • Implementation and evaluation. We evaluate SQ and SHI for local inference on a consumer GPU, measuring accuracy and speed at the projection, full-model, and serving levels. Our comparisons include llama.cpp quantization formats [ 9 ] , NVFP4, and the community-developed NInfer serving engine [ 20 ] .

Section 3 defines the representations and execution profiles. Section 4 isolates one MLP projection to show the effects of the execution paths on latency and error. Section 5 evaluates full checkpoints against BF16 references and measures prefill, and Section 6 compares the four-bit serving systems on prefill and speculative decode.


### 2 Background and related work

Groupwise scaling gives each group a range suited to its values. The idea predates neural networks and appears in block-floating-point arithmetic for digital filters [ 26 ] . VS-Quant studied finer-grained scaling for neural network inference [ 4 ] . Smaller groups can represent local ranges more accurately. They also require more scale metadata and make the kernel apply scales more often.

Several low-bit methods use groupwise or blockwise scaling. GPTQ and AWQ support groupwise integer weight quantization [ 8 , 14 ] ; ZeroQuant combines fine-grained quantization with optimized inference kernels [ 33 ] . QLoRA’s NF4 combines block scales with nonuniform element values [ 7 ] , while llama.cpp provides Q and IQ formats for low-bit inference [ 9 ] . These formats divide their storage among weight codes, scales, and other metadata. We report packed bits per weight alongside accuracy and speed in the projection benchmarks.

Group scales also affect how a GPU computes a dot product. For weight codes $q_{k}$ , group scales $s_{g}$ , and activation values $\xi_{k}$ , an output is $\sum_{g}s_{g}\sum_{k\in g}q_{k}\xi_{k}$ . When scales differ across groups, each partial sum must be scaled before the results are combined. Atom uses integer MMA to compute group products and applies their scales within the kernel [ 34 ] . QServe identifies weight and partial-sum dequantization as substantial sources of overhead in low-bit GPU inference [ 15 ] . SQ instead incorporates group information into reconstructed integer operands. SHI expresses group corrections as additional integer products over group sums.

Block-scaled floating-point formats combine local scales with hardware-supported arithmetic [ 28 ] . MXFP4 and MXFP8 share an exponent scale across 32 elements [ 29 , 25 ] . NVFP4 uses an FP8 scale per 16 four-bit elements and a global scale [ 24 ] . Recent GPU instructions support specific block-scaled formats and group sizes [ 23 , 2 ] . Because SHI4 and NVFP4 use similar amounts of weight storage, we compare their accuracy and speed.

Outliers make low-bit quantization harder. LLM.int8() isolates outlier features in a higher-precision computation [ 6 ] . SmoothQuant redistributes quantization difficulty across activation and weight channels [ 32 ] . QuaRot uses rotations to spread outliers across channels [ 3 ] . We use channel scales and signed Hadamard rotation to condition the inputs and weights. SQ and SHI then determine how the transformed weights are stored and used in integer computation.


### 3 Quantization formats and execution


#### 3.1 Linear projections and the quantization basis

Consider a linear projection with row-oriented activations $X\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times K}$ and weights $W\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times K}$ :

[Tableau] $$Y=XW^{\top}.$$ (1)

Here $M$ denotes the number of input tokens, $K$ the reduction dimension, and $N$ the output width. We quantize linear projections while retaining the surrounding attention, recurrent-state and nonlinear computation. Different projections may use different representations; the bit width assigned to an MLP bank therefore does not describe the average precision of the complete model.

We condition each projection using an orthogonal transform $U$ and diagonal channel scales $C_{i}$ and $C_{o}$ . An integer reconstruction $P$ is fitted such that

[Tableau] $$WU\simeq C_{o}PC_{i},\qquad Y\simeq(XUC_{i})P^{\top}C_{o}.$$ (2)

The first relation defines the fitted weight basis, and the second follows from $UU^{\top}=I$ . Input-channel scales act before the contraction; output-channel scales act after it. Projections fitted independently may have different $C_{i}$ even when they share an input, so fused execution retains their separate scale vectors.

Our block transform is a signed, normalized Hadamard rotation. For blocks of $B=512$ input channels, write

[Tableau] $$U_{B}=D_{B}H_{B},\qquad H_{B}H_{B}^{\top}=I,$$ (3)

where $D_{B}$ contains the signs and $H_{B}$ is the normalized Sylvester Hadamard matrix in natural binary order. The full transform $U$ is block diagonal. Row-oriented inputs receive the signs, the Hadamard butterfly and then input-channel scaling; the inverse transform reverses the transform order. Explicitly stored signs make the rotation reproducible across backends. We apply it at linear boundaries without moving it through nonlinearities. Rotation is an established conditioning technique [ 3 ] ; it conditions the quantization basis independently of the SQ and SHI code constructions.

Throughout the method, we distinguish stored codes from reconstructed operands and scaled weights. A three-bit code can expand to an INT8 operand while remaining compact in storage, although retaining the expanded bank increases resident memory. Memory accounting therefore separates code bits, channel parameters, transform data, prepared weight planes and temporary workspace.


#### 3.2 SQ integer reconstruction

SQ expands compact codes into INT4 or INT8 operands through three bitwise operations: append shared bits, apply a grouped shift, and truncate to the target width. These integer operations incorporate group-level information before multiplication, replacing floating-point groupwise reconstruction. The complete path is

[Tableau] $\displaystyle z$ $\displaystyle=(q\mathbin{\ll}m)+a_{g}$ $\displaystyle\text{(1) append shared bits},$ $\displaystyle v$ $\displaystyle=(z\mathbin{\ll}\ell)\mathbin{\gg}r_{g}$ $\displaystyle\text{(2) apply the grouped shift},$ $\displaystyle P$ $\displaystyle=\operatorname{clip}_{t}(v\mathbin{\gg}\delta)$ $\displaystyle\text{(3) truncate to the target width}.$

Here $q$ is the signed base code, $a_{g}$ and $r_{g}$ are shared group fields, and $t$ is the native operand width. The symbols $\ll$ and $\gg$ denote integer left shift and arithmetic right shift, with signed rounding defined below. Fixed alignment $\ell$ and final reduction $\delta$ determine the retained bit positions; they add no learned group fields. Appending expands the code, shifting supplies local power-of-two adaptation, and truncation produces the target-width operand. Local adaptation is thus incorporated during integer preparation, leaving channel scaling and output conversion outside the integer reduction.

We name an SQ format SQ $x$ -G $y$ A $m$ S $n$ : $x$ is the width of each base code $q$ , $y$ is the number of weights per group, $m$ is the width of the shared append field $a_{g}$ , and $n$ is the width of the group shift selector $r_{g}$ . For example, SQ2-G4A1S1 uses two-bit codes in groups of four, with one append bit and a one-bit shift selector per group. We describe the three operations below.


##### 3.2.1 Append shared bits

Let $q_{j,k}$ be a signed $x$ -bit code for output channel $j$ and input channel $k$ . Partition each weight row into groups of $y$ consecutive input channels, with $g(k)=\lfloor k/y\rfloor$ . Each group stores an $m$ -bit append field $a_{j,g}$ . The expanded integer is

[Tableau] $$z_{j,k}=2^{m}q_{j,k}+a_{j,g(k)},\quad\begin{cases}-2^{x-1}\leq q_{j,k}<2^{x-1},\\ 0\leq a_{j,g}<2^{m}.\end{cases}$$ (4)

The left shift opens $m$ low-order positions, which are filled by the shared field. This expands each code without storing all appended bits per element. For example, an INT4 base with four appended bits spans the INT8 range, while sharing those bits across $y=8$ weights adds only $4/8=0.5$ bits per weight. Shift fields and channel parameters contribute separately to storage.


##### 3.2.2 Apply the grouped shift

A shared selector $r_{j,g}$ positions the expanded code within the operand. Before integer rounding, a right shift by $r$ scales the value by $2^{-r}$ : shifts of one, two and three positions correspond to factors $1/2$ , $1/4$ and $1/8$ . Grouped shifting therefore provides local scale adaptation using integer instructions rather than floating-point multiplication.


##### 3.2.3 Truncate to the hardware width

The final step reduces the positioned value to a signed $t$ -bit operand. Define

[Tableau] $$R_{d}(v)=\left\lfloor v/2^{d}\right\rfloor,\qquad d\geq 0,$$ (5)

as arithmetic right shift, including its rounding for negative inputs. Combining the three steps gives

[Tableau] $$P_{j,k}=\operatorname{clip}_{t}\!\left(R_{\delta}\!\left(R_{r_{j,g(k)}}(2^{\ell}z_{j,k})\right)\right),$$ (6)

where $\ell\geq 0$ specifies alignment, $\delta\geq 0$ specifies the final reduction, and $\operatorname{clip}_{t}$ applies saturation if required. These parameters and the saturation rule are part of the representation. Saturation is inactive when all reduced values fit. Before rounding, the effective factor is $2^{\ell-r-\delta}$ ; fixed normalization is absorbed into the channel scales.

The same effective shift can be indexed in the opposite direction: a nonnegative expansion selector $s$ corresponds to $r=\ell-s$ when the selector ranges permit. Either convention specifies the same reconstruction only if rounding and range handling also agree. Truncation discards low-order bits after sufficiently wide intermediate computation; retaining the low byte of an overflowing value would instead cause wraparound.

A worked example of SQ reconstruction is given in Appendix A .


##### 3.2.4 Storage and arithmetic

For complete groups, the code storage is

[Tableau] $$B_{\mathrm{SQ}}=x+\frac{m+n}{y}\quad\text{bits per weight}.$$ (7)

Channel scales, optional offsets, transform signs and padding are additional. Signedness, shift interpretation and range handling complete the descriptor by specifying Equation ( 6 ).

SQ2–SQ6 primarily reconstruct INT8 operands. A tiled implementation unpacks and shifts codes into compute fragments or bounded staging buffers, then executes INT8 MMA with INT32 accumulation. Because $P$ already incorporates local adaptation, the dot product requires no floating-point group scale along $K$ . The resulting efficiency depends on decoding throughput, staging traffic and fusion with the contraction, all of which are included in timing. Power-of-two scaling and shared exponents are established techniques [ 28 ] ; SQ combines them with shared bit appending and width reduction to target conventional integer operands.


#### 3.3 SHI four-bit products and grouped corrections

SQ reconstructs an integer operand before multiplication. For some low-bit formats, that operand requires INT8 even though the stored code has four bits or fewer. We developed Shifter Integer (SHI) as a variant of SQ designed to use native INT4 arithmetic. When the reconstructed weight fits in INT4, it can be used directly; otherwise, SHI evaluates a low-bit base and a shared group correction through separate INT4 products. Ignoring channel scales for now, the latter representation can be written as

[Tableau] $$w_{j,k}=\rho q_{j,k}+r_{j,g(k)},$$ (8)

Here $q$ is the base operand for an individual weight, $r$ is a signed correction shared by its group, and $\rho$ sets the relative size of the two parts. The operands use a balanced representation of the stored fields; the exact conversion and any clipping are part of the numerical profile. SHI2–SHI4 use INT2 through INT4 base codes, so their base operands fit in signed INT4 and can use INT4 arithmetic. A five-bit or wider base would require a wider operand, such as INT8, and would not retain this advantage. For example, SHI4 G8 uses a four-bit base per weight and one four-bit correction per eight weights. These fields occupy $4+4/8=4.5$ bits per weight, before scales and other metadata.

Like other groupwise formats, SHI spends extra bits to improve the representation of each group. NVFP4 stores an eight-bit scale per 16 four-bit values, giving 4.5 bits per value before global scales and padding; MXFP4 stores an eight-bit scale per 32 values, giving 4.25 bits per value [ 24 , 25 ] . When both weights and activations use these formats, their effective rates are therefore W4.5A4.5 and W4.25A4.25, respectively. On hardware with native block-scaled FP4 instructions, the scales are applied within the matrix operation, allowing these formats to use native FP4 matrix throughput. SHI instead evaluates its group corrections through integer products.

The following SQ field structures illustrate how SHI preserves four-bit arithmetic:

- • SHI2: SQ2-G4A1S1. The expanded and shifted weight fits in a signed INT4 operand. It can therefore be reconstructed within the kernel and multiplied directly by an INT4 activation in one product. Because the activation has more bits than the two-bit base code, we primarily use the single-product path without a separate activation correction.

- • SHI3: SQ3-G8A3S1. The three-bit base and one-bit shift allow a four-bit main operand; the three append bits and shift allow a four-bit grouped correction operand under the selected reconstruction and range handling. We use two products to evaluate these weight components. The four-bit activation has more bits than the base code, so we primarily use the two-product path without an activation correction.

- • SHI4: SQ4-G8A4S0. With no shift selector, the four-bit base and four-bit shared append field provide the main and grouped weight components; a balanced representation maps them to signed operands. Because the activation also uses INT4, we use a corresponding SQ4-G8A4S0-style activation representation and a third product to include its grouped correction.

For SHI4 G8, we refer to the base-only, weight-corrected, and weight- and activation-corrected computations as W4A4, W4.5A4, and W4.5A4.5, respectively. The fractional bit counts describe the shared four-bit fields averaged over eight values; they do not change the INT4 operand width. The same product structures also apply to other SQ formats, whose effective bit counts may differ.

For SHI2, the single-product path uses the complete reconstructed INT4 weight. For a representation split into a base and correction, the base-only expression below illustrates the effect of omitting the correction. The weight- and activation-correction equations use the unsaturated representation in Equation ( 8 ); clipping a combined INT8 operand changes values at clipped entries.


##### 3.3.1 Main product (W4A4)

The simplest option multiplies the weight base by a four-bit approximation of the activations. Let $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ be an input row after rotation and input-channel scaling, and approximate each activation by $\xi_{k}\simeq sa_{k}$ , where $a_{k}$ is a four-bit code and $s$ is its scale. Using only the weight base gives

[Tableau] $$\widehat{y}^{\mathrm{base}}_{j}=c_{o,j}\rho s\sum_{k}q_{j,k}a_{k}.$$ (9)

This is one INT4 product across all $K$ input channels. It is the least expensive option, but it leaves out the stored weight correction.


##### 3.3.2 Grouped weight correction (W4.5A4)

The weight-corrected path adds the shared weight correction. Because every weight in a group shares the same correction, we can first sum the activations in that group and then multiply the sum by the correction:

[Tableau] $$\sum_{k}(\rho q_{j,k}+r_{j,g(k)})\xi_{k}=\rho\sum_{k}q_{j,k}\xi_{k}+\sum_{g}r_{j,g}\sum_{k\in g}\xi_{k}.$$ (10)

The first product still covers all $K$ weights. The correction product needs only one value per group, so it has length $K/y$ : for groups of eight, it is one eighth as long as the main product.

To run the correction product in INT4, we sum the activation codes, $h_{g}=\sum_{k\in g}a_{k}$ , and quantize the scaled sums as $sh_{g}\simeq tb_{g}$ . Here $b_{g}$ is a four-bit code and $t$ is a per-token scale. The two products compute

[Tableau] $$\widehat{y}^{\mathrm{weight}}_{j}=c_{o,j}\left[\rho s\sum_{k}q_{j,k}a_{k}+t\sum_{g}r_{j,g}b_{g}\right].$$ (11)

The second product approximates the contribution of the weight correction. The group sums can exceed the INT4 range, so quantizing them introduces error. The activations themselves still use only their base four-bit approximation.


##### 3.3.3 Grouped activation correction (W4.5A4.5)

The activation-corrected path adds a correction shared by each activation group:

[Tableau] $$\xi_{k}\simeq s(\tau a_{k}+c_{g(k)}),$$ (12)

Here $a$ and $c$ are four-bit codes and $\tau$ sets their relative size. To calculate the corrections efficiently, define the sum of the activation bases and the sum of the reconstructed weights in each group:

[Tableau] $$S_{a,g}=\sum_{k\in g}a_{k},\qquad S_{w,j,g}=\rho\sum_{k\in g}q_{j,k}+yr_{j,g}.$$ (13)

The product of the weight and activation representations can then be written as

[Tableau] $$y_{j}\simeq c_{o,j}s\left[\rho\tau\sum_{k}q_{j,k}a_{k}+\tau\sum_{g}r_{j,g}S_{a,g}+\sum_{g}S_{w,j,g}c_{g}\right].$$ (14)

The first term is the main product, the second accounts for the weight correction, and the third accounts for the activation correction. The weight sum includes $yr$ , so the third term also accounts for the interaction between the two corrections; no fourth product is needed.

The group sums can be wider than INT4, so we quantize them before the two correction products:

[Tableau] $$s\tau S_{a,g}\simeq tb_{g},\qquad S_{w,j,g}\simeq u_{j}d_{j,g},$$ (15)

Here $b$ and $d$ are four-bit codes, $t$ is a per-token scale, and $u_{j}$ is a scale fitted per output row. The executed three-product approximation is

[Tableau] $$\widehat{y}^{\mathrm{weight+activation}}_{j}=c_{o,j}\left[\rho\tau s\sum_{k}q_{j,k}a_{k}+t\sum_{g}r_{j,g}b_{g}+su_{j}\sum_{g}d_{j,g}c_{g}\right].$$ (16)

In the evaluated SHI4 construction, $\rho=\tau=16$ and all three products use four-bit operands. Quantizing the group sums introduces additional error, so the executed result in Equation ( 16 ) approximates the expansion in Equation ( 14 ).

SHI is designed to use the structure of the quantized weights during matrix multiplication, avoiding a full-length INT8 product when INT4 products are available. As a throughput-normalized comparison, if INT4 has twice the arithmetic throughput of INT8, an INT8 product of length $K$ corresponds to $2K$ of INT4 work. Table 1 counts only the INT4 contractions, not preparation or data movement. For groups of eight, the two- and three-product paths require $1.125K$ and $1.25K$ of work; with groups of sixteen, these fall to $1.0625K$ and $1.125K$ . Larger groups reduce correction work but may affect quantization accuracy. Runtime also depends on preparing and packing activations, forming group sums, moving data and converting the output. Multiple products can be fused into one kernel, so the number of products need not equal the number of launches.

A prepared weight-sum plane $d$ can increase resident memory. If four-bit $q$ , $r$ and $d$ are all retained, they require $4+8/y$ logical code bits per weight, excluding scales and padding. For G8, the serialized base and correction occupy 4.5 bits per weight, while this prepared three-plane representation occupies 5.0. The derived plane is included in resident-memory accounting when retained, even if it is generated at load time.

### 4 Projection-level evaluation

We first isolate one MLP projection to measure the accuracy and latency of each quantization path.


#### 4.1 Benchmark protocol

We isolate the layer-31 MLP down projection of Qwen3.8-27B ( $K=17{,}408$ , $N=5{,}120$ ) on an RTX 5090. All formats are fitted to the same BF16 weights using unweighted squared reconstruction error. We measure weight error by the relative Frobenius norm and output error by the mean relative $L_{2}$ error on 255 activation vectors per WikiText-2 context. We set the residual to zero when measuring output error and round the output to BF16.

We time each projection at 4,096 tokens. For cold-cache timing, llama.cpp starts with resident FP32 inputs and a ready FP32 residual and writes FP32 output. The Shifter and floating-point CUDA paths use BF16 inputs, residuals and outputs. The timed interval includes activation preparation, matrix multiplication, scaling, residual addition, backend conversions, host dispatch and synchronization. The tables report median latency and use llama.cpp Q2_K and Q4_K as the respective throughput baselines. Q2_K also serves as the reference format in the full-checkpoint comparison (Table 5 ). Packed bits per weight (bpw) count weight codes and group fields. Shifter also stores channel scales and H512 signs. The llama.cpp formats serve as software baselines [ 9 ] .

### 7 Conclusion

We introduced Shifter Quantization (SQ) and Shifter Integer (SHI) for low-bit inference with conventional integer matrix operations. SQ stores compact weight codes with shared group information and reconstructs integer operands through bitwise operations. This places local adaptation in the operand rather than requiring a floating-point group scale inside the matrix reduction. SHI builds on this representation to use four-bit products, evaluating grouped weight and activation corrections through shorter integer products when needed. Both approaches retain compact weights while allowing the main product to accumulate across the full reduction dimension. This structure aligns the computation with hardware-accelerated integer matrix instructions, helping translate low-bit storage into faster inference.

In the weight-only two-bit comparison, SQ2 and SHI2 reduce WikiText-2 KL by 28.2% and 23.8%, respectively, relative to a Q2_K recipe refit without calibration. SQ and SHI can assign shifts or corrections to small groups without introducing a floating-point scale for each group inside the main reduction. This finer granularity gives the representation more local flexibility and may contribute to the observed quality gains. SQ2 also achieves $2.77\times$ the Q2_K throughput on the tested projection with lower output error. Against the released Q2_K checkpoint, SQ2 and SHI2 reach $2.71\times$ and $3.77\times$ the 4,096-token prefill throughput, respectively.

SQ4 improves quality against the tested groupwise integer checkpoints and achieves $1.43$ – $2.47\times$ their NInfer prefill throughput across the two Qwen models and prompt lengths. At concurrency eight, SQ4 reaches $1.31\times$ and $1.25\times$ the decode throughput of the corresponding NInfer checkpoints for the 27B and 35B models, respectively. At eight bits, SQ8 reduces WikiText-2 KL by 72.7% and improves batch-one prefill throughput by 27.4% over online FP8.

Four-bit activation quantization and grouped corrections introduce additional approximation error, and their preparation and data movement contribute to runtime. SHI4 remains slower than native NVFP4 in the tested serving configurations.

Across four GPUs spanning Ampere, Ada, and Blackwell, SQ4 prefill throughput tracks dense INT8 throughput, while structured MTP3 decode tracks memory bandwidth.

### Appendix A SQ worked example

Consider four transformed and channel-scaled weights $(-0.28,-0.12,0.09,0.31)$ . To illustrate SQ2-G4A1S1, choose the base codes $q=(-2,-1,0,1)$ , a shared append bit $a_{g}=1$ , and a shift selector $r_{g}=1$ . Let the fixed alignment be $\ell=2$ , the final reduction be $\delta=1$ , and the output operand width be $t=8$ . The three operations give

[Tableau] $\displaystyle z$ $\displaystyle=2q+1=(-3,-1,1,3),$ $\displaystyle v$ $\displaystyle=(4z)\mathbin{\gg}1=(-6,-2,2,6),$ $\displaystyle P$ $\displaystyle=\operatorname{clip}_{8}(v\mathbin{\gg}1)=(-3,-1,1,3).$

All four values fit in INT8. With a row output scale of $0.1$ , they reconstruct to $(-0.3,-0.1,0.1,0.3)$ . Setting the append bit to zero with the same base codes and shifts gives $(-0.4,-0.2,0,0.2)$ . The shared bit therefore moves all four reconstructed weights by $0.1$ while costing only one bit for the group.


### Appendix B Activation quantization and numerical profiles


#### B.1 A4 clipping and grouped correction

For ordinary A4 quantization, choose a per-token step $S$ and form $a=\operatorname{clip}(\operatorname{round}(x/S))$ . The dynamic selection used in the evaluated implementation minimizes activation reconstruction error over clipping factors

[Tableau] $$\alpha\in\{0.5,\,0.625,\,0.75,\,0.875,\,1\},\qquad S_{\alpha}=\max(\alpha\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}/7,\epsilon).$$ (17)

Codes use nearest-even rounding and the specified clipping range. The selected scale is shared across the token. A static variant obtains the clipping factor from calibration while retaining token-dependent magnitude estimation. We evaluate static and dynamic clipping separately.

For the activation representation with grouped correction, set $s=S/16$ after ordinary scale selection. At this scale, fit $a$ and $c$ using two shared-correction initializations, 0 and 7. For each initialization, alternate clipped main-code rounding with a clipped, rounded group-mean residual correction, then perform one further update of each. Choose the lower squared-error result independently per group. The main codes preceding the final correction update are retained. This provides a small discrete search for Equation ( 12 ); it does not search all clipping factors again for each correction candidate. The activation-sum conversion has its own declared scale and clipping rule. The evaluated activation preparation rounds the rotated values to BF16 before input-channel scaling; fusing these stages preserves that rounding boundary.


#### B.2 A8 and A16 execution

A8 quantizes activations with per-token scales and executes INT8 products with INT32 accumulation, using the decoded SQ operand $P$ directly. A16 uses the specified BF16 or FP16 arithmetic and converts packed weights on demand or through staging. For small token counts, avoiding activation quantization can offset the cost of wider arithmetic. This path can operate without materializing the complete floating-point weight matrix.

Optional A8 outlier protection excludes selected large activations from the main quantized product and adds their contributions using the original values. The RMS threshold, per-token cap and correction arithmetic determine both its accuracy and cost. Each result therefore identifies activation precision and protection separately from the weight format.
