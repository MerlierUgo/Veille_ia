## FrameForge: Harnessing Priors of Video Foundation Models for Unified Image Generation

Unified conditional image generation requires a single model to preserve different aspects of a visual input while producing a target image that follows textual guidance. Pretrained video models provide a promising foundation by encoding visual transformations across time, yet existing approaches typically assign the condition and target to fixed temporal positions. We observe that condition-target relationships vary substantially across generation tasks, while video models naturally encode visual transformations at different temporal scales. This motivates us to model conditional generation through input-adaptive virtual temporal relations. We propose FrameForge , which captures two complementary aspects of such relations. Mixture of Temporal Experts (MoTE) models how far the target lies from the condition by dynamically selecting temporal RoPE experts with different virtual intervals. Chain of Temporal Reasoning (CoTR) models how the condition is transformed into the target through a latent intermediate state, with a reasoning-bottleneck attention mask encouraging information propagation through this pathway. Experiments on DreamBench, COCO2017, and GEdit-Bench show that FrameForge achieves superior performance in subject-driven generation, canny-to-image generation, and image editing among the compared methods.


### 1 Introduction

With recent advances in generative modeling [ 19 , 21 , 57 , 35 , 33 , 67 , 8 ] , foundation models such as Stable Diffusion [ 13 , 41 , 14 ] and FLUX [ 25 ] offer strong text-to-image generation capabilities. However, real-world generation often involves diverse inputs ( e.g . , text, reference images, and structural maps) that impose constraints at different semantic and spatial levels. Modeling these heterogeneous condition-target relations within a unified framework remains challenging.

> Figure 1: Motivation of FrameForge. Top: The LPIPS distances between frame pairs sampled from natural videos at different temporal intervals. Bottom: The condition-to-target LPIPS distances for image editing, subject-driven generation, and canny-to-image generation, where the dashed lines denote the median distance of each task.

Existing approaches to heterogeneous conditional image generation are predominantly image-centric. Some train unified models on large-scale multi-task datasets [ 42 , 2 , 46 , 9 , 11 , 56 , 53 ] , while others extend pretrained generators with control branches or lightweight adapters [ 63 , 60 , 61 , 47 , 65 ] . These approaches typically inject conditions as auxiliary spatial or semantic signals without explicitly modeling how strongly the condition and target differ or how one should be transformed into the other. This can make it difficult for a shared model to accommodate substantially different generation behaviors.

Pretrained video generation models provide a natural alternative by learning visual transformations across time. Recent studies exploit these priors by arranging the condition and target as different frames [ 54 , 27 , 31 , 6 ] . However, existing approaches typically assign the condition and target to fixed temporal positions, resulting in an invariant temporal positional relation across different tasks and samples. Such a fixed offset overlooks the variation in condition-target relations and may limit a single shared adapter.

Our key observation is that heterogeneous condition-target relations can be interpreted through a virtual temporal interval . In natural videos, frames separated by different temporal intervals exhibit different degrees of visual variation. A similar phenomenon arises in conditional image generation: image editing typically requires relatively small changes while preserving most source content, whereas subject-driven and canny-to-image generation often involve larger variations in appearance, structure, or scene composition. As shown in Fig. 1 , these tasks exhibit clearly different condition-target LPIPS distributions, while their variation ranges substantially overlap with those observed in natural video transitions. This suggests that different conditional generation tasks may correspond to different regions of the temporal transformation prior learned by a video model. The broad distributions within individual tasks further suggest that a task label alone cannot specify the required transformation magnitude. An input-dependent relation can account for both differences across tasks and variations among samples within the same task. Therefore, instead of assigning all condition-target pairs a fixed temporal offset, we model their relation with an input-adaptive virtual temporal interval.

This perspective reveals two complementary aspects of conditional generation: how far the target lies from the condition under the learned temporal prior, and how the condition should be transformed into the target. An adaptive interval addresses the former but does not uniquely determine the transformation. Modeling the latter requires a latent mechanism that learns condition-to-target transformations without explicit intermediate-frame supervision.

To this end, we propose FrameForge , a unified conditional image generation framework built upon a pretrained video foundation model. To model how far the target lies from the condition, Mixture of Temporal Experts (MoTE) selects among temporal RoPE experts with different virtual intervals using the joint textual and visual context. To model how the condition evolves into the target, Chain of Temporal Reasoning (CoTR) inserts a noise-initialized latent to form a condition-reasoning-target chain. The latent is progressively contextualized through attention with textual and visual tokens. During an intermediate training stage, a reasoning-bottleneck attention mask prevents target tokens from directly accessing text or condition tokens, encouraging information propagation through the reasoning latent. The MoTE router and a single shared LoRA are jointly optimized across all tasks, while the pretrained video backbone remains frozen. This design enables heterogeneous conditional generation without task-specific adapters.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

Conceptual contribution. We formulate heterogeneous conditional image generation from the perspective of input-adaptive virtual temporal relations in pretrained video models. Our analysis shows that different generation tasks exhibit distinct condition-target transformation magnitudes, motivating adaptive temporal relation modeling.

Technical contribution. We propose FrameForge, a unified adaptation framework that jointly models two complementary aspects of conditional generation. MoTE adaptively determines how far the target lies from the condition through input-dependent temporal positional priors, while CoTR provides a latent pathway to model how the condition is transformed into the target without explicit intermediate supervision. The entire framework is jointly trained across tasks with a single shared LoRA.

Experimental contribution. We conduct extensive experiments on subject-driven generation, canny-to-image generation, and instruction-guided image editing. Results on DreamBench, COCO2017, and GEdit demonstrate that a single unified FrameForge model achieves superior performance across all three tasks compared with task-specific and unified baselines. Comprehensive ablations further validate the effectiveness of the proposed MoTE and CoTR modules.


### 2 Related Work


#### 2.1 Unified Image Generation

Conditional image generation includes structure-guided synthesis [ 63 , 38 ] , subject-driven generation [ 44 , 60 ] , and instruction-guided editing [ 3 , 62 ] . Unified models use shared representations and parameters to preserve spatial layout, subject identity, or content outside the requested edit.

One line of research develops native unified models through large-scale multi-task training. PixWizard [ 30 ] adapts a flow-based diffusion transformer with textual and visual conditions. OmniGen [ 56 ] represents text, conditions, and targets as a multimodal sequence, while UniReal [ 9 ] treats input and output images as discontinuous video frames. OmniGen2 [ 53 ] connects a vision-language model to a diffusion transformer through decoupled text and image pathways. BAGEL [ 11 ] extends unification to visual understanding through interleaved multimodal pretraining, and Lance [ 15 ] combines shared context modeling, separate capability pathways, and staged image/video training.

Another line adapts pretrained image generators. OminiControl [ 47 ] integrates condition-image tokens into a DiT’s multimodal sequence and reuses its attention parameters for spatially aligned and non-aligned controls. ACE++ [ 37 ] organizes references, masks, and target latents into unified context units. EasyControl [ 65 ] uses isolated condition branches for plug-and-play integration and zero-shot composition, with position-aware training and KV caching for flexible resolution and efficient inference. Concurrent work, TAG-MoE [ 58 ] , alleviates inter-task interference through sparse FFN experts and routing aligned with hierarchical task semantics. Unlike TAG-MoE, which routes image tokens to semantically specialized FFN experts, our MoTE retains shared feature-processing parameters and selects among temporal RoPE experts to model an input-dependent virtual temporal interval.


#### 2.2 Video Foundation Model Adaptation

Video diffusion models learn appearance and inter-frame variation through motion modules, as in AnimateDiff [ 17 ] , or video diffusion transformers, as in Latte [ 36 ] , SANA-Video [ 7 ] , CogVideoX [ 59 ] , Wan [ 49 ] , and HunyuanVideo [ 23 , 48 ] . Adapting these priors to image tasks requires relating the condition and target to the pretrained temporal structure.

RealGeneral [ 31 ] formulates conditional generation as two-frame prediction with unified conditional embeddings, decoupled modulation, and attention masking. DRA-Ctrl [ 6 ] uses mixup-based shot transitions to bridge continuous video dynamics and discontinuous image pairs. iMontage [ 16 ] supports variable-length many-to-many generation by treating images as pseudo-frames and using Marginal RoPE to distinguish input and output sets from natural temporal ordering. Video4Edit [ 27 ] transfers intermediate evolution trajectories from a video-pretrained teacher to a student editor. ChronoEdit [ 54 ] targets physically consistent editing by jointly denoising the target and temporal reasoning tokens, which are discarded during later denoising steps.

Beyond task-specific adaptation, Wiedemer et al. [ 52 ] report emergent zero-shot abilities. Moreover, Acuaviva et al. [ 1 ] examine these capabilities under controlled adaptation, providing evidence that video pretraining offers transferable priors for structured visual transformations.

Existing studies demonstrate the potential of video models as general foundations for image-level generation and visual reasoning. However, they are either tailored to individual tasks or fail to explicitly mitigate inter-task interference within a shared parameter-efficient adapter. Our framework addresses this issue by adapting the virtual temporal relation to each input and modeling the condition-to-target transformation.


### 3 Method

FrameForge adapts a pretrained video diffusion model to conditional image generation through two complementary operations: selecting an input-dependent temporal positional relation and learning an intermediate representation for condition-to-target prediction. We first introduce the preliminaries and framework overview in Sections 3.1 and 3.2 , and then describe MoTE and CoTR in Sections 3.3 and 3.4 . The training stages are detailed in Section 3.5 .

> Figure 2: Overview of FrameForge. Heterogeneous conditional image generation is formulated as target-frame prediction with a pretrained video diffusion model. The MM-DiT backbone processes text, condition, reasoning, and noisy target tokens to predict the target velocity. Mixture of Temporal Experts (MoTE) selects a temporal RoPE configuration at each denoising step, adapting the virtual interval between the condition and target. Chain of Temporal Reasoning (CoTR) adds a noise-initialized intermediate latent whose hidden representation is updated through attention. During the intermediate training stage, a stochastic attention mask blocks direct text/condition-target attention to encourage use of this intermediate pathway.


#### 3.1 Preliminaries

Modern video generation models typically combine a 3D VAE with a diffusion transformer to model visual content and temporal dynamics in a compressed latent space. We instantiate FrameForge with the first-stage video diffusion model of HunyuanVideo1.5 [ 48 ] . Its VAE encoder $\mathcal{E}(\cdot)$ compresses the spatial and temporal dimensions by factors of $16$ and $4$ , respectively. Given a video $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{(4f+1)\times 16h\times 16w\times c}$ , the encoded latent is

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{z}_{0}=\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{(f+1)\times h\times w\times 32}.$$ (1)

The MM-DiT jointly processes textual and visual tokens, with 3D rotary positional embeddings (3D-RoPE) encoding the temporal and spatial coordinates of visual tokens. The backbone is trained with flow matching [ 32 ] , where the clean latent $\mathbf{z}_{0}$ is interpolated with Gaussian noise to obtain $\mathbf{z}_{t}$ , and the corresponding velocity $\mathbf{v}$ is predicted by minimizing

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diffusion}}=\left\|\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{t},t,\mathbf{c})\right\|_{2}^{2},$$ (2)

where $\mathbf{c}$ denotes the conditioning inputs.


#### 3.2 Framework Overview

As shown in Fig. 2 , FrameForge formulates conditional image generation as target-frame prediction guided by textual and visual conditions. The target image is encoded and perturbed, while the visual conditions are encoded into $\mathbf{z}^{\mathrm{cond}}$ using the frozen VAE encoder. In addition, CoTR introduces a noise-initialized reasoning latent $\mathbf{z}^{\mathrm{reason}}$ . Together with the text representations $\mathbf{z}^{\mathrm{text}}$ , these inputs are jointly processed by the backbone, while MoTE determines their temporal positional configurations. The overall conditioning is expressed as

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{ours}}=\left[\mathbf{z}^{\mathrm{text}},\mathbf{z}^{\mathrm{cond}},\mathbf{z}^{\mathrm{reason}}\right].$$ (3)


#### 3.3 Mixture of Temporal Experts (MoTE)

The 3D-RoPE in the video backbone encodes the temporal coordinates of visual tokens and thereby determines the positional prior for cross-frame interaction. Under conditional frame prediction, using a fixed temporal gap assigns the same positional relation to all condition-target pairs, despite their variations across tasks, samples, and denoising steps. To provide a more flexible temporal prior, we introduce Mixture of Temporal Experts (MoTE), which constructs multiple temporal RoPE experts corresponding to different virtual intervals and uses a lightweight router to select one expert according to the text, condition, and noisy target representations.

Temporal RoPE experts. We construct $E$ temporal positional experts with candidate latent spans $\mathcal{S}=\{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{E}\}$ , where $s_{e}$ denotes the number of positions along the virtual latent temporal axis for expert $e$ . Assigning the condition and target to the first and last positions yields a virtual temporal interval of $\Delta_{e}=s_{e}-1$ . For a causal 3D VAE with temporal compression factor $r_{t}$ , this latent span corresponds to an equivalent video-frame span of $F_{e}=r_{t}(s_{e}-1)+1$ , including both endpoints. In our main implementation, $r_{t}=4$ , and the default video-frame spans $\{17,69,121\}$ correspond to latent spans $\{5,18,31\}$ .

For expert $e$ , we first construct the rotary embeddings over a virtual latent grid of size $(s_{e},h,w)$ :

[Tableau] $$\left(\mathbf{R}^{\cos}_{e},\mathbf{R}^{\sin}_{e}\right)=\operatorname{RoPE}\left(s_{e},h,w\right).$$ (4)

For condition-target prediction, we retain the embeddings at temporal coordinates $0$ and $s_{e}-1$ . With CoTR, we additionally retain the embeddings at an intermediate temporal position for the reasoning latent.

Sample-wise Top-1 routing. For each sample, the router takes the concatenated text, condition, and noisy target representations as input, and produces token-level routing logits:

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{A}=G\left(\left[\mathbf{z}^{\mathrm{text}},\mathbf{z}^{\mathrm{cond}},\mathbf{z}^{\mathrm{target}}_{t}\right]\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times E},$$ (5)

where $L$ denotes the routing sequence length and $E$ the number of temporal experts. We then perform masked average pooling over valid tokens to obtain a sample-level logit vector $\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{E}$ , excluding padded text tokens.

The routing probabilities and Top-1 expert assignment are given by

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{p}=\operatorname{Softmax}\left(\frac{\mathbf{a}}{\tau_{\mathrm{router}}}\right),\qquad e^{*}=\mathop{\arg\max}_{e\in\{1,\ldots,E\}}p_{e},$$ (6)

where $\tau_{\mathrm{router}}$ controls the sharpness of the routing distribution. Moreover, to enable end-to-end optimization with discrete routing, we adopt a straight-through estimator:

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{g}=\operatorname{sg}\left(\mathbf{g}^{\mathrm{hard}}-\mathbf{p}\right)+\mathbf{p},$$ (7)

where $\mathbf{g}^{\mathrm{hard}}$ is the one-hot gate corresponding to the selected expert $e^{*}$ , and $\operatorname{sg}(\cdot)$ denotes the stop-gradient operation. Therefore, the forward pass uses a single selected expert, while gradients are propagated through the soft routing probabilities. At inference time, we directly set $\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{g}^{\mathrm{hard}}$ .

The routed rotary embeddings are then computed as

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{R}^{\cos}=\sum_{e=1}^{E}g_{e}\mathbf{R}^{\cos}_{e},\qquad\mathbf{R}^{\sin}=\sum_{e=1}^{E}g_{e}\mathbf{R}^{\sin}_{e},$$ (8)

and applied to the visual queries and keys in self-attention.

Load-balancing regularization. To prevent routing collapse and encourage balanced utilization of the temporal experts, we introduce a batch-level load-balancing loss. Let $\bar{g}_{e}$ denote the mean gate value of expert $e$ over a batch of size $B$ . The balancing loss is defined as

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{balance}}=-\sum_{e=1}^{E}\log\left(\bar{g}_{e}+\epsilon\right),$$ (9)

where $\epsilon$ is a small constant for numerical stability.

The overall training objective is

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{final}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diffusion}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{bal}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{balance}},$$ (10)

where $\lambda_{\mathrm{bal}}$ controls the strength of regularization.


#### 3.4 Chain of Temporal Reasoning (CoTR)

Intermediate latent representation. CoTR introduces an intermediate latent state between the condition and target to model the implicit condition-to-target transformation. Specifically, we insert a single reasoning latent $\mathbf{z}^{\mathrm{reason}}$ , sampled from a standard Gaussian distribution with the same spatial and channel dimensions as the visual latents. Rather than being explicitly supervised or decoded, this latent is progressively contextualized through the MM-DiT blocks by interacting with the text, condition, and target representations. It therefore serves as a latent pathway for aggregating conditional information before target prediction. The model is trained solely with the target-frame objective in Eq. 10 , without requiring intermediate supervision.

Reasoning-bottleneck attention mask. Simply inserting the reasoning latent does not ensure that the model makes effective use of it, since target tokens can still directly attend to the text and condition tokens. To discourage this shortcut, we apply a stochastic reasoning-bottleneck attention mask with probability $p_{\mathrm{mask}}$ during the intermediate training stage.

Let $\mathcal{T}$ , $\mathcal{C}$ , $\mathcal{R}$ , and $\mathcal{X}$ denote the text, condition, reasoning, and target token sets, respectively. The mask $\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{cotr}}$ is added to the self-attention logits:

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{cotr}}_{ij}=\begin{cases}-\infty,&i\in\mathcal{T}\cup\mathcal{C},\;j\in\mathcal{X},\\ -\infty,&i\in\mathcal{X},\;j\in\mathcal{T}\cup\mathcal{C},\\ 0,&\mathrm{otherwise},\end{cases}$$ (11)

where $i$ and $j$ index query and key tokens, respectively.

The mask removes direct attention between the target and the text/condition tokens while preserving the reasoning latent as a communication bridge between them. Consequently, target tokens receive conditional information through the evolving reasoning representation rather than through a direct attention path. For samples without masking, the original full-attention pattern is retained. This masking only modifies attention connectivity and does not affect the temporal positional conditioning introduced by MoTE.


#### 3.5 Training Strategy

We freeze the pretrained video-backbone weights and the VAE encoder, and insert a single LoRA adapter [ 20 ] into the attention and feed-forward projections of the MM-DiT.

Three-stage optimization. In the first stage, we optimize only the MoTE router with the condition-target formulation, keeping the LoRA parameters fixed and omitting the reasoning latent. This stage learns temporal routing before introducing the additional intermediate pathway. In the second stage, we add the reasoning latent, jointly optimize the router and LoRA, and apply the stochastic mask in Eq. 11 . In the final stage, we retain both modules and continue joint optimization with full attention. The final stage matches the attention configuration used at inference, where the router selects a temporal expert at each denoising step. Furthermore, the shared adapter is used for every task.


### 4 Experiments


#### 4.1 Implementation Details

Tasks. We evaluate FrameForge on three representative conditional image generation tasks: subject-driven generation, canny-to-image generation, and instruction-guided image editing. These tasks respectively assess subject consistency, structural fidelity, and semantic controllability, covering diverse condition-to-target relations within a unified framework.

Training data. For subject-driven generation, we filter Subject200K [ 47 ] and UNO-1M [ 55 ] to obtain high-quality reference-target subject pairs with variations in pose, viewpoint, and scene. For canny-to-image generation, we use a subset of 512-2M [ 47 ] , where the extracted edge map serves as the condition and the corresponding image as the target. For image editing, we sample instruction-based editing pairs from the HQ-Edit and UltraEdit subsets of GPT-Image-Edit-1.5M [ 50 ] . We use 170K samples per task, resulting in 510K training samples in total. All images are resized to $512\times 512$ .

Benchmarks and metrics. For subject-driven generation, we evaluate on DreamBench [ 44 ] using one reference image for each of 30 subjects and 25 prompts per subject. We adopt VIEScore [ 24 ] with GPT-4o and report semantic consistency ( $SC$ ), perceptual quality ( $PQ$ ), and overall score ( $O$ ).

For canny-to-image generation, we use the 5,000-image COCO2017 validation set [ 29 ] , resized to $512\times 512$ , with text descriptions generated by GPT-4o. We report FID [ 18 ] , SSIM [ 51 ] , and CLIP-T [ 43 ] for distributional quality, structural fidelity, and text-image alignment, respectively.

For image editing, we evaluate on the English subset of GEdit-Bench [ 34 ] , containing 606 samples across 11 editing categories. Following its official protocol, we report semantic consistency ( $G$ - $SC$ ), perceptual quality ( $G$ - $PQ$ ), and overall score ( $G$ - $O$ ) using GPT-4.1. The $G$ - prefix distinguishes GEdit-Bench metrics from the VIEScore metrics used for subject-driven generation.

Training and Inference. Our model, based on HunyuanVideo1.5-T2V-720P and fine-tuned using LoRA with a rank and alpha of 32, has 182M trainable parameters. The model is trained for 12,000 iterations on two NVIDIA A800 GPUs, with a global batch size of 60 and a learning rate of $3\times 10^{-4}$ , requiring approximately 6 days. For the MoTE router, the softmax temperature is fixed to $\tau_{\mathrm{router}}=1.0$ , and the load-balancing loss is weighted by $\lambda_{\mathrm{bal}}=0.01$ . During the intermediate training stage, we apply the reasoning-bottleneck attention mask with probability $p_{\mathrm{mask}}=0.5$ ; the final stage and inference use full attention. During inference, we apply classifier-free guidance to the text condition, with the guidance scale $\omega$ set to 6 for subject-driven generation and editing, and to 2 for canny-to-image generation. We use 50 denoising steps for all tasks.

##### 4.2.4 MoTE Routing Analysis

Fig. 6 reports expert selection ratios over 50 denoising steps, averaged over 500 cases. The MoTE router exhibits distinct task-dependent and timestep-dependent preferences.

> Figure 6: Visualization of MoTE routing behavior for editing, subject-driven generation, and canny-to-image. Gate 0, Gate 1, and Gate 2 denote the short-, medium-, and long-interval temporal RoPE experts, respectively. Each curve reports the average expert usage ratio over 500 cases at each denoising step.

Canny-to-image generation, which has the largest condition-target distance, predominantly selects the long-interval expert, particularly during early denoising. Subject-driven generation and editing initially favor the medium-interval expert while target latents remain dominated by noise, with a higher selection ratio for subject-driven generation. As target content emerges, both shift toward the short-interval expert. Editing makes this transition earlier and more strongly, consistent with greater source-content preservation. These within-task shifts suggest that expert selection responds to the evolving target representation, rather than assigning each task a fixed temporal interval. These results complement the dataset-level analysis in Fig. 1 , showing that MoTE dynamically adapts the virtual temporal relation to both the task and denoising stage.


#### 4.3 Ablations

Unless otherwise specified, ablations report overall scores for subject-driven generation and editing (higher is better), and FID for canny-to-image generation (lower is better).

Table 4 compares task-specific and shared LoRA adaptation. Joint training improves subject-driven generation but degrades editing, suggesting greater task interference for editing. Adding MoTE or CoTR individually mainly improves editing, raising its score from 4.94 to 5.77 or 5.56, respectively, with small changes on the other tasks. When combined, the full model achieves the best overall performance, with the highest scores on subject-driven generation and editing and the lowest FID on canny-to-image generation. This demonstrates that MoTE and CoTR are complementary in adapting the video diffusion backbone to heterogeneous generation tasks.

Notably, neither module alone recovers the task-specific editing score of 5.99, whereas their combination reaches 6.12 and also improves the other two tasks. ### 5 Conclusion

In this work, we presented FrameForge, a unified adaptation framework that leverages the temporal priors of pretrained video foundation models for heterogeneous conditional image generation. Rather than assigning a fixed temporal relation to all condition-target pairs, we introduced an input-adaptive virtual temporal relation perspective. Based on this formulation, MoTE selects an appropriate temporal positional prior from multiple interval experts according to the textual and visual context. CoTR introduces an unsupervised reasoning latent and a reasoning-bottleneck attention mask to model the unobserved condition-to-target transformation. The proposed framework enables joint adaptation across subject-driven generation, canny-to-image generation, and image editing while keeping the video foundation model frozen. Experiments on DreamBench, COCO2017, and GEdit demonstrate strong performance across all three tasks, and the ablation studies verify the effectiveness of both adaptive temporal relation modeling and implicit temporal reasoning.
