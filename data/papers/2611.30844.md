## DyLoMo-DiT: Dynamic Looped Mixture-of-Experts Diffusion Transformers

Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) scale model capacity by increasing both parameter count and computation. Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) variants expand parameter capacity while keeping per-token computation roughly fixed, but still require substantial parameter storage. In this paper, we propose Dynamic Looped MoE DiT (DyLoMo-DiT), which reuses parameters rather than adding them: a weight-shared loop repeatedly applies the middle layers, while sparse experts allocate parameter capacity across tokens. This design deepens the network without adding parameters, freeing the parameter budget for other uses, such as more experts or more distinct layers. It also allocates compute dynamically, varying the number of passes across noise levels. Experiments show that DyLoMo-DiT makes more effective use of parameter and compute budgets, with further gains from assigning more passes to low-noise steps. On ImageNet $256\times 256$ , DyLoMo-DiT reduces FID by 11–24% relative to DiT and 36–56% relative to DiT-MoE at matched parameter and compute budgets. For text-to-image generation, a scaled DyLoMo-DiT achieves a higher GenEval score than FLUX.1 (0.714 vs. 0.673), with less than one tenth of its active parameters (1.05B vs. 12B).

### 1 Introduction

> Figure 2 : FID versus training steps on ImageNet-256: DiT (top; B, L, XL tiers) and DiT-MoE (bottom; S, B, L tiers) against the DyLoMo-DiT with the same parameters and forward compute. Percentages give the relative FID reduction at 400k steps.

As diffusion models grow in size, scaling them to improve generation quality also increases computational and memory costs [ 21 , 6 , 68 ] . More efficient scaling therefore requires making better use of model parameters and computation, rather than simply increasing both.

DiT [ 52 ] applies the same dense layer stack at every denoising step. Increasing its width raises both the parameter count and per-token computation, while adding independently parameterized layers increases both depth and parameter count. Although timestep conditioning changes the network’s activations, it does not change the layers or computation used at each step. MoE-DiTs such as DiT-MoE [ 23 ] use sparse experts [ 61 ] to expand parameter capacity while keeping per-token computation roughly fixed, but still require substantial parameter storage. Both designs maintain uniform depth across noise levels, even though high-noise steps primarily establish global structure and low-noise steps refine detail [ 5 ] .

Depth offers another way to use model parameters more efficiently. Recurrently applying a group of weight-shared layers increases effective depth while storing only one set of parameters for the repeated layers. This reduces the parameter cost of added depth and frees budget for experts. Recurrent depth has been explored in language models and, more recently, in diffusion Transformers [ 28 , 12 , 19 , 73 ] . However, recurrence alone does not determine where the additional computation is most useful: a fixed pass count applies the same extra computation at every noise level, while dense feed-forward networks lack MoE’s token-dependent parameter capacity. In our matched-budget ablation, a uniform two-pass loop does not improve FID at the DiT-MoE-B budget ( Sec. 5.4 ). These observations motivate combining parameter-efficient recurrent depth with sparse experts and adapting computation across the denoising trajectory.

In this paper, we propose Dynamic Looped MoE DiT (DyLoMo-DiT), an architecture that jointly allocates parameter capacity, computation, and depth under a target parameter and compute budget. Its feed-forward networks use multi-head sparse experts to route each token to a small subset of experts, expanding parameter capacity across tokens while keeping per-token computation nearly fixed. A group of middle Transformer layers shares weights across recurrent passes, adding effective depth without storing a separate copy of the layer parameters. The resulting parameter savings can be reinvested in more experts. DyLoMo-DiT also uses a dynamic loop schedule that varies the number of passes with the noise level. Assigning more passes to low-noise inputs, rather than applying the same number of passes throughout denoising, brings further gains. This design differs from DiT, which uses a fixed dense layer stack, and MoE-DiT, which expands token-dependent parameter capacity but retains uniform depth across noise levels. The expert configuration, looped segment, routing heads, and pass schedule are jointly selected for the target budget.

We evaluate DyLoMo-DiT on ImageNet $256\times 256$ and in text-to-image generation. At matched parameter and forward-compute budgets, DyLoMo-DiT reduces FID by 11%, 14%, and 24% relative to DiT in the B, L, and XL tiers, respectively, and by 36%, 56%, and 56% relative to DiT-MoE in the S, B, and L tiers under the same training protocol. The advantage persists against scaled-up baselines: a DiT with three times the parameters and compute still has 13% higher FID, while the original DiT-MoE-XL configuration, with six times the parameters, has 63% higher FID. Further analyses show how the joint design contributes to these gains: at the DiT-MoE-B budget, progressively adding the design choices lowers FID from 16.06 to 5.28 ( Sec. 5.4 ). Extra passes improve results primarily at low noise, while routers without explicit timestep inputs learn to specialize by noise level. Finally, a scaled text-to-image variant scores higher than FLUX.1 on GenEval at $1024^{2}$ (0.714, compared with 0.673 for FLUX.1-schnell and 0.671 for FLUX.1-dev) with less than one tenth as many active parameters (1.05B vs. 12B).

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

- • Parameter-efficient recurrent depth for diffusion Transformers. DyLoMo-DiT reuses a group of middle layers to increase effective depth without duplicating their parameters, freeing budget for sparse experts.

- • Dynamic loop allocation across noise levels. DyLoMo-DiT assigns different numbers of passes along the denoising trajectory, with more passes at low noise.

- • Joint allocation of parameters, computation, and depth under a shared budget. Sparse experts allocate capacity across tokens, weight sharing controls the parameter cost of added depth, and the loop schedule distributes computation across noise levels.

- • A scalable design space for dense and sparse-expert diffusion Transformers. DyLoMo-DiT scales across model sizes and includes DiT, MoE-DiT, and looped DiT as special cases.


### 2 Related Work

Parameters without compute: sparse experts in Transformers. DiT-MoE [ 23 ] introduces per-token sparse experts into DiT. Subsequent work improves expert routing or extends sparse experts to other backbones and tasks [ 79 , 64 , 62 , 74 , 36 , 1 ] . DiffMoE and ProMoE report that token-choice sparse experts often underperform dense models when active parameter counts are matched. Another line of work assigns experts explicitly to different noise stages [ 5 , 24 , 22 , 50 , 49 , 69 , 11 ] . Our method does not use such an assignment, but Sec. 5.7 shows that its routing learns a similar split. Per-head routing has been explored in Multi-Head Mixture-of-Experts [ 75 ] , Multi-Head LatentMoE [ 14 ] , and MAGI-2 [ 59 ] . None of these methods shares weights across depth, so depth remains tied to parameter count.

Depth without parameters: weight-shared Transformers. The Universal Transformer [ 17 ] applies the same transformation recurrently across depth. SMELT [ 72 ] compares looped and non-looped MoE language models at matched compute, parameter count, and KV cache, and recommends looping through roughly half of the layers—located in the middle of the network—for two passes, with residual updates scaled by $1/r$ . For visual generation, Fixed Point Diffusion Models [ 4 ] incorporate fixed-point solver layers into the denoiser. ELT, Looped DiT, LiFT, and LoopREG [ 28 , 12 , 19 , 73 ] , three of which are concurrent with this work, apply loops to some or all layers of a DiT. However, they retain dense feed-forward networks, leaving parameter count tied to computation.

Removing both couplings. Several language-model studies combine sparse experts with recurrence [ 65 , 13 , 8 , 25 , 72 ] . Lee et al . [ 38 ] find that looped MoE models benefit more from scaling than the standard baseline, whereas looped dense models do not. Subsequent work explores using more passes, the roles of the two visits, scaling laws with respect to pass count, experts shared across layers, and per-token recursion depth [ 30 , 31 , 9 , 35 , 54 , 2 ] . To our knowledge, no diffusion Transformer has removed both couplings while allocating depth by noise level. This work does so under the standard DiT protocol. We evaluate multi-stream residuals [ 82 , 77 , 29 , 67 , 26 ] separately; they are not part of our method ( App. I ).


### 3 Method


#### 3.1 Three Resources and the Design Space

DiT ties parameter count, forward compute, and depth (the number of layers each token traverses) to its width and layer count, using the same configuration for every token and noise level. DyLoMo-DiT makes these choices separately configurable ( Fig. 3 ): width $d$ ; $L$ physical layers, including a middle segment of $m$ weight-shared layers repeated according to the noise-dependent schedule $r(t)$ ; and $H$ routing heads per feed-forward network, each with $E$ experts and $k$ selected per token. Weight sharing adds effective depth without duplicating parameters, freeing budget for experts. DiT, MoE-DiT, and looped DiT correspond to one expert and one pass, one pass, and one expert, respectively ( Table 1 ).

Otherwise, DyLoMo-DiT follows DiT [ 52 ] : patchified VAE latents are conditioned through adaLN-Zero using timestep and class embeddings, and the output layer predicts noise and variance. Training uses the DiT objective without auxiliary losses, loop-specific objectives, or pass embeddings. Further implementation details are in App. H .

(b) A looped layer: adaLN-Zero modulates the input and gates the output, scaled by $1/r$ . #### 3.2 Parameters per Token: Multi-Head Sparse Experts

Except in the first and last layers, every feed-forward network is a multi-head sparse mixture of experts: a $d\times d$ linear layer transforms the sublayer input $x$ and splits it into $H$ heads of $d/H$ dimensions, each with its own router and $E$ experts. Each router gives its experts sigmoid scores, selects the top $k$ after adding a load-balancing bias, and weights them by the scores $L_{1}$ -normalized over the $k$ and multiplied by a fixed constant $\alpha$ ( App. H ). The head outputs are concatenated, passed through a linear layer, and added to the output of a shared expert that is always on:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle y$ $\displaystyle=W_{\mathrm{merge}}\Big[\textstyle\sum_{e\in\mathcal{T}^{(j)}}w^{(j)}_{e}\,E^{(j)}_{e}\big(u^{(j)}\big)\Big]_{j=1}^{H}+E_{\mathrm{shared}}(x),$ (1) $\displaystyle u$ $\displaystyle=W_{\mathrm{split}}\,x.$

Each expert is a gated feed-forward network $d/H\to f\to d/H$ with a gpt-oss-style [ 48 ] clamped SwiGLU [ 60 ] activation; at the XL tier, $H=4$ , $E=16$ , $k=6$ and $f=640$ , so each token activates 24 routed experts per layer (other tiers and single-head models: App. H ). The structure draws on Multi-Head Mixture-of-Experts [ 75 ] and the public code and model of MAGI-2 [ 59 ] , the shared expert follows DeepSeekMoE [ 15 ] , and the balancing follows DeepSeek-V3 [ 70 , 16 ] . No auxiliary loss is used: after each optimizer step the expert biases, which take part only in selection, are updated from the routing counts.


#### 3.3 Depth from a Weight-Shared Loop

The $L$ physical layers form a front, a middle and a back segment, the middle one of $m$ consecutive layers: a forward pass executes the front segment, the middle segment $r$ times, and the back segment, with the $r$ passes sharing one set of parameters (attention, experts, routers and adaLN modulation), so depth grows with $r$ while the parameter count does not. Each sublayer of the middle segment writes to the residual with a factor of $1/r$ :

[Tableau] $$h\leftarrow h+\tfrac{1}{r}\,g(c)\odot F\big(\mathrm{adaLN}(h,c)\big),$$ (2)

where $F$ is the attention or feed-forward sublayer and $g(c)$ the adaLN-Zero gate; layers outside the segment have $r=1$ . Routing is recomputed in every pass, so a token may select different experts in different passes. The recipe follows SMELT [ 72 ] , whose ablation favors looping about half of the layers, in the middle, over the full stack, and the factor $1/r$ follows Loop- $\mu$ P [ 71 ] , keeping the updates of the shared weights from accumulating across visits; under the ImageNet protocol the middle segment is also the best among front, back, middle and all layers ( App. G ).


#### 3.4 Depth by Noise Level: Allocating the Passes

The number of passes is a function of the noise level: the recommended schedule runs $r=3$ for $t<500$ and $r=1$ for $t\geq 500$ , two passes on average over uniformly spaced sampling steps, so the mean compute equals that of two passes at every timestep while a token passes through 30 layers at low noise and 16 at high noise (XL tier). The schedule is part of the model: the timestep of each training sample decides its number of passes, sampling uses the same schedule, and a model trained under one schedule must be run under that schedule; when the number of passes is instead drawn from $\{1,2,3\}$ at every training step, one set of weights can be run under several schedules, so depth can be chosen at inference. Section 5.6 compares schedules; the return on extra passes ( Sec. 5.5 ) is also observed on an in-house video generation model ( App. G ).


#### 3.5 Configuring a Model for a Budget

Because the three resources share one budget, a member is configured as a whole: the width and the number of layer visits set the forward compute, matched within 1%; the loop decides how many of these visits are distinct layers (a shared middle segment of a third to a half of the layers leaves fewer physical layers than a loop-free model has at the same compute); and the number of experts fills the parameter budget, matched within about 2%, so the parameters that the loop saves go to the experts. At the budget of DiT-XL/2 (28 layers, 0.68B parameters, 118.6 GMAC), DyLoMo-DiT-XL has 16 physical layers and 23 layer visits on average; removing the loop and spending the saved parameters on independent layers gives the single-pass member at the same budget (23 layers), which Secs. 5.1 and 5.4 use to isolate the loop (all configurations: App. H ).


### 4 Experimental Setup

To ensure a fair comparison with DiT and DiT-MoE, we train all class-conditional models on ImageNet [ 18 ] using the same training setup. We also train a single text-to-image model, without ablations, to assess whether DyLoMo-DiT extends to text-conditioned generation. This model scales up the architecture in Sec. 3 : it has width 1024, 32 physical layers with the middle 16 looped, 4 routing heads with 128 experts each, 15.48B total parameters, and 1.05B active parameters per image token. It differs from the class-conditional models in three respects: text and image tokens form a single sequence, with conditioning written into each token’s input rather than injected through adaLN; it uses a 4-stream residual, which trains normally without adaLN and improves performance ( App. I ); and it is trained with the rectified-flow objective [ 43 ] .

Training details. All ImageNet models follow a common pipeline based on the DiT recipe: VAE latents [ 56 ] , the DDPM objective [ 33 , 47 ] , a global batch size of 256, 400k training steps, AdamW [ 44 ] with a constant learning rate of $10^{-4}$ , and EMA with decay 0.9999 ( App. H ). We train the DiT baselines from scratch using the official model definitions; their unguided FID agrees with Table 4 of the DiT paper ( App. H ). We implement DiT-MoE [ 23 ] based on its public code ( Sec. 5.2 , App. J ).

ImageNet-256. We evaluate class-conditional generation on ImageNet at $256\times 256$ , following the DiT protocol [ 52 ] . We use EMA weights and sample with 250-step DDPM and classifier-free guidance [ 32 ] at scale 1.5. Every 100k training steps, we generate 50k images per checkpoint and evaluate FID, sFID [ 46 ] , IS [ 58 ] , Precision, and Recall [ 37 ] using the ADM evaluator [ 20 ] .

Compute matching. Because both model capacity and inference cost matter, we match each DyLoMo-DiT model to its baseline in total parameter count and forward compute, unless stated otherwise. We measure forward compute as the number of multiply-accumulates per image in one forward pass. GMAC denotes $10^{9}$ multiply-accumulates, counted in the same way as the Gflops reported in the DiT paper. For models with noise-dependent loop schedules, we average compute over the 250 uniformly spaced sampling steps. Parameter counts include the adaLN modulation layers, which account for about one third of DiT’s parameters while adding negligible compute; these layers are shared within the looped segment. Excluding adaLN, DyLoMo-DiT has 1.2–1.3 $\times$ the parameters of the DiT in the same tier ( App. B ). All reported results are means over seeds, and differences and percentages are computed from unrounded values.

Text-to-image evaluation. We report GenEval [ 27 ] ; DPG-Bench [ 34 ] and COCO FID [ 41 ] are reported in App. A . We rerun and rescore public models using the same evaluation pipeline at their native resolutions. Differences smaller than 0.03 are treated as not meaningful ( App. A ).

#### 5.2 Comparison with DiT-MoE at Its Own Budget

At matched total parameter counts and forward compute, DyLoMo-DiT reduces FID by 36%, 56%, and 56% relative to DiT-MoE in the S, B, and L tiers, respectively ( Table 3 ; Fig. 2 , bottom). We compare at each DiT-MoE tier’s own budget because these models retain the width and depth of the corresponding DiT while adding experts to every layer, resulting in about six times as many parameters and 2.7 times the compute. For each tier, we configure DyLoMo-DiT to match DiT-MoE’s total parameter count and compute: one routing head, 16 experts per layer with 2 selected, 16 layers with the middle 6 looped, and the same pass allocation as above ( Sec. 3.5 ). We train DiT-MoE under the same protocol, using 16 experts per layer with 4 selected. Thus, both models have the same number of experts per layer. These results reflect our DiT-MoE implementation under this training protocol, rather than the results reported in its paper ( App. J ).

We also evaluate the original DiT-MoE configuration, which uses 8 experts per layer, selects 2, and includes a shared expert. It obtains FIDs of 30.80, 12.91, and 7.50 in the S, B, and L tiers; DyLoMo-DiT reduces these by 36%, 46%, and 53%, respectively (XL results: Sec. 5.3 ). We further compare against ProMoE [ 74 ] . At the ProMoE-L budget and after 500k training steps, matching the schedule in its paper, DyLoMo-DiT reaches an FID of 4.05, compared with 4.86 for our ProMoE-L rerun and 5.12 as reported. At 400k steps, our ProMoE-XL rerun reaches an FID of 4.78, compared with 4.22 for DyLoMo-DiT-XL, despite using $2.3\times$ as many parameters ( App. J ).

#### 5.3 Scaling Up the Baselines

To test whether the gains can be recovered simply by increasing baseline size, we scale up DiT and DiT-MoE ( App. B ). DiT-XL/2 scaled to $1.6\times$ , $2.2\times$ , and $3.0\times$ gives FIDs of 5.22, 4.57, and 4.75. Even at three times the parameters and compute, DiT remains 13% worse than DyLoMo-DiT-XL at its original scale (4.22 FID). The original XL DiT-MoE, with $6.2\times$ the parameters and $2.8\times$ the compute, reaches 6.87 FID, 63% worse than DyLoMo-DiT-XL. DiT-MoE scaled to $2.2\times$ in both parameters and compute reaches 6.65 FID, 58% worse.

#### 5.5 The Loop’s Parameter–Compute Trade-off

> Figure 6 : The two roles of the loop (B tier, single routing head). (a) Fewer parameters at fixed compute: at $0.31\times$ the parameters, the model is still ahead of both DiT-MoE-B configurations. (b) More compute at fixed parameters: one to four passes at every timestep (filled) and passes allocated by noise level (open; 3 or 5 passes for $t<500$ , 1 for $t\geq 500$ ), each close to or level with one more pass.

We next examine the loop’s two resource trade-offs ( Fig. 6 ). At fixed compute (63 GMAC), reducing parameters to $0.72\times$ , $0.58\times$ , and $0.31\times$ of the two-pass model raises FID by 14%, 27%, and 41%, respectively. Even with less than one third of its parameters, the smallest model achieves 13% and 30% lower FID than the two DiT-MoE configurations at the same compute. Conversely, at fixed parameters (796M), increasing passes from one to four reduces FID by 21%, 15%, and 2.7% across successive increases. Allocating passes by noise level is worth roughly one extra pass: at two-pass compute, it approaches three passes throughout (7.01 vs. 6.72 FID), and at three-pass compute, it matches four passes throughout (6.47 vs. 6.54).

We next examine where additional loop computation is useful along the denoising trajectory. Compared with two passes at every timestep, allocated-pass DyLoMo-DiT-XL has lower noise-prediction error at every $t<500$ (by up to 0.6% around $t=150$ –200), slightly higher error just above 500, and similar error beyond 600 ( Fig. 7 a). At the DiT budget, noise-dependent allocation lowers FID by 4.2%, 4.7%, and 3.4% in the B, L, and XL tiers, using all available seeds; at the DiT-MoE budget, it improves the single-head B model by 12% ( App. B ). Reversing the allocation worsens the B-tier FID by 33% (to 26.14), while a three-band schedule performs similarly to the two-band schedule ( App. G ).

The pass schedule can also be chosen after training ( App. G ). A DyLoMo-DiT-XL trained with pass counts sampled from $\{1,2,3\}$ can run under different schedules; any schedule with at least two passes at low noise reaches nearly the same FID (within 3%). When run with two passes, this model reaches 4.68 FID, 7% worse than the model trained with two passes throughout (4.36), reflecting the cost of choosing depth at inference.


#### 5.7 Routing Specialization by Noise Level

We analyze routing in the DyLoMo-DiT-XL model in Table 2 (protocol and two-pass control: App. F ).

The second pass reuses most of the experts. On the second pass, each token reuses an average of 4.61 of the 6 experts per head selected on the first pass, compared with 2.57 for the same position in another image and 2.25 under random selection. The two passes also produce similarly directed residual writes, with cosine similarities of 0.68 for attention and 0.62 for feed-forward networks. These aligned writes motivate the $1/r$ scaling across passes in Sec. 3.3 .

Routing specializes by noise level and can switch between adjacent steps. Across adjacent timesteps within either $t\leq 550$ or $t\geq 650$ , the expert distribution changes little (JS divergence of about 0.005 nats). Between $t=550$ and $t=650$ , it shifts sharply, reaching 0.30, and every layer switches ( App. F ). On a finer grid with step size 10, the switch occurs between $t=550$ and $t=560$ (JS divergence 0.287); all other adjacent pairs on this grid have divergence at most 0.0005. Experts specialize across the two ranges: 47% receive more than 80% of their calls in one range ( Fig. 7 b), compared with 36% in the two-pass control ( App. F ). The number of active experts also falls from 15.0 at $t=50$ to 9.2 at $t=950$ .

The router receives no explicit timestep input; timestep information reaches it only through adaLN-modulated features, and the learned timestep embedding itself changes abruptly where the routing switches, unlike that of DiT ( App. F ). The routing switch does not align with the pass-allocation boundary at $t=500$ (the JS divergence from $t=499$ to $t=500$ is 0.002), and a model without the loop also switches within one step ( App. F ). These findings suggest that the router’s noise-level specialization is learned from its input features, rather than imposed by the pass schedule or the loop.

### 6 Conclusion

DyLoMo-DiT improves matched-budget efficiency by combining sparse experts with weight-shared recurrence, with further gains from dynamically assigning more passes to low-noise steps. Routers also learn noise-level specialization without explicit timestep inputs. On ImageNet, DyLoMo-DiT reduces FID by 11–24% versus DiT and 36–56% versus DiT-MoE at matched budgets; at equal compute, it outperforms DiT-MoE-B with less than one third of its parameters, and a DiT with three times the parameters and compute remains 13% behind; at 1.6M steps, it surpasses the FID reported for DiT-XL/2 after 7M steps. Scaled up to 15B parameters, it becomes a production-scale text-to-image model that scores above FLUX.1 on GenEval with less than one tenth of its active parameters, and it transfers to video ( App. C ).

### Appendix A Sampling and Evaluation Details of the Text-to-Image Experiments

This appendix gives the details behind Sec. 4 (text-to-image setup) and Sec. 5.8 , and lists the prompts of the head figure ( ). The experiment asks whether the same architecture carries over from class-conditional generation on ImageNet to text-to-image; only one model is trained, with no ablation. Images are encoded with the Wan2.2 VAE [ 69 ] and text with a frozen Qwen2.5-VL-7B [ 3 ] ; sampling uses 50 Euler steps with APG [ 57 ] at guidance scale 5.


##### Model.

The first and last layers use dense feed-forward networks, and the 30 layers in between use sparse-expert feed-forward networks; each token selects 6 experts per head, and there is one always-on shared expert (width 1280). The looped segment is the middle 16 layers, with 8 layers before it and 8 after it, so one forward pass executes $8+16\times 2+8=48$ layers. Active parameters are counted as follows: the layers of the looped segment are counted once; the routers, which score all experts, are counted in full; the image input and output adapter layers are counted once. The timestep is written into the first 64 channels of the input as a 64-dimensional sinusoidal encoding.


##### Encoders.

The VAE has 48 channels and downsamples spatially by $16\times$ ; there is no further patching, so one latent position is one token. Text features are taken from the output of the third-to-last layer of the text encoder, before its final normalization layer, with at most 1,000 tokens.


##### Checkpoints.

Table 5 uses the checkpoint evaluated at $1024\times 1024$ (the checkpoint of the head figure and of the gallery below). Earlier checkpoints score 0.705 at $448\times 256$ and 0.700 at $784\times 448$ ; the samples in Fig. A1 come from the latter.


##### Sampling.

Euler method with 50 steps; $u$ is taken at equally spaced points from 1 to 0 and then passed through the shift transform $\sigma=s\cdot u/(1+(s-1)\cdot u)$ with $s=1.6$ . APG [ 57 ] acts on the predicted clean image, with guidance scale 5, momentum $-0.5$ , parallel-component weight $\eta=0.15$ and no rescaling; the unconditional branch uses the empty text, and no negative prompt is used. All three metrics use this one set of settings.


##### Metrics.

GenEval [ 27 ] has 553 prompts with 4 images each, 2,212 images in total; we use the official implementation, with Mask2Former (Swin-S) [ 10 ] as the detector and colors judged by CLIP [ 55 ] . DPG-Bench has 1,065 prompts with 4 images each, scored with the script released by ELLA [ 34 ] and mPLUG-large [ 39 ] ; the official script reads a $2\times 2$ grid and splits it, whereas we feed the 4 single images directly; we also fixed one issue in the upstream parsing script, which skipped the first question of every prompt. COCO FID uses the first 10,000 captions of the commonly used 30,000-caption MS-COCO [ 41 ] val2014 list, one image per caption, with the original images of these captions as the reference set; generated and reference images are both resized directly to $256\times 256$ (no cropping, so the aspect ratio changes), and FID is computed with clean-fid [ 51 ] . DyLoMo-DiT scores 83.6 on DPG-Bench at $448\times 256$ and 85.2 at $784\times 448$ , and its COCO FID is 20.5 at $448\times 256$ .

Only one model was trained on the text-to-image line, so the only available reference for fluctuation comes from consecutive checkpoints, whose GenEval scores differ by at most about 0.03. On this basis, we do not interpret GenEval differences within 0.03.

##### Swapping the timestep.

Without a timestep input, the router can tell the noise level in two ways: from the timestep, which reaches it through the adaLN modulation, or from the noisy latent itself. We separate the two by noising the input to $t_{x}$ while giving $t_{c}$ to the timestep embedding, for $t_{x},t_{c}\in\{50,250,450,550,650,850,950\}$ , with the inputs and noise of the protocol above (pairs with $t_{x}\neq t_{c}$ never occur in training); on the diagonal $t_{x}=t_{c}$ , the expert counts equal those of the original analysis exactly. We use the three sparse-expert layers before the loop, whose routing does not depend on the number of passes. For the 24 pairs with $t_{x}$ and $t_{c}$ on opposite sides of the switch, the expert distribution is closer to that of $t_{c}$ than to that of $t_{x}$ in 95.5% of the 288 layer–head–pair cases (mean JS divergence 0.017 against 0.207 nats), and for all 12 routing heads on average; $t_{c}$ accounts for 97% of the variance of the phase score in Fig. F4 (the JS divergence to the mean diagonal distribution of the low-noise range, divided by the sum of the divergences to those of the low- and high-noise ranges), $t_{x}$ for 1%. The routing thus follows the timestep the model is given, not the noise level of its input: the noise-level specialization is read from the timestep conditioning. The exception is $t_{c}=50$ with a noisier input, where the routing moves partway toward the high-noise experts (phase score about 0.6).

##### Relation to step-wise adaptive computation.

Prior step-wise adaptive computation in diffusion models acts at inference: AdaDiff [ 66 ] exits early at some timesteps, DyDiT [ 80 ] changes the width per timestep, and DDSM [ 78 ] selects a network size per step. The allocation of passes here is part of the model and of its training, and total parameters and mean compute stay equal to those of the baseline.


##### Sampling steps against passes at equal compute.

A looped model spends its sampling compute on passes; a single-pass model can spend the same compute on more sampling steps. To separate the two, the XL models trained with one to four passes at every timestep ( Table B3 ) are each sampled with their own pass count and with the number of DDPM steps set so that the total compute of sampling, the number of steps times the compute of one forward pass, is equal across pass counts ( Table G2 ): equal to two passes at 250 steps (29.4 TMAC per image) and to four passes at 250 steps (48.0 TMAC per image), within 0.4%. ##### Return on passes in a video model.

The return on extra passes at fixed parameters ( Sec. 5.5 ) is also observed on a separate in-house video generation model: image-to-video, 24 layers, 64 experts per head, with the multi-stream residual of App. I , 2.87B parameters in total, looping layers 7 to 18. The same weights are then evaluated with one to four passes by the validation loss on 2,000 held-out video clips (the flow-matching [ 42 ] mean squared error, with fixed noise, so the four settings are comparable per sample; Table G5 ). With each additional pass the gain falls to about three tenths of the previous one: the third pass executes 33% more layers and lowers the validation loss by 0.0036; the fourth adds another 25% and lowers it by 0.0011.

Two points bear on reading Table G5 . During training each of the four settings received only a quarter of the steps, and the single pass is diluted the most, so the decrease at the second pass is inflated (when the same architecture is trained with the pass count drawn only from $\{1,2\}$ , this term is 0.0074); the increments at the third and fourth passes are diluted about equally on both sides of the comparison and can be compared with each other. The differences in the table are obtained by spending more compute on the same weights and are not a comparison at equal compute; the ImageNet models trained separately at a fixed number of passes ( Fig. 6 b, Fig. B4 ) show the same diminishing return. In addition, LOOM [ 30 ] , with a separate router and residual scaling per pass, still gains from pushing the loop beyond five passes in language models, so the diminishing return at the third and fourth passes may depend on the recipe and need not be a limit of looping itself.

##### Configurations.

Table H1 lists the configurations at the budget of DiT and Table H2 the single-head configurations at the budget of DiT-MoE; the layout of every other arm is given with its results in App. B . The middle segment is about half of the layers: at the XL tier $L=16$ with layers 5 to 11 looped; at the B and L tiers $L=12$ with layers 4 to 9 looped, so that a token passes through 24 layers at low noise and 12 at high noise. At the XL tier $H=4$ , $E=16$ and $k=6$ , the intermediate width of both the experts and the shared expert is 640, and each token activates 24 routed experts per layer. In the single-head B model the experts have intermediate width 1256 and the shared expert 760; the three-head model of Table 4 keeps the expert width and has a shared expert of width 776.


##### Input, conditioning and normalization.

The input is the VAE latent, split into $2\times 2$ patches and linearly projected into tokens; the timestep embedding and the class embedding are summed into a conditioning vector $c$ , which every sublayer injects through adaLN-Zero; positional information is supplied by rotary position embeddings [ 63 ] inside attention; the output layer predicts the noise and the variance. All normalization in the network is RMSNorm, applied once each to $q$ and $k$ ; the DiT baselines keep the official implementation. The rotary position encoding is a two-dimensional Fourier RoPE with 16 frequency bands, rotating only the first $3/4$ of the dimensions of each attention head; the sigmoid gate of each attention head reads the normalized input. Normalization is computed in FP32 with eps $=10^{-6}$ .


##### Initialization.

Router weights are drawn from a normal distribution with standard deviation 0.02. The experts’ up-projection has standard deviation $256^{-1/2}$ and the down-projection $f^{-1/2}$ . The remaining linear layers have standard deviation equal to the input dimension to the power $-1/2$ , none with bias; the exceptions are the adaLN modulation layers (with bias, zero-initialized) and the patch embedding layer (PyTorch default initialization, with bias). The load-balancing biases start at zero. The RMSNorm $w$ , the attention sink and the output layer are also zero-initialized. The timestep and class embeddings use the PyTorch default initialization.


##### Routing weights.

The constant $\alpha=4.9$ that multiplies the normalized routing weights in Sec. 3.2 is the default of the implementation and was not tuned.


##### Activation function.

The experts, the shared expert and the dense feed-forward networks of the first and last layers all use a clamped SwiGLU: the gate branch $g$ is clipped to at most 7, the other branch $u$ is clipped to $[-7,7]$ , and the output is $g\cdot\sigma(1.702\cdot g)\cdot(u+1)$ . This SwiGLU [ 60 ] variant with clipping and a residual term appears in gpt-oss [ 48 ] .


##### Protocol details.

Images are encoded with sd-vae-ft-ema [ 56 ] with scaling factor 0.18215 into $32\times 32\times 4$ latents. Training uses a global batch of 256 for 400k steps, AdamW [ 44 ] with $\beta=(0.9,0.999)$ , a constant learning rate of $10^{-4}$ , no weight decay, and an EMA coefficient of 0.9999. DDPM [ 33 ] uses a 1000-step linear noise schedule, predicting the noise and learning the variance [ 47 ] ; evaluation follows Sec. 4 , using ADM’s [ 20 ] TensorFlow evaluator and reference statistics.

### Appendix I Full Results for the Multi-Stream Residual

This appendix gives the evaluation of the multi-stream residual that Sec. 2 and Sec. 4 refer to: on ImageNet it is on par when training completes normally but makes training unstable together with adaLN, so it is not part of the method; without adaLN it trains normally and brings a gain, so the text-to-image model keeps it.


##### Multi-stream residual.

Hyper-Connections [ 82 ] widen the residual stream into several streams and learn the connections between them; its appendix reports on DiT-XL/2 that static HC (SHC $\times$ 2) improves FID from 2.36 to 2.18 (a baseline with FP16 and QK normalization, 1400 epochs, guidance scale 1.5). mHC [ 77 ] constrains the mixing matrix between streams to be doubly stochastic; oHC [ 29 ] constrains it to the rotation group and parameterizes it with a pair of unit quaternions; other models fix it to the identity (iHC), such as the language model Hy4-preview [ 67 ] and Chimera [ 26 ] . The experiments of mHC and oHC are only on language models. In diffusion models, MAGI-2 [ 59 ] uses mHC; Chimera uses iHC together with sparse experts and reports that both improve the compute efficiency of pretraining (measured by the compute needed to reach the same training loss); Liang et al . [ 40 ] compare single-stream and four-stream mHC on ImageNet-256 and find that the gain depends on the prediction target: at the B tier in pixel space, FID goes from 10.19 to 9.78 with $x$ -prediction and from 139.83 to 25.36 with $v$ -prediction. We evaluate oHC separately as an optional component on top of DyLoMo-DiT, with the results below.

The DyLoMo-DiT of this paper has no multi-stream residual. We additionally trained versions with oHC, in which the residual stream is widened to 4 streams mixed by an $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ rotation, with the training and evaluation protocol unchanged and 1.2% more compute. These experiments were run on the XL family with 32 experts per head and two passes at every timestep (1.13B parameters; App. B ) and on its ablations. When training completes normally, the version with oHC is on par with the version without: the final FID is 4.20, against 4.21 without. Under this recipe, however, training with oHC is unstable: of the 9 runs across the arms with oHC, 7 have FID rising rather than falling at later evaluation points (back up to tens, at most over three hundred); in the 11 runs of these arms without oHC (9 at the XL tier, 2 at the L tier), FID decreases monotonically with training steps. In these abnormal runs the model itself degrades in the same way: the quality of individual images drops while diversity does not. Evaluating the abnormal checkpoints directly with the raw (non-EMA) weights gives FID from 23.96 to 46.24, Precision from 0.43 to 0.58 and Recall from 0.62 to 0.69; the normally completed checkpoints give 7.03 to 10.50, 0.70 to 0.74 and 0.55 to 0.59 respectively. In 2 of these runs (300.19 and 255.67) the EMA weights collapse further on top of this, with FID in the hundreds and Recall close to 0. The phenomenon also appears in the arms with dense feed-forward and no routing, so it does not depend on the routing of the sparse experts. We therefore did not include oHC in the method proper.

##### Implementation and the two configurations.

DiT-MoE is implemented from the code released by its authors [ 23 ] . A model keeps the width, depth and attention heads of the DiT of the same name; the feed-forward network of every layer is replaced by routed experts plus one shared expert, with SwiGLU experts. The router takes a softmax over the experts, selects those with the highest probability and weights them by the probabilities without renormalization; the shared expert is computed for every token with weight 1. Load balancing uses an expert-level auxiliary loss with coefficient 0.005, the value in the text of the DiT-MoE paper (the default of the released code is 0.01), with statistics computed locally in each data-parallel process. The training protocol, optimizer, EMA, sampling and evaluation are those of all other models ( Sec. 4 ). We train two configurations ( Table J1 ). The original configuration has 8 experts per layer with 2 selected, each of intermediate width $4d$ , and a shared expert of width $2d$ (the released code implements the two shared experts of the paper as one feed-forward network of this width). The second configuration cuts every expert in two: 16 experts per layer of width $2d$ with 4 selected and the same shared expert, so that total parameters, active parameters and compute are unchanged up to the router; it has the number of experts per layer of the DyLoMo-DiT it is compared with in Table 3 . At the B tier, the original configuration (12.91) is 20% below the configuration with 16 experts (16.06), and DyLoMo-DiT (7.01) is 46% and 56% below the two. At the S tier the two configurations are level: the original one gives 30.80 and the one with 16 experts 30.83, and DyLoMo-DiT (19.79) is 36% below both, so the advantage of the original configuration at the B tier does not carry over to the S tier. At the L tier the original configuration is at 34.23, 16.38, 10.33 and 7.50 after 100k, 200k, 300k and 400k steps, against 35.64, 17.27, 10.94 and 7.92 for the configuration with 16 experts, so it is 5% below at 400k, and DyLoMo-DiT (3.50) is 53% and 56% below the two. Over the three tiers the original configuration is therefore level with or below the one with 16 experts, and DyLoMo-DiT is 36%, 46% and 53% below the stronger of the two at S, B and L.


##### Configurations at other budgets.

The DiT-MoE models of Sec. 5.3 use the same code (costs and FID over training: Tables B3 and B1 ). Scaled down to the budget of DiT-XL/2, DiT-MoE has width 1024 and 3 experts per layer with 2 selected (expert width 536, shared expert 2768; 7.90). At the compute of DiT-XL/2 with more parameters, it has width 1152 and 8 experts with 2 selected, either with experts of width 768 and a shared expert as wide as the two selected experts together (1536; $1.7\times$ the parameters, 9.19) or with the proportions of the released code, in which the shared expert is half as wide as a routed expert (1232 and 616; $2.1\times$ , 10.61). The model scaled to $2.2\times$ in both parameters and compute has width 1536 (expert width 808, shared expert 4136; 6.65), and the original XL configuration has experts of width 4608 and a shared expert of width 2304 (4.17B parameters, 328.0 GMAC; 6.87). Without guidance, the two eight-expert models at the compute of DiT-XL/2 reach 26.89 and 29.24 at 400k steps, against 18.91 for DiT-XL/2 ( Table H3 ).


##### Differences from the recipe of the DiT-MoE paper.

The DiT-MoE paper trains with a batch size of 1024 for 500k, 1M and 7M iterations and reports, with guidance and after long training, an FID of 1.72 for its XL configuration against 2.27 for DiT-XL/2. Our runs use the protocol of all other models in this paper (batch 256, 400k steps, DDPM, 250 sampling steps, guidance scale 1.5). The DiT-MoE numbers of this paper therefore characterize its recipe under this protocol and say nothing about the results in its paper. Training every model under one protocol is also what ProMoE [ 74 ] and DiffMoE [ 62 ] do when they compare with this kind of routing; both in addition reconfigure the sparse-expert baselines to the active parameters of the dense model, whereas Sec. 5.2 keeps the width, depth, routing, shared expert and auxiliary loss of DiT-MoE and configures our model for its budget.


##### Verification against the released code.

The checks were made on the two eight-expert models at the compute of DiT-XL/2. Given the same inputs and weights, our implementation and the released code agree bitwise in BF16 on the forward output of the training branch and on the gradients of all parameters; both expert widths were checked (the check at expert width 1232 used a shared expert of width 608; the shared expert is an ordinary dense feed-forward network and does not go through the expert kernel). The remaining checks were done on the model with experts of width 768: a full-model FP32 forward pass on CPU with the 100k-step EMA weights gives a relative $L_{2}$ error of $2.4\times 10^{-5}$ to $3.5\times 10^{-4}$ , and the gradient injected by the auxiliary loss agrees bitwise with explicit differentiation (CPU, FP32). A control trained with the original implementation of the released code (without kernel fusion) reaches FID 36.14 at 100k steps, comparable to the 37.20 of our implementation. The ordering of the training loss across evaluation points agrees with that of FID. FID evaluated with the raw (non-EMA) weights at 100k steps is 41.36, worse than the 37.20 with EMA weights, so the gap to DiT does not come from a mismatch between EMA weights and routing. The routing of the two models stays close to uniform over training: the normalized entropy, averaged over layers and positions, is between 0.96 and 0.98 at every checkpoint, and no expert is ever unused; the share of selections that goes to one expert (12.5% when uniform) ranges from 7% to 18% in the model with experts of width 1232 and from 1.3% to 29.4% at 400k steps in the model with experts of width 768.


##### Independent evidence.

Two independent works that retrain this kind of routing next to a dense DiT under one protocol find the same direction as Secs. 5.2 and 5.3 . ProMoE (Table 10 of [ 74 ] ; DDPM, L tier, 500k steps) reports FID 23.12 without guidance and 7.55 at guidance scale 1.5 for its DiT-MoE-L, which has 8 experts with 1 selected and no shared expert, against 20.81 and 6.29 for the dense DiT-L (its Table 4). DiffMoE (Table 11 of [ 62 ] ; DDPM, L tier, 700k steps) reports 20.81 for token-choice routing with 16 experts against 17.87 for the dense DiT-L. In both, token-choice sparse experts are worse than the dense model at equal active parameters.


##### A controlled comparison with ProMoE.

DiT-MoE is the baseline of the main text because the later models start from its design: ProMoE [ 74 ] , DiffMoE and EC-DiT keep a DiT backbone with a single pass over distinct layers and change how tokens are routed to the experts, a different axis from the budgeting of depth, parameters and compute studied here. We also compare with ProMoE at equal total parameters and compute. Its model code is ported from the authors’ release and checked against it: with the same inputs and weights, the forward pass and all gradients are bit-identical. ### Appendix K Flow-Matching Objective

The ImageNet experiments of the main paper use the DDPM objective of the DiT protocol ( Sec. 4 ). To test whether the comparison with DiT depends on this objective, we retrain the L-tier and XL-tier models of Table 2 , DiT-L/2 and DiT-XL/2 and the DyLoMo-DiT of each tier, with a flow-matching objective [ 42 , 43 , 45 ] : the input is the linear interpolation $x_{\sigma}=(1-\sigma)\cdot x_{0}+\sigma\cdot\epsilon$ with $\sigma$ drawn uniformly from $[0,1]$ , the network predicts the velocity $\epsilon-x_{0}$ , and the time embedding receives $1000\,\sigma$ . The architectures, their parameters and compute, and all other training settings are those of the DDPM runs. DyLoMo-DiT keeps its pass schedule with the boundary at $\sigma=0.5$ : three passes for $\sigma<0.5$ and one otherwise, two passes on average. All models are sampled from the EMA weights with 250 Euler steps uniformly spaced in $\sigma$ and evaluated as in Sec. 4 .
