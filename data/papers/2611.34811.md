## HOG-WAM: Improving Visual Robustness through Future Gradient Prediction

World-action models jointly predict future observations and actions, yet visual corruption can disrupt the resulting policies. We introduce HOG-WAM, which adds future Histograms of Oriented Gradients (HOG) as structural supervision derived directly from demonstration images. An auxiliary HOG diffusion transformer (DiT) module reads RGB features through asymmetric attention, allowing its objective to shape the visual representation used for control. The module is used only during training and removed at inference, preserving FastWAM’s RGB-to-action architecture. We evaluate four controlled observation corruptions through a visual-corruption extension of RoboTwin, termed RoboTwin-Plus; HOG-WAM achieves the highest success among the compared policies under all four. Mixed-noise success increases from 78.86% to 82.86% relative to FastWAM, with comparable performance on clean scenes. On LIBERO Plus, overall success increases from 74.49% to 75.26%, with the largest gain under sensor noise, from 74.14% to 78.89%. Across four Franka tasks, average success increases from 40% to 59% under clean observations and from 17% to 24% under noisy observations. A representation ablation favors combining RGB observations with auxiliary HOG prediction over replacing RGB with HOG. These findings support future-gradient prediction as complementary supervision for visually robust control, with larger absolute gains on physical tasks than in simulation.

### 1 Introduction

Robotic manipulation requires recognizing task-relevant objects and anticipating how their spatial relations change during interaction. Vision-language-action policies use pretrained visual and language representations to map observations to actions [ 40 , 17 , 5 ] . World-action models (WAMs) additionally learn to predict future observations, making demonstrated scene evolution a source of policy supervision [ 35 , 18 , 3 ] . Fast-WAM [ 38 ] , denoted FastWAM here, preserves this predictive objective during training while generating actions from the current observation at inference. The prediction target therefore determines which visual information this training objective emphasizes.

RGB prediction supervises object structure together with color, texture, and illumination. These appearance cues help identify objects, but they also vary with lighting and sensor conditions while the manipulation goal remains unchanged. An RGB objective alone thus ties the learned features to appearance factors that can change at deployment. For placement, stacking, and insertion, local boundaries describe the image-space arrangement of objects and their surroundings. Requiring a visual representation to predict these boundaries provides an explicit structural learning signal alongside RGB reconstruction. The question is how to introduce this signal while retaining the appearance information needed for language-conditioned control.


##### Why gradients?

Histograms of Oriented Gradients (HOG) [ 10 ] summarize local edge orientations through spatial pooling and contrast normalization. MaskFeat [ 30 ] demonstrates that predicting HOG features in masked image and video regions can learn useful visual representations, identifying local contrast normalization as an important component. This motivates a different prediction problem for manipulation: learning the gradient structure of future demonstration frames alongside actions. The target emphasizes where local intensity transitions occur and how they are oriented, making spatial organization explicit without object annotations or a learned target generator.


##### Why training-only supervision?

A descriptor useful for supervising a representation need not replace its sensory input. Our HOG raster compresses orientation statistics and removes chromatic information; RGB retains cues for object identity and instruction grounding. WAM4D [ 19 ] offers a related architectural principle: future-depth supervision shapes the visual features used for control through a removable readout. We apply this principle to deterministic gradient targets, which require no additional sensor or pretrained estimator. In HOG-WAM , an auxiliary HOG diffusion transformer (DiT) reads RGB features through asymmetric attention, and its loss updates the RGB backbone. Action prediction remains independent of HOG tokens, allowing the auxiliary module to be removed at inference while retaining its effect on the learned RGB representation.

We evaluate visual robustness on RoboTwin 2.0 [ 7 ] , LIBERO Plus [ 13 ] , and four Franka tasks. RoboTwin-Plus denotes our visual-corruption extension of the existing RoboTwin evaluation, with fixed tasks and controlled changes to image inputs. On LIBERO Plus, gains concentrate under sensor noise. A LIBERO Plus ablation compares HOG as auxiliary supervision with HOG as a replacement for RGB. The physical experiments show larger absolute gains over FastWAM than the simulation averages, extending the evidence to manipulation with real camera observations. Our contributions are:

- • A deterministic future-gradient target that makes local structure an explicit supervision signal for world-action learning.

- • An asymmetric coupling of RGB, HOG, and action DiT modules that transfers this supervision to the visual backbone while preserving RGB-based inference.

- • Evidence from controlled visual-corruption tests and physical manipulation, together with a representation ablation that supports complementary HOG supervision over RGB replacement.


### 2 Related work


##### Generative models and robot policies.

Transformers [ 29 , 11 ] provide token-based architectures for visual learning, while diffusion models [ 16 ] , latent diffusion [ 26 ] , and DiTs [ 25 ] support conditional image generation. Flow matching [ 20 ] and rectified flow [ 22 ] learn velocity fields that transport noise toward data. Diffusion Policy [ 9 ] applies conditional denoising to action sequences. RT-1 and RT-2 [ 6 , 40 ] scale observation-to-action learning, while Octo and OpenVLA [ 14 , 17 ] provide generalist policies adaptable to new robot settings. The $\pi_{0}$ family [ 5 , 4 ] combines pretrained vision-language representations with continuous action generation and heterogeneous training data. HOG-WAM instead studies how the visual prediction objective within a WAM can improve the representation used for control.


##### Predictive learning for control.

UniPi [ 12 ] derives actions from text-conditioned video plans, and GR-1 [ 31 ] jointly predicts future images and actions after video pretraining. Video foundation models such as Wan [ 27 ] supply visual dynamics priors for recent WAMs. DreamZero [ 35 ] , LingBot-VA [ 18 ] , and MOTUS [ 3 ] couple visual dynamics and action generation through joint models. FastWAM [ 38 ] separates future-video training from action inference. Our auxiliary target builds on this separation, changing what the RGB representation is trained to predict.


##### Structural supervision in world models.

TesserAct [ 39 ] models RGB, depth, and normals, while Kinema4D [ 33 ] uses kinematic robot trajectories to condition RGB and point-map simulation. WAM4D [ 19 ] transfers geometric priors through future-depth readouts that are removed at inference. MaskWAM [ 36 ] uses mask prompting and prediction, FlowWAM [ 8 ] adopts optical flow as an action representation, and Robust-WAM [ 34 ] aligns action-stream features with semantic targets. DreamWAM [ 37 ] combines appearance, motion, geometry, and semantics. HOG-WAM complements these learned or task-specific targets with local gradient statistics computed directly from each demonstration frame.


##### Masked prediction and gradient targets.

The target representation determines the information emphasized by a predictive objective. BEiT [ 2 ] predicts discrete visual tokens; MAE and SimMIM [ 15 , 32 ] reconstruct pixels; VideoMAE [ 28 ] extends masked reconstruction to videos; and I-JEPA [ 1 ] predicts learned target embeddings. MaskFeat [ 30 ] identifies HOG [ 10 ] as an effective feature-regression target, connecting local shape information with representation learning. HOG-WAM carries this target-design insight into predictive robot learning: a HOG-derived raster is predicted in the video latent space alongside future RGB and actions. The structural predictor is an auxiliary training objective, while the policy retains RGB observations for control.


### 3 HOG-WAM


#### 3.1 Predictive policy learning

At each decision, the policy receives a multi-camera RGB observation $I_{0}$ , an instruction $\ell$ , and proprioceptive state $s_{0}$ , and predicts an action chunk

[Tableau] $$A=(a_{0},\ldots,a_{K-1})\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid I_{0},\ell,s_{0}).$$ (1)

Here, $K$ is the action horizon, $a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}}$ is an action of dimension $d_{a}$ , and $\theta$ denotes trainable policy parameters. Demonstrations additionally provide $T$ sampled future observations $I_{1:T}$ for predictive training. FastWAM, on which we build, combines a Wan2.2-TI2V-5B visual DiT module with an action DiT module. A frozen causal variational autoencoder (VAE) $\mathcal{E}$ maps the observation sequence to RGB latents $Z^{v}=\mathcal{E}(I_{0:T})$ . Language embeddings and a trainable proprioceptive projection condition both streams. The model jointly learns RGB and action velocity fields, with action queries attending to the observed RGB frame.

HOG-WAM adds a future-gradient objective through an auxiliary DiT that reads RGB features ( Fig. 1 ). This makes the structural loss act on the visual representation used for action prediction.


#### 3.2 Future-gradient targets

> Figure 2 : Construction of future-gradient supervision. Demonstration frames provide deterministic structural targets, encoded by the same frozen VAE as RGB. The HOG DiT predicts velocities over noisy HOG latents, and future frames contribute to the auxiliary loss. Monochrome gradients illustrate local boundaries; the three-channel training raster follows Eq. 2 .

For each demonstration frame, we compute a deterministic HOG-derived raster $\mathcal{H}(I_{t})$ , as illustrated in Fig. 2 .


##### Local orientation histograms.

We convert each frame to grayscale and compute horizontal and vertical finite differences. Gradient magnitudes contribute to nine unsigned orientation bins within $8\times 8$ cells, with linear interpolation between neighboring bins. Overlapping $2\times 2$ -cell blocks undergo L2-Hys normalization, including clipping at $0.2$ and renormalization. Averaging contributions from overlapping blocks gives a normalized histogram $h_{qk}$ for each cell $q$ and orientation bin $k$ . This local normalization reduces sensitivity to intensity scale, while the spatial grid retains the arrangement of boundaries.


##### Rasterization for latent prediction.

We summarize each normalized histogram by its energy and second angular moments. For bins $k\in\{0,\ldots,8\}$ with centers $\beta_{k}=(k+\tfrac{1}{2})\pi/9$ , the three quantities are

[Tableau] $\displaystyle e_{q}$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{k}h_{qk},$ (2) $\displaystyle c_{q}$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{k}h_{qk}\cos(2\beta_{k}),$ $\displaystyle d_{q}$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{k}h_{qk}\sin(2\beta_{k}).$

The doubled angle represents unsigned orientations consistently, treating directions separated by $\pi$ as equivalent. The energy channel measures aggregate histogram magnitude; the angular channels encode orientation concentration and direction. With a small stabilizer $\varepsilon>0$ , we set $b=\max(\max_{q}e_{q},\varepsilon)$ and form the cell raster $(2e_{q}/b-1,c_{q}/b,d_{q}/b)$ . We clip its values to $[-1,1]$ and repeat each cell value over its pixels to recover the original image resolution. This three-channel representation makes gradient supervision compatible with the pretrained video encoder. The same frozen VAE encodes the resulting sequence:

[Tableau] $$Z^{h}=\mathcal{E}\bigl(\mathcal{H}(I_{0:T})\bigr).$$ (3)

RGB and HOG targets share the frozen encoder, latent convention, and temporal positions, pairing appearance and structure from the same future demonstration frames.


##### Role of the gradient target.

Let $Y$ denote grayscale intensity and $Y^{\prime}=aY+\delta$ a photometric change with $a>0$ and no clipping. Finite differences satisfy $\nabla Y^{\prime}=a\nabla Y$ : the offset disappears, orientations are preserved, and local normalization reduces the shared magnitude scale. HOG therefore directs supervision toward boundary arrangement rather than absolute intensity. Cellwise orientation pooling further summarizes local structure without requiring exact pixel reconstruction [ 10 , 30 ] . Additive noise perturbs gradients directly, so we compute targets from clean demonstrations and evaluate the RGB-input policy under corruption.

Future targets connect this structural emphasis to scene evolution. Their spatial layout changes as objects move, contact one another, or become occluded, so predicting future HOG encourages visual features informative about these changes. RGB prediction simultaneously preserves appearance detail. The two objectives assign complementary descriptions to the same observed transition.

##### Coupled predictors.

During training, the model contains RGB, HOG, and action DiT modules. The HOG DiT shares the pretrained initialization of the RGB DiT and has independently optimized parameters. Each module embeds its noisy state and flow time, with asymmetric mixed attention coupling the streams at every layer. The RGB and HOG heads predict velocities in the video latent space, and the action head predicts velocities in normalized action coordinates. Visual latents are divided into $1\times 2\times 2$ spatiotemporal patches. The visual and action DiTs use different hidden widths but project their queries, keys, and values to a common attention-head geometry. Separate feature transformations and output heads preserve each modality’s prediction objective.


##### Attention mask.

The attention structure connects HOG prediction to RGB learning while preserving the information available to the action policy at inference. Figure 3 shows the permitted query–key connections among RGB, HOG, and action tokens. RGB queries attend to RGB keys, while HOG queries attend to both RGB and HOG keys. These visual connections follow a first-frame-causal rule: observed-frame queries use observed-frame keys, whereas future queries can use the observed frame and all noisy future frames. Action queries attend to the observed RGB frame and their own action chunk, with independently sampled action copies kept separate. All three DiT modules additionally receive language and proprioceptive context through cross-attention.


##### Why the auxiliary module can be removed.

Observed RGB tokens use observed RGB keys and shared conditioning at every layer, so they remain independent of future visual and HOG tokens throughout the network. HOG queries read the same observed-frame RGB tokens as action queries, allowing the HOG loss to update features used for control through the RGB keys and values. For fixed parameters, these dependencies give

[Tableau] $$\frac{\partial\widehat{u}^{a}}{\partial Z^{h}}=0,\qquad\nabla_{\theta_{v}}\mathcal{L}_{h}\not\equiv 0,$$ (4)

where $\widehat{u}^{a}$ is the predicted action velocity, $\theta_{v}$ denotes RGB DiT parameters, and $\mathcal{L}_{h}$ is the HOG prediction loss. The policy consequently learns from gradient supervision without depending on a predicted HOG raster during execution. RGB observations preserve object-identity cues, and auxiliary predictions are excluded from action inputs. The representation ablation examines the alternative of supplying HOG as the policy input.


#### 3.4 Joint training and inference

We train all three DiT modules with flow matching [ 20 ] . For modality $m\in\{v,h,a\}$ , let $X^{v}=Z^{v}$ , $X^{h}=Z^{h}$ , and $X^{a}=A$ denote the clean states. Gaussian noise $\epsilon^{m}$ and noise levels $\sigma_{m}$ are sampled independently for each modality:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\widetilde{X}^{m}$ $\displaystyle=(1-\sigma_{m})X^{m}+\sigma_{m}\epsilon^{m},$ (5) $\displaystyle u^{m}$ $\displaystyle=\epsilon^{m}-X^{m}.$

The observed RGB and HOG latent frames remain clean conditioning inputs with flow time zero. The joint objective supervises future visual frames and valid action positions:

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}=\sum_{m\in\{v,h,a\}}\lambda_{m}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[w(\sigma_{m})\|\widehat{u}^{m}-u^{m}\|_{\mathrm{valid}}^{2}\right].$$ (6)

Here, $\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{valid}}^{2}$ averages squared error over valid target positions and feature dimensions, and $w$ is the baseline time-dependent weighting function. We use equal loss weights, $\lambda_{v}=\lambda_{h}=\lambda_{a}=1$ . The RGB, HOG, and action DiT modules and the proprioceptive projection are optimized jointly; the VAE and text encoder remain frozen. Each visual example is paired with eight copies of its action chunk, with independently sampled noise and flow times. The action loss is averaged over these copies, as in FastWAM.

At inference, the HOG DiT module and future visual tokens are omitted. The policy encodes the current RGB observation and conditions action generation on these visual features. Observed RGB keys and values are computed once per observation and reused across action denoising steps. For $N$ integration steps with $1=\sigma_{N}>\cdots>\sigma_{0}=0$ , let $A_{i}$ be the action chunk at noise level $\sigma_{i}$ . An update takes the form

[Tableau] $\displaystyle A_{i-1}=A_{i}+{}$ $\displaystyle(\sigma_{i-1}-\sigma_{i})$ (7) $\displaystyle\widehat{u}^{a}_{\theta}(A_{i},\sigma_{i}\mid I_{0},\ell,s_{0}).$

Starting from Gaussian $A_{N}$ , integration yields an action chunk in normalized coordinates. The policy converts the chunk to task action units, executes its initial segment, and replans from a new observation.


### 4 Experiments

We test whether future-gradient supervision improves control under visual corruption and whether it is more effective as an auxiliary target than as an RGB replacement. Simulation benchmarks assess controlled visual and task variations; four Franka tasks assess physical manipulation.


#### 4.1 Experimental setup

FastWAM provides the RGB–action baseline; HOG-WAM adds an auxiliary HOG DiT module during training. Both policies condition actions on RGB observations, language instructions, and proprioception. We train a separate pair of policies for each benchmark, using the same demonstration collection within each pair. We report success rate (SR) using each benchmark’s task-completion criterion.

On RoboTwin, both policies use 27,500 demonstrations across 50 tasks, with 50 clean and 500 randomized demonstrations per task. A trajectory-level split assigns 27,225 demonstrations to training and 275 to validation; both policies are evaluated at step 29,365. We also include the released RoboTwin-adapted $\pi_{0.5}$ [ 4 , 24 ] and MOTUS [ 3 , 23 ] policies as additional baselines. All four models are evaluated on all 50 tasks, with 100 valid episodes per task in each of six conditions: clean, randomized, and the four corruptions defined in Sec. 4.2 . For LIBERO, FastWAM and HOG-WAM use the same 2,000 demonstrations spanning 40 tasks from the Spatial, Object, Goal, and LIBERO-10 suites [ 21 ] . We evaluate both policies on the same 10,030 LIBERO Plus variants, using one rollout per variant from the first official initial state [ 13 ] . This benchmark includes changes in background, lighting, sensor noise, camera viewpoint, language, object layout, and robot initialization.


##### Matched policy comparisons.

Within each benchmark, FastWAM and HOG-WAM use the same action representation and prediction and execution horizons. Both predict 32 actions; RoboTwin executes the first 24 joint-position targets, while LIBERO executes the first eight end-effector actions. Both comparisons also match normalization statistics, effective batch size, optimization steps, and the ten-step action integration schedule. The released RoboTwin baselines retain their respective execution horizons: 32 actions for $\pi_{0.5}$ and 16 for MOTUS.


##### Aggregation and sampling.

RoboTwin conditions assign equal episode counts to all tasks, making the reported average equal to pooled success over the valid episodes. The benchmark’s expert-solvability criterion selects initial scenes; the same task populations and budgets are used across policies. Scene seeds are unpaired across policies. LIBERO Plus uses matched initial states, instructions, random seeds, and action budgets for each variant. Its overall success rate pools variants, while category and suite tables group the same outcomes by perturbation and source task family.


#### 4.2 Visual-corruption testing on RoboTwin


##### Controlled visual perturbations.

RoboTwin 2.0 [ 7 ] supplies native clean and randomized scene settings. We extend its clean-scene evaluation with four image corruptions: mixed noise, warm filtering, underexposure, and reduced contrast. We use RoboTwin-Plus as shorthand for this visual-corruption test, which retains the original tasks, scenes, and success criteria. Each operator acts on the policy’s RGB inputs before visual encoding, leaving the task, language, and proprioceptive inputs unchanged. The RoboTwin-Plus score averages success over the four corruption conditions. Native clean and randomized results are reported separately.

Mixed noise combines additive Gaussian noise with standard deviation 10 in 8-bit intensity units and multiplicative speckle noise with standard deviation 0.05. The operator rounds and clips the resulting values to the valid intensity range. Warm filtering scales RGB channels by $(1.05,0.955,0.92)$ , underexposure multiplies intensity by $2^{-0.5}$ , and reduced contrast scales deviations from mid-gray by $0.80$ . These controlled changes isolate sensitivity to visual appearance within the existing task distribution.

##### Visual robustness.

HOG-WAM achieves the highest success among the four policies in every RoboTwin-Plus condition ( Tab. 1 ). The gain concentrates under mixed noise, which causes the largest reduction in FastWAM success. HOG-WAM also improves success under all three photometric corruptions. Performance on native clean and randomized scenes remains comparable to FastWAM. This pattern is consistent with learning to aggregate boundary evidence over neighborhoods through cellwise pooling and block normalization, reducing sensitivity to independent pixel noise.


##### Variation across tasks.

Under mixed noise, HOG-WAM is higher than FastWAM on 28 of the 50 tasks, tied on 6, and lower on 16. Gains are pronounced for block handover and placing bottles in a dustbin, while success is lower when placing a can in a basket or placing a shoe. Under the photometric conditions, improved and lowered tasks are closer to balanced, in line with the small average differences.

#### 4.4 Real-world Franka manipulation

We evaluate FastWAM and HOG-WAM on four Franka tasks: bracket reorientation, produce placement, block stacking, and cylinder insertion ( Fig. 4 ). The collection contains 1,000 demonstrations, with 200 for each task except produce placement, which has 400. Both policies use the same demonstrations, camera views, language instructions, and joint-position action representation. We evaluate both models at training step 30,000.

The tasks require distinct final spatial relations: an inverted bracket, produce contained in a basket, a stable block stack, and a seated cylinder. The policies observe a main-camera view and a wrist-camera view, together with measured joint positions and gripper width. Demonstrations are recorded at 30 Hz and divided by trajectory into 900 training and 100 validation examples. Both models use normalization statistics from the training trajectories and predict absolute joint targets with a binary gripper command. Each trial lasts at most 200 steps and terminates early on success or a failure/error. After every trial, the robot is reset using the reset command.

Each task includes 25 trials per model under clean observations and 25 under the RoboTwin-Plus mixed-noise condition, giving 400 physical trials overall. Table 4 reports task success rates and their macro-average.

### 5 Discussion

The experiments favor assigning gradients and RGB different roles: gradients structure the learning objective, while RGB preserves the information available to the policy. The design combines MaskFeat’s use of HOG as a predictive target [ 30 ] with a removable structural readout in the manner of WAM4D [ 19 ] , obtaining the supervision from deterministic local image statistics. Its gains concentrate under sensor noise and viewpoint changes.

The physical results extend the evidence beyond rendered observations, with larger absolute gains than the simulation averages. Each domain uses its own demonstrations, so these results establish effectiveness in both simulation and physical manipulation. The HOG DiT is removed at inference, so both policies execute the same architecture with the same parameter count, and the benefit resides in the trained RGB and action weights.


### 6 Conclusion

HOG-WAM adds deterministic future-gradient supervision to predictive policy learning through an auxiliary DiT module and asymmetric attention. The resulting policy improves average success on RoboTwin-Plus, LIBERO Plus, and four Franka tasks while preserving the FastWAM inference architecture. The representation ablation supports retaining RGB observations and using structural prediction as complementary supervision; the gain is clearest under sensor noise.

### Appendix B Training and evaluation details


#### B.1 Training configurations

The RoboTwin collection contains 27,500 demonstrations from 50 tasks: 50 clean and 500 randomized demonstrations per task. Both policies use the same trajectory-level split of 27,225 training and 275 validation demonstrations and eight independently noised action copies per visual sample. Training uses AdamW with peak learning rate $10^{-4}$ , weight decay $10^{-2}$ , and effective batch size 1,024 for five epochs; evaluation uses the final step-29,365 checkpoints. The learning rate warms up for 1,468 steps and then decays by a cosine schedule to $10^{-6}$ . The optimizer uses $(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(0.9,0.95)$ , $\epsilon=10^{-8}$ , and gradient-norm clipping at 1. Section B.1 summarizes the benchmark-specific settings. For LIBERO, FastWAM and HOG-WAM use the same 40-task, 2,000-demonstration collection, normalization statistics, effective batch size of 256, and action-copy count $\tau=8$ . Training uses a peak learning rate of $10^{-4}$ , weight decay of $10^{-2}$ , and ten epochs. We evaluate the final checkpoints at step 13,200. The models with visual and action DiT modules assign unit weights to their visual and action objectives; HOG-WAM additionally assigns unit weight to its HOG objective. The auxiliary HOG DiT module increases training computation and parameter count.

#### B.3 Evaluation sampling and action prediction

For RoboTwin, each completed model–condition evaluation contains 100 valid episodes for each of 50 tasks. Episodes interrupted by simulation errors are excluded from the success-rate denominator. Initial scenes are selected by the benchmark’s expert-solvability criterion. The comparisons use the same task populations and episode budgets. The expert-solvability criterion can yield different scene seeds across policies, so initial scenes are unpaired.

FastWAM and HOG-WAM predict 32 absolute joint-position actions and execute the first 24 before replanning. Their four RoboTwin-Plus conditions each contain 5,000 episodes per model. The equally weighted mean across conditions is also the pooled success rate over these episodes. Both policies are evaluated in all six conditions with these episode budgets. The additional $\pi_{0.5}$ and MOTUS baselines are described below.

For LIBERO Plus, FastWAM and HOG-WAM use the same 10,030 variants, instructions, initial states, random seeds, and action-step budgets. Each policy predicts 32 actions with ten flow-integration steps, executes the first eight, and replans synchronously. The overall score pools all variants; category and suite breakdowns group these same outcomes in two ways. Each variant uses its first official initial state.


#### B.4 Additional $\pi_{0.5}$ baseline

We evaluate the publicly released motus-robotics/pi0.5_robotwin2 model [ 4 , 24 ] , using the weights at training step 40,000 and normalization statistics for the combined clean and randomized training data. The policy receives measured joint positions, predicts 32 actions with ten denoising steps, and executes all 32 before obtaining the next observation. This measured-joint-state protocol defines our reproduction setting, with a longer execution horizon than the 24 actions used by FastWAM and HOG-WAM. The release metadata describes constructing states from actions; the source for that preprocessing is unavailable.

Each of the six conditions covers all 50 tasks with 100 episodes per task. The supplementary task tables report the complete per-task and aggregate success counts.


#### B.5 Additional MOTUS baseline

We evaluate the publicly released motus-robotics/Motus_robotwin2 model [ 3 , 23 ] at training step 40,000. The policy receives three RGB camera views, language instructions, and measured joint positions, and uses the released action-normalization statistics. It predicts and executes 16 actions per observation with ten denoising steps. The evaluation covers the same six conditions, with 100 valid episodes for each of the 50 tasks per condition.


#### B.6 Evaluation coverage

The reported results evaluate one trained checkpoint per model and benchmark at the specified corruption severities. The RoboTwin photometric comparisons use the parameters specified above, and mixed noise follows the prescribed Gaussian-plus-speckle distribution. LIBERO Plus contributes one rollout per evaluated variant. These choices define the sampling protocol for all reported aggregate and category-level results. Where differences are tabulated for LIBERO Plus, pp denotes percentage points and values are computed before rounding.

### Appendix C Additional method details


#### C.1 Deterministic HOG targets

We apply a deterministic transform $\mathcal{H}$ independently to each frame of the composed RGB clip. After mapping RGB values to $[0,1]$ , we compute the grayscale intensity

[Tableau] $$Y=0.2989R+0.5870G+0.1140B.$$ (S3)

Centered finite differences with replicated boundaries yield horizontal and vertical gradients $g_{x}$ and $g_{y}$ . Their magnitude and unsigned orientation are

[Tableau] $$m=\sqrt{g_{x}^{2}+g_{y}^{2}},\qquad\phi=\operatorname{atan2}(g_{y},g_{x})\bmod\pi.$$ (S4)

Within each $8\times 8$ cell, gradient magnitudes are accumulated into nine orientation bins using linear interpolation between neighboring bins. We apply L2-Hys normalization over overlapping $2\times 2$ -cell blocks by normalizing, clipping at $0.2$ , and renormalizing with a small positive stabilizer. Contributions from overlapping blocks are averaged for each cell.

To match the video VAE input, we rasterize the normalized cell histograms $h_{qk}$ into three channels. Here, $q$ indexes a cell and $k\in\{0,\ldots,8\}$ indexes an orientation bin. With $\beta_{k}=(k+\tfrac{1}{2})\pi/9$ , define

[Tableau] $\displaystyle e_{q}$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{k}h_{qk},$ $\displaystyle c_{q}$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{k}h_{qk}\cos(2\beta_{k}),$ (S5) $\displaystyle d_{q}$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{k}h_{qk}\sin(2\beta_{k}),$ $\displaystyle b$ $\displaystyle=\max(\max_{q}e_{q},\varepsilon).$

The cell raster $r_{q}=(2e_{q}/b-1,c_{q}/b,d_{q}/b)$ is clipped to $[-1,1]$ and repeated across the pixels in the cell. The doubled angle provides a consistent representation of unsigned orientation. The result is a HOG-derived three-channel image that summarizes the nine-bin cell histograms. It is encoded using the same frozen VAE weights and latent convention as the RGB stream:

[Tableau] $$Z^{h}=\mathcal{E}\bigl(\mathcal{H}(I_{0:T})\bigr).$$ (S6)

Targets are derived from recorded demonstration frames, including ground-truth future frames. The transform has no trainable parameters and requires neither object annotations nor a pretrained model for target generation.

Both are initialized from the pretrained RGB DiT and retain separate trainable parameters. The action DiT and its initialization follow the matched FastWAM configuration, with benchmark-specific input and output projections. Section C.2 summarizes the backbone configuration.

Each DiT embeds its noisy state and flow time using modality-specific transformer parameters. At each layer, queries, keys, and values are projected to a common head geometry and combined through masked mixed attention. The attention output is split into three streams, each with its own output projection, contextual cross-attention, and feed-forward network. The RGB and HOG output heads predict latent velocities, while the action head predicts velocities in normalized action coordinates. Masked attention couples the three DiT modules during training.


#### C.3 Asymmetric attention and removable auxiliary supervision

The attention diagram in the main paper depicts the visibility pattern at each coupled DiT layer. RGB queries attend to RGB keys, and HOG queries attend to both RGB and HOG keys. Both visual streams follow the first-frame-causal rule: observed-frame queries attend only to observed-frame keys, whereas future queries attend to the observed frame and all noisy future frames. Future visual tokens can therefore interact in both temporal directions. Action queries attend to observed RGB keys and to keys from the same action copy. The mask isolates independently sampled action copies and excludes HOG features from action conditioning.

This structure has two properties. First, RGB and action forward computations are independent of HOG states, allowing inference to use the RGB and action DiT modules alone. Observed RGB features depend on the current observation, keeping action conditioning consistent between training and inference. Second, HOG predictions depend on RGB features through the permitted HOG-to-RGB attention connections, allowing the auxiliary loss to update the RGB backbone through backpropagation. For fixed parameters and conditioning, these dependencies can be expressed as

[Tableau] $$\frac{\partial\widehat{u}^{a}}{\partial Z^{h}}=0,\qquad\nabla_{\theta_{v}}\mathcal{L}_{h}\not\equiv 0,$$ (S7)

where $\widehat{u}^{a}$ is the action velocity prediction and $\theta_{v}$ denotes RGB DiT parameters. The first relation expresses forward independence; the second identifies the gradient path from HOG prediction to the RGB backbone. In the standard configuration, the HOG DiT receives its clean first latent frame during training, but RGB and action queries cannot attend to this conditioning frame.


#### C.4 Joint flow-matching training

We retain the flow-matching formulation [ 20 ] of the baseline. For each modality $m\in\{v,h,a\}$ , let $X^{v}=Z^{v}$ , $X^{h}=Z^{h}$ , and $X^{a}=A$ be the clean training states. Gaussian noise $\epsilon^{m}$ and flow times are sampled independently for the three streams. Using $\sigma_{m}\in[0,1]$ to denote the resulting noise level, the corrupted state and target velocity are

[Tableau] $$\widetilde{X}^{m}=(1-\sigma_{m})X^{m}+\sigma_{m}\epsilon^{m},\qquad u^{m}=\epsilon^{m}-X^{m}.$$ (S8)

The baseline time sampler applies a shift to a uniform draw, $\sigma=\eta r/[1+(\eta-1)r]$ , where $r\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)$ and $\eta=5$ . After corruption, the first RGB and HOG latent frames are replaced by their clean conditioning values. Their token-wise flow times are also set to zero, matching the clean observed-frame computation used at inference. Predictions for these first frames are excluded from the corresponding visual losses; only future latent frames are supervised.

The model jointly predicts $\widehat{u}^{v}$ , $\widehat{u}^{h}$ , and $\widehat{u}^{a}$ using the mixed-attention backbone. For each modality, the loss is a weighted velocity-prediction error,

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{m}=\mathbb{E}\left[w(\sigma_{m})\left\|\widehat{u}^{m}-u^{m}\right\|_{\mathrm{valid}}^{2}\right],$$ (S9)

where $\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{valid}}^{2}$ averages over feature dimensions and valid target positions, excluding padded observations and actions. We retain the baseline time-dependent weight,

[Tableau] $$w(\sigma)=\frac{\exp[-2(\sigma-\tfrac{1}{2})^{2}]-\exp(-\tfrac{1}{2})}{0.1894955536+10^{-10}},$$ (S10)

and combine the objectives as

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}=\lambda_{v}\mathcal{L}_{v}+\lambda_{h}\mathcal{L}_{h}+\lambda_{a}\mathcal{L}_{a}.$$ (S11)

The configuration with three DiT modules sets all three coefficients to one. The HOG term supervises velocity prediction in the frozen video latent space.

Our full-training configurations use eight copies of the same action chunk per visual sample, each with independently sampled noise and flow time. The copies are packed together; each attends to the observed RGB frame and to itself, but not to other copies, and the action loss is averaged across them. The setting $\tau=8$ denotes this Monte Carlo replication of the action objective. The packed copies share a single RGB/HOG visual prediction, and the baseline uses the same number of action copies.

For full-parameter training, the RGB DiT, HOG DiT, action DiT, and proprioceptive projection are optimized, while the video VAE and text encoder remain frozen. The HOG transform itself has no parameters to optimize. The frozen encoders preserve the pretrained latent representations, while the DiT modules adapt to the joint prediction objectives.


#### C.5 Inference

Section C.4 shows action generation from the current observation.

> Figure S2 : HOG-WAM inference. The RGB DiT module encodes the current observation into cached keys and values. Conditioned on these features, language, and proprioception, the action DiT module integrates a velocity field from Gaussian noise to an action sequence $A$ . Receding-horizon execution applies a prefix of the sequence before updating the observation. Auxiliary HOG prediction is confined to training, preserving the FastWAM inference architecture.

At inference, the HOG DiT and future visual tokens are omitted. The current RGB canvas is encoded by the frozen VAE, and the observed RGB features are computed once with the instruction and proprioceptive context. Their per-layer keys and values are cached while the action DiT integrates its velocity field from Gaussian noise to an action chunk. With $N$ integration steps and a decreasing schedule $1=\sigma_{N}>\cdots>\sigma_{0}=0$ , $A_{i}$ denotes the action chunk at noise level $\sigma_{i}$ . A solver step is

[Tableau] $$A_{i-1}=A_{i}+(\sigma_{i-1}-\sigma_{i})\widehat{u}^{a}_{\theta}(A_{i},\sigma_{i}\mid I_{0},\ell,s_{0}).$$ (S12)

The output is transformed back to the action units used by the benchmark. The controller executes the configured chunk prefix, receives a new observation, and replans. For RoboTwin, FastWAM and HOG-WAM predict 32 absolute joint-position actions and execute 24 before replanning; for LIBERO, they predict 32 end-effector actions and execute eight. Both policies use the same action representation and prediction and execution horizons within each benchmark. HOG-WAM inference uses the RGB and action DiT modules.


### Appendix D Real-robot tasks and evaluation


##### Task suite.

The four Franka tasks span reorientation, containment, stacking, and insertion. Reorientation requires the white plastic bracket to remain in the intended upside-down pose; placement requires the target produce to lie fully inside the basket. Stacking requires the red block to rest stably on the blue block; insertion requires the cylinder to be seated in its circular holder. The main paper shows four recorded demonstration frames in the upper row and their corresponding gradient visualizations in the lower row, with one task per column. These monochrome displays use the same Sobel-based visualization as the method figures; the three-channel HOG target is defined in Sec. C.1 .


##### Demonstrations and policy training.

The collection contains 407,143 frames from 1,000 demonstrations recorded at 30 Hz. Produce placement contributes 400 demonstrations, and each other task contributes 200. Observations include images from a main camera and a wrist camera. The state comprises seven measured joint positions and the gripper width; each action comprises seven absolute joint targets in radians and a binary gripper command. Training preserves this sampling rate. FastWAM and HOG-WAM use the same trajectory-level split of 900 training and 100 validation demonstrations, with normalization statistics computed only from training trajectories. Both models use an effective batch size of 128, $\tau=8$ , and a peak learning rate of $2.5\times 10^{-5}$ . The learning rate uses a 300-step warmup and follows a 6,000-step cosine schedule through step 3,000. It then decays continuously from that value to $2.5\times 10^{-7}$ at step 30,000. Optimizer state is preserved across the schedule transition. The physical evaluation uses the final step-30,000 checkpoint for each model.


##### Observation conditions and scoring.

The comparison uses RGB-input policies under clean observations and synthetic mixed noise. The noisy condition applies the same mixed-noise operator as RoboTwin-Plus ( Appendix B ) to the composed RGB observation before visual encoding. It combines additive Gaussian noise with standard deviation 10 in 8-bit intensity units and multiplicative speckle noise with standard deviation 0.05. The resulting pixel values are rounded and clipped to $[0,255]$ . Language and proprioceptive conditioning are unchanged. Each task–model–condition group contains 25 recorded binary outcomes, giving 100 trials per model and condition and 400 trials overall. Each trial has a maximum budget of 200 steps and ends early when the task succeeds or a failure/error occurs. After each trial, the robot is reset using the reset command. All 25 recorded outcomes enter the denominator of their corresponding group. Appendix D reports the success counts. The macro-average assigns equal weight to the four tasks; because trial counts are equal, it also equals the pooled success rate within a model and condition.
