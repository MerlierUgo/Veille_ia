## WeVisForensics: Self-Evolving Agent for Omni AI-Generated Video Detection

Recent advances in video generation call for reliable detection grounded in explicit forensic evidence. However, current methods primarily depend on single-pass chain-of-thought reasoning that overlooks brief or localized artifacts, or on predefined multi-agent workflows that offer limited flexibility in selecting relevant inspections. Even with adaptive investigation, optimizing the investigation policy alone does not directly improve the forensic capabilities of fixed experts. We introduce WeVisForensics , an agentic framework in which a main investigator agent coordinates a self-evolving library of forensic inspector sub-agents spanning visual and audio cues. The investigator learns which artifact type to examine, over which window, and when to conclude, and these interactions provide shared feedback for both levels of agents. Our dual-timescale policy optimization strategy, DTPO , closes this loop through fast reinforcement learning of the investigator and slower refinement of the inspectors, guided by annotated artifact intervals, to report subtle artifacts they once dismissed. Furthermore, we curate ForenCue-30K , combining role-specific forensic annotations and interaction trajectories to supervise individual inspections and their coordination, with an accompanying reinforcement-learning pool. Experiments show that WeVisForensics averages 95.2% detection accuracy across in-domain, out-of-domain and agentic benchmarks, 9.0% above the strongest prior detector, while grounding its verdicts in localized evidence.

### 1 Introduction

Rapid advances in video generation have improved the visual fidelity, temporal coherence, and realism of synthetic content, bringing new possibilities to media creation. The same progress makes fabricated recordings increasingly difficult to distinguish from authentic ones, raising concerns over misinformation, impersonation, and public trust. Reliable AI-generated video detection is therefore becoming an essential component of media forensics, where decisive cues span visual, textual, and audio dimensions and call for role-specific inspection (Figure 1 ).

To meet this need, recent studies [ 44 , 45 , 28 , 20 , 36 , 15 , 34 , 23 , 18 , 33 , 56 , 30 , 46 ] use multimodal large language models (MLLMs) either to reason about authenticity in natural language or to coordinate expert analysis. However, as illustrated in Figure 12 , these approaches still face two challenges in how forensic evidence is examined and used. a) Holistic single-pass reasoning. CoT-centric methods [ 36 , 20 , 28 , 44 , 45 ] judge the whole video in one pass, with one model covering every artifact type. Artifacts confined to a brief interval or a small region are easily missed or explained away by hallucinated rationales (Figure 6 a), and claims entangled in one free-form trace cannot be checked or supervised individually. b) Static workflows or coordinator-only learning. Agent-based detectors either run a fixed expert pipeline [ 46 , 30 , 17 ] or train only the coordinator over fixed specialists. ARGUS [ 56 ] collects four expert reports and freezes the observers while training the judge, so irrelevant reports can outweigh the one that finds the artifact (Figure 6 b). Tool-augmented detectors [ 33 , 47 ] instead learn when to invoke tools, yet keep tool backends fixed. Both remain bounded by the local accuracy of frozen sub-agents, since an inspector that misses a subtle cue limits what better routing or aggregation can recover (Figure 6 , right).

To overcome these challenges, we introduce WeVisForensics (Figure 2 ), an agentic paradigm that couples adaptive evidence collection with a self-evolving library of forensic inspector sub-agents coordinated by a main investigator agent along two complementary designs. 1) Adaptive forensic analysis. The Forensic Sub-agent Library (FSL) holds trainable inspectors that examine visual appearance, physical consistency, commonsense plausibility, and on-screen text, while audio tools inspect the soundtrack when one is present. Each call specifies a forensic role and a temporal scope, and the selected inspector returns findings that inform subsequent reasoning. The investigator uses these observations to request further inspections or issue an authenticity verdict, so evidence collection adapts to each video beyond a predefined workflow. 2) Investigator-inspector self-evolution. Adaptive dispatch still relies on what each inspector can detect, so the inspectors must also improve from the calls they serve. Yet because the investigator decides which inputs the inspectors encounter while their responses shape its decisions, updating both on every batch makes each a moving target for the other. Our DTPO ( D ual- T imescale P olicy O ptimization) therefore trains both from shared trajectories on two timescales (Figure 3 ). The investigator’s inspection policy is updated on every rollout batch using outcome rewards and call-level feedback. The inspectors accumulate the calls issued by the investigator, keep those grounded in annotated artifact intervals, select responses with interaction feedback for privileged distillation, revise their skill cards of reporting rules, and periodically refresh the FSL. The refined inspectors then return to subsequent rollouts, so the agent evolves from its own environment.

Moreover, existing forensic video datasets [ 44 , 15 , 20 ] supervise a single detector with a verdict or a holistic rationale, providing neither role-specific evidence for sub-agents nor trajectories for coordinating them. We therefore curate ForenCue-30K , which decomposes forensic supervision by role. Each record isolates one forensic perspective and specifies a verdict, a confidence, and the temporal interval of the artifact, so that a sub-agent learns to report what its role can verify, including when the evidence is inconclusive. Agentic trajectories are assembled from executed sub-agent calls rather than imagined observations, so every decision is conditioned on evidence the main agent would actually receive. An accompanying RL pool supports DTPO. Since existing benchmarks score only the final verdict, we build AgenticCue , an agentic benchmark that groups generated videos by forensic category and evaluates both detection accuracy and whether an agent invokes the relevant sub-agents. Our contributions are threefold:

- • We propose DTPO , an RL algorithm in which the investigator and its inspectors co-evolve from shared interactions on fast and slow timescales. Inspectors refine their weights and skill cards on the very calls the investigator issues, so its rollouts become their curriculum.

- • We present WeVisForensics , an agentic video forensics framework in which a main agent adaptively dispatches inspections to the FSL. The invocation strategy and sub-agent competence are jointly learned through DTPO.

- • We curate ForenCue-30K , a role-structured dataset with agentic trajectories covering diverse generators, and introduce AgenticCue , the first agentic forensic benchmark that evaluates both accuracy and call precision.


### 2 WeVisForensics Agent Framework

WeVisForensics treats AI-generated video detection as an investigation rather than a single act of perception. A human forensic analyst rarely judges a video from one glance: they form a hypothesis from the overall content, zoom in on the moment and the region where it can be tested, consult the appropriate specialist, and keep collecting evidence until the conclusion is supported. WeVisForensics turns this workflow into an agent system with two levels. A main agent, the investigator , plans the investigation, and a Forensic Sub-agent Library (FSL) of inspectors examines the video from complementary forensic perspectives and returns structured evidence.

The framework is built on three design principles. (i) Separation of planning and perception. The investigator decides what to check and where , while each inspector decides whether the artifact of its specialty is present in the requested scope. Planning thus scales with the complexity of a video, and perception remains specialized and verifiable. (ii) Role-decomposed evidence. Every inspection returns a verdict, a calibrated confidence, and a visibly grounded explanation for a single forensic perspective. Each piece of evidence can therefore be audited, supervised, and credited on its own, instead of being entangled in one free-form rationale. (iii) Evolvable components. The parts of the system whose competence depends on the changing distribution of generators, namely the investigation policy and the visual inspectors, are trained to improve from the system’s own interactions, whereas measurement-like tools stay fixed and provide a stable reference. Sec. 2.1 specifies the investigation protocol, Sec. 2.2 describes each member of the FSL, and Sec. 4 presents the training pipeline.

#### 2.1 Investigation Protocol


##### Observation and actions.

An investigation starts from a global overview of the video: frames sampled at approximately 1 FPS across the full duration and uniformly reduced to at most 16, each tagged with its absolute timestamp, together with the duration and an indicator of whether an audio stream is present. At every turn, the investigator emits a single structured message consisting of a reasoning field and an action. The reasoning states the current hypothesis, what the evidence gathered so far does or does not establish, and why the next inspection is informative. Before the final answer, it synthesizes all returned evidence, including contrary and inconclusive findings. The action either calls one tool with its arguments, a temporal window $[t_{s},t_{e}]$ for the window-based inspectors, a single timestamp for OCR, and no arguments for the whole-track audio tools, or terminates the investigation with a real/fake verdict.

Formally, a video $x$ with label $y\in\{\text{real},\text{fake}\}$ and duration $T_{x}$ is first summarized by the overview

[Tableau] $$o_{x}=\big(\{(f_{k},\nu_{k})\}_{k=1}^{K},\;T_{x},\;\alpha_{x}\big),$$ (1)

where $f_{k}$ is the frame at absolute timestamp $\nu_{k}$ and $\alpha_{x}\in\{0,1\}$ indicates an audio stream. Let $\pi_{\theta}$ denote the main policy and $\{p_{\phi_{s}}\}_{s\in\mathcal{S}}$ the FSL members. At turn $t$ , the history $h_{t}=(o_{x},a_{1},z_{1},\ldots,a_{t-1},z_{t-1})$ contains the overview and all preceding interactions. An inspection action $a_{t}=(s_{t},u_{t})$ specifies the sub-agent $s_{t}$ and its scope $u_{t}$ :

[Tableau] $$a_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h_{t}),\qquad z_{t}\sim p_{\phi_{s_{t}}}(\cdot\mid v(x,u_{t}),q_{s_{t}}),$$ (2)

where $v(x,u_{t})$ is the input extracted from scope $u_{t}$ (sampled frames for visual sub-agents and the soundtrack for audio tools), $q_{s_{t}}$ is the role instruction together with the sub-agent’s skill card (Sec. 4.2 ), and $z_{t}$ is the response appended to the history. The investigation ends with an answer action $a_{L}=\textsc{answer}(\hat{y})$ , producing the trajectory $\tau=(o_{x},a_{1},z_{1},\ldots,a_{L-1},z_{L-1},a_{L})$ .


##### Call budget and protocol rules.

Let $C_{t}$ be the number of tool calls in $h_{t}$ and $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{aud}}\subset\mathcal{S}$ the audio tools. An inspection action $a_{t}=(s,u)$ is admissible only if

[Tableau] $$C_{t}<4,\qquad(s,u)\notin\{a_{t^{\prime}}\}_{t^{\prime}<t},\qquad u\subseteq[0,T_{x}],\qquad s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{aud}}\Rightarrow\alpha_{x}=1,$$ (3)

that is, at most four calls, no repeated call, scopes inside the video, and no audio tools on silent videos. The answer action is admissible once at least one call has succeeded, so an investigation has at most five assistant turns. A trajectory is valid if every message parses and every action is admissible, and it is scored as

[Tableau] $$R(\tau)=\mathbb{1}[\tau\text{ is valid}]\cdot\mathbb{1}[\hat{y}=y],$$ (4)

so malformed messages and rule violations count as errors. Within these limits, the investigator is instructed to choose the smallest sufficient set of inspections rather than exhausting the budget: an obvious, well-localized artifact often needs one call, and a further call is justified only by an independent uncertainty or complementary evidence. Temporal scopes are kept within one continuous shot. For videos longer than 30 seconds, the investigator targets a localized event, normally within a 15-second window, instead of a near-full-video range.


##### How reports are interpreted.

Inspector reports are dimensional and may conflict. Each visual report carries one of three verdicts, issue , no_issue , or inconclusive , with a confidence of high, medium, or low. The investigator treats inconclusive as moderate evidence that records a genuine limitation, never as a hidden decision in either direction, and treats no_text , tool failures, and purely descriptive audio profiles as neutral unless they contain a concrete inconsistency. A no_issue finding in one scope is not sufficient to establish the authenticity of the whole video, so the final verdict must integrate evidence across calls.


#### 2.2 Forensic Sub-agent Library

The FSL covers six forensic perspectives (Table 1 ). Four visual inspectors, one each for appearance, physical consistency, common sense, and on-screen text, share the main agent’s architecture but keep separate parameters, so that each can specialize without interfering with the others or with the investigation policy. Two audio tools analyze the soundtrack when one is present. Every visual inspector returns a structured report

[Tableau] $$z=(v,\,c,\,e),\qquad v\in\mathcal{V}_{s},\quad c\in\{\text{high},\text{medium},\text{low}\},$$ (5)

where $\mathcal{V}_{s}$ is the verdict set of role $s$ (Table 1 ), $c$ is the confidence, and $e$ is an explanation that cites timestamps inside the inspected scope.

##### Window-based visual inspectors.

The appearance, physical, and commonsense inspectors receive the frames of the requested window decoded at 2 FPS, each labeled with its absolute timestamp,

[Tableau] $$v\big(x,[t_{s},t_{e}]\big)=\big\{(x(\nu),\nu)\;\big|\;\nu\in[t_{s},t_{e}],\ 2\nu\in\mathbb{Z}\big\},$$ (6)

where $x(\nu)$ is the frame at time $\nu$ , and follow a role-specific checklist. The appearance inspector checks texture stability under motion, the integrity of fine details and boundaries such as hair, fingers, teeth, and small objects, unexplained local changes in sharpness, ghosting or duplication, and the temporal and geometric consistency of lighting, shadows, and reflections. The physical inspector checks biological and kinematic motion, structural integrity of bodies and rigid objects, object permanence across occlusion, contact and grasping, and the dynamics of fluids and deformable materials. The commonsense inspector checks causality, contextual consistency of objects and roles, affordances, and event order. The investigator calls appearance and physical checks on most videos and reserves commonsense checks for overviews that suggest a specific semantic or causal anomaly, such as an animal performing a human task or an event whose outcome contradicts ordinary world knowledge. All three inspectors share one reporting discipline. issue requires a concrete anomaly supported by the supplied frames, and no_issue requires the relevant content to be sufficiently observable and consistent. inconclusive is returned when the required evidence is absent, not meaningfully exercised, or obscured by sampling, blur, occlusion, or resolution. Confidence is calibrated to the strength and coverage of the evidence: a largely static subject cannot support a high-confidence physical judgment, and one clean frame cannot support a high-confidence appearance judgment. Normal cuts, camera motion, motion blur, compression, stylization, and animation are explicitly not anomalies by themselves, which prevents the inspectors from equating post-production with generation.


##### OCR inspector.

Video generators still struggle to render text: glyphs that start legible often fuse, break, drift, or melt as the clip proceeds, which makes on-screen text an independent and highly diagnostic forensic channel. Text, however, occupies a small fraction of the frame, and fine stroke structure is lost once the frame is resized for a vision-language model. The OCR inspector is therefore a three-stage pipeline operating on the single frame $x(\nu)$ at the timestamp $\nu$ chosen by the investigator. (1) Localization. PaddleOCR [ 6 ] detects and recognizes candidate text lines $\mathcal{B}=\mathrm{Det}(x(\nu))$ , each with a normalized bounding box and detection and recognition confidences. A frame with $\mathcal{B}=\varnothing$ returns no_text . (2) Text-presence routing. OCR detectors fire on textures, faces, and compression patterns. The inspector views the full frame with the candidate list, decides only whether genuine text exists, and selects informative regions $\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{B}$ with $|\mathcal{R}|\leq 6$ , including subtitles, signs, labels, license plates, UI elements, and text on background objects. If every candidate is a false positive, $\mathcal{R}=\varnothing$ and it returns no_text . (3) Crop-level glyph inspection. Each region $b_{i}\in\mathcal{R}$ is cropped with a 15% margin at a higher pixel budget than the full frame. The inspector judges every crop independently against the full-frame context, assigning a crop label $g_{i}\in\mathcal{Q}=\{$ regular , warped , generation-degraded , too blurry , stylized or logo , inconclusive $\}$ , and looks for fused or broken strokes, malformed character topology, inconsistent repeated characters, and drifting baselines. Given its proposed verdict $\hat{v}$ , the reported verdict is

[Tableau] $$v=\begin{cases}\texttt{inconclusive},&\hat{v}=\texttt{text\_suspicious}\ \text{and}\ g_{i}\notin\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{gen}}\ \forall\,b_{i}\in\mathcal{R},\\ \hat{v},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}$$ (7)

where $\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{gen}}=\{$ warped , generation-degraded $\}$ . A text-level forgery claim thus always rests on at least one crop with visibly generated glyphs. The investigator calls OCR whenever the overview contains captions, signage, packaging, handwriting, or other text, and targets the timestamp where the text looks least stable. Figure 15 in Appendix F shows a complete example, in which an appearance check first flags an unnaturally smooth facial surface and a targeted OCR call then confirms malformed glyphs on the enlarged crops.


##### Audio tools.

Generated videos increasingly carry synthesized speech, and an inconsistency between what is heard and what is seen is often more conspicuous than any visual artifact. The voice profile tool performs speaker diarization, separates overlapping speech, and estimates for each speaker an age, female/male/child probabilities, and arousal, dominance, and valence scores. The report is descriptive by design: a strong age or gender conflict with a clearly visible speaking face is useful supporting evidence, whereas dubbing, voice-over, and multiple speakers must be discounted. The ASR analysis tool transcribes the soundtrack with word-level timestamps and asks a frozen language model to review only concrete transcript and timing anomalies, such as robotic, uniformly spaced segments, against natural patterns such as hesitations and breathing pauses. Both tools are invoked on videos with an audio stream, typically when a speaking face or a talking-head scene is visible.


##### Trainable and fixed members.

The FSL splits into $\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{evo}}\cup\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{fix}}$ , where $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{evo}}$ contains the four visual inspectors and $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{fix}}$ the two audio tools. Each member $s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{evo}}$ evolves during training through two complementary channels: its LoRA adapter $\phi_{s}$ is distilled from the investigator’s own calls, and its skill card, a short list of Report and Ignore rules appended to the role instruction $q_{s}$ , is revised by a reflector model (Sec. 4.2 ). Visual artifacts are the cues that shift most rapidly as generators improve, and the calls issued by the investigator expose exactly the inputs, scopes, and difficulty levels on which the inspectors are consulted. The audio tools and the PaddleOCR detector remain fixed: their outputs are measurements, such as speaker attributes, transcripts, and text boxes, whose meaning should not drift while the investigator learns to interpret them, and the scarcity of labeled audio forgeries would make their adaptation unreliable. The investigator itself is optimized with reinforcement learning, so the system improves both in what it asks and in what it is told.


### 3 Data Construction

Built from 30,000 curated videos, ForenCue-30K provides multi-level supervision for our framework, comprising 117,552 role-specific records for the sub-agents, 33,896 interaction trajectories for the main agent, and a 7,500-row RL pool for DTPO (Figure 4 and Table 2 ). Its construction follows three principles: broad generator coverage , a role-aligned forensic taxonomy , and temporally grounded evidence annotation .

Generated videos are primarily curated from public datasets (GenBuster-200K [ 44 ] , RewardData [ 7 ] , VBench-2.0 [ 55 ] , and AIGVDBench [ 24 ] ) covering open-source and earlier-generation commercial models, supplemented with videos generated through AI video-generation platforms and specialized forgery domains including digital humans and speaker-mismatched audio forgeries. The generated training videos include CogVideoX [ 52 ] , HunyuanVideo [ 16 ] , LTX-Video [ 10 ] , EasyAnimate [ 49 ] , Wan 2.1/2.2 [ 38 ] , Gen-2/3, Pika 1.5, Kling 1.0/1.5, MiniMax-Video-01, Veo 3, and Vidu Q1. Real videos are sourced from OpenVid-1M [ 25 ] and Koala-36M [ 40 ] , supplemented with videos collected from the stock media platforms Pexels and Pixabay.

#### 3.1 Role-Grounded Supervision Construction

Supervision is constructed in a pipeline that mirrors the structure of the agent (Figure 4 ). Each curated video is first annotated separately for every forensic role, the annotations are then converted into training records for the inspectors, and executed inspector calls are finally assembled into investigation trajectories for the investigator. Every stage keeps the same role taxonomy, so the evidence an inspector learns to report is exactly the evidence the investigator learns to request and interpret.


##### Role-structured taxonomy.

Instead of one holistic rationale per video, supervision is organized by forensic perspective and subdivided into fine-grained artifact dimensions. Matching the six FSL roles, we categorize artifacts into Visual appearance (26,378 records), Physical consistency (32,348), Common sense (40,246), OCR consistency (18,580), ASR analysis , and Audio profile . Visual appearance covers texture and detail integrity, boundary blending, ghosting, and the consistency of lighting, shadows, and reflections. Physical consistency covers body and object structure, kinematics, object permanence, contact, and fluid or deformable dynamics. Common sense covers causality, affordances, contextual roles, and event order, and OCR consistency covers glyph structure and the temporal stability of rendered text. The audio roles cover the plausibility of speech timing and the agreement between the voice and the visible speaker. Each record isolates one perspective and specifies a verdict (issue, no-issue, or inconclusive), a confidence, an explanation, and the temporal interval of the artifact. Real videos contribute records under the same format, so every role sees both outcomes. This balance teaches each inspector to report only what its role can verify, rather than to equate “generated” with “an artifact of my type is visible”.


##### Evidence annotation.

For each video and role, a rubric-conditioned VLM annotator examines the timestamped frames under a role-specific guideline listing the artifact dimensions above and source-provenance constraints, localizing observable artifacts within temporal intervals and writing frame-grounded explanations of what is visibly wrong. Explanations must refer strictly to observable visual evidence, and roles without visible anomalies are recorded as no-issue even on generated videos, preventing forced attribution. A cross-family VLM verifier then checks each candidate against the video and refines or rejects it, filtering hallucinated explanations, over-generalized commonsense claims, and imprecise intervals. Human reviewers inspect sampled annotations per source and role during prompt development and source onboarding, and their findings are fed back into the guidelines and filtering rules, while audio inconsistencies are annotated directly by reviewers who watch each clip with sound (Appendix B ). In a quantitative audit of 340 video–role annotations, $99.1\%$ are supported by the visual evidence.

##### Inspector records.

Each annotation is converted into a record whose input is exactly what the inspector observes at inference: the frames of the requested window at 2 FPS for the window-based roles, and the frame at the requested timestamp for OCR. The target is the structured report, comprising the verdict, the confidence, and an explanation that cites timestamps within the window. Because the windows are specified in absolute video time, the same annotation can supervise the inspector and later serve as the grounding interval $I_{x,s}$ for selecting calls during DTPO.


##### Trajectory synthesis.

Interaction trajectories are constructed through execution-grounded synthesis. Given the video overview and verified role evidence as training-only context, a VLM trajectory planner decides which roles and temporal scopes to consult, modeling how an expert investigation prioritizes relevant checks. Every planned call is then executed by the corresponding inspector, and the planner writes the reasoning of each turn conditioned on the reports actually returned, so that the investigator never learns from imagined observations. All training-only metadata are stripped from student-facing messages, conditioning each decision strictly on inference-time observations. Trajectories cover both real and generated videos, use about two calls on average under the four-call budget, and reach $96.0\%$ appropriate tool selection in a human audit of 100 trajectories.


##### RL pool.

The name ForenCue-30K refers to the core video supervision set, while expanded annotation records, trajectories, and the accompanying RL pool are counted separately (Figure 4 ). The RL pool contains 7,500 rows spanning 15 collections and 56 source subsets, with 3,750 unique real and 3,180 unique generated clips repeated with source-dependent weights to balance labels and sources. Each generated clip carries its role-specific artifact intervals, which ground the selection of inspector calls in the slow process, and each clip carries a verified set of relevant roles, which defines the tool-coverage reward (Sec. 4.2 ).


##### Quality control.

Export checks validate record structure, role-specific fields, video duration, and the availability of sampled frames. For interaction trajectories, construction checks match call identifiers to stored responses and enforce the action budget and the protocol rules of Sec. 2.1 , and automated text checks screen the student-facing messages for training-only metadata leakage. Each trajectory record retains the video and source identifiers, sampled-frame timestamps, ordered messages, and target verdict.


#### 3.2 AgenticCue Benchmark

Existing benchmarks score only the final verdict, so an agent that reaches the right answer through irrelevant inspections, or that cannot handle a particular class of artifacts, remains invisible. AgenticCue is designed to expose both. Unlike the training set, which is dominated by open-source and earlier commercial models, it targets newer-generation commercial releases (Veo 3.1, Kling 2.5/3.0, Seedance 1.5/2.0, Hailuo 2.3/H3, Wan 2.6, Sora 2, Jimeng, and HappyHorse 1.0) alongside held-out digital-human, face-swap, and voice-mismatch audio forgeries, and thus also measures generalization to generators unseen during training.


##### Category construction.

The benchmark spans five forensic categories, Visual , Physical , OCR , Common sense , and Audio , each containing exactly 500 generated videos verified to exhibit that category’s artifact. Candidate clips are first annotated by model annotators and then confirmed by human reviewers. A clip may belong to several categories, and 170 clips do, giving 2,330 unique generated videos. All categories share the same 500 real videos, so each category forms a balanced set of 1,000 videos. None of its videos appear in ForenCue-30K.


##### Metrics.

For each category, we report binary detection accuracy on its balanced set, counting invalid or missing outputs as errors. To evaluate whether the agent consults the right specialist, we additionally report invocation precision, the fraction of generated videos on which the agent invokes tool $s$ that actually call for role $s$ . The reference set of relevant roles for the four visual tools comes from dense annotations in which two cross-family VLM annotators independently mark every role worth inspecting on each generated video and a label-blind judge resolves disagreements, while for audio it is the Audio category. Table 3 lists the resulting label counts and the base rates obtained by invoking a tool on every video. Precision is always read together with accuracy, which would drop if the agent skipped relevant inspections. Details are given in Appendix A .

### 4 Training Pipeline

WeVisForensics is trained in two stages. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on ForenCue-30K first teaches the investigator the investigation protocol and each inspector its role-specific reporting format (Sec. 4.1 ). DTPO then lets the investigator and the inspectors improve together from their own interactions (Sec. 4.2 ), and a scalable implementation keeps the joint training practical (Sec. 4.3 ). Every model is adapted with low-rank adapters on a shared backbone, so that the investigator and all inspectors can be loaded and served as one backbone plus a few lightweight adapters, and both stages update every model in the same parameterization.


#### 4.1 Stage I: Supervised Initialization

All models share the Qwen3.5-4B [ 31 ] backbone. The investigator and the four visual inspectors are trained as five separate rank-64 LoRA [ 11 ] adapters ( $\alpha{=}128$ ) on all linear layers of the language model, and the backbone, the vision encoder, and the vision-language projector are frozen throughout, so that adaptation concentrates on forensic reasoning rather than low-level visual features.

Each model is trained with the standard masked next-token objective. A training sample is a token sequence $y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{L_{y}})$ with a loss mask $m_{y,j}\in\{0,1\}$ that equals one on model-authored tokens and zero on instructions, overview frames, inspector reports, tool outputs, and padding. For a model with parameters $\psi$ trained on a dataset $\mathcal{D}$ ,

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\psi;\mathcal{D})=-\frac{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{j=1}^{L_{y}}m_{y,j}\log p_{\psi}(y_{j}\mid y_{<j})}{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{j=1}^{L_{y}}m_{y,j}},$$ (8)

so the loss averages the negative log-likelihood over all supervised tokens. The investigator minimizes $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\theta;\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{inv}})$ on interaction trajectories, where the mask covers the reasoning, every action, and the final verdict, and each inspector $s$ minimizes $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\phi_{s};\mathcal{D}_{s})$ on its role-specific records, where the mask covers the structured report of Eq. ( 5 ).


##### Investigator.

The investigator is trained on the 33,896 agentic trajectories of ForenCue-30K, extended with trajectories on additional videos covering a broader range of content. Each trajectory contains every executed call and returned report together with the reasoning that motivates each step and the final verdict, and is packed into one multi-turn sample with a maximum length of 32,768 tokens. The adapter is trained with a learning rate of $2{\times}10^{-4}$ and a cosine schedule with 5% warm-up in two consecutive single-epoch stages with different data orderings, and the stage-1 adapter is merged into the backbone before stage 2 begins.


##### Inspectors.

Each visual inspector is trained on its own portion of the 117,552 role-specific records, extended with records on additional videos. Each record pairs a scoped input with a verdict, a confidence, and an evidence-grounded explanation. Real and generated videos are kept in every role, so that an inspector does not learn to report an issue by default. The inspectors follow the same adapter recipe as the investigator, with a maximum length of 32,768 tokens (16,384 for OCR, whose input is a single frame). The resulting system consists of a single backbone plus five small adapters, which makes it easy to store and serve.


#### 4.2 Stage II: DTPO

DTPO makes interaction experience the link between learning to request evidence and refining the inspectors that provide it. The investigator determines which inputs the inspectors encounter, and the resulting reports influence both its subsequent decisions and which interactions are selected for inspector training. Updating both at the same time would make each a moving target for the other. The fast process therefore updates only the investigator on every rollout batch, while the slow process accumulates role-specific interaction queues over multiple batches and aligns the inspectors with a slowly changing EMA teacher [ 37 ] . The updated FSL then supplies observations for later rollouts, closing the loop. Video-level labels and artifact annotations are used only during training. At inference, the learned investigator and inspectors execute the protocol of Sec. 2.1 with fixed parameters.


##### Fast process: investigator policy learning.

For each input, we collect $G$ interaction trajectories $\{\tau_{g}\}_{g=1}^{G}$ with the current main policy and a fixed FSL snapshot. Each trajectory receives the correctness reward $R_{g}=R(\tau_{g})$ of Eq. ( 4 ), so invalid trajectories receive zero. We use GRPO [ 32 ] to normalize rewards within the group,

[Tableau] $$A_{g}=\frac{R_{g}-\overline{R}}{\operatorname{std}(R_{1},\ldots,R_{G})+\delta},$$ (9)

where $\delta$ stabilizes normalization. The terminal reward says whether the investigation succeeded, but not which of its inspections contributed. We therefore augment it with CLUE ( C all- L evel U tility E stimation), a per-action credit signal that measures the change in a frozen reference model’s support for the correct label after each inspection call. The reference scores the known label $y$ before and after the response to call $j$ . With probabilities normalized over the real/fake alternatives, let $\ell_{j}^{\mp}=\log p_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid h_{j}^{\mp})$ , where $h_{j}^{-}$ and $h_{j}^{+}$ are the histories before and after the response. The CLUE score is

[Tableau] $$c_{j}=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\log\frac{-\ell_{j}^{-}+\epsilon}{-\ell_{j}^{+}+\epsilon},-b,b\right),$$ (10)

where $\epsilon$ smooths the ratio and $b$ bounds the score. A positive score means that the returned report moved the reference toward the correct label, and a negative score means that it misled the reference. Because the reference is distinct from both the investigator and the inspector teachers, the score measures the informativeness of the evidence itself rather than the policy’s own belief. We center scores within each GRPO group. Writing $c_{g,j}$ for the score of call $j$ in trajectory $g$ , $\tilde{c}_{g,j}=c_{g,j}-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}_{j}|}\sum_{g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{j}}c_{g^{\prime},j}$ , where $\mathcal{G}_{j}$ is the set of rollouts in the group that issue at least $j$ calls. The token advantages become

[Tableau] $$\widehat{A}_{g,t}=\begin{cases}A_{g}+\lambda\tilde{c}_{g,j},&t\in\mathcal{I}_{g,j},\\ A_{g},&t\in\mathcal{M}_{g}\setminus\mathcal{I}_{g},\\ 0,&t\notin\mathcal{M}_{g},\end{cases}$$ (11)

where $\mathcal{M}_{g}$ contains main-agent output tokens, $\mathcal{I}_{g,j}$ is the token span of inspection action $j$ , $\mathcal{I}_{g}=\bigcup_{j}\mathcal{I}_{g,j}$ , and $\lambda$ weights call feedback. A clipped policy-ratio objective uses $\widehat{A}_{g,t}$ to update the investigator. Final-answer tokens retain $A_{g}$ , and inspector observations and padding are masked from the loss. Inspection choices thus receive feedback on the evidence they produced, while the final decision remains trained by its outcome.


##### Tool-coverage reward.

Correctness alone tolerates investigations that reach the right verdict through an irrelevant inspection, and such shortcuts generalize poorly to videos whose decisive artifact lies elsewhere. Each training video therefore carries a verified set of relevant forensic roles, derived from its role-specific annotations, and each trajectory receives the F1 score between this set and the set of tools it actually invoked. The F1 score is centered within the GRPO group and added to $A_{g}$ with weight $0.3$ after the correctness normalization, so it ranks investigations that share the same outcome without overriding correctness.


##### Slow process: collecting interactions for refinement.

Every call to a trainable inspector during rollouts is a candidate refinement example, so the main policy determines which inputs, temporal scopes, and difficulty levels the inspectors are refined on. Candidates are first grounded in annotations. Let $I_{x,s}$ be the annotated artifact intervals of role $s$ on a generated video $x$ , or the whole video if $x$ is real, and $u_{j}$ the scope inspected by call $j$ . The calls kept for refinement are

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{OPD}}=\biguplus_{x}\biguplus_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\big\{\,j_{x,s}\mid u_{j}\cap I_{x,s}\neq\varnothing\,\big\}.$$ (12)

Calls on generated videos without an overlapping annotation are excluded, since failing to find an artifact in such a scope is not an error of that role. We then partition each kept response by its verdict and the video label. On a real video, a reported issue is a false alarm and a no-issue verdict is correct . On a generated video, a call that reports no issue is a miss and one that reports an issue is a hit .

A correct verdict can still hinder subsequent forensic reasoning, for example an inconclusive or low-confidence report that leaves the investigator without usable evidence. The CLUE score in Eq. ( 10 ) identifies such responses: a correct response is relabeled as a hedge when $c_{j}<\kappa$ and its verdict is inconclusive or not stated with high confidence. False alarms, misses, and hedges are the responses whose decisions must change, while the remaining correct responses are retained so that refinement preserves decisions that are already right. The final trajectory need not be incorrect: a locally unhelpful report warrants refinement even when the investigator recovers the correct answer. All retained calls, rather than one call per prompt and role, enter role-specific queues. When a role is refreshed, its examples are arranged into batches with equal numbers of real and generated videos by subsampling the majority label.


##### Privileged inspector distillation.

Each example supplies its inspector input $u_{i}$ , collected response $z_{i}$ , and privileged context $g_{i}$ . For role $s$ , an exponential-moving-average (EMA) teacher receives $g_{i}$ , following the training-only supervision principle of privileged-information distillation [ 29 ] , while the student retains its inference-time input. For a real video, $g_{i}$ states the label. For a generated video, $g_{i}$ lists the annotated intervals that overlap the inspected scope within the role’s specialty and instructs the teacher to locate the corresponding anomaly in the supplied frames, treating the pointer as a cue for where to look rather than as evidence. As in on-policy distillation [ 1 ] , teacher and student distributions at a collected response prefix are

[Tableau] $$p^{T}_{i,t}=p_{\bar{\phi}_{s}}(\cdot\mid u_{i},g_{i},z_{i,<t}),\quad p^{S}_{i,t}=p_{\phi_{s}}(\cdot\mid u_{i},z_{i,<t}).$$ (13)

Let $t_{i}^{\star}$ be the position where the response commits to its verdict, $v_{i}^{\star}$ the label-consistent verdict token (issue or text-suspicious for generated videos, no-issue or text-ok for real ones), and $\bar{v}_{i}$ its opposite. The per-role objective is

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{s}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big[$ $\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{|z_{i}|}w_{i,t}\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\operatorname{sg}[p^{T}_{i,t}]\,\|\,p^{S}_{i,t}\right)}{\sum_{t=1}^{|z_{i}|}w_{i,t}}$ (14) $\displaystyle-\beta\log\frac{e^{o_{i}(v_{i}^{\star})}}{e^{o_{i}(v_{i}^{\star})}+e^{o_{i}(\bar{v}_{i})}}\Big],$

where $\operatorname{sg}$ stops teacher gradients, $w_{i,t}=\omega$ at decision-field tokens and $1$ elsewhere, $o_{i}(\cdot)$ denotes student logits at $t_{i}^{\star}$ , and $\beta$ weights the label correction of the verdict. The correction contrasts only the two opposing verdicts, so it corrects the decision without suppressing the inconclusive option or other vocabulary entries. For OCR, the same objective is further grounded on double-checked text-ok regions drawn from generated videos, so the inspector learns that regular text on a forged clip is not itself evidence of forgery. For responses whose decisions must change, the distillation term is dropped when the privileged teacher still assigns its highest probability at $t_{i}^{\star}$ to the student’s original verdict, in which case only the label correction is applied. The teacher supplies token distributions at the student’s collected prefixes, so no replacement trajectory has to be generated. The teacher tracks the student via $\bar{\phi}_{s}\leftarrow(1-\rho)\bar{\phi}_{s}+\rho\phi_{s}$ , and the student learning rate decays linearly over the investigator’s schedule, so the FSL settles as the inspection policy converges.


##### Skill-card evolution.

Adapter updates change how an inspector perceives. Some failure modes, however, are better expressed as explicit reporting rules, for example that motion blur on fast-moving hands is not a structural defect, or that a soft object deforming under direct contact is not a physics violation. Alongside its adapter weights, each trainable inspector therefore carries a skill card [ 39 ] , a short list of Report and Ignore rules appended to its role instruction and seen by student and teacher. After each distillation round, a frozen reflector LLM reviews recent calls that the current inspector still judges wrongly, with their labels, and proposes rule edits. A candidate card replaces the current one only if it yields more correct verdicts on held-out recent calls without losing any correct verdict on either real or generated videos. This acceptance test makes card edits monotone on recent experience and prevents the reflector from trading false alarms for misses. Examples of evolved cards are given in Appendix D .


##### Synchronizing fast and slow updates.

Training alternates rollout collection, investigator optimization, interaction selection, and periodic FSL refinement. Each batch is collected with the current main policy and a fixed inspector snapshot. After the investigator update, selected interactions enter role-specific queues. Every $H$ investigator steps, a synchronization barrier checks whether each queue contains enough examples to update its inspector. Eligible students are distilled, their skill cards are revised, and the new FSL snapshot is published before rollout collection resumes, while roles with insufficient examples retain their current parameters and keep their queues for the next barrier. This schedule lets the investigator learn between FSL updates while each refinement stage draws on accumulated interaction experience.


#### 4.3 Scalable Implementation

A single DTPO step runs $32\times G$ multi-turn investigations, each invoking up to four inspectors on video inputs, so inspector serving and FSL refreshes dominate the cost of training. The investigator and the four visual inspectors are served by separate vLLM engines that share the training cluster, and the rollout workers execute investigations asynchronously, so that a slow audio or OCR call does not stall other trajectories in the batch. The audio tools and PaddleOCR run as independent services. At each synchronization barrier, the four inspector refreshes are packed into two parallel lanes of GPUs, one handling visual appearance followed by common sense and the other physical consistency followed by OCR, which balances the per-lane workload because the roles accumulate very different numbers of calls. Refreshed adapters are merged into the backbone weights and published to the inference engines as full checkpoints rather than hot-swapped as LoRA modules, which we found to be numerically more robust on our hardware. Skill-card revision runs after publication. With a refresh every 20 steps, the full joint schedule of a few hundred investigator steps completes in about a day on our cluster.


#### 4.4 Implementation Details


##### Main models.

The base Qwen3.5-4B serves as the frozen CLUE reference $p_{\mathrm{ref}}$ . DTPO starts from the SFT adapters of Sec. 4.1 and keeps every model in the adapter parameterization. The fast process runs GRPO with $G{=}4$ rollouts per video, a batch of 32 videos, and at most 4 inspector calls per trajectory for one epoch over the RL pool. The investigator adapter is trained with a learning rate that warms up linearly to $1{\times}10^{-5}$ over the first 60 steps. CLUE uses $\epsilon{=}0.1$ , $b{=}1$ , and $\lambda{=}0.2$ , the tool-coverage bonus has weight $0.3$ , and the hedge threshold is $\kappa{=}{-}0.05$ . In the slow process, each visual inspector adapter is trained every $H{=}20$ investigator steps on class-balanced batches of 32 grounded calls, with a learning rate decayed linearly from $3{\times}10^{-5}$ over training progress, $\omega{=}5$ , $\beta{=}1$ , and $\rho{=}0.001$ . The RL pool used here extends the pool of Sec. 3 with additional labeled videos.

At the prompt level, accepted skill-card edits add reporting rules to the appearance and commonsense sub-agents. At the case level, the same inspection returns different evidence over training. The early sub-agent calls each scene coherent and the agent answers real, whereas the final sub-agent names the regenerating texture, the color-shifting paint stream, or the rigid milk stream, and the agent answers fake. Figure 6 shows the same effect at the agent level, where only the evolved sub-agents confirm the artifact. The gains thus come from sub-agents that learn to report artifacts they once called coherent.

### 6 Related Work


#### 6.1 AI-Generated Video Detection

Early detectors treat the task as binary classification over generation traces in images [ 41 , 27 , 43 , 35 ] and videos [ 2 , 5 , 54 , 13 , 22 ] , outputting a score without verifiable evidence, and benchmarks such as GVD [ 2 ] , GenVideo [ 5 ] , GenVidBench [ 26 ] , and AIGVDBench [ 24 ] scale up binary evaluation across generators. Building on MLLM-based image forensics [ 14 , 53 , 21 , 51 , 12 ] , recent video detectors pair each verdict with a natural-language account of the observed artifacts. Methods such as BusterX [ 44 ] , BusterX++ [ 45 ] , and VidGuard-R1 [ 28 ] train this reasoning with reinforcement learning, while Skyra [ 20 ] , VideoVeritas [ 36 ] , and VidForensics-M1 [ 23 ] strengthen it with artifact grounding, perception pretext tasks, and temporal-grounding rewards, respectively. IVY-FAKE [ 15 ] and Omni-Fake [ 18 ] provide annotated data for explainable detection, while OmniVL-Guard [ 34 ] unifies detection and grounding across text, images, and videos. However, these detector-centered formulations do not learn when to request a specialized follow-up inspection or how to adapt it to intermediate observations.


#### 6.2 Agent-Based Forensic Reasoning

General video agents query specialized models to gather evidence over long inputs [ 42 , 48 , 19 ] , while forensic agents organize analysis through explicit tools or specialized roles. Tool-augmented detectors such as ClueAegis [ 4 ] , OmniVL-Guard Pro [ 33 ] , and TUE-Detector [ 47 ] train a single MLLM to invoke specific toolchains or visual operations such as cropping, zooming, and segmentation. Multi-agent methods such as SafeGuard [ 46 ] , Hermes [ 17 ] , and DVAR [ 30 ] instead distribute evidence extraction, verification, and deliberation across specialized agents. ARGUS [ 56 ] and Deepfake-Agent [ 9 ] further train a judge or aggregator over fixed expert agents. However, the invoked experts in these designs never learn from the interactions they serve while the coordinating policy is trained, leaving the experts’ forensic response policies outside the ongoing policy-learning loop.


### 7 Conclusion

This paper presents WeVisForensics, an agentic framework for AI-generated video detection in which an investigator coordinates a self-evolving library of forensic inspectors. Within this framework, DTPO is introduced to optimize the investigator on a fast timescale while refining the inspectors from its calls on a slow one. To support training and evaluation, ForenCue-30K is constructed with role-specific supervision and executed trajectories, and AgenticCue is established to assess both detection verdicts and the inspections behind them. Extensive experiments across benchmarks demonstrate the effectiveness of WeVisForensics, highlighting the value of co-evolving the investigator with its inspectors.

### Appendix A Implementation Details


##### Training and inference setup.

The main agent and the four visual sub-agents are rank-64 LoRA adapters on a shared Qwen3.5-4B backbone, initialized from SFT adapters trained on 33,896 interaction trajectories and 117,552 role-specific records, while the base Qwen3.5-4B serves as the frozen CLUE reference $p_{\mathrm{ref}}$ . The main agent observes up to 16 uniformly sampled frames of the full video, and each window-based visual sub-agent inspects its requested window at 2 FPS, whereas the OCR sub-agent inspects the frame at its requested timestamp and the two audio tools analyze the whole soundtrack in Sec. 2.2 . In the fast process, GRPO samples $G{=}4$ rollouts per video with 32 videos per batch and at most 4 sub-agent calls per trajectory for one epoch over the RL pool, and the main-agent learning rate warms up linearly to $1{\times}10^{-5}$ . CLUE uses $\epsilon{=}0.1$ and $b{=}1$ in Eq. ( 10 ) and call-advantage weight $\lambda{=}0.2$ , and the tool-coverage F1 bonus has weight $0.3$ . In the slow process, calls with utility below $\kappa{=}{-}0.05$ are relabeled as hedges, and each visual sub-agent’s adapter is refreshed every $H{=}20$ main-agent steps on class-balanced batches of 32 grounded calls, with the sub-agent learning rate decayed linearly from $3{\times}10^{-5}$ over training progress, decision-token weight $\omega{=}5$ , label-correction weight $\beta{=}1$ , and teacher EMA rate $\rho{=}0.001$ . The audio tools remain fixed during training.


##### AgenticCue composition and evaluation metrics.

AgenticCue comprises $2{,}830$ unique videos in total: $2{,}330$ unique generated videos and $500$ fixed real videos. Each of the five forensic categories ( Visual , Physical , OCR , Common sense , and Audio ) contains exactly $500$ generated videos verified to exhibit that category’s artifact ( $170$ generated videos carry annotations for more than one category, yielding $2{,}330$ unique generated clips across the five $500$ -clip category sets). For category $s$ , binary detection accuracy ( $\text{Acc.}_{s}$ ) is evaluated under a balanced 1:1 pairing of its $500$ generated videos and the $500$ real videos ( $1{,}000$ videos per category), counting invalid or missing outputs as errors. Sub-agent invocation precision ( $\text{Prec.}_{s}$ ) measures whether the agent calls the relevant tool for its artifact: computed at the video level over the full pool of generated videos, with $\text{Prec.}_{s}=|C_{s}\cap G_{s}|/|C_{s}|$ where $C_{s}$ is the set on which the agent invokes tool $s$ at least once and $G_{s}$ is the set of videos that call for role $s$ . Two label systems are involved. The $500$ -clip category membership above only defines the accuracy subsets, and a clip assigned to one category can still exhibit artifacts of other roles. $G_{s}$ for the four visual roles therefore comes from the dense role annotations of Section 5 , where two cross-family VLM annotators independently mark every role worth inspecting on each generated video and a label-blind judge resolves disagreements. Two videos without a resolved annotation are excluded, leaving $2{,}328$ eligible videos for the visual roles. These annotations do not cover audio, so $G_{s}$ for audio is the $500$ -clip Audio category over all $2{,}330$ generated videos. An agent that invokes tool $s$ on every eligible video attains the base rate $|G_{s}|/|\text{eligible}|$ , which is $91.1\%$ , $78.6\%$ , $49.2\%$ , $24.0\%$ , and $21.5\%$ for the appearance, physical, OCR, commonsense, and audio roles. Table 3 summarizes these label counts. Precision is read together with category accuracy, which would drop if the agent skipped relevant inspections. DTPO raises accuracy on every AgenticCue category (Table 12 ), including OCR, commonsense, and audio.


### Appendix B Human Review of Annotations

Role-specific evidence annotations and main-agent interaction trajectories are produced by model annotators and coupled with human feedback (Figure 4 ) before entering the training records of Section 3 . During prompt development and source onboarding, human reviewers inspect sampled annotations per source and role, flagging hallucinated explanations, over-generalized commonsense claims, imprecise temporal windows, or redundant/omitted tool calls. These failure modes are fed back to refine the annotation prompts and filtering rules, while unplayable clips are discarded. For the audio role, reviewers work through a per-source queue, watch each video with its audio, and record whether the soundtrack is inconsistent with the visible speech along with the offending interval (Figure 11(a) ).

##### Paired review of evolved sub-agent evidence.

Verdict accuracy alone does not show whether evolution improves the evidence a sub-agent reports. We pair $200$ inspections from the evaluation benchmarks, each answered by the SFT and the evolved sub-agent under the same main agent and inspection window. The pairs are stratified by role, video label, and verdict agreement. A reviewer sees both responses in random order with model identities hidden (Figure 11(b) ), marks which response gives more accurate evidence, and flags in each response unsupported or mislocalized claims and missed visible anomalies. A second reviewer re-checks the judgments. The evolved sub-agents are preferred in $65$ pairs against $20$ , and the share of responses with any flaw falls from $30.5\%$ to $8.0\%$ (Table 10 ). ### Appendix C Detailed Detection Results

This section expands the main comparisons along two axes: the quality of the evidence returned by individual sub-agents (Table 8 ), and detection accuracy, fake recall, and F1 across benchmarks and generator categories (Table 12 ).


##### Sub-agent evidence improves across roles.

Evolving the library improves the evidence returned by every trainable role (Table 8 ). The physical sub-agent moves the most, with F1 on GB rising from 66.5% to 97.8%, and the largest gains of the appearance and OCR sub-agents reach 7.0% and 11.8%.

Three patterns explain these gains. First, the physical sub-agent stops hedging. Its inconclusive rate falls on every benchmark, from 26.9% to 0.3% on GB, 12.3% to 0.9% on GB++, 10.5% to 1.2% on MintVid, and 6.4% to 0.4% on AgenticCue, and its F1 rises by 9.1–31.3%. This matches the slow process, which relabels correct but inconclusive low-utility answers as hedges. Second, appearance evidence becomes more decisive. The share of annotated generated calls it flags rises from 96.0% to 99.8% on GB and from 66.0% to 76.2% on MintVid, and its MintVid F1 rises by 7.0%. Third, OCR evidence becomes sharper, with F1 rising on every benchmark and most on GB++ by 11.8%. The gains on the out-of-distribution MintVid, 7.0% for appearance and 19.2% for physical, show that the evolved evidence transfers to unseen generators.


##### Detection improves across evaluated categories.

Relative to the SFT agent, DTPO improves accuracy on all six MintVid categories and all five AgenticCue categories (Table 12 ), including an OCR fake-recall increase from 95.0% to 98.4%. Fake recall alone can be misleading. Across the 13 evaluated categories, WeVisForensics ranks first in both accuracy and F1 on 12. The largest gains over the SFT agent appear on the unseen MintVid generators, where accuracy rises by 7.5% on Kling, 6.5% on Sora2, and 5.1% on Hailuo.

### Appendix D Learned Skill Cards

This section lists the complete skill cards learned by the forensic library across four training runs. Each rule is appended to the user prompt of the corresponding sub-agent alongside the inspection query. Report rules specify generation artifacts to flag, while Ignore rules describe benign camera, motion, or compression effects that should not be mistaken for synthesis errors. The learned rules are local and contrastive. A typical Report rule describes an artifact against an intact reference in the same frame, such as a single fused glyph among sharp neighboring characters or melting facial contours against a sharp background, and the matching Ignore rule names the global degradation that produces a similar look, such as uniform blur or compression across the whole region. Because each edit is mined from calls that the current inspector still judges wrongly, the cards are specific to the failures of that run and differ in concrete content across runs. For example, the physical card of Run 2 targets finger articulation and garment topology, whereas that of Run 3 targets rigid tools sliding over granular surfaces and morphing sculptures.
