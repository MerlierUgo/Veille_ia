## Embo-Harness: Towards Understanding Frontier-Model-as-Policy Harness https://dravenalg.github.io/projects/Embo-Harness/

Frontier multimodal models exhibit extraordinary generalization in generating robot actions from observations and instructions, enabling a new paradigm in which they serve directly as policies within closed-loop robotic harnesses. However, the design principles of such harnesses, and the capabilities underlying their strong performance, remain unclear. We present Embo-Harness, a framework for systematically studying frontier-model-as-policy harnesses. We investigate four components of the robotic agentic loop, namely, prompting, planning, action execution, and tool use, and distill six empirical findings into a practical recipe. This recipe prioritizes interfaces that exploit the model’s native visual reasoning and tool-use capabilities over handcrafted plans, skills, or action priors, and identifies effective choices for action representation, chunking, reasoning effort, visual observations, short multi-action sequences, and policy refinement. On RoboLab-120, Embo-Harness achieves an 83.3% success rate, outperforming the strongest reported baseline by 26.2 percentage points. It also succeeds in 15 of 20 zero-shot trials across four real-world manipulation tasks. Beyond performance, controlled interventions indicate that the studied model depends more strongly on accurate spatial target estimation than on explicit low-level joint or trajectory generation. These results provide both a practical recipe for building strong frontier-model-as-policy harnesses and a clearer account of the capabilities that drive their performance.


### 1 Introduction

Recent advances in frontier models, such as GPT-6-Astra [ 49 ] , are reshaping robotic manipulation [ 74 , 62 ] . Their remarkable generalization capabilities enable them to handle complex instructions, diverse environments, long-horizon manipulation, and failure recovery, substantially outperforming existing robot foundation models (RFMs), such as Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) [ 78 , 6 , 38 , 36 , 67 ] and World Action Models (WAMs) [ 71 , 42 , 73 ] , on recent benchmarks [ 46 , 77 , 68 , 14 ] , offering a different possibility for future robotic policies.

A robotic harness is the software loop that connects observations and instructions to planning, tool use, action generation, execution, and feedback. As illustrated in Figure 2 , existing robotic harnesses can be broadly divided according to how actions are produced. Tool- or code-as-policy [ 31 , 44 , 33 , 32 , 20 , 24 , 16 , 65 , 29 ] harnesses use multimodal models [ 3 , 27 ] for high-level planning but execute actions through predefined tools or generated code. Model-as-policy [ 58 , 75 , 56 ] harnesses instead delegate action generation to specialized learned policies. Recent frontier-model-as-policy harnesses take a further step by using the frontier model itself to plan and generate robot actions within a closed loop. Recent studies have established the feasibility of this paradigm [ 15 , 61 , 74 , 43 , 63 , 62 ] . For example, SenseNova-RoboRSI [ 62 ] improves a frontier-model policy through recursive self-improvement based on prior training datasets and test-time execution rollouts, while GPT-as-Policy [ 61 ] and related studies [ 49 , 17 ] profile the native robotic capabilities of frontier models. However, these works primarily demonstrate or improve the performance of particular systems. They do not systematically study which harness components are responsible for the gains, or why general-purpose frontier models can outperform policies specialized for robotic control.

We address these questions through Embo-Harness , a controlled study for frontier-model-as-policy harnesses. Starting from a common baseline, we vary individual design factors and progressively integrate the effective choices, rather than optimizing the complete system as a single configuration. Our study covers four dimensions of the robotic agentic loop: prompting, planning, action execution, and tool use. The resulting six findings can be summarized into three principles. First , suitable action representations and execution strategies are essential: frontier models more effectively generate end-effector actions than joint-space actions and favor short, fixed-length action chunks over adaptive chunking. Second , their performance benefits substantially from stronger reasoning and richer visual observations, including higher resolutions and complementary viewpoints. Third , tools and learned policies should complement the model’s native capabilities rather than replace them: manually designed plans and failure-derived reminders provide limited benefits, whereas short multi-action sequences and VLA refinement improve effectiveness and efficiency. Guided by this recipe, Embo-Harness achieves an 83.3% success rate on RoboLab-120, compared with 57.1% for SenseNova-RoboRSI, the strongest existing baseline. It also completes 15 of 20 zero-shot trials across four real-world manipulation tasks.

> Figure 2 : Typical Robotic Harness Paradigms. (1) Tool/code-as-policy harnesses leverage frontier models for high-level planning while relying on predefined tools or generated code for action execution. (2) Model-as-policy harnesses delegate action generation to specialized policy models. (3) Frontier-model-as-policy harnesses directly generate robot actions using frontier models. All three paradigms operate within a closed-loop framework, differing primarily in how robot actions are generated.

Beyond performance, we further investigate why frontier models succeed as robotic policies. Although they can generate joint-level and trajectory-level actions commonly learned by VLAs and WAMs, explicitly requiring such generation degrades performance. In contrast, directly predicting the final EEF destination outperforms short-range action generation, suggesting a stronger reliance on spatial perception than low-level action generation. We further validate this hypothesis through spatial position estimation experiments, observing broadly aligned trends with manipulation performance across reasoning efforts and visual resolutions, as well as comparable errors between direct position estimates and gripper positions in failed grasps, both measured against ground-truth target positions. These findings further highlight the central role of spatial perception in frontier models’ robotic capabilities.


### 2 Revisiting Agentic Robotics

Robot policies map task instructions and observations to executable actions [ 2 , 40 , 47 , 51 ] . Traditionally, these policies relied on handcrafted rules and specialized tools [ 8 , 59 , 19 , 60 ] , while recent advances in neural networks and large-scale robot data have enabled learned policies [ 55 , 7 , 18 , 76 , 26 ] and robot foundation models (RFMs), including Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) [ 78 , 6 , 38 , 36 , 67 ] and World Action Models (WAMs) [ 71 , 42 , 73 ] .

Beyond standalone policies, agentic robotics employs software-based harnesses to coordinate perception, planning, action execution, and tool use within a closed-loop framework. As illustrated in Fig. 2 , existing robotic harnesses can be broadly categorized into three paradigms based on how robot actions are generated.

Tool/code-as-policy. These harnesses typically leverage vision-language models for perception and high-level planning, while relying on predefined tools or generated code for action execution [ 31 , 57 , 30 , 44 , 33 , 32 , 72 , 41 , 20 , 24 , 16 , 65 , 29 , 12 , 25 , 21 , 10 ] . Such tools include object detection [ 48 ] , segmentation [ 39 , 52 , 9 ] , grasp detection [ 22 , 66 , 11 ] , and motion planning [ 60 , 19 ] . Code-as-Policy methods further enable flexible composition and invocation of these tools through code [ 44 , 24 , 29 , 25 ] .

Model-as-policy. Rather than relying solely on predefined tools, these harnesses delegate action generation to learned policy models, while VLMs coordinate high-level planning, execution, and feedback [ 35 , 34 , 58 , 28 , 53 , 75 , 56 , 70 , 64 ] . By integrating specialized policy models into the agentic loop, these approaches combine high-level reasoning with learned low-level control .

Frontier-model-as-policy. Recent advances in frontier models, such as GPT-6-Astra, have introduced a new paradigm in which frontier models directly generate robot actions, integrating perception, planning, and action generation within a unified agentic loop [ 15 , 61 , 74 , 17 , 43 , 63 , 37 , 62 , 45 ] . Recent studies show that frontier models exhibit remarkable generalization, outperforming several VLA and WAM baselines on robotic manipulation benchmarks [ 62 , 74 ] . However, existing studies have primarily focused on demonstrating their capabilities, leaving open how to effectively design frontier-model-as-policy harnesses and what underlying capabilities drive their performance.

Thus, we introduce Embo-Harness to systematically develop a practical harness recipe and investigate the underlying capabilities of frontier models. A concurrent online blog, Astranomy [ 69 ] , similarly explores frontier models for robotic manipulation. Our work further develops a unified recipe through progressive exploration and investigates their underlying capabilities, highlighting the importance of spatial perception over low-level action generation.


### 3 Developing Embo-Harness

Frontier models have reshaped robotic harnesses while introducing a fundamental design challenge: how to fully leverage their capabilities within an agentic loop. Toward this end, we systematically explore four key dimensions of the robotic agentic loop: prompting, planning, action execution, and tool use, as illustrated in Fig. 3 . Specifically, prompting covers text prompts and visual inputs; planning involves context management, planning strategies, and model selection; action execution considers action representations, action chunk sizes, and asynchronous inference; and tool use further extends the harness through combined action generation and integration with robot policies.

Baseline Setup. For our exploration, we follow the experimental setup of $\pi_{0.5}$ [ 36 ] . Specifically, we adopt absolute joint positions as the action representation with an action chunk of 15, providing only task instructions and dual-view images at a resolution of $224\times 126$ as inputs. We use GPT-6-Astra with medium reasoning effort as our baseline, operating in goal mode for task execution. We adopt a progressive evaluation protocol, where each experiment varies a single design factor while keeping all others fixed. The best-performing configuration identified at each stage is then carried forward to subsequent experiments, progressively refining the harness recipe. The full exploration results are shown in Table 1 . All experiments are conducted on a subset of 20 tasks from the RoboLab benchmark [ 68 ] , with $3$ trials per task to reduce the impact of randomness.


#### 3.1 Action Execution

Action Format. We first investigate action execution, the dimension most closely related to robot control, by examining whether frontier models can understand and generate different action representations and identifying the most effective one. In robotic manipulation, two common action representations are end-effector (EEF) actions, which specify the spatial position of the robot end-effector, and joint-space actions, which specify the angles of individual robot joints. Given the robot’s kinematic parameters, EEF actions can be converted into joint-space actions through inverse kinematics (IK). Both representations can be further expressed as absolute values or relative offsets (delta) from the previous timestep, resulting in four action formats. As shown in Table 1 , frontier models can handle all four formats with comparable success rates. However, generating joint-space actions is more challenging and incurs longer execution times. We observe that frontier models often resort to writing code to use IK to compute joint values, directly generating them in only approximately 30% of cases, typically for simple or repeated actions. Absolute and delta formats exhibit comparable performance and time cost.

Action Chunk. We next investigate whether frontier models can adapt their generated actions to different action chunk sizes. We observe that frontier models tend to predict similar target positions regardless of the specified chunk size, suggesting limited awareness of action execution horizons (detail in appendix). Consequently, larger chunks result in slower movements and may exhaust the episode step budget, while smaller chunks may provide insufficient execution time to reach the target, requiring repeated action execution to the same target. As shown in Table 1 , a chunk size of 8 achieves the best performance by balancing movement speed and execution horizon, outperforming both larger and smaller chunk selections.

Asynchronous Execution. We next investigate whether frontier models can support asynchronous inference, a technique commonly used in robot policies to reduce execution latency. Specifically, asynchronous inference starts generating the next action chunk before the current chunk finishes executing, allowing the next chunk to be ready upon completion and thereby reducing the idle time between consecutive chunks. For frontier models, this requires anticipating the future robot state after executing the current chunk and generating subsequent actions accordingly. However, Table 1 shows that frontier models struggle to anticipate such future states and instead focus primarily on immediate movements . Therefore, we do not employ asynchronous inference in subsequent experiments.


#### 3.2 Planning

Model Types. Having established the recipe for action execution, we next turn our attention to model selection and planning. We first investigate the impact of different frontier models and reasoning efforts. For instance, GPT models [ 49 ] offer different variants ( e.g . , 6-Astra, 6.1-Sol, 6.1-Terra) and reasoning efforts ( e.g . , xhigh, high, and medium). As shown in Table 1 , stronger model variants and higher reasoning efforts generally achieve better performance. Interestingly, medium reasoning effort outperforms high, suggesting that more reasoning does not necessarily translate into better action execution.

Explicit Planning. We next investigate whether explicit planning improves robotic manipulation performance. Specifically, we compare two strategies: implicit planning, where the goal model directly plans and executes the task, and explicit planning, where an additional model call in plan mode decomposes the task into subtasks, which are then sequentially executed by the goal model. As shown in Table 1 , explicit planning provides no performance improvement while increasing execution time due to additional planning overhead.

Context Management. We further investigate whether frontier models require the full interaction history for action generation. During task execution, the interaction history continuously grows with new observations, although earlier observations may not always be necessary for subsequent actions. Our experiments show that removing historical observations reduces execution time while maintaining comparable performance. However, this substantially decreases the KV-cache hit rate from over 95% to approximately 25%, resulting in a 7–8 $\times$ increase in uncached token consumption.


#### 3.3 Prompting and Visual Inputs

Notice Reminder. Having explored model selection and planning, we next investigate whether additional prompt guidance improves robotic manipulation performance. Specifically, we employ recursive self-improvement (RSI) to analyze failure cases from baseline rollouts and distill 12 task-agnostic textual reminders, also removing all task-specific information to retain only task-agnostic guidance. As shown in Table 1 , these reminders decrease performance and also increase execution time, suggesting that introducing failure-derived robotic skills offers limited benefits to frontier models. Details of reminders are provided in the appendix.

Visual Views. Multi-view observations are a key design choice in robotic policies, providing complementary visual information for manipulation. We next investigate whether this design is equally important for frontier-model-as-policy harnesses. Following conventional robot policies, our baseline uses both third-person and wrist-view observations, which we compare against using only a single third-person view. As shown in Table 1 , removing the wrist view leads to a performance drop, highlighting the importance of complementary visual viewpoints for robotic manipulation.

Visual Resolution. We next investigate the impact of visual input resolution on robotic manipulation performance. Specifically, we evaluate different input resolutions to examine their effects on action execution. As shown in Table 1 , higher-resolution visual inputs provide finer spatial details, leading to substantial improvements in harness performance. For the remaining experiments, we adopt a higher visual resolution of $640\times 360$ .


#### 3.4 Tool and Policy Integration

Short Multi-action Sequences. We further investigate whether additional tools can further enhance harness performance, focusing on short multi-action sequences that enable multiple consecutive actions to be executed together. Specifically, we introduce an additional tool that allows frontier models to generate multiple consecutive actions in a single output without observing intermediate states. As shown in Table 1 , this strategy further improves both task performance and execution speed. We further analyze the short multi-action sequences employed during task execution and identify three common patterns: (1) approach, grasp, and retreat; (2) lower, release, and withdraw; and (3) repetition of previously executed action sequences. Overall, short multi-action sequences are used in approximately 30% of cases, with an average of three actions generated per sequence. These results motivate the inclusion of short multi-action sequences as a tool in our harness.

VLA Integration. Finally, we investigate how to integrate VLAs into the harness to assist action execution. Specifically, we explore two strategies: (1) VLA selection , where frontier models decide at each step whether to execute actions themselves or delegate execution to a VLA; and (2) VLA refinement , where a VLA proposes an action at each step, which frontier models then refine and execute. As shown in Table 1 , VLA selection substantially reduces execution time but also degrades task performance. We observe that frontier models struggle to accurately assess VLA capabilities and tend to delegate most actions to the VLA, even when its predictions are unreliable. In contrast, VLA refinement achieves the best performance, suggesting that VLAs and frontier models possess complementary capabilities and that refining VLA-proposed actions leads to more effective task execution .

It is noteworthy that this result does not rule out selection as a strategy: calibrated exposure to the VLA’s successes and failures, or an explicit estimate of its confidence, may enable more reliable delegation. We leave this possibility to future work.


#### 3.5 Recipe Summary

Through systematic exploration, we distill a practical recipe for building strong frontier-model-as-policy harnesses, as illustrated in Fig. 4 . Overall, our findings highlight the importance of leveraging frontier models’ native capabilities through effective choices for action representation, chunking, reasoning effort, visual observations, short multi-action sequences, and policy refinement.

#### 3.6 Evaluating Embo-Harness

Benchmark Evaluation. We evaluate our method on RoboLab-120 [ 68 ] , a high-fidelity simulation benchmark built on NVIDIA Isaac Lab. The benchmark consists of 120 tabletop manipulation tasks spanning three key competencies: visual, relational, and procedural reasoning. We adopt the DROID robot configuration, featuring a 7-DoF Franka Panda arm equipped with a Robotiq 2F-85 gripper and ZED camera observations. All methods are evaluated out of the box without task-specific fine-tuning, ensuring a fair and consistent comparison. We report two performance metrics, success rate (SR) and task completion score (Score), following Yang et al . [ 68 ] . SR measures the percentage of successfully completed episodes, while Score measures the average proportion of successfully completed subtasks per episode.

Real-World Evaluation. To further validate the effectiveness of our method in real-world environments, we establish a physical robot setup closely matching the RoboLab configuration, as seen in Figure 5 . We evaluate our method on four real-world tabletop manipulation tasks.

##### 3.6.1 Benchmark Evaluation Results

We evaluate Embo-Harness on the full RoboLab-120 benchmark against three categories of baselines: VLAs, WAMs, and agentic methods. Among the agentic baselines, SenseNova-RoboRSI [ 62 ] is a closely related frontier-model-as-policy approach that, like ours, employs GPT-6-Astra for robot action generation. As shown in Table 2 , Embo-Harness achieves an SR of 83.3% and a Score of 89.2, outperforming all compared methods on both metrics where reported. ##### 3.6.2 Real-World Evaluation Results

We further evaluate Embo-Harness on four real-world manipulation tasks: block stacking, fruit packing, dish washing, and flower arranging. All experiments are conducted in a purely zero-shot setting, without any task-specific memory or hints. As shown in Table 3 , Embo-Harness consistently outperforms our baseline setup, achieving 5/5 successes in both block stacking and fruit packing, 2/5 in dish washing, and 3/5 in flower arranging. The remaining failures primarily stem from limitations in fine-grained spatial perception. Specifically, dish washing requires precise localization of a small faucet switch, which remains challenging for current frontier models. Similarly, flower arranging requires accurate alignment with the vase opening, which becomes difficult to observe from the wrist-view camera at tilted orientations. Despite these challenges, Embo-Harness demonstrates promising zero-shot generalization to real-world robotic manipulation.


### 4 Why Frontier Models Succeed in Robotics

The emergence of frontier models as competitive robot policies raises a broader question for embodied intelligence: what capabilities enable general-purpose multimodal models, not specifically designed for low-level robot control, to rival or even surpass specialized VLA and WAM policies? Answering this question is important not only for understanding current systems, but also for determining which capabilities and training signals future robot foundation models should prioritize. We investigate three related questions: whether frontier models encode low-level joint and trajectory patterns, whether such knowledge contributes to successful manipulation, and, if not, which more general capability accounts for their performance.


#### 4.1 Evaluating Low-level Action Generation

To answer the first two questions, we investigate whether frontier models exhibit characteristic biases of low-level robotic actions and whether these biases contribute to their manipulation capabilities. Specifically, we consider two levels of low-level actions: 1) trajectory-level bias , which captures frame-to-frame motion patterns, and 2) joint-level bias , which captures the movements of individual robot joints. Existing VLAs and WAMs typically learn low-level action patterns through imitation learning, with most methods modeling trajectory-level patterns with an action chunking strategy and some, such as $\pi_{0.5}$ [ 36 ] , additionally modeling joint-level patterns. We therefore examine whether frontier models exhibit these two biases and whether explicitly leveraging them improves manipulation performance.

Joint-level bias. We compare direct joint-angle generation (disable inverse kinematics (IK) usage) with joint-angle generation assisted by inverse kinematics (IK). As shown in Table 4 , direct joint control achieves a 20.0% zero-shot success rate, indicating that frontier models possess some understanding of joint-level actions. However, this performance is 23.3 percentage points lower than that of IK-assisted control, suggesting that explicitly generating joint-level actions does not improve manipulation performance.

Trajectory-level bias. We further compare explicit multi-frame action generation, where frontier models predict an action for each frame, with predicting a future target pose several frames ahead. As shown in Table 4 , explicit multi-frame action generation (indicated by the row ‘direct chunk 8’) achieves a 40.0% zero-shot success rate, demonstrating that frontier models can generate low-level trajectories. But this approach underperforms target pose prediction by 18.3 percentage points (indicated by the row ‘chunk 8’), suggesting that explicitly modeling frame-level motion patterns also fails to improve manipulation performance.

Overall, these results show that frontier models possess some ability to generate joint-level actions and frame-level trajectories. However, the evaluated harness performs substantially better when the model predicts spatial targets and delegates low-level execution to IK or trajectory interpolation. This suggests that its strong manipulation performance does not primarily depend on explicit low-level action generation, although the model may still use implicit action knowledge during spatial reasoning and target selection. We further find that frontier models generate similar action displacements across different execution horizons, indicating limited sensitivity to frame-level temporal information. Their rollout trajectories also exhibit broader spatial distributions than those of $\pi_{0.5}$ [ 36 ] , suggesting distinct motion patterns from policies trained on low-level actions. These findings provide additional evidence for our conclusions, with detailed results presented in the supplementary material.

#### 4.2 Evaluating Spatial Perception

We now turn to the third question: if low-level action understanding is not the primary driver of frontier models’ manipulation capabilities, where do these capabilities come from? We hypothesize that their strength primarily stems from accurate 3D spatial perception. To investigate this hypothesis, we compare two action generation strategies: generating short-range action chunks, as in our previous experiments, and directly predicting the final EEF destination. As shown in Table 5 , directly predicting the final destination achieves slightly higher success rates than short-range action generation, suggesting that spatial perception may play a more fundamental role than low-level action generation.

To further examine this hypothesis, we directly evaluate the 3D position estimation accuracy of frontier models under different reasoning efforts, visual resolutions, and movement priors. As shown in Table 6 , higher reasoning effort and visual resolution substantially reduce spatial position estimation errors, broadly aligning with the manipulation performance trends in Table 1 . These results suggest that improved spatial perception may contribute to stronger manipulation capabilities. Interestingly, the position estimation errors are comparable in magnitude to the gripper-to-target errors observed in failed grasps under short-range action generation (11.04 cm on average), suggesting that manipulation accuracy is closely related to spatial position estimation. We further observe overlapping trajectory distributions between the two strategies, suggesting shared spatial characteristics. These analyses, detailed in the supplementary material, further support the important role of spatial perception in frontier models’ robotic capabilities.

#### 4.3 Discussion

Frontier models are most effective when used to infer task-relevant spatial targets, rather than low-level actions. Three observations from our experiments support this view. First, direct joint and frame-level trajectory generation is possible but consistently underperforms IK-assisted or target-pose control. Second, stronger reasoning and higher visual resolution reduce 3D localization errors in parallel with improvements in task success. Third, real-world failures are concentrated in tasks requiring precise localization and alignment. These findings do not imply that frontier models lack implicit action knowledge or that low-level action data are unnecessary, as the observed performance gaps may also reflect limitations in action interfaces and numerical generation. Nevertheless, they highlight the importance of spatial perception and motivate greater emphasis on spatially grounded models, datasets, and benchmarks. More broadly, these insights suggest a promising direction for developing new robot policy paradigms beyond conventional VLAs and WAMs, potentially achieving strong manipulation performance and efficient execution without relying heavily on low-level action imitation.


### 5 Conclusion

We have introduced Embo-Harness to investigate how to build strong frontier-model-as-policy harnesses and why frontier models achieve remarkable robotic manipulation performance. Through a systematic exploration of prompting, planning, action execution, and tool use, we distilled six key findings into a practical recipe that emphasizes leveraging frontier models’ native capabilities rather than imposing handcrafted plans, skills, or action priors. Guided by this recipe, Embo-Harness achieves state-of-the-art performance on RoboLab-120 and demonstrates strong performance in real-world experiments. Beyond harness design, we further investigated the underlying capabilities of frontier models, revealing that their manipulation performance primarily stems from accurate spatial perception rather than low-level action understanding. These findings not only provide practical guidance for developing stronger robotic harnesses but also suggest a promising direction for future robot datasets and foundation models.

Limitations and Future Research. Our findings depend on the capabilities of current frontier models and may evolve with future advances. While spatial perception may enable general-purpose gripper-based manipulation, dexterous manipulation, locomotion, and tactile perception require additional low-level and dynamic capabilities, presenting promising directions for future research.

Action Displacement Analysis. To better understand how frontier models perceive action execution horizons, we analyze the generated action displacements under different chunking strategies. As shown in Table 7 , despite increasing the fixed chunk size from 4 to 30, the average action displacement remains nearly unchanged, ranging from 0.95 $\times$ to 1.10 $\times$ relative to chunk size 8. Interestingly, adaptive chunking also exhibits a comparable overall displacement of 0.96 $\times$ , despite dynamically selecting different chunk sizes. These observations suggest that frontier models tend to generate similar target displacements regardless of the chunking strategy, indicating limited sensitivity to execution horizons. Overall, these findings highlight the difficulty frontier models face in consistently aligning action displacements with execution horizons, motivating the use of a short, fixed action chunk.

Trajectory Distribution Analysis across Policy Strategies. To further investigate the motion patterns of frontier models, we compare the rollout trajectories of GPT Harness with those of $\pi_{0.5}$ [ 36 ] , a VLA trained on low-level robotic action data. As shown in Fig. 6 , the two methods exhibit comparable translation speed distributions, with median values of 0.0475 and 0.0398 m/s, respectively. However, GPT Harness exhibits substantially lower angular speed (0.0109 vs. 0.0938 rad/s) and jerk magnitude (0.0735 vs. 2.1 m/s 3 ), although these differences may also be influenced by their action execution strategies. More importantly, the PCA visualization reveals that GPT Harness exhibits a broader trajectory distribution, whereas $\pi_{0.5}$ produces more concentrated trajectories. These observations highlight distinct motion characteristics between frontier models and conventional VLAs, providing complementary evidence that frontier models do not simply reproduce the low-level action patterns learned by imitation-based policies.

Trajectory Distribution Analysis across Action Strategies. To further investigate whether short-range action generation and direct destination prediction share similar spatial behaviors, we compare their rollout trajectory distributions. Specifically, EEF absolute control predicts short-range target poses, with execution velocities determined by the predicted displacements, whereas EEF destination control predicts the final target pose and moves toward it incrementally at predefined velocities. As shown in Fig. 7 , despite differences in motion magnitudes caused by their execution rules, the two strategies exhibit broadly similar distributional patterns in translation speed, angular speed, and acceleration, along with overlapping trajectory distributions in the PCA projection. These observations suggest shared spatial motion characteristics, supporting our hypothesis that frontier models’ action generation is primarily guided by spatial target estimation rather than precise low-level action modeling.
