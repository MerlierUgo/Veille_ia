# ALIVE: Making Streaming Video Models Interactive

- **arXiv** : 2611.21366 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21366

- **Code** : https://github.com/lijinzhao30/ALIVE
- **Source du texte** : pdf

## Résumé

Streaming video LLMs can continuously observe the world, yet often handle user requests in isolation. We introduce ALIVE, an interactive streaming video model supporting proactive monitoring, streaming task coordination, and feedback-seeking interaction. We construct ALIVE-SFT-288K and ALIVE-RL-3K for two-stage training. Contrastive supervised fine-tuning teaches when and how to respond, while reinforcement learning with Behavior-Aligned Credit Assignment (BACA) combines trajectory-level and local advantages to refine decisions, responses, and silence across multi-turn interactions. We also introduce FSI-Bench to evaluate feedback seeking and subsequent task completion. ALIVE achieves the highest overall scores among the compared models on four public benchmarks and FSI-Bench, demonstrating strong streaming understanding and interactive capabilities.

1 Introduction
Advances in large language models (LLMs) are pushing conversational AI beyond multi-turn dialogues toward
interactive assistants that can respond to diverse user requests, manage multiple concurrent tasks, and, when
appropriate, proactively engage users as those tasks evolve [32, 56]. Bringing these capabilities into the
physical world points toward a long-standing vision of multimodal interactive AI [8, 19, 23]: systems such as
JARVIS in Iron Man that continuously perceive streams of inputs while seamlessly interacting with human
users, augmenting our ability to understand and act in complex, dynamic environments.
Streaming video LLMs lie at the very heart of this vision. Recent models [4, 52, 53] can continuously process
incoming video, reason about past observations [58], describe ongoing activities [5, 50], and monitor events as
they occur. Yet today’s streaming video LLMs remain largely passive, often responding to individual requests
in isolation. They lack a critical capability of interactive assistants: the ability to maintain and revise user
tasks over time and proactively engage users as the video unfolds. This gap in interactivity substantially
limits their potential as a foundation for multimodal interactive AI.

In this work, we aim to transform streaming video LLMs from passive observers into interactive assistants.
Given that modern video LLMs largely inherit the architecture of LLMs [25, 28], we ask a key question: What
data representation and learning framework can endow existing video LLMs with interactive capabilities?
Addressing this question poses two challenges. First, constructing streaming interaction data requires aligning
user requests, feedback, and assistant responses with the visual evidence available at each moment. Second,
models must learn when and how to respond as context and task states evolve across multiple turns, where
similar observations can call for different behaviors.
To address these challenges, we first identify a set of core capabilities required for interactive streaming
video understanding and curate ALIVE-SFT-288K and ALIVE-RL-3K for two-stage post-training. We then
develop a learning framework that explicitly supports these capabilities. Contrastive SFT teaches strategy
selection and response gating through controlled variations in interaction conditions and available evidence.
Reinforcement learning with Behavior-Aligned Credit Assignment (BACA) further refines these behaviors by
combining trajectory-level advantages with local advantages for strategy selection, task response, and critical
silence. We also establish FSI-Bench to evaluate feedback-seeking interaction in streaming video LLMs.
Our experiments show that an offline video LLM can indeed be transformed through post-training into a
capable interactive streaming video LLM. The resulting model preserves its ability to understand a streaming
video while acquiring substantially richer interactive behaviors: it can simultaneously manage multiple
user-specified tasks, modify or cancel existing tasks, and proactively seek user feedback based on prior requests
and events unfolding in the video. These results suggest a path toward streaming video LLMs that do not
merely watch and respond, but sustain ongoing interactions with users as the video streams. Our contributions
are summarized as follows:
• We formulate interactive streaming video understanding as a set of core capabilities, including proactive
monitoring, streaming task coordination, and feedback-seeking interaction. Building on this formulation,
we introduce ALIVE-SFT-288K and ALIVE-RL-3K for two-stage post-training, together with FSI-Bench, a
benchmark for evaluating feedback-seeking interaction.
• Weintroduce ALIVE,aninteractivestreamingvideomodeltrainedthrough contrastive SFTand reinforcement
learning with BACA.
• We show that ALIVE achieves highly competitive performance across multiple streaming video benchmarks,
preserving strong video understanding capabilities while substantially improving performance on interactive
tasks.

2 Related Work
Streaming Video Models. Streaming video models process continuously arriving observations [20, 30] and
determine when to respond [48]. VideoLLM-online couples temporally aligned dialogue with continuous
inference [4], while Dispider separates perception, decision, and reaction for asynchronous interaction [37].
StreamBridge and AURA address sustained observation through memory and context management [31, 44].
JoyAI-VL-Interaction integrates silence, response, and delegation with an asynchronous background model [55].
ALIVE focuses on maintaining evolving user requests and resolving missing prerequisites through clarification,
user execution, or continued monitoring.
Post-training for Streaming Video Understanding. Streaming SFT learns response content and timing from
temporally aligned dialogues, with supervision of response states and balancing of silence and speech [4, 31,
51]. Our contrastive SFT further supervises how interaction conditions and available evidence change the
appropriate strategy and response gate. Beyond SFT, PPO [39] and GRPO [41] provide policy-optimization
frameworks for LLM post-training. GiGPO [10] further addresses multi-turn credit assignment through
episode-level and step-level advantages. In streaming video, MMDuet2 uses RL to improve response timing
and correctness while penalizing repetition [46]. StreamPro uses GRPO with aggregated turn-level and
trajectory-level rewards [22]. Our BACA combines trajectory-level advantages with local advantages over
behavioral units for strategy selection, task response, and critical silence.
Streaming Video Datasets and Benchmarks. Streamo-Instruct covers diverse streaming tasks, while ProAssist
provides proactive assistant–user dialogues grounded in egocentric videos [51, 61]. StreamingBench and
OVO-Bench evaluate online perception and temporal understanding [27, 29]. ProactiveVideoQA focuses on
proactive response timing and quality, while IPIBench covers task updates and interleaved requests [24, 47].
Table 1 compares training datasets. ALIVE-SFT-288K and ALIVE-RL-3K support two-stage training across
monitoring, task coordination, and feedback seeking. FSI-Bench evaluates strategy selection and subsequent
task completion when additional information, user action, or observation is required.
3 Streaming Interactivity: Problem Formulation and Datasets
3.1 Problem Formulation
We consider a streaming video model that continuously receives video observations and user utterances. At
decision time t, its output depends only on the observed video prefix V1:t and the interaction history Ht,
comprising received user utterances and prior model outputs. All mappings below describe causal rollouts:
each output uses only information available at its own decision time.

3.2 Interactive Streaming Task Taxonomy
As aforementioned, passive streaming models primarily focus on proactive monitoring, while interactive
streaming further extends to task coordination and feedback seeking. Accordingly, we briefly introduce the
core interactive streaming capabilities across three complementary families.
Proactive Monitoring. The user specifies a condition to monitor, and the model observes the stream until
sufficient evidence supports a response:
• Proactive Timing: The model issues a notification when the target condition is first verifiably satisfied,
completing the task.
• Proactive Understanding: The model answers an associated visual question once both the trigger and
sufficient answer evidence are available.
• Repeated Proactiveness: The model maintains the request across multiple occurrences and responds once
for each distinct occurrence.
Streaming Task Coordination. The model maintains and updates active commitments as user requests evolve
or coexist:
• Task State Update: The user cancels or modifies an existing monitoring task, and the model updates
subsequent behavior accordingly.
• Concurrent Requests: The model handles multiple monitoring objectives or interleaved immediate questions
while preserving all remaining active commitments.

Feedback-Seeking Interaction. When the current context is insufficient to resolve a request, the model
identifies the missing prerequisite and actively seeks the required feedback:
• Ask-for-Monitor: When the answer depends on future observations, the model asks whether the user wants
to establish a monitoring task and responds once the relevant evidence appears.
• Ask-for-Information: When the request is ambiguous, the model asks for clarification and uses the additional
information to resolve the original query.
• Ask-for-Execution: When necessary visual evidence is unavailable, the model requests a concrete user
action that reveals the missing information before answering.
Across these tasks, the model must preserve valid commitments and incorporate subsequent user feedback or
visual evidence into its ongoing interaction state.
3.3 Data Construction and Datasets
We construct ALIVE-SFT-288K and ALIVE-RL-3K using public video segments and annotations from diverse
sources. These include TimeLens2 [65], LLaVA-Video [62], TimeLens [60], Shot2Story [17], VideoGPT+ [34],
VideoRefer-700K [57], QVHighlights [21], Vript [54], WebVid [2], DiDeMo [18], Ref-YouTube-VOS [40],
Nymeria [33], EPIC-KITCHENS [7], Charades-Ego [42], Ego4D [14], HoloAssist [45], Ego-Exo4D [15],
DenseStep2M [12], COIN [43], HACS [63], Charades-STA [11], STAR [49], ActivityNet [3], and Oops [9].
We pair these video segments with timestamped user requests, feedback, and assistant responses or silence to
construct interaction trajectories. As illustrated in Fig. 2, construction proceeds through temporal evidence
grounding, dialogue synthesis and alignment, and quality verification.
Temporal evidence grounding and refinement. For videos with existing descriptions and approximate
timestamps, we use them to identify candidate intervals; otherwise, an offline MLLM first detects relevant
targets and coarsely localizes their occurrence. We then refine each candidate using an offline MLLM with
access to video context both before and after the candidate event. This bidirectional temporal context helps
identify the earliest sampled time at which the required visual condition is met, such as entity recognizability,
text readability, scene visibility, or action completion.
Dialogue synthesis and temporal alignment. Given grounded targets and timestamps, we synthesize dialogues
that pair user requests with appropriate assistant responses and, where needed, task updates or feedback
5

exchanges. We align these exchanges with the evolving task state and visual evidence, so that each answer
follows the observations or user feedback needed to support it.
Quality verification. We verify two aspects of each example. Interaction-strategy validity: the selected strategy
must be appropriate for the current task state and information gap, and any requested clarification, action, or
continued observation must be relevant to resolving the request. Causal answerability: each answer must be
supported by the video observed up to its assigned timestamp and the preceding interaction history, without
relying on future frames or subsequent user feedback. Examples that fail either check are filtered.
Statistics. Through this construction process, we generate ALIVE-SFT-288K and ALIVE-RL-3K, comprising
288,436 and 3,523 interaction examples for supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning, respectively.
Both datasets span diverse scenarios and cover eight task types across proactive monitoring, streaming task
coordination, and feedback-seeking interaction. Fig. 2 summarizes the task distributions and video-content
categories.
4 Learning Interactive Streaming Video LLMs
Streaming interaction requires a model to select an appropriate interaction strategy, respond accurately when
the necessary evidence becomes available, and remain silent when a response is unwarranted. These behaviors
depend jointly on visual observations and the evolving interaction history. We train ALIVE in two stages.
Contrastive supervised fine-tuning establishes these conditional interaction behaviors, while reinforcement
learning further optimizes them on model-generated trajectories through Behavior-Aligned Credit Assignment.
Output format. At each decision time t, the model generates ot ∼ pθ (· | V1:t, Ht) from the observed video
prefix and interaction history. A control token indicates whether to respond (R) or remain silent (S). When
responding, the subsequent text expresses the appropriate strategy, such as answering directly, proposing
continued monitoring, requesting clarification, or asking the user to perform an action. All outputs are
generated by a single autoregressive model.
4.1 Contrastive Supervised Fine-Tuning
The appropriate interaction behavior is not determined by visual content alone. The same observation may
warrant different outputs depending on the active request, task status, or available user feedback. We construct
two complementary families of controlled contrasts to supervise these dependencies.
Response-gate contrasts. We hold the visual prefix and comparison time fixed while varying an interaction
condition that changes whether the model should respond. These conditions include query relevance, task
status, and feedback state. For example, the same target appearance requires a notification under an active
monitoring request but silence after cancellation or fulfillment of a single-occurrence request. These contrasts
provide explicit supervision for warranted responses and critical silence under visually similar conditions.
Action-strategy contrasts. We vary evidence availability or referential ambiguity while preserving the
underlying task. When sufficient evidence is available, the model should answer directly. An ambiguous
reference requires clarification, whereas missing visual evidence may require a concrete user action or a
proposal to continue observing. We pair these situations with their direct-answer counterparts to teach
strategy selection according to the information available at the time. Both sides use the response gate, with
the distinction learned through supervision on the response text.
Interaction-aware training. We convert interaction trajectories into streaming training instances centered
on annotated responses. For a response at time a, we sample a post-response offset δ uniformly from
{0, . . . , M } and set the instance endpoint to e = a + δ, capped before the next response or at the trajectory
end. Extending instances beyond the response exposes the model to the transition back to silence. For
response-gate contrasts, the designated comparison time takes precedence, ensuring that the instance reaches
the critical response-or-silence decision.

4.2 Reinforcement Learning with Behavior-Aligned Credit Assignment
SFT establishes interaction behaviors from annotated trajectories. RL further refines these behaviors under
histories containing the model’s own outputs. In a multi-turn interaction, however, a trajectory may contain
both successful and unsuccessful behaviors. Assigning the same trajectory-level advantage to every output
provideslimited guidanceaboutwhichinteractionrequirements were satisfied. We thereforeintroduce Behavior-
Aligned Credit Assignment (BACA), which combines trajectory-level advantages with local advantages over
annotated behavioral units.
Streaming rollouts and trajectory reward. Starting from the SFT policy, we sample G trajectories for each
training episode. Video observations and user turns are replayed chronologically, and generated outputs
become part of the subsequent interaction history. Policy sampling focuses on user turns and temporal
regions around annotated response and critical-silence events. Outside these regions, observations continue to
update the history while silence is simulated without contributing to the training loss. Annotations guide
training-time scheduling and evaluation; the policy observes only the available video and interaction history.

Behavioral units. We construct local evaluation units from annotated user turns, response targets, and silence
conditions. These units capture three complementary aspects of interaction: strategy selection, which evaluates
the appropriateness of the interaction strategy; task response, which evaluates the correctness of answers
or notifications and their timing when applicable; and critical silence, which evaluates whether the model
refrains from unwarranted responses during designated periods. For pre-event waiting, silence receives local
credit only when the subsequent target also receives positive completion credit.

determined by the relevant interaction requirement rather than a fixed number of dialogue turns. Corresponding
units are aligned across trajectories by their annotation identities, allowing comparison even when sampled
trajectories produce different numbers of responses. Each generated call is assigned to at most one local unit.

5.1 Implementation Details
We initialize ALIVE from Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct [1] and apply the two-stage training procedure described in
§4. To preserve general question-answering ability and reinforce the decision to answer directly when sufficient
evidence is available, we augment ALIVE-SFT-288K and ALIVE-RL-3K with 274K and 800 curated reactive
QA examples, respectively. These examples are drawn from LLaVA-Video [62], Molmo2 [6], and Ego4D [14].
In both stages, we freeze the vision encoder and connector and update only the language model. SFT runs for
one epoch with a batch size of 512 and a learning rate of 1 × 10−5. Videos are sampled at 1 FPS. For SFT
instance construction, we set Nmin = 10, Nmax = 30, and the maximum post-response offset M = 4 positions.
RL uses G = 4 rollouts per episode and α = 1.
5.2 Evaluation Protocols
Public Benchmarks. We evaluate on OVO-Bench [27], StreamingBench [29], IPIBench [24], and ProactiveV-
ideoQA [47] using their original task-specific protocols. We report accuracy (%) for the first three benchmarks
and PAUC for ProactiveVideoQA, with ω = 0.5. We report official results where available. Otherwise, we
adapt each model to the official evaluation code while preserving its native inference settings as closely as
possible. Full task-level results are reported in Table 2.
FSI-Bench. To evaluate feedback-seeking interaction in streaming video models (§3.2), we introduce FSI-Bench,
comprising 1,235 episodes across four tasks: Ask-for-Monitor (AFM, 154), Ask-for-Information (AFI, 446),
Ask-for-Execution (AFE, 459), and Direct Answer (DA, 176). The last task tests immediate answering when
sufficient evidence is available.
We use two protocols at query time t0. The Explicit Decision Protocol (EDP) requires a choice among Answer,
Wait, and Request, while the Free-form Decision Protocol (FDP) infers this choice from the model’s natural
response using a fixed LLM judge. Both use causal video and feedback streams with identical scoring rules.

answer without requesting the required action. An LLM judge assigns a by comparing the response with
reference answers across all four tasks. Decision (D), Timing (T), and Accuracy (A) are defined as

6 Conclusion and Limitations
We formulate interactive streaming video understanding around proactive monitoring, streaming task coordi-
nation, and feedback-seeking interaction, and introduce ALIVE to support these capabilities. We train the
model on ALIVE-SFT-288K and ALIVE-RL-3K through contrastive supervised fine-tuning and reinforce-
ment learning with Behavior-Aligned Credit Assignment (BACA). ALIVE achieves state-of-the-art overall
performance among the compared models on four public benchmarks and FSI-Bench, demonstrating strong
capabilities in both streaming video understanding and interaction. We believe ALIVE provides an empirical
foundation for developing multimodal assistants that continuously perceive the world and sustain meaningful
interactions with users as their needs evolve.
ALIVE remains a vision-language model, and enabling spoken interaction requires external automatic speech
recognition (ASR) and text-to-speech (TTS) components. Extending this framework to native omni-modal
models is a promising direction toward more natural streaming interaction across vision, language, and audio.
