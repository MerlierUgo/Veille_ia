ABSTRACT

Agent decisions depend on empirical relationships that deployment observations reveal and task descriptions alone may leave unresolved. Learning these relationships requires a mechanism that turns historical evidence into predictions. We formulate agent decision making through general-purpose conditional inference, introducing Decoupled Predictive Decision Making (DPDM), an architecture in which a shared pretrained learner serves heterogeneous decision roles. Language models provide semantic representations of current and historical records, while a general-purpose conditional predictor predicts task-defined targets from the represented evidence. Pretraining supplies a reusable inference capability; deployment evidence instantiates problem-specific predictions without updating either model’s neural parameters. Tool judgments, candidate preferences, and reasoning assessments thus share a common evidence-and-query interface, with predictions mapped to choices by a decision rule. This architecture establishes complementary sources of agent capability: semantic knowledge acquired through language pretraining and empirical inference acquired through structured-data pretraining. We instantiate the conditional predictor with LimiX-2, pretrained on structured data, which is shared across all evaluated decision roles. Across five task categories, DPDM accepts representations from semantic encoders such as Qwen3.5. With Qwen3.5-27B as the semantic encoder, DPDM raises mean accuracy across 50 benchmarks from 78.80% with few-shot prompting to 84.92% under their respective evaluated history budgets, and reaches 82.33% with identical histories and queries. On ToolPRMBench’s BFCL and ToolSandbox tasks, absolute accuracy gains are 34.26% and 28.87%, respectively, at the methods’ respective evaluated history budgets. Accuracy improves as history grows. With identical imbalanced histories, the absolute gain in mean balanced accuracy is 14.51% across five reasoning-step assessment tasks and four encoders. DPDM makes learning from experience an independently improvable foundation-model capability, creating a shared route for advances in conditional prediction to improve decisions across agent tasks.


### 1 Introduction

A generalist agent must learn how to decide, as well as understand what it is asked to do. Large language models support few-shot adaptation [ 2 ] , multi-step reasoning [ 28 ] , and interactive tool use [ 29 ] . The value of a particular choice depends on the conditions under which it is made. Adaptive retrieval studies show that queries benefit from different retrieval strategies [ 9 ] , while work on test-time computation shows that effective allocation depends on problem difficulty [ 22 ] . These findings motivate a distinction between representing a decision problem and learning the relationships that determine an appropriate choice. Semantic competence expands the range of problems an agent can interpret, while learning from feedback determines how effectively it can adapt its decisions to those problems.

Effective learning from experience connects historical feedback to current decisions. Retrieved trajectories and reflective feedback can improve subsequent behavior [ 36 , 21 ] , and language models can learn numerical relationships from in-context examples [ 26 ] . The resulting decision depends on how the learner interprets the evidence. A previously successful action may be inappropriate under different conditions, while a frequently selected action may appear effective because of the situations in which it was used. The agent must identify conditional relationships across observations and apply them to the current decision. We distinguish two sources of decision error: information that the available history does not reveal, and information that the learning mechanism fails to use. Growing histories tests the value of additional evidence (Section 3.3 ), while comparing learners on identical histories tests how effectively they use that evidence (Section 3.2 ). This distinction separates acquiring more informative experience from improving the computation that converts existing evidence into a choice.

Existing approaches organize this computation in different ways. ExpeL extracts insights and recalls experience [ 34 ] ; Rememberer updates an external experience memory using reinforcement-learning feedback [ 31 ] . Algorithm Distillation learns from reinforcement-learning histories [ 10 ] , DPT predicts optimal actions from interaction contexts [ 11 ] , and PreDeToR pretrains reward prediction across related decision tasks [ 15 ] . SayCan combines language planning with learned skill values [ 8 ] . These precedents establish several forms of experience-based adaptation and computational specialization. Within language-model-centered systems, reasoning and action generation remain closely integrated [ 29 ] , and retrieved experience can be interpreted through the same language model [ 34 ] . This organization couples semantic interpretation, empirical inference, and answer generation within one pretrained mechanism. The mechanism for learning deployment-specific relationships is not exposed as a separately replaceable component, limiting the ability to improve empirical inference independently of the language policy. Semantic competence alone does not establish how effectively that mechanism uses a given history. The architectural choice is whether the language model should both represent a decision problem and learn its deployment-specific relationships, or whether a shared conditional learner should infer those relationships from historical feedback. We develop an explicit separation between semantic representation and history-conditioned prediction, making each a replaceable and independently improvable component of the decision architecture.

We introduce Decoupled Predictive Decision Making (DPDM), a framework that reformulates task-defined agent decisions as conditional prediction problems. Each decision specifies its visible context, candidate descriptions, and target, while historical records provide observations of that target under related conditions. A language model serves as the semantic encoder, representing the problem and candidate answers. A shared conditional predictor uses the represented history to estimate the unknown target for the current decision, and a decision rule converts the prediction into a choice. Pretraining a conditional predictor across datasets provides a foundation for this construction, as established by Prior-Data Fitted Networks and Contextual Mechanism Networks [ 7 , 33 ] . DPDM connects this learning capability to semantically rich agent decisions through representations of past experience and the current decision context. Tool applicability, candidate preference, and reasoning assessment become different prediction targets for the same learning mechanism. When action–outcome observations are available, the conditional predictor can estimate consequences for objective-dependent selection.

We instantiate the conditional predictor with LimiX-2 [ 32 , 33 ] , a structured-data foundation model pretrained for conditional prediction. It conditions on the available historical feedback to estimate the current target. Both neural models remain frozen as the history changes. The architecture exposes separate routes to improvement: better semantic representations can reveal relevant distinctions, and advances in conditional prediction can improve target estimates using those distinctions and historical feedback. Predictions can also support different objectives when they retain the information required by the decision rule.

Across decision categories and semantic encoders, a single frozen conditional predictor improves mean decision accuracy over few-shot prompting. Gains persist with identical histories and queries, showing that additional records alone do not explain the advantage. On ToolPRMBench BFCL, DPDM achieves an absolute accuracy gain of 34.26% over few-shot prompting at the methods’ respective evaluated history budgets. Growing-history and imbalanced-history evaluations show benefits from both accumulating experience and making better use of the same feedback. Controls demonstrate complementary contributions from historical input–target relationships, distinct observations, and semantic features. In a separate timing study with matched histories and query sets, the equal-task mean reduction in measured full-pipeline GPU time, including historical preparation, is 80.3% relative to cached few-shot prompting. These findings support an architecture in which the mechanism for learning from experience is shared across decision roles, while each task supplies its own evidence and query without task-specific neural-parameter updates.

Learning from experience becomes an explicit target of foundation-model development. Systems such as Voyager retain accumulated experience as reusable skills [ 27 ] . DPDM develops a complementary route: a shared conditional predictor turns task-relevant observations into new judgments. Improving that predictor can benefit multiple decision roles through the same interface, alongside advances in the semantic encoder that represents the problem.

### 2 Method

Our architecture separates understanding a decision problem from using historical feedback to resolve it. A semantic encoder represents the current context and candidate choices, a shared conditional predictor estimates the decision target from represented historical examples, and a decision rule selects the answer (Figure 1 ). We instantiate the conditional predictor with LimiX-2. Every task uses the same checkpoint with its own history and current query.


#### 2.1 Decision Making as History-Conditioned Prediction

A task specification $\mathcal{T}$ defines the observable input fields, the meaning of the prediction target, and the candidate answer set $\mathcal{Y}_{\mathcal{T}}(r)$ for a record $r$ . The record contains information available before the target is known, including the task context and, when applicable, the visible interaction trajectory and ordered candidate descriptions. The specification is provided by the task and applied consistently to historical and query records.

Let $S=\{(r_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{M}$ denote the history of $M$ records and their observed targets. Historical feedback is recorded as the target $y_{i}$ for each record $r_{i}$ . A query record $r$ describes the current decision whose target $y$ is unknown. In the primary setting, $y$ is a task-defined answer, correctness judgment, or candidate preference. For candidate selection, historical labels identify the candidate that satisfies the task criterion. A trajectory contained in $r$ describes the current decision context; the history $S$ supplies completed examples from which the relevant input–target relationship is inferred.

The prediction problem is to estimate $q_{S}(y\mid r,\mathcal{T})$ . Different decision roles define different records and targets, while retaining the same inputs: represented historical examples, their observed targets, and a query. Historical and query records share the same target semantics within each task, and the conditional predictor returns probabilities over that task’s answer set.


#### 2.2 A Shared Conditional Predictor

The conditional predictor $F_{\theta}$ takes three inputs: represented historical records, their observed targets, and a represented query. It returns probabilities for the query’s target. A semantic encoder and a feature adapter prepare these inputs, allowing the same conditional predictor to accept records from different tasks and semantic encoders.


##### Representing historical and query records.

At each decision, $r$ and $\mathcal{T}$ jointly specify the observable decision state: the task description, current observations, and, when applicable, descriptions of the available actions or other candidates. A frozen semantic encoder $J_{\phi}$ represents each record in the context of its target question and candidate descriptions. This interface places decision-specific structure in the represented inputs and historical evidence, allowing the shared conditional predictor to infer the answer without task-specific updates to its neural parameters. Let $b_{\mathcal{T}}(r)$ collect available native fields and lexical information. A feature adapter maps these inputs into a structured representation:

[Tableau] $$z_{S}(r)=A_{\psi_{S}}\!\left(J_{\phi}(r;\mathcal{T}),b_{\mathcal{T}}(r)\right).$$ (1)

Here $\psi_{S}$ denotes any state fitted from historical inputs, such as a feature transformation. The same fitted mapping is applied to historical and query records. Historical targets are excluded from semantic record encoding and supplied separately as supervision; query targets remain unavailable throughout representation fitting and prediction.


##### Inferring a target from historical examples.

The conditional predictor receives the represented history and query:

[Tableau] $$\widetilde{S}=\{(z_{S}(r_{i}),y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{M},$$ (2)

[Tableau] $$q_{S}(\cdot\mid r,\mathcal{T})=F_{\theta}\!\left(\widetilde{S},z_{S}(r)\right).$$ (3)

Equation 3 returns probabilities over the task’s candidate answers. The task specification determines how records are represented and how outputs map to answers. Appendix A gives the decoding and normalization details.


##### Pretrained instantiation.

We use LimiX-2 as $F_{\theta}$ , pretrained for conditional prediction over structured data [ 33 ] , independently of the evaluated decision tasks. Both the semantic encoder parameters $\phi$ and the conditional predictor parameters $\theta$ remain fixed. Alternative pretrained conditional predictors can fill this role when they support the required representations, target types, and output distributions. Feature construction, output coding, and model configuration are specified in Appendix A .


#### 2.3 Adapting to New Observations

Deployment adaptation changes the history supplied to the conditional predictor. Newly obtained labels or observed outcomes provide additional records $\Delta S$ , yielding

[Tableau] $$S^{+}=S\cup\Delta S,\qquad\phi^{+}=\phi,\qquad\theta^{+}=\theta.$$ (4)

The history-dependent adapter state $\psi_{S^{+}}$ and the resulting prediction $q_{S^{+}}$ may change. Any required input transformations are refitted on the updated historical inputs and applied consistently to historical and query records. Semantic representations can be reused when the encoder and record specification are unchanged.

Section 3.3 evaluates this adaptation by expanding historical sets and predicting the same queries.

#### 3.1 Tasks, Models, and Evaluation

Our evaluation covers 50 benchmarks, which comprise 82 task configurations across five categories: agent tasks, knowledge QA, evidence consistency, safety and reliability, and language understanding. Each task defines a decision target for the current case. We evaluate whether an agent selects the task-appropriate answer, candidate, or judgment. DPDM reformulates each such decision as a conditional prediction problem: the current case defines the query, historical records supply observed decision targets, and the shared predictor estimates the target before a decision rule selects the answer. Decision accuracy measures agreement with the task’s reference target.

The agent-related tasks include intent recognition, tool applicability, response-mode judgments, intermediate-step evaluation, and response preference. For example, BFCL Callability asks whether any supplied function is applicable to a user request [ 17 ] , while a response-mode task derived from ToolACE distinguishes text, a single tool call, and multiple calls [ 14 ] . Appendix B summarizes the benchmarks, decision targets, and historical feedback used in the evaluation.

We use Qwen3.5-4B, 9B, and 27B and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct [ 18 , 5 ] . Each model is evaluated through direct prompting and as the semantic encoder in DPDM. Zero-shot prompting supplies only the current input. Few-shot prompting adds historical inputs and their observed targets.

DPDM assigns semantic interpretation and empirical inference to different pretrained capabilities. Few-shot prompting uses the language model to interpret the history and produce the decision; DPDM uses it to represent the decision problem and assigns history-conditioned prediction to a shared learner. In DPDM, the language model encodes each input and its candidate descriptions independently. Historical targets are supplied separately to the shared conditional predictor, not included in the semantic-encoding prompts. The predictor uses the representations and historical targets to predict the current target. We select the answer with the highest predicted probability. Each encoder is evaluated separately with the same LimiX-2 checkpoint, and both neural models’ weights remain frozen throughout the experiments. We denote an instantiation by DPDM_Encoder, for example DPDM_Qwen3.5-27B. Where a table row or figure panel already identifies the encoder, the abbreviated label DPDM denotes that instantiation. Where used in figure legends, “+LimiX-2” denotes the same encoder–predictor combination.

Jev API v1.13.0 provides an external decision-service reference [ 25 ] , evaluated in zero-shot and few-shot settings. Appendix C gives the readout settings and service histories. Supplementary model comparisons appear in Appendix D .

History size $M$ is the total number of historical examples. All methods answer the same test queries within each task, and these queries are kept separate from the history. The main comparison uses each language model’s largest completed few-shot history under the context, memory, and runtime budgets of the evaluation. For DPDM, we use the largest history with complete results for every evaluated encoder under the same task-specific history budget. Processing a historical record has different costs in the two architectures, so their completed histories can contain different numbers of records.

We average task configurations within each benchmark and then give each benchmark equal weight. Appendix C gives the sampling and history-budget details.

#### 3.4 Making Decisions under Imbalanced Histories

When most historical examples have the same answer, few-shot prompting can favor that answer on new inputs that require a different judgment. This tendency is known as majority-label bias , and prior work shows that the frequency and order of in-context labels affect a language model’s predictions [ 35 , 4 ] . BIG-Bench Mistake provides a direct example: each query contains an original question, preceding reasoning, and a current step whose correctness needs to be judged [ 24 ] . We include all five tasks—Dyck languages, logical deduction, multistep arithmetic, object tracking, and word sorting—and all 7,032 query steps. For each of the four encoders and each task, few-shot prompting and DPDM receive identical histories and queries at their largest completed common history size. The histories contain 128–1,024 records, of which 66.41–95.31% are correct steps.

Correct steps also dominate the query sets, so always answering correct can yield high ordinary accuracy. We use balanced accuracy (BA) to give equal weight to correct and incorrect steps [ 1 ] :

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{BA}=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{correct}}+\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{incorrect}}\right).$$

The two terms are the fractions of correct steps accepted and incorrect steps rejected. A constant answer scores 50%, making the ability to distinguish the two classes visible even when one class is rare.

> Figure 6: Decisions under imbalanced histories in BIG-Bench Mistake. Each task averages the three Qwen3.5 encoders and Llama-3.1-8B. The overall mean weights the five tasks equally. The dashed line marks the 50% BA of a constant answer.

Mean balanced accuracy rises from 59.86% with few-shot prompting to 74.37% with DPDM. The task-mean score improves on all five tasks (Figure 6 ), with gains in 18 of the 20 encoder–task pairs. Appendix E reports every pair, including ordinary accuracy and the two class-specific scores.

On object tracking, both methods receive the same 689 historical steps, of which 77.36% are correct, and answer the same 462 query steps. Qwen3.5-27B few-shot prompting reaches 69.07% BA and 84.85% ordinary accuracy. DPDM_Qwen3.5-27B reaches 92.62% BA and 94.37% ordinary accuracy. It correctly rejects 93 of the 104 incorrect steps, compared with 42 for few-shot prompting, while correctly accepting 95.81% of correct steps.


#### 3.5 Roles of Evidence and Semantic Representation

DPDM organizes decision making around two complementary capabilities: semantic representation of the decision context and conditional inference from historical evidence. We test the empirical basis of this division by intervening on the information supplied to the shared predictive core. Correct input–target pairings determine which relationships the predictor can learn; distinct labeled observations provide evidence beyond repeated rows; semantic features expose distinctions relevant to predicting the target. The results support all three contributions in the evaluated settings, explaining why useful representations and informative history work together in the shared prediction interface.

We next examine how historical feedback and semantic features contribute to decision accuracy. All controls use Qwen3.5-27B as the semantic encoder and the same frozen LimiX-2 checkpoint. We report task-averaged accuracy, with the mean and sample standard deviation across three sampled histories.

Each condition uses the same 128 historical inputs per task, a fixed set of 737 queries across the six tasks, and the same fitted representation. ### 4 Discussion and Conclusion


#### 4.1 Discussion


##### Learning from experience as a foundation-model capability.

The central implication is architectural: an agent’s capacity to learn from deployment experience can be developed through a pretrained model dedicated to conditional prediction. Language pretraining supplies knowledge for interpreting requests, contexts, and candidate choices. Conditional pretraining supplies a mechanism for learning relationships from the records available in a particular deployment. Connecting these capabilities allows semantic understanding and empirical learning to contribute to the same decision. The value of the separation is that it gives learning from experience an explicit role in agent design, with its own training objectives and evaluation criteria.


##### Relation to prior work.

This proposal brings together two research directions. ExpeL extracts insights from experience and retrieves examples to guide an LLM agent [ 34 ] . DPT learns to predict optimal actions from in-context interaction data [ 11 ] , while Prior-Data Fitted Networks pretrain conditional predictors across sampled prediction problems [ 16 ] . ICR-RL and BC-ICL apply general predictive foundation models to control and contextual-bandit decisions [ 20 , 6 ] . DPDM connects conditional predictors to semantically expressed decision tasks through language-model representations. Each task retains its own meaning and target, while the learning mechanism operates on represented records and their labels. The use of LimiX-2 across semantic encoders illustrates how this organization can accommodate different ways of representing a problem. It also creates a common interface for evaluating future conditional predictors with the same semantic encoders.


##### What makes experience valuable.

The information–inference distinction clarifies how this architecture can improve. New observations can reveal relationships that the existing history leaves uncertain; improving the learner can make more effective use of relationships already supported by that history. These are different research goals. A record’s value therefore depends on what it adds about the current decision and on the mechanism used to interpret it. The history-growth and imbalanced-history analyses give concrete examples of this principle. They motivate studying how agents collect and retain feedback alongside how conditional predictors are pretrained to learn from it. Under this view, deployment history helps determine the decision function instantiated for the current setting.


#### 4.2 Conclusion

We propose an architecture for agent decision making in which semantic understanding and learning from experience are distinct, connected foundation-model capabilities. By expressing task-defined decisions as conditional prediction problems, language-model representations allow a shared conditional predictor to use deployment histories across heterogeneous tasks. The LimiX-2 instantiation demonstrates gains across semantic encoders, growing histories, and imbalanced feedback. This architecture provides a common interface through which advances in learning from experience can improve decisions across agent tasks.

### Appendix A Experimental Instantiation

This section describes the evaluated DPDM implementation, which pairs a frozen semantic encoder with LimiX-2. The lexical features, native fields, and dimensionality-reduction methods described below specify this implementation.


#### A.1 Task Records and Historical Feedback

For each task, a record contains the visible input fields and candidate descriptions. The task specification defines the question to be answered and the mapping between answers and labels. For example, BFCL Callability supplies a user request and function schemas; the target indicates whether any supplied function is applicable. The same specification is used for historical records and queries. Each record becomes one feature row. LimiX-2 receives the historical rows, their observed labels, and the query row whose label is to be predicted. Labels and source-group identifiers are excluded from the record features.

Where source groups are defined, history and queries are separated by group. Numerical history budgets use nested prefixes of a randomly permuted historical pool, sampled without replacement or class balancing. Full-pool conditions retain the original record order. Appendix C gives the evaluation budgets and sampling settings.


#### A.2 Record Representations

Each record is encoded independently using its task question and candidate descriptions. Qwen and Llama use their native chat templates with text-only inputs; Qwen thinking is disabled. The feature row combines three components:

- • Semantic features. We read the final post-normalization hidden state at the last nonpadding prompt token, before answer generation. States from multiple target prompts are concatenated, L2-normalized, and compressed by PCA to at most 32 dimensions.

- • Lexical features. TF-IDF features [ 19 ] represent the visible text fields, with history-fitted dimensionality reduction using truncated SVD when applicable [ 3 ] .

- • Native fields. Available numeric and categorical fields accompany the text features. Categorical mappings and numeric scaling are fitted on the historical inputs; unseen query categories are treated as missing values.

Feature transformations are fitted only on historical inputs and applied unchanged to queries. As history grows, these transformations are refitted while both neural models remain frozen. Encoded states can be reused when the encoder, prompt renderer, and task specification are unchanged. The controls in Appendix F specify when the fitted transformation is held fixed across conditions.


#### A.3 From Predictions to Decisions

All tasks use the same frozen LimiX-2 model with 32-pipeline aggregation. Prediction uses all supplied historical rows, with retrieval disabled. LimiX-2 estimates label probabilities from these rows and the current query. For more than ten observed classes, a deterministic label hierarchy combines parent and leaf probabilities to recover probabilities for the original task labels.

Classes absent from history initially receive zero probability. A history containing one observed class produces a probability of one for that class without invoking LimiX-2. Candidates unavailable for the current query are then removed and the remaining probabilities normalized. If no probability mass remains, the valid candidates receive equal probabilities. The candidate with the highest probability is selected, with ties resolved by candidate order.

The controlled outcome example supplies numeric context–action inputs directly to LimiX-2 and selects the action with the highest predicted success probability.


### Appendix B Benchmark Inventory

The evaluation covers 50 benchmarks across five decision categories. Each benchmark defines a concrete decision and its reference target. DPDM uses historical feedback to predict that target for the current input. The 82 task configurations span five categories:

- • Agent tasks. Inputs include requests, available tools, reasoning steps, or candidate responses. Targets specify an intent, tool applicability, response type, step assessment, or preferred response. This category includes Banking77, TREC, Google Well-formed Query, BFCL Callability, ToolACE, ToolPRMBench, AgentProcess, CodeCritic, and HH-RLHF.

- • Knowledge QA. Tasks include answer selection and reasoning-step judgments from questions, passages, and candidate answers or steps. Examples include ARC, CommonsenseQA, HellaSwag, HaluEval, PRM800K, and BIG-Bench Mistake.

- • Evidence consistency. Claims, responses, or sentence pairs are judged against their supplied context. Examples include FaithBench, RAGTruth, and natural-language inference tasks.

- • Safety and reliability. Messages, responses, or reports are assigned task-defined risk or reliability labels. Examples include BeaverTails, ToxicChat, hate-speech datasets, ADE Corpus, and FaithDial’s dialogue-response hallucination judgments.

- • Language understanding. Text is assigned a topic, sentiment, emotion, or linguistic label. Examples include AG News, TweetEval Sentiment, Emo, and grammatical-acceptability tasks.

### Appendix F History and Representation Controls

The Section 3.5 controls use frozen Qwen3.5-27B representations and LimiX-2 with 32-pipeline aggregation. History-sampling seeds are 11, 23, and 47.


##### Historical-record controls.

BFCL Callability, ToolPRMBench ToolSandbox, CodeCritic Code, PRM800K, FaithDial, and TREC coarse each supply 128 historical records per seed. Query selection is independent of labels: a fixed random sample contains 128 queries per task, except ToolSandbox, which contributes all 97. This gives 737 query instances per seed, shared by every condition. Historical and query IDs are disjoint.

For each task and seed, the feature map is fitted once on the 128 unlabeled historical inputs and then reused. Label permutation selects 0, 13, 32, 64, or 128 historical positions and permutes their labels within the selected set. The position sets are nested. Class counts remain exactly unchanged; some selected positions can keep their original label by chance. The recorded outputs distinguish the selected fraction from the fraction whose label actually changes.

The distinct-record comparison supplies the first 16 labeled records, those records repeated eight times, or all 128 distinct labeled records. It uses the same fitted representation in each case. Thus, the 16-record condition has 16 labeled examples and a transformation fitted from the same 128 unlabeled reference inputs as the other conditions. Repetition changes row multiplicity without adding a new labeled observation. The historical-frequency reference predicts the most common label in the 128-record history for every query, with ties resolved by label ID.

The representation controls evaluate the complete query sets of the eight tasks, totaling 18,759 queries. The tasks are the six above plus Emotion and WANLI, with 696, 97, 962, 5,965, 3,539, 500, 2,000, and 5,000 queries, respectively. We reuse the original history and query IDs at each task’s largest numerical history within ceilings of 16, 32, 128, and 512. The corresponding actual history sizes range from 12–16, 24–32, 96–128, and 162–512 records.

Each task–history–seed group compares the full representation, the same representation with semantic feature columns removed, and the full representation with permuted historical labels. Native and lexical features are unchanged by semantic-feature removal. All variants use the same queries and history-fitted transformations.


##### Aggregation and reported contrasts.

We average task accuracies equally within each seed, then report the mean and sample standard deviation across the three seeds. Main-text contrasts compare original with permuted labels, distinct with repeated records at equal row count, and full with semantic-feature-removed representations at the same history size. The six-task and eight-task means are reported separately in Tables 2 , 3 , and 4 .

### Appendix H Information and Inference in Decision Quality

Additional observations can reveal a task relationship; a better learner can estimate it more accurately from those observations. To separate these effects, fix a task distribution and loss $L(b,Y)$ for answer $b$ and target $Y$ . Risk is the expected loss, such as the error rate under zero–one loss. Let $I$ contain the represented support and query, historical targets, and task specification. The best risk achievable using this information is

[Tableau] $$R_{I}^{\star}=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{I}}\mathbb{E}[L(\pi(I),Y)],$$ (5)

where $\Pi_{I}$ contains legal decision rules based only on $I$ . Let $R_{m}$ be the actual learner’s risk. An oracle receives a richer information set $I_{\mathrm{or}}$ containing $I$ , giving reference risk $R_{I_{\mathrm{or}}}^{\star}$ . The learner’s excess risk splits into

[Tableau] $\displaystyle R_{m}-R_{I_{\mathrm{or}}}^{\star}={}$ $\displaystyle\underbrace{R_{I}^{\star}-R_{I_{\mathrm{or}}}^{\star}}_{\text{information gap}}$ (6) $\displaystyle+\underbrace{R_{m}-R_{I}^{\star}}_{\text{inference gap}}.$

The information gap is the loss that even the best rule using $I$ cannot avoid without the oracle’s additional information. The inference gap is the learner’s loss beyond that best available-information rule. Both are nonnegative under the fixed distribution, loss, and nested information sets.

Appendix I illustrates this decomposition in a controlled environment with known success probabilities. An oracle knows the deployment mechanism, and a Bayes rule infers it from history; these references make the two gaps computable.


### Appendix I A Controlled Example

The decomposition in Appendix H distinguishes uncertainty left by a history from a learner’s ability to use that history. A small environment with known outcome probabilities makes both quantities computable.


##### Environment and prediction target.

There are four contexts, two actions, and two equally likely deployments. In deployment zero, the action-success probabilities are

[Tableau] $$P_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}.90&.55\\ .75&.40\\ .45&.85\\ .60&.90\end{pmatrix}.$$

Deployment one reverses the action columns: $P_{1}(x,a)=P_{0}(x,1-a)$ . Contexts and historical actions are sampled uniformly and independently, and outcomes are independent Bernoulli draws conditional on the context, action, and deployment. A historical record is $(x_{i},a_{i},o_{i})$ ; LimiX-2 receives two context bits and an action bit and predicts the binary outcome. It selects

[Tableau] $$\widehat{a}_{D}(x)=\arg\max_{a\in\{0,1\}}q_{D,\mathcal{T}}(O=1\mid x,a).$$ (7)

The inputs are numeric, so this example uses LimiX-2 directly.


##### Reference rules and gaps.

The oracle observes the active deployment and chooses its best action. A family-aware Bayes rule knows $P_{0}$ , $P_{1}$ , and the equal prior, and infers the deployment from the supplied history $D$ . For each rule, risk is expected failure probability under the same distribution of deployments, contexts, and outcomes. The information gap is Bayes risk minus oracle risk; the inference gap is LimiX-2 risk minus Bayes risk. Both use posterior expected risk conditional on $D$ , as in Equation 6 . The oracle sees the deployment identity but not future outcome draws.


##### Sampling and numerical illustration.

Histories contain 0, 4, 16, 64, or 256 records, with ten sampling seeds paired across the two deployments. The zero-history condition uses a fixed-action reference; nonempty histories use LimiX-2 with 32-pipeline aggregation (Appendix A ). Success is the known success probability of the selected action, averaged over contexts. At 256 records, the matching deployment history gives 85.00% expected success, compared with 50.00% for the opposite deployment’s history and 67.50% for the fixed-action reference. The supplied history therefore determines which actions are appropriate for the current deployment.

At 64 records, the mean information and inference gaps are 0.00115 and 2.624 additional failures per 100 decisions, respectively (95% paired-seed interval for the inference gap: [1.250, 3.937]). The interval uses 20,000 bootstrap resamples of the ten seeds, averaging the two deployments within each seed. At this history size, the inference gap accounts for most of the excess risk. At 256 records, LimiX-2 and Bayes choose the same actions in all twenty deployment–history conditions, and the estimated inference gap is zero.
