## DTCA: Denoising Token Credit Assignment for Reinforcement Learning in Diffusion Language Models

Diffusion language models (DLMs) generate text through iterative denoising, enabling bidirectional context modeling and parallel token refinement. Recent studies have applied reinforcement learning (RL) to DLMs and improved their reasoning performance. However, the iterative denoising process makes credit assignment more challenging, as final outcome rewards provide limited information about how different tokens contribute to the final output. In this paper, we propose Denoising Token Credit Assignment (DTCA) , a framework for token-level credit assignment in reinforcement learning for diffusion language models. DTCA estimates token contributions by measuring the improvement in prediction confidence of remaining masked positions after token commitment and groups these signals according to part-of-speech (POS) categories. These contribution weights are used to select informative denoising timesteps and assign token-level learning signals during RL training. DTCA further calibrates these learning signals according to token-level entropy changes induced by policy updates. Experiments on LLaDA-8B-Instruct show that DTCA consistently improves mathematical reasoning performance across different generation budgets, with gains of up to 9.0 points on MATH500 and 5.3 points on GSM8K over the base model.

### 2 Introduction

Diffusion language models (DLMs) have recently attracted increasing attention as an emerging paradigm for language modeling due to their ability to model bidirectional context and perform parallel token refinement ( Labs et al., 2025 ; Google DeepMind, 2025 ; Song et al., 2025 ) , offering an alternative to autoregressive generation ( Nie et al., 2025 ; Sahoo et al., 2024 ; Ye et al., 2025a ; Yu et al., 2025 ) . Recent studies have explored reinforcement learning for DLMs by leveraging the generation process, mainly constructing learning signals from denoising trajectories or intermediate states during training ( He et al., 2026 ; Wang et al., 2025b ; Oba et al., 2026 ; Monsefi et al., 2026 ; Huang et al., 2025 ) . However, DLMs generate sequences through multiple denoising steps, where tokens are gradually committed and each commitment can affect the refinement of remaining masked positions ( Ou et al., 2025 ) . Therefore, RL optimization in DLMs requires finer-grained credit assignment that considers how different tokens affect subsequent refinement.

> Figure 1: Preliminary analysis of POS-level differences in DLM denoising using rollouts generated from the GSM8K ( Cobbe et al., 2021 ) training set. (a) Commitment timing of different POS categories across denoising steps. (b) Improvement in the prediction confidence of remaining masked positions after individual token commitment, averaged within each POS category. POS tags denote NOUN (noun), NUM (numeral), PUNCT (punctuation), PROPN (proper noun), VERB (verb), ADP (adposition), DET (determiner), and ADJ (adjective).

Achieving finer grained credit assignment requires estimating the contribution of individual tokens during DLM denoising. Specifically, we measure token contribution by how much a committed token improves the model’s prediction confidence on the remaining masked positions. However, directly estimating such token-level confidence improvements during RL training is challenging because multiple tokens are committed at the same denoising step and interact through the shared denoising process, making their individual effects difficult to isolate. Moreover, the estimated contribution can vary substantially across different rollout samples. To obtain more reliable estimates, we categorize tokens by part-of-speech (POS) categories and aggregate their refinement effects within each category. POS categories provide a natural grouping criterion because they capture different linguistic functions, with nouns and verbs typically carrying lexical information while determiners and adpositions mainly providing grammatical structure ( de Marneffe et al., 2021 ) .

The denoising behaviors of different POS categories are further analyzed using rollouts generated from the GSM8K ( Cobbe et al., 2021 ) dataset. As shown in Figure 1 (a), different POS categories exhibit distinct commitment trajectories across denoising steps. NOUN maintains a relatively high commitment ratio, whereas VERB has a high ratio at the beginning that declines substantially over the denoising process. Other categories, such as ADP, DET, and ADJ, exhibit relatively lower commitment ratios with more stable variations. Figure 1 (b) further shows the confidence gain associated with different POS categories. Clear differences emerge across categories: VERB achieves the largest confidence gain, followed by NOUN and PROPN, whereas categories such as DET and ADP show relatively smaller gains. These observations indicate that POS categories capture different denoising behaviors and refinement effects, making them a useful basis for grouping tokens and estimating contribution weights.

Based on these observations, we propose Denoising Token Credit Assignment (DTCA) , a token-level credit assignment framework for reinforcement learning in diffusion language models. DTCA estimates token contributions from denoising refinement signals and aggregates them into POS-level contribution weights to obtain more stable and interpretable learning signals. These contribution signals are then used to select informative denoising timesteps and derive token-level learning signals during RL optimization. DTCA further incorporates an entropy-based credit calibration module inspired by OPEFO ( Xu et al., 2026 ) to calibrate the learning signals according to token-level entropy changes induced by policy updates. Experiments on LLaDA-8B-Instruct show that DTCA consistently improves mathematical reasoning performance across different generation budgets, with improvements of up to 9.0 percentage points on MATH500 ( Lightman et al., 2024 ) and 5.3 percentage points on GSM8K ( Cobbe et al., 2021 ) over the base model.

The main contributions are summarized as follows:

- • We analyze different denoising behaviors across POS categories in diffusion language models and use POS categories to estimate the effect of token commitments on subsequent refinement.

- • We propose DTCA , a token-level credit assignment framework that aggregates denoising refinement signals by POS categories and uses the resulting contribution weights for denoising timestep selection and token credit assignment. DTCA further adapts entropy-based credit calibration from OPEFO ( Xu et al., 2026 ) to the diffusion language model setting for improved RL optimization.

- • Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of DTCA on mathematical reasoning benchmarks and validate the benefits of its key components.


### 3 Preliminaries


#### 3.1 Diffusion Language Model Denoising

Masked diffusion language models generate responses through iterative denoising ( Nie et al., 2025 ; Sahoo et al., 2024 ) . Given a prompt $q$ , let $\tau=(x_{T},x_{T-1},\ldots,x_{0})$ denote a sampled trajectory, where $x_{t}$ is the partially masked response at denoising step $t$ and $x_{0}$ is the completed response. Under the monotonic unmasking schedule used in this work, each step reveals a subset of masked positions as predicted tokens; we call this token commitment . Committed tokens remain visible in subsequent states and can influence predictions at the remaining masked positions. The distribution $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid q)$ below is the response distribution induced by this iterative sampling procedure, not an autoregressive token factorization.


#### 3.2 Reinforcement Learning for Diffusion Language Models

Given prompts $q\sim\mathcal{D}$ and sampled responses $y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q)$ , reinforcement learning seeks to maximize expected sequence-level reward:

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{q\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q)}[R(q,y)].$$ (1)

Group-relative policy optimization converts rewards from multiple responses to the same prompt into response advantages ( Shao et al., 2024 ) . In masked diffusion RL, these advantages can be applied to predictions at selected denoising states ( He et al., 2026 ) . Let $\mathcal{T}$ be the selected steps, $\mathcal{B}_{t}$ their optimization target positions, and $v_{i}$ the target token at position $i$ . A schematic token-level surrogate is

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RL}}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}A_{t,i}\log\pi_{\theta}(v_{i}\mid q,x_{t})\right]+\beta_{\mathrm{KL}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}},$$ (2)

where $N=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}|\mathcal{B}_{t}|$ and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}$ denotes a penalty relative to a frozen reference policy ( Shao et al., 2024 ; He et al., 2026 ) . The exact target set, credit $A_{t,i}$ , KL estimator, and update loss used by DTCA are specified below and in Appendix B . A sequence-level reward alone does not identify which commitments help subsequent denoising; this motivates assigning credit at the denoising-state and target-token levels.


### 4 Method

We propose Denoising Token Credit Assignment (DTCA) , a token credit assignment framework for reinforcement learning in diffusion language models. As illustrated in Figure 2 , DTCA estimates token contributions from denoising refinement dynamics and uses the resulting contribution weights for informative denoising state selection and token-level credit assignment. DTCA further calibrates the token-level learning signals according to entropy changes induced by policy updates.

> Figure 2: Overview of Denoising Token Credit Assignment (DTCA). DTCA estimates token contributions from denoising refinement dynamics and uses the resulting contribution weights for denoising state selection and token-level credit assignment. The obtained learning signals are further calibrated according to entropy changes induced by policy updates for reinforcement learning optimization.


#### 4.1 Token Contribution Estimation by POS Category

As illustrated in Figure 2 , DTCA estimates token-level refinement contributions from denoising dynamics and aggregates these estimates into category-level contribution weights. Given a denoising trajectory $\tau$ , different token commitments may have different effects on subsequent refinement. DTCA therefore estimates the refinement contribution of each committed token by measuring the improvement in prediction confidence of the remaining masked positions after the commitment.

Specifically, for each individual token commitment at denoising step $t$ , let $\mathcal{M}$ denote the response positions that remain masked both before and after the commitment, excluding the committed position $i$ . Given a fixed prompt $q$ , we define the aggregate prediction confidence of a denoising state $x$ over $\mathcal{M}$ as the sum of the maximum prediction probabilities at those positions:

[Tableau] $$F(x;\mathcal{M})=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\max_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p_{\theta,j}(v\mid q,x),$$ (3)

where $j$ indexes a masked position, $\mathcal{V}$ is the vocabulary, and $p_{\theta,j}(v\mid q,x)$ is the probability assigned to token $v$ at position $j$ conditioned on prompt $q$ and denoising state $x$ . For a committed token $v_{i}$ , its refinement contribution estimate is computed as:

[Tableau] $$\Delta P_{i}=\max\left(0,F(x_{t}^{(i)};\mathcal{M})-F(x_{t};\mathcal{M})\right),$$ (4)

where $x_{t}^{(i)}$ denotes the denoising state after committing token $v_{i}$ individually. Negative total changes are set to zero; the estimate is not divided by the number of remaining masks. A larger $\Delta P_{i}$ indicates a stronger estimated refinement effect of the committed token on subsequent denoising.

Obtaining reliable token-level estimates directly during RL training is challenging because evaluating individual token effects requires re-evaluating token commitments with additional forward passes, introducing substantial computational overhead during policy optimization. Moreover, these estimates can vary across different rollout samples. Therefore, DTCA constructs contribution estimates from offline denoising rollouts before RL training and groups them according to part-of-speech (POS) categories to obtain more stable category-level contribution weights.

During offline estimation, DTCA uses a denoising budget equal to half of the maximum generation length. At each denoising step, two tokens are committed, and DTCA evaluates each token separately through additional forward passes based on the confidence improvement of remaining masked positions. These estimates are aggregated at the POS level to reduce variance across rollouts.

For each committed token $v_{i}$ , we assign its POS category as $c_{i}=\phi(v_{i})$ . The contribution of each POS category is obtained by averaging the refinement contribution estimates of tokens belonging to that category:

[Tableau] $$m_{c}=\frac{1}{N_{c}}\sum_{i:c_{i}=c}\Delta P_{i},$$ (5)

where $N_{c}$ denotes the number of committed tokens in POS category $c$ . The resulting category-level contribution $m_{c}$ represents the average refinement effect estimated for tokens in each category.


##### POS Contribution Weight Construction.

The estimated POS contributions are normalized into bounded contribution weights:

[Tableau] $$w_{c}=0.8+0.4\frac{m_{c}-\min_{j\in\mathcal{C}}m_{j}}{\max_{j\in\mathcal{C}}m_{j}-\min_{j\in\mathcal{C}}m_{j}},$$ (6)

where $\mathcal{C}$ denotes the set of POS categories. The weight $w_{c}$ lies in the range $[0.8,1.2]$ and represents the scaling factor derived from the estimated refinement contribution of category $c$ . We constrain this range to prevent excessive credit scaling while preserving the original RL objective. These weights are used to adjust token-level credits during RL optimization.


#### 4.2 Contribution-Based Timestep Selection

The category contribution weights are also used to identify informative denoising timesteps for RL training. Following RLDF ( He et al., 2026 ) , DTCA optimizes only a subset of denoising timesteps from each rollout. For a denoising timestep $t$ , let $\mathcal{A}_{t}$ denote the set of tokens committed at this step. We compute the importance score of each timestep as:

[Tableau] $$q_{t}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}w_{c_{i}}(1-p_{i}),$$ (7)

where $p_{i}$ denotes the confidence of token $i$ when it is committed and $w_{c_{i}}$ denotes the contribution weight of its POS category. The term $1-p_{i}$ assigns higher scores to timesteps containing lower-confidence token commitments, while $w_{c_{i}}$ incorporates the estimated refinement contribution of the committed tokens.

DTCA ranks denoising timesteps according to $q_{t}$ and selects the smallest set of timesteps whose cumulative score reaches a predefined fraction $\mu$ of the total score. The number of selected timesteps therefore adapts to the distribution of contribution signals in each rollout. Lower and upper bounds are applied to the number of selected timesteps for stable and efficient training.


#### 4.3 Contribution-Based Credit Assignment

The category contribution weights are used to assign different credits to tokens within each selected denoising timestep. For a selected timestep $t$ , let $\mathcal{B}_{t}$ denote the tokens used for optimization, including the committed tokens at timestep $t$ and the retained masked positions selected by the threshold-based filtering strategy in RLDF ( He et al., 2026 ) . POS categories for all tokens in $\mathcal{B}_{t}$ are obtained from the completed rollout, where retained masked positions are associated with their final predicted tokens. The credit weight of token $v_{i}$ is determined by the contribution weight of its POS category:

[Tableau] $$a_{t,i}=\frac{w_{c_{i}}}{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_{t}|}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}w_{c_{k}}},$$ (8)

where $c_{i}$ is the POS category of token $v_{i}$ . The normalization keeps the average credit within each timestep equal to one, so that the overall scale of the timestep advantage remains unchanged. Given the original timestep advantage $A_{t}$ , DTCA computes the token-level learning signal as

[Tableau] $$A_{t,i}=a_{t,i}A_{t}.$$ (9)

Tokens from categories with larger contribution weights receive stronger learning signals, while tokens from categories with smaller contribution receive weaker signals. In this way, DTCA transforms the original response-level feedback into more fine grained token credit during RL training.


#### 4.4 Entropy-Based Credit Calibration

Although DTCA provides token-level learning signals according to denoising contribution, different denoising states may induce different entropy changes during policy updates. To further calibrate the token-level learning signals, DTCA incorporates an entropy-based credit calibration module inspired by OPEFO ( Xu et al., 2026 ) . Unlike the original formulation for autoregressive generation, we adapt the token-level entropy change estimation to selected denoising states and their remaining target positions in diffusion language models.

For each response $b$ , selected denoising state $t$ , and remaining target position $i$ , the entropy change induced by the policy update is estimated as:

[Tableau] $$\Delta H_{t,i}^{(b)}=-\eta A_{t,i}^{(b)}(1-p_{i}^{(b)})^{2}(\log p_{i}^{(b)}+H_{i}^{(b)}),$$ (10)

where $p_{i}^{(b)}$ denotes the probability assigned by the current policy to the target token and $H_{i}^{(b)}$ denotes the entropy of the full prediction distribution at this position. This formulation follows the first-order entropy change estimation of OPEFO ( Xu et al., 2026 ) while replacing autoregressive decoding steps with selected denoising states and target positions.

Within each response, the entropy-increasing and entropy-decreasing updates are separated according to the sign of $\Delta H_{t,i}^{(b)}$ :

[Tableau] $$S_{b}^{+}=\{(t,i)|\Delta H_{t,i}^{(b)}>0\},\qquad S_{b}^{-}=\{(t,i)|\Delta H_{t,i}^{(b)}<0\}.$$ (11)

Following OPEFO ( Xu et al., 2026 ) , we compute a separate entropy flow balancing coefficient for each response:

[Tableau] $$\lambda_{b}=\operatorname{clip}\left(\frac{\sum_{(t,i)\in S_{b}^{-}}|\Delta H_{t,i}^{(b)}|-\sum_{(t,i)\in S_{b}^{+}}\Delta H_{t,i}^{(b)}}{\sum_{(t,i)\in S_{b}^{-}}|\Delta H_{t,i}^{(b)}|+\sum_{(t,i)\in S_{b}^{+}}\Delta H_{t,i}^{(b)}},-0.95,0.95\right).$$ (12)

The entropy balancing coefficient is then used to rescale token-level learning signals to balance entropy-increasing and entropy-decreasing updates. When $\lambda_{b}>0$ , entropy-increasing updates are amplified and entropy-decreasing updates are attenuated. When $\lambda_{b}<0$ , the adjustment is reversed. When $\lambda_{b}=0$ , the credits remain unchanged:

[Tableau] $$\hat{A}_{t,i}^{(b)}=\begin{cases}(1+\lambda_{b})A_{t,i}^{(b)},&\Delta H_{t,i}^{(b)}>0,\\ (1-\lambda_{b})A_{t,i}^{(b)},&\Delta H_{t,i}^{(b)}\leq 0.\end{cases}$$ (13)


### 5 Experiments


#### 5.1 Experimental Setup

Model and data. We initialize RL training from LLaDA-8B-Instruct ( Nie et al., 2025 ) without additional SFT. Following RLDF ( He et al., 2026 ) , we use the MATH ( Hendrycks et al., 2021 ) training split with the same data processing pipeline for RL optimization. To construct the fixed POS contribution table, DTCA collects offline denoising rollouts from the training data of MATH and GSM8K and builds a shared POS contribution table without updating model parameters.

Training. We train LLaDA-8B-Instruct with reinforcement learning without an intermediate SFT stage. For each rollout, DTCA selects denoising states with the highest POS-weighted uncertainty scores until covering 70% of the total score mass, with the number of selected states constrained between 2 and 32. We perform policy optimization using group-relative advantages and regularize the policy against a frozen reference model with the k3 KL estimator ( Schulman, 2020 ) . Rollouts are generated using block-wise decoding with KV cache, following Fast-dLLM ( Wu et al., 2026b ) . Full implementation details and hyperparameters are provided in Appendix C .

Evaluation and baselines. We evaluate mathematical reasoning performance on MATH500 ( Lightman et al., 2024 ) and GSM8K ( Cobbe et al., 2021 ) using final-answer accuracy. Following prior work, we evaluate all models under generation budgets of 128, 256, and 512, with denoising steps matched to the corresponding maximum generation lengths. We compare DTCA with LLaDA-8B-Instruct, Uniform SFT, LLaDA-1.5, and representative reinforcement learning methods for diffusion language models, including d1 ( Zhao et al., 2025 ) , ESPO ( Ou et al., 2026 ) , TraceRL ( Wang et al., 2025b ) , Coupled-GRPO ( Gong et al., 2026 ) , and RLDF ( He et al., 2026 ) . Additional details on training configurations and baseline evaluation settings are provided in Appendix C.2 and Appendix C.4 .


#### 5.2 Main Results

Table 1 compares DTCA with supervised and reinforcement learning baselines on MATH500 and GSM8K. DTCA consistently improves over LLaDA-8B-Instruct across both benchmarks and all evaluated generation budgets. On MATH500, DTCA improves the base model by 9.0, 6.8, and 4.8 percentage points at budgets of 128, 256, and 512, respectively. On GSM8K, the corresponding improvements are 5.3, 2.1, and 2.5 percentage points. These results show that DTCA provides consistent gains under different denoising budgets rather than relying on a specific inference setting. Compared with supervised optimization and existing diffusion language model reinforcement learning methods, DTCA achieves competitive performance across the reported settings, demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed token-level credit assignment framework.

The largest improvements on both benchmarks occur at the smallest evaluated generation budget of 128, while gains remain positive at budgets of 256 and 512. Although the relative improvements become smaller at larger generation budgets, DTCA consistently improves over the base model, highlighting its stronger gains under limited denoising computation.

#### 5.3 Ablation Study

Table 1 reports ablation results for different DTCA components. DTCA w/o Contribution Credit replaces the contribution-based token credits with uniform credits while keeping the same timestep selection strategy. DTCA w/o Entropy Calibration removes the entropy-based credit calibration module while preserving contribution-based credit assignment and timestep selection.

Removing either component decreases performance compared with the full DTCA model across both benchmarks and different generation budgets. At the 256 generation budget, removing Contribution Credit leads to decreases of 3.8 and 2.4 percentage points on MATH500 and GSM8K, respectively, while removing Entropy Calibration results in smaller drops of 1.4 and 1.4 points. At the 512 generation budget, removing Entropy Calibration causes larger performance decreases, with drops of 4.2 and 3.5 points on MATH500 and GSM8K. These results show that both components contribute to the overall performance of DTCA.


#### 5.4 Analysis of Optimization Dynamics

To understand how different DTCA components influence RL optimization, we analyze training dynamics in terms of reward, gradient norm, and KL divergence. Figure 3 compares DTCA with variants without Contribution Credit and Entropy Calibration during training.

Removing Contribution Credit results in similar reward trajectories to DTCA, but leads to substantially different update behaviors. In particular, the variant with uniform credit assignment consistently exhibits larger gradient norms throughout training while achieving comparable reward improvements. This suggests that contribution-based credit assignment mainly affects the quality of token-level learning signals, allowing the model to optimize with different update dynamics rather than simply changing the final reward trend. By considering token-level contribution differences, DTCA provides a more structured learning signal than uniform credit assignment.

Removing Entropy Calibration mainly affects the stability of later-stage policy updates. Although its reward trajectory initially follows a similar trend, the variant without entropy calibration shows a faster increase in KL divergence and lower reward in later training stages. This indicates that entropy calibration helps control policy changes by adjusting learning signals according to entropy changes, leading to more stable optimization during RL training.

#### 5.5 Analysis of Token Commitment Dynamics

To understand whether contribution-based credit assignment changes the denoising behavior of diffusion language models, we analyze token commitment patterns after RL training. Token commitment reflects when tokens become fixed during denoising, while the commitment distribution and prediction states provide insights into the learned denoising behavior. Figure 4 compares DTCA with the base model on MATH500 across early, middle, and late denoising stages. Each cell reports the difference between DTCA and the base model in token commitment ratio, prediction confidence at commitment, and target entropy at commitment.

DTCA changes the commitment distribution across token categories rather than uniformly shifting all commitments. For example, DTCA consistently reduces the commitment ratio of NUM tokens while increasing the commitment ratio of PUNCT tokens across different denoising stages. These changes suggest that DTCA learns different commitment patterns across token categories instead of simply accelerating or delaying all token predictions. Notably, changes in commitment frequency do not always correspond to changes in commitment states. For example, VERB shows limited changes in commitment ratio but substantial increases in confidence and reductions in entropy at commitment. This indicates that DTCA influences not only which tokens are committed, but also the model states when commitments occur. The observed changes are consistent with leveraging denoising refinement signals to improve token-level credit assignment during reinforcement learning.


#### 5.6 Analysis of Entropy-Based Credit Calibration

We study how entropy calibration changes token credits and prediction entropy across POS categories. We compare DTCA with a variant that keeps the same contribution-based credit assignment and timestep selection but removes entropy calibration. Figure 5 shows the entropy changes during training. Positive values mean increased entropy, and negative values mean decreased entropy. Values in parentheses show the relative change in assigned credit after calibration. We compare these credit changes with the entropy curves for each category.

In Figure 5 , the two variants show similar entropy changes early in training and gradually diverge later. For most of the shown POS categories, DTCA has a lower curve late in training. Each curve shows changes relative to that run’s early training average. For example, without calibration, the NUM curve rises above zero after about 200 updates and stays positive for most of the remaining steps. DTCA stays near or below zero for most of this period. For SYM, the curve without calibration returns toward zero late in training, while DTCA remains negative. AUX shows the opposite pattern. Its curve without calibration drops sharply below zero near the end, while the DTCA curve stays close to zero. The differences between the two variants therefore vary across POS categories, rather than following the same direction for all categories.

Credit changes and entropy changes do not always follow the same direction. For example, SYM receives more credit but stays below its early entropy average late in training. AUX receives less credit but ends above its early average. Credit changes the strength of the existing policy update. Whether entropy rises or falls also depends on the advantage signal, the current prediction distribution, and the target token. Compared with OPEFO ( Xu et al., 2026 ) , DTCA applies entropy flow balancing after assigning POS-based credits. Within each response, updates in the same entropy-change set share a scaling factor. This factor and the final normalization preserve the credit ratios within that set. Thus, entropy flow balancing still leaves different credits for tokens with different POS contribution weights. The two runs also see different denoising states and different mixes of target tokens as training progresses. The curves therefore show overall training trends, rather than measuring the same tokens immediately before and after a single policy update.

### 6 Conclusion

We presented Denoising Token Credit Assignment (DTCA) , a framework for fine-grained token-level credit assignment in reinforcement learning for diffusion language models. DTCA estimates token contributions from the confidence improvement of remaining masked positions after token commitment, aggregates these signals by POS categories, and uses the resulting contribution weights for denoising timestep selection and token-level credit assignment. DTCA further calibrates contribution credits according to entropy changes induced by policy updates, balancing entropy-increasing and entropy-decreasing updates. On LLaDA-8B-Instruct, DTCA improves the reproduced base model by 6.8 percentage points on MATH500 and 2.1 points on GSM8K under the 256-step evaluation setting. Training dynamics and component ablations show that contribution-based credit assignment improves token-level learning signals, while entropy-based calibration helps maintain more stable policy updates. These results demonstrate that denoising dynamics provide useful information for fine-grained credit assignment in reinforcement learning for diffusion language models.

### Appendix A Related Work


#### A.1 Diffusion Language Models

Building on the denoising paradigm of diffusion models ( Ho et al., 2020 ) , diffusion language models (DLMs) generate text through iterative denoising ( Hoogeboom et al., 2021 ; Dieleman et al., 2022 ; Gulrajani and Hashimoto, 2023 ) rather than autoregressive next-token prediction ( Lou et al., 2024 ; Li et al., 2022 ; Ye et al., 2024 ) . Early discrete diffusion language models explore masked generation objectives and iterative refinement for language modeling ( Austin et al., 2021 ; Sahoo et al., 2024 ; Shi et al., 2024 ) . LLaDA scales this paradigm to large language models with a masked diffusion objective and demonstrates competitive language generation performance ( Nie et al., 2025 ) . Subsequent works further study improved training objectives, generation strategies ( Wu et al., 2026a ; Xie et al., 2025 ) , scaling behaviors ( Ye et al., 2025b ; Gong et al., 2026 ; Wu et al., 2026b ) , and data efficiency under limited-data and multi-epoch training settings ( Prabhudesai et al., 2025 ; Ni et al., 2025 ; Gao et al., 2025 ) . When applying reinforcement learning to these models, their iterative generation process requires particular consideration. Unlike autoregressive models, DLMs maintain multiple intermediate denoising states, where tokens are progressively committed and remaining masked positions are refined. This generation process introduces new challenges and opportunities for reinforcement learning in diffusion models.


#### A.2 Reinforcement Learning for Diffusion Language Models

Reinforcement learning for diffusion language models requires optimization methods that account for the iterative denoising process ( Raajesh et al., 2026 ; He et al., 2025 ) . Existing approaches mainly adapt RL objectives ( Schulman et al., 2017 ; Shao et al., 2024 ; Zekri and Boulle, 2025 ; Ni et al., 2026 ) or exploit intermediate denoising information ( Xie et al., 2026 ) to construct effective learning signals ( Tang et al., 2026 ; Rojas et al., 2026 ; Zhu et al., 2026 ) . d1 introduces diffu-GRPO ( Zhao et al., 2025 ) , Coupled-GRPO reduces likelihood estimation variance through complementary masks ( Gong et al., 2026 ) , and ESPO uses evidence lower bound as a proxy for sequence likelihood during RL optimization ( Ou et al., 2026 ) . Other methods leverage denoising trajectories ( Yang et al., 2025 ) and intermediate states to improve RL training, including TraceRL, RLDF, and DiSPO ( Wang et al., 2025b ; He et al., 2026 ; Oba et al., 2026 ) . These methods demonstrate how diffusion generation characteristics can be incorporated into RL optimization ( Zhao et al., 2026 ) . However, they mainly focus on optimization objectives ( Zhong et al., 2026 ; Jiang et al., 2026 ) , likelihood estimation ( Wang et al., 2026 ) , or denoising trajectories, while how to assign fine-grained credit to individual tokens during the denoising process remains underexplored.


#### A.3 Fine-grained Credit Assignment

Sequence-level rewards in language model reinforcement learning ( Ouyang et al., 2022 ; Guo et al., 2025 ) provide limited information about which tokens or reasoning steps contribute to the final outcome, motivating recent studies on fine-grained credit assignment ( Wang et al., 2025a ; Cheng et al., 2026 ) . Existing approaches improve reward granularity through process supervision ( Uesato et al., 2022 ) , step-level feedback, and token-level reward redistribution ( Tan et al., 2026 ) or importance estimation ( Lightman et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2024 ; Li et al., 2025 ; Cui et al., 2025 ; Kazemnejad et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2026 ) . However, these methods are mainly developed for autoregressive language models, where tokens are generated sequentially. For diffusion language models, DACA-GRPO provides an initial exploration of token-level credit assignment by weighting committed tokens according to denoising progress and changes in the predicted probabilities of remaining masked positions ( Monsefi et al., 2026 ) . Different from DACA-GRPO, DTCA estimates token contributions from confidence improvements after token commitment, aggregates these signals according to POS categories, and uses the resulting contribution structure for denoising timestep selection and token-level credit assignment.

##### Training objective.

Let $A^{(b)}$ be response $b$ ’s group-normalized advantage, used as $A_{t}$ in Eq. 9 at each selected step $t\in\mathcal{T}^{(b)}$ . The targets $\mathcal{B}_{t}^{(b)}$ include masked positions with predicted token probability at least $0.2$ and positions committed at step $t$ . Since the completed rollout is available after generation, POS categories can be assigned to all targets in $\mathcal{B}_{t}^{(b)}$ . Eqs. 8 – 13 yield $\hat{A}_{t,i}^{(b)}$ for each target. We restore the response’s total credit:

[Tableau] $$\widetilde{A}_{t,i}^{(b)}=\hat{A}_{t,i}^{(b)}\frac{\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}^{(b)}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{B}_{t^{\prime}}^{(b)}}A_{t^{\prime},j}^{(b)}}{\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}^{(b)}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{B}_{t^{\prime}}^{(b)}}\hat{A}_{t^{\prime},j}^{(b)}}.$$ (14)

This response-wide factor restores the overall credit scale after entropy calibration, while clipping $\lambda_{b}$ may leave a residual entropy imbalance. The update loss is

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DTCA}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{b}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}^{(b)}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{t}^{(b)}}\left[-\widetilde{A}_{t,i}^{(b)}\log\pi_{\theta}(v_{i}^{(b)}\mid q^{(b)},x_{t}^{(b)})+\beta_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(e^{d_{t,i}^{(b)}}-1-d_{t,i}^{(b)}\right)\right],$$ (15)

where $N=\sum_{b}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}^{(b)}}|\mathcal{B}_{t}^{(b)}|$ and $d_{t,i}^{(b)}=\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(v_{i}^{(b)}\mid q^{(b)},x_{t}^{(b)})-\log\pi_{\theta}(v_{i}^{(b)}\mid q^{(b)},x_{t}^{(b)})$ . The reference policy is frozen. With one policy iteration, PPO ratio clipping is inactive. Entropy calibration modifies $\widetilde{A}_{t,i}^{(b)}$ rather than adding an entropy loss.


### Appendix C Experimental Setup and Implementation Details


#### C.1 Base Models and Datasets

The policy and frozen reference model both start from LLaDA-8B-Instruct, without an intermediate SFT stage. RL training uses the MATH training set processed for RLDF. Offline POS contribution estimates are constructed from denoising rollouts over the MATH and GSM8K training splits without parameter updates to build a shared fixed POS contribution table. We evaluate on MATH500 and the GSM8K test split; neither evaluation split is used for RL training or POS table construction.


#### C.2 Training Configuration

We reproduce the compared methods under a unified experimental setup wherever applicable. RL methods start from LLaDA-8B-Instruct and use the same RLDF-processed MATH training split and mathematical correctness reward. All methods are evaluated using the common protocol in Appendix C.4 . Each method retains its own objective and method-specific settings: we follow its original paper first, and use its released open-source code for details not specified in the paper.

For DTCA and the locally reproduced RLDF baseline, we use full-parameter bfloat16 training with AdamW, a constant learning rate of $10^{-6}$ , and zero weight decay. Each prompt produces four responses, whose rewards are standardized within the group to form response advantages. Both methods use the same low-probability threshold of 0.2 for training-target filtering, token-level loss normalization, and a frozen-reference $k_{3}$ KL penalty with coefficient $0.01$ .

Their training rollouts use a maximum response length of 256 tokens and 256 denoising steps. Decoding uses a block size of 32 and a dynamic confidence threshold of $0.9$ ; when no position crosses the threshold, the highest-confidence position is committed. This decoding threshold is distinct from the $0.2$ training-target filter. KV caching is used during rollout generation.


#### C.3 Implementation Details of DTCA

We tag completions with spaCy’s en_core_web_sm POS model and align its word spans with model token positions. The 18 native POS categories share one contribution table. Before RL training, we map the offline category means to $[0.8,1.2]$ using Eq. 6 . These weights are used for both timestep selection and token credit assignment and remain fixed throughout RL training.

DTCA changes the timestep-selection and credit-assignment rules relative to the locally reproduced RLDF baseline. For state selection, we rank observed denoising steps by $q_{t}$ and cover at least $70\%$ of the total priority mass, selecting between 2 and 32 steps. At each selected step, training targets are the remaining masked positions retained by the shared low-probability filter, plus the positions committed at that step even if they fall below the filter threshold. POS credits are normalized to preserve the mean response advantage within each selected step. Entropy-flow calibration limits the absolute balancing coefficient to $0.95$ and restores each response’s total credit after rescaling.


#### C.4 Evaluation Settings

We evaluate each checkpoint at maximum response lengths of 128, 256, and 512 tokens, using the same number of denoising steps as the response length. For every setting, we use block size 32, dynamic confidence threshold $0.9$ , temperature $0$ , and one generated response per question. The same math prompt template and decoding settings are used for DTCA and all locally reproduced baselines.

We evaluate only whether the final answer is correct, without scoring the intermediate reasoning steps. Accuracy is the fraction of questions with correct final answers on the complete test split. All baseline scores in Table 1 are obtained from our local reproductions under this evaluation protocol.

### Appendix E Limitations

While DTCA provides an effective approach for fine-grained credit assignment in diffusion language model reinforcement learning, several aspects remain open for future exploration. DTCA uses POS categories to aggregate token-level confidence improvements and obtain stable contribution estimates. This design relies on the quality of POS alignment and annotation, and more accurate token-level labeling strategies may further improve the estimation process. Moreover, DTCA currently defines token contribution based on the confidence improvement of remaining masked positions after token commitment. Developing more principled theoretical formulations for contribution estimation in diffusion language models remains an interesting direction. Finally, our experiments focus on mathematical reasoning with LLaDA-8B-Instruct. Applying DTCA to other tasks, such as code generation, may require adapting the token grouping strategy to different token distributions and generation patterns. Further evaluation on diverse diffusion language model architectures is also needed to understand the general applicability of DTCA.
