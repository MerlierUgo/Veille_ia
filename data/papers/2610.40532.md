How effectively can vision-language-action (VLA) models learn diverse language-conditioned behaviors as training data scales? Investigating this question requires large and varied robot datasets, whose collection on physical robots remains costly. We introduce BURGER, a language-conditioned robotic drawing benchmark with a scalable, prompt-driven pipeline for generating continuous drawing trajectories, both in simulation and the real world. In BURGER, an agent starts from the same blank canvas and controls a robot arm to draw the object or spatial composition specified by language, while each instruction allows an open-ended variety of valid drawings and stroke orders. Using 28,200 simulated demonstrations spanning 282 drawing tasks, we evaluate five representative VLA models across data scales, language grounding, and multimodal behavior. BURGER supports fine-grained analysis of language grounding, spatial composition, and action generation, revealing distinct failure patterns across VLA models. Our experiments suggest that some VLA models can benefit from greater task diversity and demonstration scale, capture multimodal drawing behaviors, and generalize to unseen compositions of familiar objects. Finally, we deploy a trained policy on a Franka Panda arm, demonstrating the ability to draw reactively under human intervention. Project homepage and code can be found here .

### 1 Introduction

Vision-language-action (VLA) models integrate visual perception, language understanding, and action generation into policies for robotic control [ 40 , 14 , 4 , 3 ] . As VLA models increasingly benefit from larger and more diverse datasets, an important question is whether current architectures are ready to effectively exploit large-scale language-conditioned robot trajectories. Investigating this question requires robot datasets with controllable scale and diversity, together with evaluations that distinguish different aspects of model behavior [ 21 , 39 , 35 ] . However, collecting diverse demonstrations on physical robots remains costly [ 12 , 23 ] , which motivates a more scalable setting for such studies.

Robotic drawing provides a complementary data regime with several practical advantages for such studies. Starting from the same blank canvas, the agent cannot infer the requested drawing from the initial observation alone, eliminating a potential visual shortcut [ 37 ] . Language prompts can be systematically increased in semantic complexity by composing objects, attributes, and spatial relations. The same instruction can admit many valid trajectories that differ in stroke order, starting point, and drawing direction, this intrinsic multimodality forces model to understand the concept, rather than memorizing the action sequence. The resulting canvases and execution trajectories make semantic, spatial, and action-execution failures directly inspectable, supporting fine-grained diagnosis of model behavior. Drawing therefore provides a scalable setting for testing whether VLA architectures can learn complex language-conditioned and multimodal robot trajectories before scaling up costly real-world data collection.

Motivated by these properties, we introduce BURGER, a scalable benchmark and data-generation pipeline for language-conditioned robotic drawing, shown in Fig. 1 . Our pipeline generates target sketches from natural-language prompts and converts them into robot end-effector trajectories executable in simulation and on a real robot. Using this pipeline, we construct 132 single-object tasks and 150 compositional tasks, with 100 demonstrations per task. The dataset spans diverse objects, spatial compositions, and trajectory realizations. As illustrated in Fig. 2 , drawing trajectories in BURGER exhibit substantially greater diversity in the plane for a given instruction than manipulation trajectories in LIBERO [ 18 ] . We introduce a DINOv3-based metric for scalable evaluation of generated drawings and validate its agreement with human judgments in a single-object user study.

We use BURGER to examine how representative VLA models learn from larger datasets, capture trajectory diversity, and generalize to new compositions. The flow-matching models generally improve as either the number of demonstrations per task or the number of training tasks increases, suggesting that some existing approaches can benefit from scaling robotic drawing data. The $\pi$ -family models [ 4 , 3 ] can autonomously produce recognizable drawings after fine-tuning. Beyond visual recognizability, $\pi_{0}$ retains multiple modes of the drawing trajectory distribution, as shown in Fig. 2 . We further observe that evaluated policies can generate unseen spatial compositions of familiar objects, but struggle to draw object categories absent from training. Inspecting the resulting drawings and trajectories reveals semantic, spatial, and geometric failure patterns that are difficult to distinguish through task-success metrics alone. We further demonstrate successful real-robot drawing after fine-tuning on 100 real-robot demonstrations alone, as well as sim-to-real transfer of a policy co-trained on 13,200 simulated BURGER demonstrations.

Although robotic drawing simplifies the contact-rich interaction and object dynamics involved in manipulation, it retains important demands on language grounding and action generation. Following a drawing instruction requires translating object descriptions and spatial relations into long-horizon continuous actions, while the range of valid drawings and stroke sequences provides a setting for evaluating multimodal behavior modeling. These capabilities are also central to language-conditioned manipulation, making BURGER a distinctive lens for examining the readiness and limitations of current VLA architectures in learning from larger and more diverse training datasets.

### 2 Related Work

Vision-language-action (VLA) models map visual observations and language instructions to robot actions. Recent VLAs broadly follow autoregressive or continuous generative paradigms. Autoregressive models, such as OpenVLA [ 14 ] , predict actions sequentially, with recent variants improving action representations, chunking, and parallel decoding [ 13 , 24 , 29 , 32 , 20 ] . Continuous generative models produce action chunks using flow matching or diffusion [ 17 ] : $\pi_{0}$ and $\pi_{0.5}$ use flow matching [ 4 , 3 ] , while RDT-1B and GR00T N1 adopt diffusion-based generation [ 19 , 2 ] . Hybrid methods such as DiffusionVLA [ 36 ] combine autoregressive reasoning with diffusion-based action generation. BURGER evaluates these paradigms under multimodal, long-horizon, and semantically rich drawing data.

Benchmarking of VLA systems has moved beyond measuring binary task success rates toward more diagnostic evaluation. Recent benchmarks evaluate VLA abilities in long-horizon reasoning, spatial and semantic grounding, robustness to scene or language perturbations, structured behavioral metrics, and memory-dependent manipulation [ 39 , 35 , 33 , 7 , 6 ] . These efforts show that similar success rates can hide different failure modes in grounding, planning, robustness, or temporal consistency. BURGER follows this capability-oriented evaluation direction, but uses language-conditioned drawing as the diagnostic domain. Unlike goal-oriented manipulation, drawing admits many valid stroke orders, geometries, and trajectories for the same prompt. This makes the more dedicated abilities directly observable from the generated canvas and rollout trajectory.

Data scaling in VLA learning has been studied in robot imitation learning and generalist policy training. Prior work analyzes how data quantity and data composition affect manipulation-policy generalization, including object and environment diversity, camera viewpoints, spatial arrangements, and dataset fragmentation [ 16 , 26 , 37 ] . BURGER considers a related scaling question in a controlled language-conditioned drawing domain.

Robotic drawing and painting have been studied primarily as a control and planning problem. For example, Lee et al. [ 15 ] formulate robotic sketching as a deep decoupled hierarchical reinforcement learning problem, where separate policies learn stroke-based rendering and motor control from scratch. FRIDA [ 27 ] develops a collaborative robot painting system with a differentiable real2sim2real planning environment, enabling a robot to generate paintings from high-level human inputs such as language descriptions or reference images. More recently, IMPASTO [ 34 ] studies robotic oil-painting reproduction by integrating learned pixel dynamics models with model-based planning. These works advance robotic drawing by improving stroke planning, motor control, and real-world execution. In contrast, our work studies sketching as a language-conditioned VLA benchmark.


### 3 Method

> Figure 3: Real-world deployment of BURGER. Left: The Franka Panda hardware setup equipped with a drawing tool. Right: The real-robot data collection process for a single task “birthday cake” and a compositional task “moon left sailboat”.


#### 3.1 Task Formulation

At time $t$ , a policy receives a language instruction $\ell$ , a canvas observation $I_{t}$ , and robot proprioception $q_{t}$ , and predicts an end-effector command $a_{t}$ (or an action chunk, depending on the model). The task is to produce the requested object or spatial composition through sequential drawing actions. The policy is not given a target sketch at inference. Canvas observations change as strokes accumulate, while pen-lifting transitions separate disconnected strokes. The benchmark therefore involves both drawing geometry and decisions about when to move without marking the canvas.


#### 3.2 Prompt-to-Trajectory Data Generation

> Figure 4: Cross-simulator deployment of the BURGER trajectory-generation pipeline. The same SVG drawing task is executed in SAPIEN, MuJoCo, and Isaac Sim using corresponding robot-control interfaces.

Figure 1 summarizes our prompt-to-trajectory pipeline. Diffusion-based image generation models have shown strong capabilities in producing diverse visual content from text prompts [ 38 ] . We use GPT-Image 2 [ 22 ] to generate sketch candidates and convert them into ordered SVG strokes; Appendix A.2 describes the image-processing, skeletonization, and stroke-ordering steps. We replay each SVG in ManiSkill [ 30 ] using an end-effector pose controller, lifting the pen between disconnected strokes. We record RGB observations, robot proprioception, and actions as observation-action pairs, then convert the trajectories to the robot-dataset format. Although our main experiments use ManiSkill with the SAPIEN backend, we also implement the pipeline in MuJoCo and Isaac Sim, as shown in Figure 4 .


#### 3.3 The BURGER Dataset

To collect representative and diverse drawing trajectories, we adopt the setting from the Google Quick, Draw! [ 10 ] dataset, which contains 345 object classes and millions of human-drawn sketches. We select 132 classes that are commonly found in the real world and can be easily drawn by a robot arm. We further define 150 compositional classes by pairing two of the 100 base classes with spatial relations. Among them, 100 classes use left-right relations, while the remaining 50 use vertical relations such as “over” and “under”. For each class, we generate 100 trajectories using the pipeline described above, resulting in 15,000 compositional trajectories and 28,200 trajectories in total.

To quantitatively characterize the advantage of BURGER in providing multimodal trajectory data, we compare its instruction-conditioned trajectory diversity with LIBERO, as visualized in Figure 2 . Since BURGER focuses on drawing rather than full-space manipulation, we cluster planar end-effector trajectories on the drawing plane with a shared codebook and estimate the effective number of modes from the cluster frequencies. We use $k=24$ for the reported results and observe consistently higher multimodality for BURGER across different choices of $k$ . Under this setting, LIBERO contains $1.09$ effective modes per instruction on average, compared with $7.13$ for BURGER, corresponding to a $6.6\times$ increase. The same trajectory representation and clustering procedure is also used to analyze policy rollouts in Sec. 4.3 , and more details are provided in Appendix A.4 .

We also collect real-robot trajectories to evaluate whether the BURGER data pipeline can support real-world policy learning. The real-robot dataset follows the same data format as the simulated dataset and includes two RealSense camera views from the front and back of the workspace; either view can be used as the visual observation input. Figure 3 shows the hardware setup and real-robot data-collection process. The same pipeline supports both single-object and compositional drawing tasks, as illustrated by the “birthday cake” and “moon left of sailboat” examples. In this work, we collect 10 single-object classes with 10 demonstrations per class for real-robot training and evaluation.

The dataset is, to our knowledge, the first large-scale VLA dataset for language-conditioned drawing, and we hope it can serve as a valuable resource for future research on VLA model generalization.

#### 3.4 Quantifying Robot Trajectory Quality

Valid drawings can differ in geometry and stroke order, making drawing quality difficult to evaluate with image-text similarity alone. Contrastive metrics such as CLIPScore [ 11 ] measure global image–text alignment, but prior work [ ahmadi2023robustness ] shows that such metrics can fail to detect fine-grained errors.

To obtain a more human-aligned and fine-grained evaluation metric, we design a contrastive feature-based evaluator trained directly on the BURGER drawing distribution. Our evaluator builds a sketch-specific metric space on top of a frozen DINOv3 backbone [ 28 ] , which allows us to score each drawing by its similarity to the target-class prototype. For compositional tasks, we separate the two objects at the minimum-connectivity boundary and evaluate their identities and spatial relation. Appendix B.2 provides the metric architecture, training objectives, and scoring procedure. In a user study covering 10 classes, our metric agrees more closely with human judgments than CLIPScore across Spearman correlation, Kendall’s $\tau_{b}$ , and pairwise agreement. Details are also provided in Appendix B.2 .


### 4 Experimental Results

Our experiments evaluate the capabilities and limitations of VLA models on language-conditioned robotic drawing tasks in BURGER. Concretely, we investigate three questions: (1) whether BURGER can serve as a diagnostic benchmark for comparing VLA models across several dimensions, including data scaling, language grounding, spatial-relation understanding, and multimodal behavior modeling; (2) whether the benchmark can expose characteristic failure behaviors and help diagnose differences among VLA formulations; and (3) whether policies trained with BURGER data can transfer to real-world drawing and support closed-loop interaction under human modifications.

Baselines. We evaluate representative VLA models with different action-generation formulations. $\boldsymbol{\pi}_{0}$ [ 4 ] uses a separate action expert to generate continuous actions using flow matching. $\boldsymbol{\pi}_{0.5}$ [ 3 ] extends $\pi_{0}$ with co-training on heterogeneous tasks for broader generalization. $\boldsymbol{\pi}_{0}$ -FAST is an autoregressive VLA based on the FAST action tokenizer [ 24 ] . OpenVLA [ 14 ] is an open-source autoregressive VLA model. OpenVLA-OFT [ 13 ] is an optimized fine-tuning variant of OpenVLA that predicts continuous action chunks with parallel decoding, rather than predicting step-wise discrete action tokens.

We evaluate the $\pi$ -family VLA models across different task scales, varying the number of object classes from $C=10$ to $C=132$ and the number of trajectories per class from $T=10$ to $T=100$ , as shown in Figure 6 . Drawing performance is evaluated using the DINOv3-based target similarity metric introduced in Sec. 3.4 . Overall, increasing either the number of training classes or the number of trajectories per class generally improves drawing performance, particularly for $\pi_{0}$ and $\pi_{0.5}$ . However, the scaling behavior is not uniform across models: $\pi_{0}$ -FAST shows larger fluctuations and does not consistently benefit from increasing data scale. These results suggest that BURGER provides a controlled setting for studying how different VLA formulations respond to increasing task diversity and demonstration scale.


#### 4.2 Language-conditioned Generalization

> Figure 7: Learning multimodal drawing behaviors in BURGER. Top: different drawing patterns of “helmet” within the dataset, Bottom: the generated images by $\pi_{0.5}$ unseen in the dataset.

We diagnose language grounding and semantic generalization across the $\pi$ -family models along two dimensions. First, we evaluate language-conditioned instruction following by varying the object names in the prompts. Starting from the same blank canvas, different language instructions lead the policies to generate different objects corresponding to the requested categories. Across the in-distribution tasks, we observe no evident confusion between objects. In contrast, when the instruction specifies an out-of-distribution object category, the models fail to generate the corresponding object. This suggests that the learned language grounding is effective for the trained semantic categories but does not directly generalize to unseen object concepts.

Second, we evaluate spatial understanding using compositional drawing tasks with prompts of the form “draw A on the left of B”, “draw A under B”, and “draw A over B”. Our goal is to study whether VLA models can generalize simple spatial relations beyond memorized object compositions. For this evaluation, policies are trained on these compositional tasks. As shown in the right-most column in Figure 5 , VLA models can generate valid left-right and over-under object compositions even for object pairs that are not observed during training. For example, a policy can draw a cup to the left of an apple despite never being trained on this specific composition. We also observe that the policies correctly distinguish different spatial instructions. These results suggest that the policies can generalize beyond memorized object compositions and apply learned spatial relations to unseen object pairs.


#### 4.3 VLA Models Capture Multimodal Drawing Behaviors

Sketch generation naturally admits multimodal trajectories for the same language instruction. As quantified in Sec. 3.3 and visualized in Figure 2 , BURGER exhibits substantially stronger within-instruction multimodality than LIBERO. We therefore use it to evaluate whether VLA policies can learn and preserve such multimodality after training.

We first evaluate this quantitatively by applying the same trajectory representation and clustering procedure to $\pi_{0}$ rollouts. Across the eight representative tasks shown in Figure 2 , the training demonstrations exhibit $4.0$ – $7.7$ effective trajectory modes per instruction, while the policy rollouts exhibit $2.1$ – $6.7$ modes. These results show that $\pi_{0}$ can preserve multimodality in its rollout distribution after training on BURGER.

We further examine this behavior qualitatively in Figure 7 . The first two rows show distinct demonstrations of the same object class, helmet , with variations in geometry, stroke ordering, and execution path. After training on these demonstrations, $\pi_{0.5}$ produces multiple valid helmet drawings under the same instruction. Together, the trajectory-level and visual results show that BURGER supports learning a distribution of valid drawing behaviors rather than a single deterministic route.


#### 4.4 BURGER Reveals Fine-Grained VLA Failure Modes

> Figure 8: Representative failure modes in BURGER. Left: OpenVLA and $\pi_{0}$ -FAST exhibit sensitivity to the initial end-effector position. Middle: $\pi_{0}$ and $\pi_{0.5}$ show failure to terminate. Right: $\pi$ -family models produce generic, unstructured strokes on unseen-object instructions.

Different VLA models exhibit distinct failure patterns, which BURGER can expose through process-level analysis of generated trajectories and drawings. Figure 8 shows some general failure cases.

Single-Object Tasks. OpenVLA and $\boldsymbol{\pi}_{0}$ -FAST are sensitive to the initial end-effector position: OpenVLA may become trapped in a local region, while $\boldsymbol{\pi}_{0}$ -FAST can drift away from the intended drawing area. In contrast, flow-matching policies sometimes fail to terminate after completing the object, leading to overdraw. For unseen-object instructions, the policies tend to produce generic, unstructured strokes.

Compositional Tasks. Object quality may degrade under compositional instructions even when the spatial relation is correct. This degradation becomes more evident when the instruction space expands from horizontal to both horizontal and vertical relations, suggesting that relational grounding and object-level geometry become harder to preserve jointly.

> Figure 9: Controlled circle-drawing results across representative VLA models, revealing distinct failure patterns in contact, trajectory progress, and multimodal behavior.

To further diagnose these differences, we construct a controlled circle-drawing case study with 100 demonstrations sharing the same shape and radius, while varying the circle center, starting point, and drawing direction. Table 1 summarizes contact, clockwise/counterclockwise (CW/CCW) direction, starting-point diversity, and angular sweep. As shown in Figure 9 , OpenVLA often maintains contact but stagnates in small local circles, producing excessive angular motion without stable progress along the target trajectory. OpenVLA-OFT with chunk length 1 frequently fails to establish stable contact, while increasing the chunk length to 8 substantially improves contact and trajectory coherence, indicating a clear effect of action chunking on long-horizon execution.

However, coherent execution does not guarantee faithful coverage of the demonstrated behavior distribution. OpenVLA-OFT and $\pi_{0}$ -FAST tend to concentrate on a subset of starting points or drawing directions, while the flow-matching policies better preserve trajectory diversity. In particular, $\pi_{0}$ -FAST strongly favors one drawing direction despite maintaining relatively accurate circle geometry. Both OpenVLA-OFT and $\pi_{0}$ -FAST also exhibit overdraw, continuing beyond the intended single revolution; similar patterns recur across different rollout seeds in Fig. 9 , suggesting a small set of stereotyped failure behaviors. Overall, these results indicate that action chunking mainly affects execution coherence, while the action-generation formulation also shapes geometric fidelity and multimodal mode coverage. This case study illustrates how the BURGER pipeline enables controlled diagnosis of model-specific failure patterns.

#### 4.5 Real Robot Results

> Figure 10: Real-robot drawing results. The first row shows robustness to camera-angle changes; the second shows visual feedback through object completion and human intervention; the third shows multimodal real-robot behaviors.

Using the real-robot demonstrations introduced in Sec. 3.3 , we fine-tune $\pi_{0.5}$ directly on data collected with the Franka Panda platform. As shown in Figure 10 , the resulting policy can reliably execute real-world drawing tasks, demonstrating that the BURGER data-collection pipeline can directly support real-world policy learning. The first row further shows that the policy remains effective under changes in camera viewpoint, indicating moderate robustness to visual observation variations.

We further examine closed-loop visual feedback through completion and interaction tasks. Given a partially completed cup, the policy can add the missing handle. In the bicycle example, after a human draws the rear wheel, the robot observes the updated canvas, skips the completed component, and continues with the remaining structure. The third row additionally shows that the policy can produce different valid trajectories for the same drawing task, preserving multimodal behavior on the real robot. Together, these results demonstrate visual closed-loop interaction and multimodal real-world execution.


### 5 Conclusion and Limitations

We present BURGER, a language-conditioned robotic drawing benchmark that supports scalable generation of diverse robot trajectories and fine-grained evaluation of semantic grounding, spatial reasoning, multimodal behavior modeling, and action generation. Our experiments reveal how representative VLA models respond to increasing data scale and task diversity, while exposing distinct failure patterns beyond binary task success. Real-robot experiments further demonstrate drawing with visual feedback, including responses to human-added strokes.

Although BURGER provides a scalable and diagnostic testbed for evaluating language-conditioned robotic drawing, several limitations remain in our benchmark. First, our setup abstracts away many physical complexities of painting. Unlike robotic painting systems that model brush dynamics [ 34 , 27 ] , BURGER focuses on sketch-level stroke generation with a drawing tool. This simplification makes the benchmark scalable, but limits its coverage of contact-rich artistic media such as brush painting. Second, the dataset may inherit biases from the automatic generation pipeline. Our stroke-order heuristics may introduce drawing styles that differ from human demonstrations. As a result, the dataset may not fully represent natural human drawing behaviors.

#### A.1 Prompting and Sketch Generation

PNG Generation. We use GPT-Image 2 [ 22 ] to generate sketch images from language prompts. To improve generation efficiency, each query produces a $4\times 4$ grid of subfigures, which is later split into individual PNG sketches. The image-generation prompt is shown in Figure 11 . We substitute the object name into a fixed template, while optionally varying the diversity instruction to obtain multiple minimal line-art candidates. The generated grid image is subsequently split into individual PNG sketches.

Prompt sent to the image generator: Create one image containing exactly 16 ultra-simple black line-art <object> icons arranged in a clean $4$ by $4$ grid. Each cell contains one centered icon with generous white space. All icons must be recognizable as the same object category, and their basic outer shape should remain mostly consistent. Every icon should still look slightly different through small pose, size, angle, or detail changes. Important view constraint: regardless of pose or angle, the object’s front/main recognizable face must be mostly visible. Avoid rear views, back views, extreme side views, hidden fronts, heavy occlusion, or cropped-off main faces. Make diversity come from combining several simple differences, without adding line complexity. Use clear size variation across the grid, but keep every icon isolated and easy to cut out. For animals, combine pose, slight front-facing angle, head direction, tail/ear shape, and body proportion changes. For objects and still lifes, combine front-facing orientation, silhouette, placement, proportion, and one tiny detail change. Use these variation types: small silhouette differences, slightly different sizes, small pose or action changes for animals, small orientation or placement changes for objects and still lifes, slight viewing angle changes, and one tiny contour/detail change. Avoid repeating the same design twice. Do not create very different object types, complex shapes, dense internal lines, realistic texture, horizontal compositions, extreme tall shapes, extreme wide shapes, and extra props. Hard line-count constraint: each icon should be composed of only 5 or 6 total simple strokes/lines whenever possible. Use only the outer contour plus at most 1 or 2 tiny internal detail lines. Avoid many details, many small marks, texture, shading, filling, decorations, dense internal lines, or line-heavy patterns. Each icon should be a clean minimal outline, easy to cut out, with a compact simple shape. Black lines only, white background, no color, no shading, no filled areas except tiny solid black dots used only for animal eyes as specified below, no text, no watermark, no extra objects. Each icon should fit comfortably inside an imaginary square cell with similar margins on all sides. Category-specific rules: For animals, always draw the full whole body in each cell—never head-only, face crop, or partial-body views; every animal must have clearly visible eyes (never omit eyes). For food, never show bites or eaten/missing chunks; do not draw plates, bowls, or other dishware; if the food has cream, frosting, or similar topping, keep the same topping type and overall shape across every icon (only its scale or amount may vary slightly). For furniture, clothing, or animals, every icon must stay the same specific type within that category (one furniture piece kind, one garment kind, or one animal kind—do not mix different types). For animals whose eyes would normally be a large solid black patch or filled black area, render each eye as a single small black dot instead. For simple symbolic or single-form subjects with only one natural type (for example diamond, envelope, eye, star, or heart), keep that exact same shape and meaning in every cell—do not invent alternate variants— and create variety only through slight rotation, tilt, mirroring, or small in-plane skew. Output: one PNG image containing a $4\times 4$ grid of minimal sketch icons.

These properties make them well suited for downstream conversion into robot-executable drawing trajectories. Moreover, because our pipeline is prompt-driven, it can scalably generate diverse sketch data conditioned on different object categories and language instructions.

We use a count-matched setting of 40 instructions and approximately 42 episodes per instruction. Each trajectory is resampled to 32 points and represented by its start-relative $xy$ path and step vectors. A shared $k$ -means codebook is fitted on the pooled trajectory descriptors, and the effective number of trajectory modes is defined as

[Tableau] $$N_{\mathrm{eff}}=\exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\log p_{j}\right),$$ (1)

where $p_{j}$ denotes the fraction of trajectories assigned to cluster $j$ .

We resample each trajectory to 32 uniformly spaced points in normalized time and concatenate its start-relative planar positions and successive displacements into a 126-dimensional descriptor. After standardization using pooled statistics, we fit a shared $k$ -means codebook with $k$ -means++ initialization. Cluster frequencies are computed within each instruction to obtain $N_{\mathrm{eff}}(\ell;k)$ , then averaged equally over instructions. BURGER consistently exceeds LIBERO across ten random initializations, supporting the robustness of its greater instruction-conditioned trajectory diversity across the tested clustering resolutions.


### Appendix B Experimental Details


#### B.1 VLA Post-Training Protocol

Our dataset is stored in the LeRobot format [ 5 ] . Each training sample consists of a language instruction, the current canvas observation, the robot proprioceptive state, and the corresponding expert action. The language instruction follows the task prompt used during data generation, e.g., “draw an apple” for single-object tasks and “draw an apple to the left of a cup” for compositional tasks. For visual input, we use the rendered canvas observation from the simulator. The action target is the robot end-effector command recorded during SVG replay, as described in the SVG-to-trajectory pipeline in the main paper.

We keep the post-training protocol consistent across models whenever possible. For $\pi_{0}$ and $\pi_{0.5}$ , we fine-tune the continuous-action policy using their original flow-matching action objective. For $\pi_{0}$ -FAST, actions are first converted into FAST tokens and the model is trained with the autoregressive action-token prediction objective. We use a batch size of $32$ for all models. All models are evaluated without additional task-specific adaptation after post-training.

During evaluation, the policy receives the language prompt, the current canvas observation, and the robot proprioceptive state, and predicts an end-effector command or action chunk. An episode terminates when the robot end-effector is lifted above a predefined height threshold; therefore, the policy must learn both the drawing behavior and the appropriate termination behavior. We use the same initial-state distribution and maximum episode length across models to ensure a fair comparison. For single-object evaluation, we use 10 object classes: alarm clock, apple, backpack, banana, bathtub, bench, bicycle, birthday cake, blueberry, and book. For compositional evaluation, we test on unseen spatial compositions. For each task, we run 10 evaluation episodes and report the average score computed by our evaluation metric. The final canvas is used for visual evaluation, while the full trajectory is used for analyzing failure modes and real-robot transfer.


#### B.2 Rollout Evaluation

Latent-based Similarity Metric. We construct a sketch-specific metric space on top of a frozen DINOv3 backbone. Given a rendered canvas image $I$ , DINOv3 produces a CLS token, which provides a global image representation, and a sequence of patch embeddings, which preserve local visual details. We concatenate the CLS token with the averaged patch embeddings and pass the resulting feature through a lightweight two-layer MLP projection head with output dimension $256$ , followed by $\ell_{2}$ normalization. This yields a normalized sketch embedding $z(I)\in\mathbb{R}^{256}$ .

The embedding $z(I)$ is then passed to a cosine classifier. For class $c$ , the classifier maintains a learnable class weight $w_{c}$ and computes the logit as a scaled cosine similarity:

[Tableau] $$\ell_{i,c}=\gamma z_{i}^{\top}\frac{w_{c}}{\|w_{c}\|_{2}},$$

where $z_{i}=z(I_{i})$ and $\gamma$ is a learnable logit scale.

We train the projection head and cosine classifier using a combination of cross-entropy classification and supervised contrastive learning:

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SupCon}},$$ (2)

where

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}$ $\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\log\frac{\exp(\ell_{i,y_{i}})}{\sum_{c=1}^{C}\exp(\ell_{i,c})},$ (3) $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SupCon}}$ $\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{|P(i)|}\sum_{p\in P(i)}\log\frac{\exp(z_{i}^{\top}z_{p}/\tau)}{\sum_{a\neq i}\exp(z_{i}^{\top}z_{a}/\tau)}.$ (4)

Here, $B$ is the batch size, $C$ is the number of object classes, $y_{i}$ is the ground-truth class label for image $I_{i}$ , $\tau$ is the contrastive temperature, $P(i)$ denotes the set of same-class positives for anchor $i$ , and $\mathcal{A}=\{i:|P(i)|>0\}$ is the set of valid anchors. The cross-entropy term makes the embedding predictive of the object category, while the supervised contrastive term encourages drawings from the same class to cluster together and drawings from different classes to separate in the learned metric space. During training, the DINOv3 backbone is kept frozen, and only the projection head and cosine classifier are optimized.

After training, we compute one class center for each object class using the training split. For class $c$ with training drawings $\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}_{c}$ , its class center is

[Tableau] $$q_{c}=\frac{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}_{c}|}\sum_{I_{i}\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}_{c}}z(I_{i})}{\left\|\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}_{c}|}\sum_{I_{i}\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{train}}_{c}}z(I_{i})\right\|_{2}}.$$

For a generated drawing with target class $y$ , we compare its normalized embedding with the corresponding class center:

[Tableau] $$s(I,y)=z(I)^{\top}q_{y}.$$

We use this target-class similarity as the evaluation signal. A higher similarity indicates that the generated drawing is closer to the target object class in the learned sketch embedding space.

For single-object tasks, we score the final canvas image by its similarity to the class center of the prompted object (i.e., $s(I,y)$ ). For compositional tasks, we first split the foreground strokes into object regions using a vertical or horizontal separator with the lowest foreground-pixel density, evaluate each region against its corresponding object class center, and average the two similarities. We further penalize predictions whose detected ordering does not match the prompted spatial relation.

Correlation with Human Judgments. We conduct a user study on 10 single-object classes to evaluate whether our latent-based similarity metric is aligned with human judgments. For each class, 66 participants are shown five drawings: four model-generated drawings produced by two VLA models and one ground-truth drawing from our dataset. Participants are asked to rank the five drawings from best to worst according to how well each drawing depicts the prompted object class. We then evaluate the same drawings using our DINOv3-based [ 28 ] similarity metric and CLIPScore [ 11 ] .

To quantify alignment with human judgments, we compute three complementary measures. First, we use Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient and Kendall $\tau_{b}$ correlation coefficient to compare the aggregate human ranking with rankings induced by each metric. For each drawing, the aggregate human score is computed as the negative mean human rank, so that larger values correspond to better human preference. Kendall’s $\tau_{b}$ is used because it accounts for tied ranks, which occur frequently for CLIPScore due to score saturation. Second, we compute pairwise agreement by comparing each participant’s preference over every within-class pair of drawings with the corresponding pairwise preference induced by the metric. Thus, Spearman’s $\rho$ and Kendall’s $\tau_{b}$ evaluate agreement with the aggregate human ranking, while pairwise agreement measures consistency with individual human judgments.

As shown in Table 4 , our latent-based similarity metric achieves stronger correlation with human judgments than CLIPScore across all three measures. Specifically, our metric achieves a Spearman correlation of $0.834$ , Kendall’s $\tau_{b}$ of $0.655$ , and pairwise agreement of $0.875$ , outperforming CLIPScore, which obtains $0.592$ , $0.433$ , and $0.803$ , respectively. These results suggest that the proposed latent-based similarity metric provides a practical and human-aligned proxy for the evaluation of language-conditioned drawing policies.

#### B.3 Real-Robot Implementation

Inference. For real-robot inference, we first align the real camera observations with the simulated canvas coordinate system. Our setup uses two Intel RealSense cameras, placed at the front and back of the workspace, to reduce occlusion caused by the robot arm. To extract the drawing strokes on the canvas, we use depth observations to segment out the robot arm and then estimate the visible stroke regions from each camera view. The stroke observations from the two views are merged and provided as visual input to the trained VLA policy. The policy outputs target TCP positions, which are executed by the Franka Panda robot arm.

Human–Robot Interaction. Our perception pipeline also supports human–robot collaborative drawing. During execution, depth observations are used to detect when a human hand enters the canvas region. When a hand is detected, policy inference is temporarily paused while the user modifies the drawing, either by adding new strokes or erasing existing ones. After the user leaves the workspace, the updated canvas is captured and processed using the same stroke-extraction and multi-view merging pipeline described above. The resulting canvas representation is then provided to the VLA policy as a new observation, and the robot resumes drawing conditioned on the updated canvas state. This procedure enables iterative collaboration between a human user and the robot within a shared drawing workspace.
