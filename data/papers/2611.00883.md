## Random Is Almost Enough? Stable and Low-Cost Benchmark Compression via BestRand

Benchmark compression reduces the often prohibitive time and cost of model evaluation by evaluating models on a small benchmark subset that closely matches full-benchmark results. To quantify estimation error, we derive an accurate analytical approximation to the error distribution when estimating full-benchmark scores from a subset, showing that most subsets yield small errors, while a few produce much larger errors, resulting in a long-tailed distribution. This means random sampling alone can achieve surprisingly strong performance with high probability, but remains unstable due to rare large errors. We further show that this instability is inherent to modern foundation models and persists even as more models are evaluated. Based on these findings, we introduce BestRand, a stable and low-cost benchmark compression method that preserves the efficiency of random sampling while substantially reducing its high-error tail. We validate BestRand on 70+ text, multimodal, and agent benchmarks, and further extend it to VLA evaluation. Compared with competitive methods, BestRand reduces high-error risk by 50.2% and estimation error by 16.4% on average, while requiring only 51.3% of the construction cost. It further reduces OOD estimation error by 21.8% on average across score-range, model-family, and temporal shifts. Together, our theory provides practical guidance for understanding and predicting the accuracy and reliability of benchmark compression, while BestRand enables stable and accurate compression at low cost. The code and data are publicly available on GitHub .


### 1 Introduction

Evaluating large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) on benchmarks can require substantial time and compute ( Polo et al., 2024 ) , as these benchmarks often contain many test examples and may also include redundant ones ( Zhang et al., 2025b ) . Benchmark compression reduces this burden by evaluating models on a small subset to approximate full-benchmark results ( Vivek et al., 2024 ) . Yet the accuracy and reliability of this approximation are not well characterized.

To quantify estimation error, we derive an accurate analytical approximation to the error distribution when using a subset to estimate full-benchmark scores ( Truong et al., 2025 ) . Our theoretical and empirical results show that most subsets yield small errors, while a few produce much larger errors, resulting in a long-tailed distribution. This means random sampling alone can achieve surprisingly strong performance with high probability ( Saranathan et al., 2025 ) , but remains unstable due to rare large errors ( Huang et al., 2025b ) . We further explain this high-probability effectiveness and show that the instability arises from the similarity among LLMs and VLMs and persists even as more models are evaluated. Beyond explaining these phenomena, our theory quantifies the relationship between subset size and estimation error ( Zhang et al., 2025a ) , guiding the balance between evaluation accuracy and cost ( Perlitz et al., 2024 ) . Across all theoretical results, numerical experiments consistently agree with our theory. Figure 1 and Appendix F provide extensive numerical validation.

Motivated by our theory, we introduce BestRand, a stable and low-cost benchmark compression method that preserves the efficiency of random sampling while substantially reducing its high-error tail. BestRand generates multiple random subsets and uses a few probe models to search for the best candidate. Our theory identifies a mechanism by which probe-guided selection can reduce common-shift variability, motivating BestRand’s design. Experiments demonstrate its improved MAE stability. We validate BestRand on 70+ text, multimodal, and agent benchmarks, and further extend it to VLA evaluation. Against competitive methods, BestRand reduces high-error risk by 50.2% and estimation error by 16.4% on average, while requiring only 51.3% of the construction cost. It further reduces OOD estimation error by 21.8% on average across score-range, model-family, and temporal shifts. Together, our theory provides a framework for understanding and guiding benchmark compression, while BestRand turns these insights into a stable and low-cost method.

We discuss related works in Appendix G and summarize our contributions as follows:

- • Theory. We derive an accurate analytical approximation to the error distribution for benchmark compression, explaining why random sampling is effective with high probability yet remains unstable. We further identify inherent similarity among foundation models as a primary source of this instability. Extensive numerical experiments are highly consistent with our theory.

- • Method. We propose BestRand, a stable and low-cost benchmark compression method that improves random sampling through lightweight probe-guided search, with theoretical support for improved stability. We validate BestRand on 70+ text, multimodal, and agent benchmarks, as well as VLA evaluation, showing strong accuracy, stability, and OOD generalization at low cost.

### 2 Theory-inspired Method: BestRand

In this section, we combine theoretical analysis with empirical evidence to explain why random sampling often achieves strong compression performance yet remains unstable. Building on these insights, we introduce BestRand to mitigate this instability while preserving the efficiency.

Preliminaries . Let $X\in[0,1]^{N\times M}$ be a fixed matrix for $N$ items and $M$ models, whose entry $X_{s,m}$ is the score of model $m$ on item $s$ . All randomness in our sampling analysis comes from drawing a subset $\mathcal{S}$ of $k$ items uniformly without replacement, where $1\leq k\leq N$ . The full-benchmark score, subset estimate, and error of model $m$ are

[Tableau] $$\mu_{m}=(1/N)\sum\nolimits_{s=1}^{N}X_{s,m},\qquad\hat{\mu}_{m}=(1/k)\sum\nolimits_{s\in\mathcal{S}}X_{s,m},\qquad E_{m}=\hat{\mu}_{m}-\mu_{m}.$$ (1)

Our primary quantity is the mean absolute error $\mathrm{MAE}=M^{-1}\sum\nolimits_{m=1}^{M}|E_{m}|$ of the error vector $E=(E_{1},\dots,E_{M})^{\top}$ . Subscripts $\mathcal{S}$ and $N$ indicate subset and full-benchmark averages. We write $\mathrm{Var}$ for sampling variance, $\mathrm{Var}_{N}$ for empirical variance over items with denominator $N$ , and $\Phi$ for the standard normal cumulative distribution function.

Define item difficulty as $g_{s}=M^{-1}\sum\nolimits_{m=1}^{M}X_{s,m}$ , with larger scores indicating easier items. Its full-benchmark mean and variance are $\bar{g}_{N}=N^{-1}\sum\nolimits_{s=1}^{N}g_{s}$ and $\mathrm{Var}_{N}(g)=N^{-1}\sum\nolimits_{s=1}^{N}(g_{s}-\bar{g}_{N})^{2}$ . The common shift $\bar{E}=M^{-1}\sum\nolimits_{m=1}^{M}E_{m}=\bar{g}_{\mathcal{S}}-\bar{g}_{N}$ , where $\bar{g}_{\mathcal{S}}=k^{-1}\sum\nolimits_{s\in\mathcal{S}}g_{s}$ , measures whether the subset is easier or harder on average. Its sampling standard deviation defines our central scale,

[Tableau] $$s:=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\bar{E})}=c(k)\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{N}(g)},\qquad c(k)=\sqrt{(N-k)/[k(N-1)]}.$$ (2)

The finite-population factor $c(k)$ grows as fewer items are retained and satisfies $c(N)=0$ .

Assumption 1: Consider non-degenerate models whose scores vary across test items. For binary scores, this excludes models that answer all items incorrectly or all items correctly. Define the population score covariance matrix $C\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}$ and its relation to sampling errors by

[Tableau] $$C_{ml}=\frac{1}{N}\sum\nolimits_{s=1}^{N}(X_{s,m}-\mu_{m})(X_{s,l}-\mu_{l}),\qquad\mathrm{Cov}(E)=c(k)^{2}C.$$ (3)

Assume $C_{mm}=\sigma^{2}>0$ and $C_{ml}=\rho\sigma^{2}$ for $m\neq l$ , with $\rho\in[0,1)$ . Then the common-shift scale simplifies to $s=c(k)\,\sigma\sqrt{\rho+(1-\rho)/M}$ . Empirically, $\sigma^{2}$ and $\rho$ are read directly from $C$ as the mean diagonal entry and the mean normalized off-diagonal entry, $\sigma^{2}=M^{-1}\sum\nolimits_{m}C_{mm}$ and $\rho=[M(M-1)]^{-1}\sum\nolimits_{m\neq l}C_{ml}/\sigma^{2}$ , so $\rho$ is the average pairwise correlation of the model errors.

Assumption 2: Let $x_{s}=(x_{s,1},\dots,x_{s,p})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}$ contain the scores of $p$ probe models on item $s$ , with means $\bar{x}_{\mathcal{S}}=k^{-1}\sum\nolimits_{s\in\mathcal{S}}x_{s}$ and $\bar{x}_{N}=N^{-1}\sum\nolimits_{s=1}^{N}x_{s}$ . Regress difficulty on probe scores by least squares with intercept $\alpha\in\mathbb{R}$ , coefficients $\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}$ , and residual $e_{s}$ . We assume $\mathrm{Var}_{N}(g)>0$ and positive explained variance,

[Tableau] $$g_{s}=\alpha+\beta^{\top}x_{s}+e_{s},\qquad R^{2}:=\mathrm{Var}_{N}(\beta^{\top}x)/\mathrm{Var}_{N}(g)>0.$$ (4)

Derivations are provided in Appendix F , and empirical checks across numerous benchmarks in Appendix F.2 support these assumptions as useful approximations rather than exact descriptions of the data. The resulting theoretical predictions closely match empirical observations (e.g., Figure 1 ), further supporting the value of these simplifications for tractable analysis.


#### 2.1 Why Random Sampling is almost enough for compression but unstable?

In this Section, Theorem 2.1 fixes the shape of the error distribution and its tail, and show why the random sampling is unstable. Theorem 2.1 helps explain why random sampling may be almost enough in many cases: for large $M$ , the mode of the MAE distribution lies close to the lower endpoint of its limiting distribution. Proofs are in Appendix F .

Theorem 1. Under Assumption 2 , when the effective sample size $\min\{k,N-k\}$ is large, the error vector is approximately zero-mean multivariate normal:

[Tableau] $$E\ \overset{\cdot}{\sim}\ \mathcal{N}\big(0,\,c(k)^{2}C\big),\qquad\mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{M}\sum\nolimits_{m=1}^{M}|E_{m}|.$$ (5)

Moreover, $\mathrm{MAE}\geq|\bar{E}|$ holds for every sampled subset, and the common shift $\bar{E}=M^{-1}\sum\nolimits_{m}E_{m}$ is approximately $\mathcal{N}(0,s^{2})$ . Hence, for every threshold $t>0$ ,

[Tableau] $$\Pr(\mathrm{MAE}>t)\ \geq\ \Pr(|\bar{E}|>t)\ \approx\ 2\,\Phi\!\Big(-\tfrac{t}{s}\Big),$$ (6)

where $s=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\bar{E})}$ is the common-shift scale defined above.

Why random sampling is unstable. Instability means that a single random draw occasionally lands far from the true scores, which is exactly the right tail of the MAE distribution. Theorem 2.1 controls this tail through its lower bound $\Pr(\mathrm{MAE}>t)\geq 2\Phi(-t/s)$ , whose only free quantity is the scale $s$ . At any fixed threshold $t$ , a larger $s$ pushes $-t/s$ toward zero, and because $\Phi$ is increasing it raises the probability of a large error. A larger $s$ therefore raises this lower bound on high-error risk, so it remains to ask what makes $s$ large. Under Assumption 2 , $s=c(k)\sigma\sqrt{\rho+(1-\rho)/M}$ . For $M>1$ , the term under the square root increases with the behavioral correlation $\rho$ , since its derivative is $1-1/M>0$ . Stronger correlation therefore increases $s$ and raises the lower bound on the probability of large errors. Increasing the number of evaluated models reduces the contribution $(1-\rho)/M$ , but cannot eliminate the correlation-dependent component: for fixed $k<N$ and $\rho>0$ , $s\to c(k)\sigma\sqrt{\rho}>0$ as $M\to\infty$ . Thus, expanding the model panel alone cannot remove this source of instability. Numerical experiments across diverse benchmarks in Figure 1 provide empirical support for this analysis based on Theorem 2.1 .

For foundation models, such behavioral correlation is closely tied to how the models are built and trained. Language, vision-language, and action models often share similar or identical backbones and overlapping training corpora, while knowledge distillation can further align their behavior. These shared design and training choices can lead models to succeed and fail on the same items, sustaining a sizable $\rho$ . In this sense, random sampling instability is rooted in the shared characteristics of foundation models, rather than merely in the finite number of models evaluated. The non-vanishing limit above describes a consequence of this intrinsic correlation: evaluating more similarly constructed models does not average away their shared errors.

Theorem 2. Under Assumption 2 and Theorem 2.1 with fixed $c(k)>0$ , $\sigma>0$ , and $0<\rho<1$ , the lower endpoint of the limiting $\mathrm{MAE}$ distribution as $M\to\infty$ is $y_{0}=\sqrt{2/\pi}\,c(k)\,\sigma\sqrt{1-\rho}$ . This is not a lower bound for finite $M$ , where the MAE distribution can have probability mass below $y_{0}$ . The mode $\hat{y}(M)$ of the $\mathrm{MAE}$ distribution approaches $y_{0}$ at the universal rate

[Tableau] $$\frac{\hat{y}(M)}{y_{0}}-1=\frac{C_{0}}{\sqrt{M}}+O\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{M}\Big),\quad C_{0}=\sqrt{\tfrac{\pi}{2}-1}\,\arg\max_{u>0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!v^{-1/2}\varphi(u-v)\,\mathrm{d}v\approx 0.578,$$ (7)

independent of $c(k),\sigma$ and $\rho$ , with $\varphi$ the standard normal density.

Why random sampling is almost enough. Theorem 2.1 gives an asymptotic description of the low-error region: for large $M$ , the mode lies near the lower endpoint $y_{0}$ of the limiting distribution, with a leading relative gap of $C_{0}/\sqrt{M}$ . This prediction is consistent with the empirical observation in Figure 1 and more experiments are shown in Appendix F.6 , where the mode lies close to the lower end of the observed MAE distribution. Together, the asymptotic analysis and the observed concentration at small errors help explain why random sampling is often already effective. The long tail nevertheless leaves a small but non-negligible fraction of high-error draws, motivating BestRand to improve stability while preserving the low cost of random sampling.


#### 2.2 How to build more stable and low-cost benchmark compression methods.

Theorem 2.1 leaves a clear target. The asymptotic analysis and empirical observations suggest that random sampling already favors low-error subsets, so a good compressor should preserve its low cost while reducing the risk of high-error draws. BestRand does this by drawing several random candidate subsets and selecting the one that a few cheap probe models judge most representative. It runs the target models on the selected subset alone and reuses the probe scores across all candidates, so its only overhead beyond random sampling is a one-time probe pass over a small pool.

The BestRand procedure. Given a benchmark of $N$ items, a target size $k$ , $p$ probe models, a pool multiplier $n$ , and a candidate count $m$ , BestRand proceeds in four steps.

- 1. Draw a pool $P$ of $\min(nk,N)$ items uniformly without replacement and score the $p$ probe models on $P$ , recording $x_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}$ for each item $s$ .

- 2. Form the reference $H=|P|^{-1}\sum_{s\in P}x_{s}$ , the average probe score over the pool.

- 3. Repeat $m$ times: draw a candidate $k$ -subset $S_{j}$ from $P$ uniformly without replacement, and compute its probe mean $h_{j}=k^{-1}\sum_{s\in S_{j}}x_{s}$ and its imbalance $d_{j}=\|h_{j}-H\|^{2}$ .

- 4. Return $S^{*}=S_{j^{*}}$ with $j^{*}=\arg\min_{j\leq m}d_{j}$ , and evaluate every target model on $S^{*}$ alone.

The single probe pass costs $p\times|P|$ model-item evaluations, and the pool reference $H$ replaces the full-set probe mean so the probes need not run on the whole benchmark when $nk<N$ . The $m$ candidate draws in step 3 reuse the cached probe scores and add no model calls, computing only averages of cached vectors, so they run in parallel as a single batched tensor operation on a GPU and make the search essentially instant. The selected subset $S^{*}$ is reused across all target models.

Theorem 3. Under Assumptions 2 and 2 , least-squares orthogonality splits the common shift into a probe-explained part and a residual part,

[Tableau] $$\bar{E}=\bar{E}_{\parallel}+\bar{E}_{\perp},\qquad\bar{E}_{\parallel}=\beta^{\top}(\bar{x}_{\mathcal{S}}-\bar{x}_{N}),\quad\bar{E}_{\perp}=\bar{e}_{\mathcal{S}}=\tfrac{1}{k}\!\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}e_{s},$$ (8)

which are uncorrelated with variances $\mathrm{Var}(\bar{E}_{\parallel})=R^{2}s^{2}$ and $\mathrm{Var}(\bar{E}_{\perp})=(1-R^{2})s^{2}$ , so $R^{2}$ is the fraction of the common-shift variance that the probes explain. Draw $m$ candidates independently, each uniformly among the $k$ -subsets of the full benchmark, and select the candidate with the smallest full-reference probe imbalance, breaking ties by a fixed index rule:

[Tableau] $$S^{*}=\arg\min_{j\leq m}\big\|\bar{x}_{S_{j}}-\bar{x}_{N}\big\|,\qquad D^{*}=\big\|\bar{x}_{S^{*}}-\bar{x}_{N}\big\|,$$ (9)

Within a joint Gaussian model of probe and residual imbalances, the selected common-shift variance satisfies

[Tableau] $$s_{S^{*}}^{2}=\mathrm{Var}\big(\bar{E}_{\parallel}(S^{*})\big)+(1-R^{2})\,s^{2},\qquad 0\leq\mathrm{Var}\big(\bar{E}_{\parallel}(S^{*})\big)\leq\|\beta\|^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\big[D^{*2}\big],$$ (10)

Thus, if $\mathbb{E}[D^{*2}]\to 0$ , the common-shift scale converges to $\sqrt{1-R^{2}}\,s$ within this model.

Why BestRand is better than random sampling. Theorem 2.2 motivates BestRand by identifying the probe-explained component of the common shift as a target for selection. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, $|\bar{E}_{\parallel}|\leq\|\beta\|\,\|\bar{x}_{\mathcal{S}}-\bar{x}_{N}\|$ . Matching the full-benchmark probe mean therefore controls this component without knowing $\beta$ or evaluating the target models, motivating BestRand’s probe-based selection criterion. To implement this criterion at low cost, BestRand uses the pool mean $H$ to approximate $\bar{x}_{N}$ and selects the candidate that best matches $H$ . The connection follows from $\|\bar{x}_{S^{*}}-\bar{x}_{N}\|\leq\|\bar{x}_{S^{*}}-H\|+\|H-\bar{x}_{N}\|$ . The first term is the imbalance minimized by the candidate search, while the second is the reference error controlled by the pool size. Choosing a sufficiently large $n$ makes $H$ close to $\bar{x}_{N}$ in mean square, with exact equality when the pool covers the full benchmark. Increasing $m$ then improves or preserves the best match to this reference without additional model evaluations. Thus, $n$ controls reference accuracy and $m$ controls search quality.

This design aims to reduce the common-shift scale. Within the selection model of Theorem 2.2 , the probe-explained variance is bounded by $\|\beta\|^{2}\mathbb{E}[D^{*2}]$ , while the residual variance remains $(1-R^{2})s^{2}$ . If $\mathbb{E}[D^{*2}]\to 0$ and $s>0$ , then

[Tableau] $$s_{S^{*}}^{2}=\mathrm{Var}\big(\bar{E}_{\parallel}(S^{*})\big)+(1-R^{2})\,s^{2}\ \longrightarrow\ (1-R^{2})\,s^{2}<\ s^{2}.$$ (11)

A larger explained fraction $R^{2}$ yields a greater potential reduction in the shared source of high-error risk identified by Theorem 2.1 . This limit describes the idealized benefit of probe matching, rather than a guaranteed contraction for finite-pool selection, where reference error and nonzero attainable imbalance remain. Our numerical results are consistent with this analysis. Figure 2 shows that BestRand concentrates MAE near zero and suppresses the upper tail relative to random sampling across benchmarks, supporting the practical value of probe-guided selection for improving stability.

### 3 Experiments

We evaluate BestRand on 70 text, multimodal, and agent benchmarks, focusing on estimation accuracy and stability. We compare average errors across methods and compression budgets, then examine error distributions and high-error risk, with VLA results reported in the Appendix C . We first introduce the benchmarks, then describe the baselines, evaluation protocol, and metrics.

Our evaluation spans 70 benchmarks, comprising 44 text, 16 multimodal, and 10 agent benchmarks. These include widely used text benchmarks such as MMLU ( Hendrycks et al., 2020 ) , GSM8K ( Cobbe et al., 2021 ) , and HumanEval ( Chen et al., 2021 ) , multimodal benchmarks such as MMMU ( Yue et al., 2024 ) , MathVista ( Lu et al., 2024 ) , and MME ( Fu et al., 2025a ) , and agent benchmarks such as OSWorld ( Xie et al., 2024 ) and tau-bench ( Yao et al., 2024 ) . To compare methods comprehensively across compression levels, we evaluate six retention ratios ranging from 2% to 50%. Details of all benchmarks are provided in Appendix A.2 .

We compare against six representative baselines. Random uniformly samples a subset and averages its scores. SparseEval ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) uses a linear or MLP predictor to estimate full-benchmark scores from subset scores. Anchor Points , TinyBenchmarks , and ZipBench ( Vivek et al., 2024 ; Polo et al., 2024 ; Huang et al., 2026 ) construct item representations and cluster them to select a representative subset. All methods use the same model split and item budget. Random and BestRand use 10,000 seeds, while other methods use three. BestRand uses $p=5$ , $m=128$ , $n=4$ , random probe selection, and $L_{2}$ matching. It probes only the candidate pool and thus incurs a lower construction cost. Section 4 further compares four additional baselines. Appendices A.1 and A.3 provide details on the data splits and baselines, respectively.

We use mean absolute error (MAE) as our primary metric. We also evaluate ranking fidelity using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (SP). Since rank correlations are generally strong for leading methods in both our experiments and prior work ( Yauney et al., 2026 ) , we focus on MAE in the main results and report detailed SP results in Appendix E , Tables 9 – 11 .

We then compare BestRand with existing methods, showing its high accuracy, low cost, and stability.

As shown in Table 1 , Random performs strongly on average. BestRand consistently improves on this baseline, reducing both mean MAE and its variation across all settings. Against three competitive baselines, namely Random, ZipBench, and TinyBenchmarks, BestRand reduces MAE by 16.4% on average, achieving the best overall performance. By probing only a candidate pool, BestRand uses just 8.0% of data-dependent baselines’ construction records at 2% retention and 51.3% on average. BestRand therefore achieves the lowest error at roughly half the construction cost. Beyond average performance, we next compare the stability and high-error risk of all methods.

Figure 2 shows that BestRand concentrates errors near zero and suppresses Random’s upper tail, yielding more stable estimates. Figure 3 quantifies the risk of falling into the high-error region of Random’s long-tailed distribution. We measure the probability that each method’s error exceeds Random’s 70th percentile or mean, corresponding to the worst 30% of random subsets and worse-than-average random performance, respectively. Under both thresholds, BestRand achieves the lowest risk across benchmark categories and reduces high-error probability by 50.2% versus three strong baselines. Additional results are reported in Appendix E . Together, these results demonstrate that BestRand enables accurate and stable benchmark compression.

### 4 Analysis and ablation study

This section analyzes the key factors affecting BestRand and evaluates its effectiveness, efficiency, and robustness across practical settings. Additional analyses are provided in Appendix C .

We first examine how the probe count $p$ , candidate count $m$ , and pool multiplier $n$ affect compression accuracy. As shown in Figure 4 (a), more probes improve the reliability of candidate comparison, more candidates increase the chance of finding a representative subset, and a larger pool provides a more accurate reference and greater candidate diversity. Performance improves with all three quantities, but the gains from increasing $m$ and $n$ quickly diminish. We therefore use $p=5$ , $m=128$ , and $n=4$ by default. This setting achieves strong accuracy at low construction cost.

Because our main experiments use only five construction models, we next test whether BestRand remains competitive when more models are available. Figure 4 (b) shows that BestRand performs best with only a few construction models. Learning-based methods improve as they gain more response records, but BestRand remains comparable to the strongest alternatives even with hundreds or thousands of models. Thus, BestRand offers the greatest gains when construction data are limited while retaining competitive accuracy when they are abundant. The low-data setting is also more practical because collecting complete benchmark responses from many models is often costly.

We next assess the sensitivity of BestRand to probe selection and matching norm. We compare probes sampled uniformly across the score range, at random, or from its low and high ends. Using two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests across benchmarks, Figure 4 (c) shows no significant differences except between Uniform and Low, where Uniform performs better. This suggests that probe selection should avoid using only low-scoring models. Figure 4 (d) shows similar results for $L_{1}$ , $L_{2}$ , and $L_{\infty}$ , with no significant differences according to the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. BestRand is therefore robust to both choices, and we use random selection with $L_{2}$ matching by default.

Methods differ in how many historical model responses they use. Response-free methods require no historical model-item scores but generally yield much higher MAE. Among response-based methods, the same number of construction models can also incur different costs because BestRand evaluates each model only on its candidate pool, whereas several baselines require full-benchmark responses. We therefore define normalized construction cost as $\mathsf{Cost}=R/N$ , where $R$ is the total number of observed model-item scores and $N$ is the benchmark size. Thus, $\mathsf{Cost}=1$ corresponds to one full evaluation of one model. Under this common budget, Figure 5 shows that BestRand achieves lower MAE at the same cost and requires less cost to achieve the same MAE.

Table 2 evaluates BestRand under three types of OOD shifts. Score-range OOD covers Mid $\to$ Tails, which uses middle-scoring models for construction and low- and high-scoring models for testing, as well as Low $\to$ High and High $\to$ Low. Family OOD uses disjoint model families for construction and testing. Temporal OOD uses older models for construction and newer models for testing. Methods that learn from historical performance records perform worse than Random in every setting, showing that fitting these records can hurt under distribution shifts. In contrast, BestRand achieves the lowest error in all five settings and reduces average OOD error by 21.8% relative to competitive baselines.


### 5 Conclusion

In this paper, we developed a theoretical framework that characterizes the error distribution of benchmark compression and provides a principled basis for understanding and predicting its accuracy and reliability. Extensive empirical results closely match our theoretical results. Guided by our theory, we introduced BestRand, which improves the stability of random sampling at low construction cost. Across 70+ text, multimodal, and agent benchmarks, together with VLA evaluation, BestRand improved accuracy and stability over strong baselines and remained robust to distribution shifts. More broadly, our findings suggest that improving benchmark compression may not require complex selection, but rather understanding when and why simple sampling fails and correcting it at low cost.

#### A.3 Details of Baselines Considered

We group the baselines by whether benchmark construction uses historical model responses. Response-based methods use these records to identify representative items and, when needed, learn how to estimate full-benchmark scores from selected-item responses. Random and the data-only methods use no historical performance records and are collectively termed response-free methods.

Response-based baselines differ in their item representations and score estimators. SparseEval ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) represents each item by its response vector across construction models. Starting from a clustered or random subset, it iteratively replaces items to reduce full-score prediction error on the construction models. A linear model or MLP then maps a new model’s selected-item responses to its estimated full score. Anchor Points ( Vivek et al., 2024 ) derives item similarities from correlations between response patterns and applies $k$ -medoids clustering. It selects each cluster medoid and estimates performance using a cluster-size-weighted average of the selected-item scores. TinyBenchmarks ( Polo et al., 2024 ) fits an item response theory model to learn item difficulty and discrimination, clusters the resulting item representations, and selects one representative per cluster. For a new model, it infers ability from the selected responses and predicts performance on unobserved items. We use its gp-IRT estimator, which combines these predictions with the weighted observed-item score. ZipBench ( Huang et al., 2026 ) is designed for settings with limited construction budgets. It constructs synthetic response records by majority voting within a group of construction models and retains records spanning the observed performance range. It then fits an item response theory model to the augmented records, clusters the learned item representations, and estimates the full score using a cluster-size-weighted average of the selected-item scores.

Among the response-free baselines, Random uniformly samples a subset of items, whereas the four data-only methods select subsets using prompt-derived representations. All five methods estimate each model’s full-benchmark score by averaging its scores on the selected items. Facility Location, MIS, and Coverage use Sentence-BERT embeddings ( Reimers and Gurevych, 2019 ) , whereas Agreement uses multilingual BERT representations ( Devlin et al., 2019 ) . Facility Location ( Yao et al., 2026 ) greedily selects items that maximize semantic coverage, ensuring that each benchmark item is close to a selected item. MIS ( Kjorvezir et al., 2026 ) connects semantically similar prompts in a graph and greedily selects an independent set to reduce redundancy. Agreement ( Purificato et al., 2026 ) applies an eight-head attention module to the BERT representations, ranks prompts by the variance of their attention weights, and retains those with the greatest disagreement. Coverage ( Purificato et al., 2026 ) begins with a random prompt and repeatedly selects the prompt farthest from the most recently chosen one, encouraging coverage of distinct regions in the embedding space.


#### A.4 Implementation and Hyperparameters

BestRand uses five probe models, 128 candidate subsets, a pool multiplier of four, and $L_{2}$ matching. We evaluate Random and BestRand over 10,000 trials to obtain stable estimates. We evaluate other baselines with three seeds to obtain reliable results. Anchor Points uses FasterPAM for $k$ -medoids clustering. TinyBenchmarks and ZipBench use multidimensional two-parameter logistic IRT models with 10 and 5 dimensions, respectively. Both IRT models are trained for 2,000 epochs with an initial learning rate of $0.1$ and a decay factor of $0.9999$ . ZipBench adds six synthetic response profiles. SparseEval performs 10 subset-refinement rounds and trains each proxy for 2,000 epochs. Its MLP has hidden widths of 128, 64, and 32 with dropout $0.1$ . Both the linear and MLP variants use a learning rate of $5\times 10^{-4}$ and train the final predictor for up to 20,000 epochs, with early stopping checked every 500 epochs using a patience of 30.

We evaluate data-only methods in Section 4 and Appendix C.3 as response-free baselines. We evaluate the four methods at 20% retention on the 12 text benchmarks used in the cost analysis. The primary setting uses 384-dimensional Sentence-BERT representations, while RoBERTa, RoBERTa’s last-layer representation, and Longformer are used only for embedding sensitivity. Agreement uses bert-base-multilingual-uncased , a maximum sequence length of 64, eight attention heads, and a batch size of 32. MIS estimates its similarity threshold from 10,000 randomly sampled prompt pairs and searches percentiles 80 through 100 until the item budget can be met. Facility Location chooses its neighborhood scale from $\{0.1,0.5,1,5,10,100\}$ by three-fold validation based only on geometric coverage.

In Section 4 , we evaluate OOD generalization at 20% retention under score-range, model-family, and temporal shifts. For score-range OOD, we rank models by their full-benchmark scores, randomly sample construction models from the lowest, highest, or middle 75%, and reserve the complementary 25% for testing. Full scores are used only to define the OOD splits, and no test-model responses are used for item selection or estimator fitting. Model-family OOD uses disjoint families for construction and testing, while temporal OOD uses only models released earlier than every test model for construction. We use five splits with different random seeds for each shift setting to reduce sensitivity to any single model split. For each split, we evaluate Random and BestRand with 100 seeds and all other baselines with three seeds. We average MAE over seeds and splits within each benchmark, then report the mean and standard deviation across benchmarks.

In Appendix E.3 , we evaluate five additional baselines on 12 benchmarks using three random seeds for each stochastic method and a single run for the deterministic DatBench. SubLIME uses BERT representations and a rank correlation predictor to select from 1,000 candidate subsets after transport filtering. MINCE estimates score drift from 10,000 Monte Carlo subsets per retention ratio. EssenceBench uses BGE-M3 representations, redundancy thresholds of $0.9$ , three genetic search rounds, and a regularized predictor with 25 spline terms. FlashEval retains the top 100,000 of three million candidates according to Kendall’s $\tau_{b}$ . DatBench ranks binary items by point-biserial correlation and uses Pearson correlation for nonbinary scores. All methods use identical data splits and item budgets, and test responses are used only for final evaluation.

### Appendix C Additional Analysis

Section 4 analyzes the key design choices of BestRand and evaluates its effectiveness, efficiency, robustness, and OOD generalization. This section complements those results with additional experiments on VLA benchmarks, multi-metric evaluation, and data-only compression methods.


#### C.1 Effectiveness of BestRand on VLA Benchmarks

#### C.2 Effectiveness of BestRand on Multi-Metric Benchmarks

Although smaller, these gains are consistent across compression levels and maintain balanced in-distribution and generalization performance. #### C.3 Can Data-Only Methods Achieve Effective Benchmark Compression?

Some benchmark compression methods require no historical model responses and select items solely from representations of the benchmark data. This makes subset construction inexpensive and practical when performance records are unavailable. We study four such methods. Facility Location ( Yao et al., 2026 ) selects items that cover the prompt embedding space, while MIS ( Kjorvezir et al., 2026 ) constructs a similarity graph and chooses a nonredundant independent set. Agreement selects prompts with the greatest variation in attention weights, while Coverage iteratively selects distant prompts to span diverse regions of the representation space ( Purificato et al., 2026 ) . Appendix A.3 describes these methods, and Appendix A.4 provides their implementation settings.

Figure 10 compares the four data-only methods with Random and BestRand at 20% retention. Although inexpensive to construct, the data-only subsets often yield substantially higher MAE than Random and BestRand, with performance varying markedly across benchmarks. BestRand achieves the lowest MAE on five of the six benchmarks and is nearly tied for the lowest on GPQA, yielding the strongest overall accuracy. These results show that a small initial investment in probe-model inference clearly improves accuracy over methods that rely only on benchmark data.

### Appendix D Additional Statements


#### D.1 Limitations of BestRand

BestRand provides an efficient approximation to full evaluation, but several limitations remain.

Although BestRand outperforms many existing benchmark compression methods at lower construction cost, it cannot guarantee zero estimation error. This is a common limitation of benchmark compression, but the compression ratio can be adjusted to balance evaluation efficiency and reliability. BestRand is most useful for rapid model iteration and comparison, but compressed benchmarks cannot fully replace comprehensive evaluation. We therefore recommend reporting full-benchmark results for final evaluations when feasible.

BestRand still requires probe models to run full inference on some benchmark items, so its construction cost is not zero. However, this cost is small in practice and brings clear accuracy gains. BestRand substantially outperforms data-only methods that use no model responses. Moreover, only a few probe models are needed, and they score only the candidate pool, which can be much smaller than the full benchmark at low retention ratios. BestRand therefore achieves better accuracy at a lower construction cost than existing response-based methods.


#### D.2 Broader Impact of BestRand

BestRand provides stable, accurate, and low-cost benchmark compression, reducing evaluation costs while preserving reliable results. Its efficient construction process also keeps the cost of creating compressed benchmarks low. Together, these benefits can reduce compute and energy use, lower associated carbon emissions, and support faster experimental iteration. By making reliable evaluation more affordable, BestRand can also lower barriers for smaller laboratories and researchers with limited computing resources, enabling broader participation in model development and evaluation.


#### D.3 Computational Resources Statement

Our experiments use historical model performance records collected through prior research, so their original computational cost cannot be reliably estimated. All benchmark compression experiments can run on a single GPU. BestRand’s subset selection requires only a CPU and completes in under 0.1 seconds with parallel execution. We will release the code and data to support reproducibility.

### Appendix F Full Proofs and Derivations


#### F.1 Notation and basic conventions

Fix the score matrix $X\in[0,1]^{N\times M}$ , where $X_{s,m}$ is the score of model $m$ on item $s$ over $N$ items and $M$ models. In the finite-population analysis, $X$ is non-random and randomness comes only from subset sampling. A single subset $\mathcal{S}$ is drawn uniformly without replacement from $\{1,\dots,N\}$ , with $|\mathcal{S}|=k$ and $1\leq k\leq N$ , where $N>1$ . The non-degenerate distributional analysis below concerns $k<N$ ; at $k=N$ , all errors vanish. The selection analysis additionally considers multiple candidate draws. Following the main text,

[Tableau] $$\mu_{m}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s=1}^{N}X_{s,m},\quad\hat{\mu}_{m}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}X_{s,m},\quad E_{m}=\hat{\mu}_{m}-\mu_{m},\quad\mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}|E_{m}|,$$ (12)

and the errors are collected into $E=(E_{1},\dots,E_{M})^{\top}$ . Item difficulty and its empirical mean and variance are

[Tableau] $$g_{s}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}X_{s,m},\qquad\bar{g}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s=1}^{N}g_{s},\qquad\mathrm{Var}_{N}(g)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s=1}^{N}\big(g_{s}-\bar{g}_{N}\big)^{2}.$$ (13)

The common shift is $\bar{E}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}E_{m}$ and the finite-population factor is $c(k)=\sqrt{(N-k)/[k(N-1)]}>0$ with $c(N)=0$ .

Two variance symbols must be kept apart. A subscript $N$ denotes an empirical average over the fixed population of $N$ items and carries no randomness, whereas $\mathrm{Var}(\cdot)$ and $\mathrm{Cov}(\cdot)$ without a subscript denote sampling variance and covariance taken as the subset $\mathcal{S}$ is redrawn. We write $\Phi$ for the standard normal cumulative distribution function and $\varphi(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-t^{2}/2}$ for its density.

Let $z_{s}=X_{s,\cdot}-\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{M}$ be the $s$ -th row of $X$ with the column means $\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{M})^{\top}$ removed. Because each column is centered,

[Tableau] $$\sum_{s=1}^{N}z_{s}=\sum_{s=1}^{N}X_{s,\cdot}-N\mu=0.$$ (A.1)

In line with Assumption 2 , the population score covariance matrix is

[Tableau] $$C_{ml}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s=1}^{N}\big(X_{s,m}-\mu_{m}\big)\big(X_{s,l}-\mu_{l}\big),\qquad C=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s=1}^{N}z_{s}z_{s}^{\top},$$ (A.2)

which is positive semidefinite since $a^{\top}Ca=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s}(a^{\top}z_{s})^{2}\geq 0$ for every $a\in\mathbb{R}^{M}$ . Finally, let $I_{s}=\mathbf{1}\{s\in\mathcal{S}\}$ be the sampling indicators, so that $\sum_{s=1}^{N}I_{s}=k$ .

The matrix $C$ is the population score covariance, whereas the sampling error covariance is $\mathrm{Cov}(E)=c(k)^{2}C$ . Theorem 2.1 uses $C_{0}$ for the scalar constant $C_{0}=\sqrt{\pi/2-1}\,u^{*}\approx 0.578$ . The subscript distinguishes it from the covariance matrix $C$ . To keep the limiting-density prefactor separate from both, Appendix F.6 denotes it by $A_{0}$ . Exact finite-population identities are distinguished below from distributional conclusions obtained within the Gaussian approximation of Theorem 2.1 .


#### F.2 Numerical validation of the assumptions

The empirical checks concern how well Assumptions 2 and 2 approximate the benchmark data. Assumption 2 posits equal positive diagonal entries $\sigma^{2}$ and equal off-diagonal entries $\rho\sigma^{2}$ in $C$ , with $\rho\in[0,1)$ . The empirical summaries used in the main text are $\sigma^{2}=M^{-1}\sum_{m}C_{mm}$ and, for $M>1$ , $\rho=[M(M-1)]^{-1}\sum_{m\neq l}C_{ml}/\sigma^{2}$ . Under equal variances, the latter is the average pairwise correlation; with unequal variances it is a normalized average covariance. Assumption 2 requires $\mathrm{Var}_{N}(g)>0$ and positive explained variance $R^{2}>0$ in a least-squares regression with an intercept. The normal equations ensure that the residual has zero empirical mean and is orthogonal to the probes; they do not require a perfect linear fit or independence of residuals and probe scores.

We validate Assumption 2 by testing the equal-variance and equal-covariance conditions directly from the observed model scores. For each benchmark, we remove only models whose scores are constant across all items, as required by Assumption 2 . We then center each remaining model over the $N$ items and form the empirical covariance matrix

[Tableau] $$C=\frac{1}{N}Z^{\top}Z,\qquad Z_{sm}=X_{s,m}-\mu_{m}.$$

Let $d=(C_{11},\ldots,C_{MM})$ contain the score variances of the $M$ models. Let $o$ contain the $K=M(M-1)/2$ covariances between unordered model pairs. We compute

[Tableau] $$A_{\rm diag}=100\frac{\langle d,\mathbf{1}\rangle}{\lVert d\rVert_{2}\sqrt{M}},\qquad P_{+}=\frac{100}{K}\sum_{m<l}\mathbf{1}\{C_{ml}>0\},\qquad A_{\rm off}=100\frac{\langle o,\mathbf{1}\rangle}{\lVert o\rVert_{2}\sqrt{K}}.$$

$A_{\rm diag}$ measures variance similarity. $P_{+}$ is the proportion of model pairs with positive covariance. $A_{\rm off}$ measures covariance similarity. The two alignment scores are cosine similarities to a positive constant vector. They reach 100% only when the corresponding entries are equal and positive. Higher values therefore indicate closer agreement with Assumption 2 .

Table 17 shows that score variances are highly similar across models and that almost all model pairs have positive covariance. The pairwise covariance values also remain close to a common level for most benchmarks. This agreement in both variance and covariance strongly supports Assumption 2 as a compact approximation of the observed covariance structure.

#### F.3 Basic lemmas: moments of sampling without replacement

The two lemmas below are the only source of every variance and covariance computed later.

Lemma B.1. Under uniform sampling without replacement, for any $s\neq t$ ,

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}[I_{s}]=\frac{k}{N},\qquad\mathrm{Var}(I_{s})=\frac{k(N-k)}{N^{2}},\qquad\mathrm{Cov}(I_{s},I_{t})=-\frac{k(N-k)}{N^{2}(N-1)}.$$ (B.1)

Proof. Every item is equally likely to be chosen and every pair is equally likely to be chosen together,

[Tableau] $$\Pr(s\in\mathcal{S})=\frac{k}{N},\qquad\Pr(s,t\in\mathcal{S})=\frac{k(k-1)}{N(N-1)}\quad(s\neq t).$$

The first probability holds because the $k$ chosen positions are spread uniformly over $N$ items, and the second because fixing two given items to be chosen amounts to choosing the remaining $k-2$ from the other $N-2$ , with probability $\binom{N-2}{k-2}/\binom{N}{k}=\frac{k(k-1)}{N(N-1)}$ . Hence $\mathbb{E}[I_{s}]=k/N$ , and since $I_{s}\in\{0,1\}$ gives $I_{s}^{2}=I_{s}$ ,

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{Var}(I_{s})=\mathbb{E}[I_{s}]-\mathbb{E}[I_{s}]^{2}=\frac{k}{N}-\Big(\frac{k}{N}\Big)^{2}=\frac{k(N-k)}{N^{2}}.$$

For $s\neq t$ ,

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{Cov}(I_{s},I_{t})=\Pr(s,t\in\mathcal{S})-\Pr(s\in\mathcal{S})\Pr(t\in\mathcal{S})=\frac{k(k-1)}{N(N-1)}-\frac{k^{2}}{N^{2}}.$$

Over a common denominator the numerator is $k[(k-1)N-k(N-1)]=k[kN-N-kN+k]=k(k-N)=-k(N-k)$ , which gives $\mathrm{Cov}(I_{s},I_{t})=-k(N-k)/[N^{2}(N-1)]$ . $\blacksquare$

A compact form that we use repeatedly aligns the variance and the covariance,

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{Cov}(I_{s},I_{t})=\frac{k(N-k)}{N^{2}}\Big(\delta_{st}-\frac{1-\delta_{st}}{N-1}\Big),\qquad\delta_{st}=\mathbf{1}\{s=t\}.$$ (B.2)

Lemma B.2. Let $h,h^{\prime}:\{1,\dots,N\}\to\mathbb{R}$ be any two per-item scalars, write the subset-minus-population mean deviation as $\Delta\bar{h}:=\bar{h}_{\mathcal{S}}-\bar{h}_{N}$ with $\bar{h}_{\mathcal{S}}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}h_{s}$ and $\bar{h}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s}h_{s}$ , and let $\mathrm{Cov}_{N}(h,h^{\prime})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s}(h_{s}-\bar{h}_{N})(h^{\prime}_{s}-\bar{h}^{\prime}_{N})$ . Then

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}[\Delta\bar{h}]=0,\qquad\mathrm{Cov}(\Delta\bar{h},\Delta\bar{h}^{\prime})=c(k)^{2}\,\mathrm{Cov}_{N}(h,h^{\prime}).$$ (B.3)

In particular $\mathrm{Var}(\Delta\bar{h})=c(k)^{2}\,\mathrm{Var}_{N}(h)$ .

Proof. Without loss of generality take $h,h^{\prime}$ centered, so that $\bar{h}_{N}=\bar{h}^{\prime}_{N}=0$ , since replacing $h_{s}$ by $h_{s}-\bar{h}_{N}$ leaves $\Delta\bar{h}$ unchanged. Then $\Delta\bar{h}=\bar{h}_{\mathcal{S}}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{s}I_{s}h_{s}$ . From $\mathbb{E}[I_{s}]=k/N$ ,

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}[\Delta\bar{h}]=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{s=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[I_{s}]\,h_{s}=\frac{1}{k}\cdot\frac{k}{N}\sum_{s=1}^{N}h_{s}=\bar{h}_{N}=0.$$

Because the mean is zero,

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{Cov}(\Delta\bar{h},\Delta\bar{h}^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\tfrac{1}{k}\sum_{s}I_{s}h_{s}\Big)\Big(\tfrac{1}{k}\sum_{t}I_{t}h^{\prime}_{t}\Big)\Big]=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\sum_{s,t}\mathbb{E}[I_{s}I_{t}]\,h_{s}h^{\prime}_{t}.$$

Writing $\mathbb{E}[I_{s}I_{t}]=\mathrm{Cov}(I_{s},I_{t})+\mathbb{E}[I_{s}]\mathbb{E}[I_{t}]$ and using $\sum_{s}h_{s}=\sum_{t}h^{\prime}_{t}=0$ , the product-of-means term $\frac{k^{2}}{N^{2}}(\sum_{s}h_{s})(\sum_{t}h^{\prime}_{t})$ vanishes and only the covariance term survives. Substituting ( B.2 ) ,

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{Cov}(\Delta\bar{h},\Delta\bar{h}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\sum_{s,t}\frac{k(N-k)}{N^{2}}\Big(\delta_{st}-\frac{1-\delta_{st}}{N-1}\Big)h_{s}h^{\prime}_{t}.$$

The diagonal contributes $\sum_{s}h_{s}h^{\prime}_{s}$ , and the off-diagonal contributes

[Tableau] $$\sum_{s\neq t}h_{s}h^{\prime}_{t}=\Big(\sum_{s}h_{s}\Big)\Big(\sum_{t}h^{\prime}_{t}\Big)-\sum_{s}h_{s}h^{\prime}_{s}=-\sum_{s}h_{s}h^{\prime}_{s}.$$

The bracket therefore equals $\big(1+\tfrac{1}{N-1}\big)\sum_{s}h_{s}h^{\prime}_{s}=\tfrac{N}{N-1}\sum_{s}h_{s}h^{\prime}_{s}$ , and

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{Cov}(\Delta\bar{h},\Delta\bar{h}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\cdot\frac{k(N-k)}{N^{2}}\cdot\frac{N}{N-1}\sum_{s}h_{s}h^{\prime}_{s}=\frac{N-k}{k(N-1)}\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s}h_{s}h^{\prime}_{s}.$$

Here $\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s}h_{s}h^{\prime}_{s}=\mathrm{Cov}_{N}(h,h^{\prime})$ for centered $h,h^{\prime}$ and $\frac{N-k}{k(N-1)}=c(k)^{2}$ , which gives ( B.3 ) . $\blacksquare$

The factor $c(k)^{2}=\frac{N-k}{k(N-1)}$ has two parts, the usual $\frac{1}{k}$ dilution from averaging over $k$ draws and the finite-population correction $\frac{N-k}{N-1}$ from sampling without replacement, which vanishes at $k=N$ where no fluctuation remains ( Cochran, 1977 ) .


#### F.4 Proof of Theorem 2.1

Theorem 2.1 . Under Assumption 2 , when the effective sample size $\min\{k,N-k\}$ is large,

[Tableau] $$E\ \overset{\cdot}{\sim}\ \mathcal{N}\big(0,c(k)^{2}C\big),\qquad\mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}|E_{m}|.$$

Under this Gaussian approximation, the MAE distribution is that of the average absolute value of correlated zero-mean Gaussian variables. The pointwise inequality $\mathrm{MAE}\geq|\bar{E}|$ and the approximation $\bar{E}\ \overset{\cdot}{\sim}\ \mathcal{N}(0,s^{2})$ give, for every threshold $t>0$ ,

[Tableau] $$\Pr(\mathrm{MAE}>t)\geq\Pr(|\bar{E}|>t)\approx 2\Phi(-t/s),$$

where $s=c(k)\sigma\sqrt{\rho+(1-\rho)/M}$ is the standard deviation of the common shift $\bar{E}=M^{-1}\sum_{m}E_{m}$ .

The proof has four parts, the exact first and second moments of $E$ , its multivariate normal approximation and the induced MAE distribution, the identity for the common shift together with a two-sided sandwich for the $\mathrm{MAE}$ , and the right-tail lower bound. The tail bound follows because $\mathrm{MAE}\geq|\bar{E}|$ holds pointwise and $\bar{E}$ is approximately $\mathcal{N}(0,s^{2})$ under Assumption 2 , so $\Pr(\mathrm{MAE}>t)\geq\Pr(|\bar{E}|>t)\approx 2\Phi(-t/s)$ .


##### F.4.1 Exact first and second moments of the error vector

Proposition C.1. Under uniform sampling without replacement, for any finite $N$ and $1\leq k<N$ ,

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}[E]=0,\qquad\mathrm{Cov}(E)=c(k)^{2}\,C.$$ (C.1)

This is exact and requires no distributional assumption.

Proof. Write $E$ as a linear combination of the indicators. From $\hat{\mu}_{m}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}X_{s,m}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{s=1}^{N}I_{s}X_{s,m}$ and $X_{s,\cdot}=z_{s}+\mu$ ,

[Tableau] $$E=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{s=1}^{N}I_{s}X_{s,\cdot}-\mu=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{s=1}^{N}I_{s}z_{s}+\frac{1}{k}\Big(\sum_{s=1}^{N}I_{s}\Big)\mu-\mu.$$

Since $\sum_{s}I_{s}=k$ , the middle term equals $\mu$ and cancels $-\mu$ , which leaves the clean linear form

[Tableau] $$E=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{s=1}^{N}I_{s}z_{s}.$$ (C.2)

For the first moment, $\mathbb{E}[I_{s}]=k/N$ and $\sum_{s}z_{s}=0$ from ( A.1 ) give

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}[E]=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{s=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[I_{s}]z_{s}=\frac{1}{k}\cdot\frac{k}{N}\sum_{s=1}^{N}z_{s}=0.$$

For the second moment, since the mean is zero,

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{Cov}(E)=\mathbb{E}[EE^{\top}]=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\sum_{s,t}\mathbb{E}[I_{s}I_{t}]\,z_{s}z_{t}^{\top}=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\sum_{s,t}\mathrm{Cov}(I_{s},I_{t})\,z_{s}z_{t}^{\top},$$

where the product-of-means term again vanishes because $\sum_{s}z_{s}=0$ . Splitting into $s=t$ and $s\neq t$ and using Lemma F.3 ,

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{Cov}(E)=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\Bigg[\frac{k(N-k)}{N^{2}}\sum_{s}z_{s}z_{s}^{\top}-\frac{k(N-k)}{N^{2}(N-1)}\sum_{s\neq t}z_{s}z_{t}^{\top}\Bigg].$$

With $\sum_{s\neq t}z_{s}z_{t}^{\top}=(\sum_{s}z_{s})(\sum_{t}z_{t})^{\top}-\sum_{s}z_{s}z_{s}^{\top}=-\sum_{s}z_{s}z_{s}^{\top}$ , the bracket becomes $\frac{k(N-k)}{N^{2}}\big(1+\frac{1}{N-1}\big)\sum_{s}z_{s}z_{s}^{\top}=\frac{k(N-k)}{N(N-1)}\sum_{s}z_{s}z_{s}^{\top}$ , hence

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{Cov}(E)=\frac{N-k}{k(N-1)}\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s}z_{s}z_{s}^{\top}=c(k)^{2}\,C.$$

$\blacksquare$

##### F.4.2 Multivariate normal approximation

Proposition F.4.1 gives exact moments, but a statement about near normality needs convergence, which requires embedding the fixed population into a sequence indexed by a growing $N$ .

For each $N$ , fix a population $X_{N,1},\dots,X_{N,N}\in[0,1]^{M}$ and a subset size $k_{N}$ , with the associated quantities carrying the subscript $N$ , namely $\mu_{N}$ , $z_{N,s}=X_{N,s}-\mu_{N}$ , $C_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s}z_{N,s}z_{N,s}^{\top}$ , $c_{N}=\sqrt{(N-k_{N})/[k_{N}(N-1)]}$ , and $E_{N}=\frac{1}{k_{N}}\sum_{s}I_{s}z_{N,s}$ . Define the effective sample size $h_{N}=\min\{k_{N},N-k_{N}\}$ . We impose two conditions. First, $h_{N}\to\infty$ , so both the retained and omitted item counts diverge. Second, $C_{N}\to\Sigma$ for a positive semidefinite $\Sigma$ , possibly singular, which fixes the limiting covariance after scaling. These conditions specify the asymptotic regime underlying the normal approximation; a large effective sample size alone does not guarantee its accuracy for an arbitrary finite population ( Bowyer et al., 2025 ) .

The second condition above requires the score covariance to stabilize as $N$ grows. We examine its finite sample behavior by fixing all models with nonconstant scores and recomputing the covariance from nested random subsets of $n$ items. Figure 14 reports the relative Frobenius error of $C_{n}$ and the relative errors of $\sigma_{n}$ and $\mathrm{Var}_{n}(g)$ , each measured against its full benchmark estimate. Here $\sigma_{n}^{2}=M^{-1}\sum_{m}(C_{n})_{mm}$ . Across all eight benchmarks, the errors fall sharply when item coverage increases from 1% to 10%. By 25% of the items, all three estimates are close to their full benchmark values and improve further as more items are used. This consistent behavior provides empirical support for the covariance stability required by the asymptotic regime.

Proposition C.2. Under the framework above, if $M$ is fixed and the two conditions hold, then

[Tableau] $$c_{N}^{-1}E_{N}\ \xrightarrow{\ d\ }\ W\sim\mathcal{N}_{M}(0,\Sigma).$$ (C.3)

Moreover, writing $\mathrm{MAE}_{N}=M^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{M}|E_{N,m}|$ , we have

[Tableau] $$c_{N}^{-1}\mathrm{MAE}_{N}\ \xrightarrow{\ d\ }\ \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}|W_{m}|.$$

Hence the working Gaussian approximation for $E$ also determines the approximate MAE distribution through the average of its absolute coordinates. Here $W$ denotes only the limiting vector of the scaled errors in ( C.3 ) .

Proof. We use the Cramér–Wold device, so that convergence of every scalar projection $a^{\top}(c_{N}^{-1}E_{N})$ to the matching one-dimensional normal implies convergence of the vector. Fix a direction $a\in\mathbb{R}^{M}$ and study the projection.

First, express the projection as a sample-mean deviation. Define the scalar population $y_{N,s}=a^{\top}z_{N,s}$ , whose population mean is $\bar{y}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s}y_{N,s}=0$ by $\sum_{s}z_{N,s}=0$ , and whose population variance normalized by $N-1$ is

[Tableau] $$v_{N,a}=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{s=1}^{N}(a^{\top}z_{N,s})^{2}=\frac{N}{N-1}\,a^{\top}C_{N}a.$$

With $\bar{y}_{\mathcal{S}}=\frac{1}{k_{N}}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}y_{N,s}$ and $E_{N}=\frac{1}{k_{N}}\sum_{s}I_{s}z_{N,s}$ from ( C.2 ) , the sample mean minus the population mean is the projection itself,

[Tableau] $$\bar{y}_{\mathcal{S}}-\bar{y}_{N}=a^{\top}E_{N}.$$ (C.4)

Second, treat a nondegenerate direction with $a^{\top}\Sigma a>0$ . Because $X_{N,s}\in[0,1]^{M}$ gives $z_{N,s}\in[-1,1]^{M}$ , each term is bounded by $|y_{N,s}|=|a^{\top}z_{N,s}|\leq\|a\|_{1}<\infty$ . The second condition gives $v_{N,a}\to a^{\top}\Sigma a>0$ and the first gives $h_{N}\to\infty$ , so

[Tableau] $$\frac{\max_{s}y_{N,s}^{2}}{h_{N}\,v_{N,a}}\leq\frac{\|a\|_{1}^{2}}{h_{N}\,v_{N,a}}\longrightarrow 0,$$

which is the Lindeberg-type condition of the Hájek finite-population central limit theorem ( Hájek, 1960 ; Li and Ding, 2017 ) . That theorem gives, for simple random sampling without replacement,

[Tableau] $$\frac{\bar{y}_{\mathcal{S}}-\bar{y}_{N}}{\sqrt{(\tfrac{1}{k_{N}}-\tfrac{1}{N})v_{N,a}}}\ \xrightarrow{\ d\ }\ \mathcal{N}(0,1).$$

The variance in the denominator simplifies through $v_{N,a}=\frac{N}{N-1}a^{\top}C_{N}a$ ,

[Tableau] $$\Big(\frac{1}{k_{N}}-\frac{1}{N}\Big)v_{N,a}=\frac{N-k_{N}}{k_{N}N}\cdot\frac{N}{N-1}\,a^{\top}C_{N}a=c_{N}^{2}\,a^{\top}C_{N}a,$$

so with ( C.4 ) it reads $a^{\top}E_{N}/(c_{N}\sqrt{a^{\top}C_{N}a})\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ . Since $a^{\top}C_{N}a\to a^{\top}\Sigma a$ , Slutsky’s theorem yields

[Tableau] $$c_{N}^{-1}a^{\top}E_{N}\ \xrightarrow{\ d\ }\ \mathcal{N}(0,\,a^{\top}\Sigma a).$$ (C.5)

Third, treat a degenerate direction with $a^{\top}\Sigma a=0$ , so that $a^{\top}C_{N}a\to 0$ . By Proposition F.4.1 , $\mathbb{E}[c_{N}^{-1}a^{\top}E_{N}]=0$ and

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{Var}(c_{N}^{-1}a^{\top}E_{N})=c_{N}^{-2}\,a^{\top}\mathrm{Cov}(E_{N})a=a^{\top}C_{N}a\to 0.$$

Chebyshev’s inequality then gives $c_{N}^{-1}a^{\top}E_{N}\xrightarrow{p}0$ , which is the normal limit $\mathcal{N}(0,0)$ and agrees with ( C.5 ) at $a^{\top}\Sigma a=0$ .

Fourth, reassemble the vector. For every $a$ we have $c_{N}^{-1}a^{\top}E_{N}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,a^{\top}\Sigma a)$ . Taking $W=\Sigma^{1/2}G$ with $G\sim\mathcal{N}_{M}(0,I_{M})$ and $\Sigma^{1/2}$ the positive semidefinite square root, every projection satisfies $a^{\top}W\sim\mathcal{N}(0,a^{\top}\Sigma a)$ , so all one-dimensional limits come from the single $W\sim\mathcal{N}_{M}(0,\Sigma)$ . The Cramér–Wold device gives $c_{N}^{-1}E_{N}\xrightarrow{d}W\sim\mathcal{N}_{M}(0,\Sigma)$ .

Finally, the map $v\mapsto M^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{M}|v_{m}|$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}^{M}$ . Applying the continuous mapping theorem to ( C.3 ) yields

[Tableau] $$\frac{\mathrm{MAE}_{N}}{c_{N}}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left|\frac{E_{N,m}}{c_{N}}\right|\ \xrightarrow{\ d\ }\ \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}|W_{m}|.$$

This establishes the induced MAE limit without assuming independence of the error coordinates. $\blacksquare$

Using the finite- $N$ covariance $C$ and scale $c(k)$ gives the working formulation of Theorem 2.1 , in the asymptotic regime specified above:

[Tableau] $$E\ \overset{\cdot}{\sim}\ \mathcal{N}_{M}\big(0,c(k)^{2}C\big),\qquad\mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}|E_{m}|.$$

The MAE identity is exact; its distribution is approximated by evaluating this function under the joint Gaussian law for $E$ . Each absolute coordinate then has a half-normal marginal, but the coordinates are generally dependent, so MAE is not itself asserted to be Gaussian.


##### F.4.3 The common-shift identity and the two-sided sandwich

Proposition C.3. The common shift satisfies the identity

[Tableau] $$\bar{E}=\bar{g}_{\mathcal{S}}-\bar{g}_{N},\qquad\bar{g}_{\mathcal{S}}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}g_{s},\quad\bar{g}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s=1}^{N}g_{s},$$ (C.6)

and the $\mathrm{MAE}$ is sandwiched by

[Tableau] $$|\bar{E}|\ \leq\ \mathrm{MAE}\ \leq\ |\bar{E}|+\mathrm{floor},\qquad\mathrm{floor}:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}|R_{m}|,\quad R_{m}:=E_{m}-\bar{E}.$$ (C.7)

Both are identities or exact inequalities and need no assumption.

Proof. For ( C.6 ) , substitute the definitions and exchange the order of summation,

[Tableau] $$\bar{E}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}E_{m}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}X_{s,m}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s=1}^{N}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}X_{s,m}=\bar{g}_{\mathcal{S}}-\bar{g}_{N},$$

since the inner average over $m$ is exactly $g_{s}$ . The common shift is therefore the mean difficulty of the drawn subset minus that of the full set, which depends only on which items are drawn and acts identically on every model. For the lower bound, the triangle inequality gives $\mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}|E_{m}|\geq|\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}E_{m}|=|\bar{E}|$ . For the upper bound, write $E_{m}=\bar{E}+R_{m}$ and use $|\bar{E}+R_{m}|\leq|\bar{E}|+|R_{m}|$ , then average over $m$ to obtain $\mathrm{MAE}\leq|\bar{E}|+\mathrm{floor}$ . $\blacksquare$

The sandwich ( C.7 ) shows that a large common shift necessarily produces a large $\mathrm{MAE}$ . The quantity $\mathrm{floor}$ is a random residual term, not a deterministic lower endpoint or a quantity known to remain fixed under selection. Thus the sandwich identifies the common shift as one source of high-error risk, but does not imply that it is the only source or that the entire $\mathrm{MAE}$ distribution is determined by $s$ .


##### F.4.4 The scale of the common shift and the right-tail lower bound

Proposition C.4. The sampling variance of the common shift is

[Tableau] $$s^{2}:=\mathrm{Var}(\bar{E})=c(k)^{2}\,\mathrm{Var}_{N}(g)=\frac{c(k)^{2}}{M^{2}}\,\mathbf{1}^{\top}C\,\mathbf{1},$$ (C.8)

with $\mathbf{1}=(1,\dots,1)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}$ . When the effective sample size is large, $\bar{E}\ \overset{\cdot}{\sim}\ \mathcal{N}(0,s^{2})$ and therefore

[Tableau] $$\Pr(\mathrm{MAE}>t)\ \geq\ \Pr(|\bar{E}|>t)\ \approx\ 2\,\Phi\!\Big(-\frac{t}{s}\Big).$$ (C.9)

Proof. By ( C.6 ) , $\bar{E}=\bar{g}_{\mathcal{S}}-\bar{g}_{N}=\Delta\bar{g}$ is the sample-mean deviation of the per-item difficulty $g_{s}$ , so Lemma F.3 with $h=h^{\prime}=g$ gives $\mathrm{Var}(\bar{E})=c(k)^{2}\mathrm{Var}_{N}(g)$ . To express $\mathrm{Var}_{N}(g)$ through $C$ , note $g_{s}-\bar{g}_{N}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}(X_{s,m}-\mu_{m})=\frac{1}{M}\mathbf{1}^{\top}z_{s}$ , hence

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{Var}_{N}(g)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s=1}^{N}\Big(\frac{1}{M}\mathbf{1}^{\top}z_{s}\Big)^{2}=\frac{1}{M^{2}}\,\mathbf{1}^{\top}\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s=1}^{N}z_{s}z_{s}^{\top}\Big)\mathbf{1}=\frac{1}{M^{2}}\,\mathbf{1}^{\top}C\,\mathbf{1},$$ (C.10)

which is the mean of every entry of $C$ , and ( C.8 ) follows. For the tail, the lower bound $\mathrm{MAE}\geq|\bar{E}|$ of Proposition F.4.3 gives $\{|\bar{E}|>t\}\subseteq\{\mathrm{MAE}>t\}$ , so $\Pr(\mathrm{MAE}>t)\geq\Pr(|\bar{E}|>t)$ . The one-dimensional case of Proposition F.4.2 with $a=\frac{1}{M}\mathbf{1}$ makes $\bar{E}$ approximately $\mathcal{N}(0,s^{2})$ , so for $Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,s^{2})$ ,

[Tableau] $$\Pr(|\bar{E}|>t)\approx\Pr(|Z|>t)=2\big(1-\Phi(t/s)\big)=2\,\Phi(-t/s).$$

$\blacksquare$

Since $2\Phi(-t/s)$ increases with $s$ , a larger scale raises the Gaussian approximation to the common-shift tail and hence the lower bound on high-error risk at any fixed threshold. This proves Theorem 2.1 ; it does not identify the lower bound with the actual $\mathrm{MAE}$ tail probability. Within the Gaussian model, $2\Phi(-t/s)\sim\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}\,\tfrac{s}{t}\,e^{-t^{2}/(2s^{2})}$ as $t/s\to\infty$ . This asymptotic describes the Gaussian common-shift tail, not a relative-error guarantee for extreme tails of the finite-population distribution. In particular, “long-tailed” here describes the observed right-skewed error distributions, not a heavy-tailed asymptotic law. The empirical comparison discussed in the main text is Figure 1 .


#### F.5 Closed forms under Assumption 2

This part inserts Assumption 2 , the homogeneous variance and homogeneous pairwise correlation, and derives closed forms for the common-shift scale and the mean $\mathrm{MAE}$ , together with the single-common-factor representation used by Theorems 2.1 and 2.2 .


##### F.5.1 The equicorrelation structure

Assumption 2 sets $C_{mm}=\sigma^{2}>0$ and $C_{ml}=\rho\sigma^{2}$ for $m\neq l$ with $\rho\in[0,1)$ , which is exactly

[Tableau] $$C=\sigma^{2}\big[(1-\rho)\,I+\rho\,\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\big].$$ (D.1)

The diagonal is $\sigma^{2}[(1-\rho)+\rho]=\sigma^{2}$ and each off-diagonal is $\sigma^{2}[\,0+\rho\,]=\rho\sigma^{2}$ , as required.


##### F.5.2 Closed form of the common-shift scale

Proposition D.2. Under Assumption 2 ,

[Tableau] $$s=c(k)\,\sigma\sqrt{\rho+\frac{1-\rho}{M}}.$$ (D.2)

Proof. From ( C.8 ) , $s^{2}=\frac{c(k)^{2}}{M^{2}}\mathbf{1}^{\top}C\mathbf{1}$ . With ( D.1 ) and $\mathbf{1}^{\top}I\mathbf{1}=M$ , $\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{1}=M^{2}$ ,

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{1}^{\top}C\mathbf{1}=\sigma^{2}\big[(1-\rho)M+\rho M^{2}\big],$$

so $s^{2}=\frac{c(k)^{2}}{M^{2}}\sigma^{2}[(1-\rho)M+\rho M^{2}]=c(k)^{2}\sigma^{2}\big(\rho+\frac{1-\rho}{M}\big)$ , and taking the square root gives ( D.2 ) . $\blacksquare$

For $M>1$ , the squared scale increases with $\rho$ because $\partial[\rho+(1-\rho)/M]/\partial\rho=1-1/M>0$ . For fixed $k<N$ , $\sigma>0$ , and $\rho>0$ , the expression tends to $c(k)\sigma\sqrt{\rho}>0$ as $M$ grows. Thus increasing the number of similarly behaving models does not remove the correlation-dependent component. At $\rho=0$ , by contrast, $s=c(k)\sigma/\sqrt{M}$ vanishes in this limit.


##### F.5.3 The mean of the MAE

Proposition D.3. Under Assumption 2 and the normal approximation of Theorem 2.1 ,

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{MAE}]\approx\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}\,c(k)\,\sigma.$$ (D.3)

Proof. By Theorem 2.1 the marginal of each error is approximately $E_{m}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,c(k)^{2}\sigma^{2})$ , since the diagonal of $C$ is $\sigma^{2}$ . A zero-mean normal $Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\tau^{2})$ has $\mathbb{E}|Z|=\sqrt{2/\pi}\,\tau$ , which is the folded-normal mean of Lemma F.6.2 at zero mean, so with $\tau=c(k)\sigma$ each $\mathbb{E}|E_{m}|=\sqrt{2/\pi}\,c(k)\sigma$ . Averaging the equal marginals over $m$ leaves the value unchanged, giving ( D.3 ) . $\blacksquare$

Within the Gaussian approximation, the mean does not depend on $\rho$ , since it is fixed by the single-model marginal scale $c(k)\sigma$ . Dividing ( D.2 ) by the Gaussian-model mean in ( D.3 ) gives the common-shift scale relative to the mean error,

[Tableau] $$\frac{s}{\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{MAE}]}=\sqrt{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\,\sqrt{\rho+\frac{1-\rho}{M}}\ \xrightarrow{\ M\to\infty\ }\ \sqrt{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\,\sqrt{\rho},$$ (D.4)

where $\sigma$ and $c(k)$ cancel and only $\rho$ and $M$ remain.


##### F.5.4 The single-common-factor representation

Proposition D.4. Within the Gaussian approximation of Theorem 2.1 , Assumption 2 gives the representation

[Tableau] $$E_{m}\ \overset{d}{=}\ c(k)\,\sigma\big(\sqrt{\rho}\,G+\sqrt{1-\rho}\,Z_{m}\big),\qquad G,Z_{1},\dots,Z_{M}\ \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\ \mathcal{N}(0,1).$$ (D.5)

Proof. The right side is a linear combination of independent normals, so $(E_{1},\dots,E_{M})$ is jointly normal and it suffices to match the mean and covariance with $\mathcal{N}(0,c(k)^{2}C)$ . The mean is zero, and the variance is

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{Var}(E_{m})=c(k)^{2}\sigma^{2}\big(\rho+(1-\rho)\big)=c(k)^{2}\sigma^{2}=c(k)^{2}C_{mm}.$$

For $m\neq l$ , only the shared $G$ contributes, so

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{Cov}(E_{m},E_{l})=c(k)^{2}\sigma^{2}\,\rho=c(k)^{2}C_{ml}.$$

Two zero-mean Gaussian vectors with equal covariance are identical in distribution. The equality in distribution in ( D.5 ) is exact for this Gaussian model, not for the original finite-population sampling distribution. $\blacksquare$

The representation ( D.5 ) separates the randomness into a common factor $G$ , which reflects whether the drawn items are collectively harder or easier and is shared by all models, and an individual noise $Z_{m}$ , which is the sampling luck of each model and is independent across models. This is the starting point of the asymptotic analysis of Theorem 2.1 .


#### F.6 Proof of Theorem 2.1

Theorem 2.1 . Under Assumption 2 with $0<\rho<1$ and the Gaussian approximation of Theorem 2.1 , as $M\to\infty$ the $\mathrm{MAE}$ converges to a limiting distribution with lower endpoint $y_{0}=\sqrt{2/\pi}\,c(k)\sigma\sqrt{1-\rho}$ . The mode $\hat{y}(M)$ of the sampling distribution of the $\mathrm{MAE}$ approaches $y_{0}$ at the universal rate $\hat{y}(M)/y_{0}-1=C_{0}/\sqrt{M}+O(1/M)$ , with $C_{0}=\sqrt{\pi/2-1}\,\arg\max_{u>0}\int_{0}^{\infty}v^{-1/2}\varphi(u-v)\,\mathrm{d}v\approx 0.578$ , and the constant $C_{0}$ does not depend on $c(k),\sigma,\rho$ . For brevity the proof writes $u^{*}=\arg\max_{u>0}\int_{0}^{\infty}v^{-1/2}\varphi(u-v)\,\mathrm{d}v\approx 0.765$ for the maximizer.

Throughout this part write $c:=c(k)>0$ and work within the Gaussian representation ( D.5 ) , holding $c$ , $\sigma>0$ , and $0<\rho<1$ fixed as $M\to\infty$ . This is a large- $M$ analysis of the Gaussian model, separate from the fixed- $M$ , large- $N$ justification in Proposition F.4.2 ; it is not a joint-limit theorem for arbitrary growing score matrices. The remainder constants may depend on the fixed parameters, so the expansion is not uniform as $\rho\to 0$ or $\rho\to 1$ . For $Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and a real argument $g$ , define

[Tableau] $$r_{\rho}(g):=\mathbb{E}\big|\sqrt{\rho}\,g+\sqrt{1-\rho}\,Z\big|,\qquad v_{\rho}(g):=\mathrm{Var}\big(\big|\sqrt{\rho}\,g+\sqrt{1-\rho}\,Z\big|\big).$$ (E.0)

By ( D.5 ) , given the common factor $G=g$ the single-model absolute error is $|E_{m}|=c\sigma|\sqrt{\rho}\,g+\sqrt{1-\rho}\,Z_{m}|$ , with conditional mean $c\sigma\,r_{\rho}(g)$ and conditional variance $c^{2}\sigma^{2}v_{\rho}(g)$ . Write $\mathrm{MAE}_{M}:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}|E_{m}|$ .
