## Intrinsic Distribution Matching

One-step and few-step text-to-image generation must accurately render the details specified in the prompt despite limited opportunities for refinement. Distribution matching distillation (DMD) uses score differences to bring the generated distribution closer to a reference distribution. Following prior work, we estimate this difference with internal velocity branches, avoiding a separate fake-score network. We ask whether an explicit moment constraint improves this learned score supervision. A decomposition of the reverse-KL field into a Gaussian moment component and a non-Gaussian residual identifies statistics that can be constrained explicitly. We introduce Intrinsic Distribution Matching ( IDM ) , which retains the full DMD correction and matches feature means and per-coordinate standard deviations using the generator’s exponential-moving-average (EMA) copy, requiring no external feature encoder. Experiments use full-parameter and low-rank adaptation (LoRA) tuning on Z-Image-Turbo 6B and Qwen-Image 20B. At two network evaluations, IDM reaches 94.25% and 90.20% on Chinese and English LongText-Bench, the highest scores among the compared methods at this budget. Controlled ablations show gains from the added moment loss, while generator EMA features perform competitively with an external DINOv2 encoder across the five reported metrics. ### 1 Introduction

Diffusion and flow-matching models produce high-quality images, but their repeated network evaluations impose substantial inference cost ( Ho et al., 2020 ; Song et al., 2020 ; Lipman et al., 2022 ; Liu et al., 2023 ) . One and few-step generation reduces this cost by learning a much shorter sampling process. The challenge is to accurately render the details specified in the prompt using only one or a few sampling steps. Long-text rendering makes this challenge concrete: many characters must remain legible and correct within a coherent image ( Geng et al., 2025b ; Wu et al., 2025a ) . It therefore provides a demanding test of whether fast generation retains detailed conditional information.

Distribution matching distillation (DMD) trains a fast generator to match a reference distribution ( Yin et al., 2024b ; Yin et al., 2024a ) . A score describes the local direction of increasing log density. A pretrained teacher supplies the reference score, while an auxiliary fake-score network estimates the score of the current generated distribution. Their difference guides updates to generated samples. These updates are applied sample by sample, but their direction depends on both distributions. Distribution matching can complement trajectory-based training: rCM combines score regularization with continuous-time consistency training ( Zheng et al., 2025 ) .

> Figure 1: Overview of IDM for one-step generation. Multi-step sampling refines samples iteratively, whereas one-step generation learns a direct noise-to-data map. IDM combines an internal DMD loss with a feature-moment loss in the generator’s EMA feature space. The right panel illustrates idealized alignment with single-evaluation inference.

TwinFlow and APEX obtain this score difference from internally separated velocity branches of the generator ( Cheng et al., 2025 ; Liu et al., 2026a ) . The model-score estimate is still learned, but no separate fake-score network is needed. We adopt this internal construction and ask whether the generator can also supply an explicit constraint on statistics across samples. Although the exact DMD field contains full distributional information, learned score estimates and finite training can leave discrepancies that an additional statistical objective may help reduce.

To motivate such a constraint, we revisit the reverse-KL score difference underlying DMD. Building on the Gaussian-plus-residual score representation ( Wang and Vastola, 2023 ; Wang and Vastola, 2024 ) , we separate a component determined by means and covariances from the remaining score structure in Section 3.3 . This identifies statistics that can be constrained explicitly, while showing why moments alone do not determine the full distribution. We therefore retain the full DMD correction and add a moment loss, testing its benefit through controlled ablations in a shared training setting.

We introduce Intrinsic Distribution Matching ( IDM ) , which augments internal DMD with moment matching in the generator’s own feature space. Its exponential-moving-average (EMA) copy provides a shared representation for generated and reference samples; we match the means and per-coordinate standard deviations of these features. Unlike objectives that obtain features from an external encoder or teacher ( Yang et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2026b ) , this design reuses the generator for both score estimation and feature extraction. Both training signals are obtained from the generator and its EMA copy, without a separate discriminator, score model, or external feature encoder.

We evaluate IDM using Z-Image-Turbo 6B and Qwen-Image 20B with full-parameter and LoRA tuning in Section 5 . LongText-Bench evaluates the Chinese and English long-text rendering fidelity emphasized above, with subsets denoted LongText-CN and LongText-EN ( Geng et al., 2025b ) . At two network evaluations (2 NFE), IDM on Z-Image-Turbo achieves state-of-the-art scores among the compared methods: 94.25% and 90.20%, improving over Z-Image-Turbo++ by 2.63 and 0.32 percentage points, respectively in Table 5 .

Our main contributions are:

- a. Intrinsic distributional supervision. We augment internal DMD with an explicit loss on the means and per-coordinate standard deviations of generator EMA features. This adds statistical supervision across samples without a separate external feature encoder.

- b. Distribution-gradient explanation. Building on the Gaussian-plus-residual score representation, we motivate an explicit moment constraint alongside the full DMD correction.

- c. Few-step training and evaluation. We demonstrate full-parameter and LoRA training on 6B and 20B backbones. Ablations assess the added moment loss, EMA features, and regression choices, while LongText-Bench evaluates fine-grained text fidelity.

### 2 Background

Diffusion and flow-matching models transform noise into data through repeated network evaluations ( Ho et al., 2020 ; Song et al., 2020 ; Lipman et al., 2022 ) . Distillation learns a shorter process ( Salimans and Ho, 2022 ; Song et al., 2023 ) . We use rectified flow to define the generator and then explain the DMD supervision used to train it.

Let $p_{z}=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{d})$ be the noise distribution and $p_{r}$ the reference-data distribution. Rectified flow uses the path $\mathbf{x}_{r,t}=(1-t)\mathbf{x}_{r}+t\bm{\epsilon}$ , with independently sampled $\mathbf{x}_{r}\sim p_{r}$ and $\bm{\epsilon}\sim p_{z}$ ( Liu et al., 2023 ) . Thus $t=0$ denotes clean data and $t=1$ pure noise. A network $\bm{F}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{r,t},t,\mathbf{c})$ regresses the pair velocity $\bm{\epsilon}-\mathbf{x}_{r}$ , where $\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}$ is a text-conditioning vector. At the squared-error optimum it recovers the marginal velocity, the conditional average of pair velocities at the same noisy input and condition. Few-step generation integrates this field from $t=1$ to $t=0$ with a small number of forward-Euler steps. At one network evaluation, the generator is $G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c})=\mathbf{z}-\bm{F}_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},1,\mathbf{c})$ for $\mathbf{z}\sim p_{z}$ .

The generator induces a distribution $p_{\theta}$ . DMD aims to match it to $p_{r}$ through the reverse-KL gradient ( Wang et al., 2023 ; Yin et al., 2024b ) . At a fixed condition and noise level $t\in(0,1)$ , let $p_{\theta,t}$ and $p_{r,t}$ be their noised distributions. A score $\mathbf{s}_{i,t}=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p_{i,t}$ points locally toward increasing log density. Evaluated at the same noised model sample, $\mathbf{s}_{r,t}-\mathbf{s}_{\theta,t}$ points toward a lower model-to-reference density ratio in Section 3.1 . The reference score is supplied by a frozen pretrained teacher. Since the generator provides samples but no tractable score, DMD trains a separate fake-score network on its outputs. This network estimates the generated distribution; the generator itself produces the images. The fake-score estimate must track the changing generator. The reference score guides samples toward regions supported by the target, while the model score accounts for the generator’s current concentration of probability mass. Both enter the distributional update, so the model score must still be learned when a separate network is removed.

For independent $\mathbf{x}\sim p$ and $\bm{\epsilon}\sim p_{z}$ , set $\mathbf{x}_{t}=(1-t)\mathbf{x}+t\bm{\epsilon}$ . For $t\in(0,1)$ , the score $\mathbf{s}_{t}:=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}$ and marginal velocity $\mathbf{v}_{t}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{t}):=\mathbb{E}[\bm{\epsilon}-\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}]$ satisfy

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{s}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=-\frac{\mathbf{x}_{t}+(1-t)\mathbf{v}_{t}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{t})}{t}\,.$$ (1)

Thus a velocity estimate also provides a score estimate in Appendix B.3 . Subtracting two velocities at the same input gives a scaled score difference.

TwinFlow and APEX use two internally separated velocity branches of one generator to estimate the reference and model scores ( Cheng et al., 2025 ; Liu et al., 2026a ) . This removes the separate fake-score network while retaining model-score learning. Following APEX, we use this construction for DMD in Section 4.1 . We next examine the statistics represented by its target field to motivate additional supervision from the generator’s features.


### 3 A Distribution-Gradient View

The score difference in DMD defines a direction for changing the generated distribution. Separating the contribution of means and covariances from the remaining score structure clarifies what moment matching can constrain and why the full DMD correction is still needed.


#### 3.1 Reverse-KL Field

The one-step generator maps $\mathbf{z}\sim p_{z}$ to $\mathbf{x}_{\theta}=G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c})$ , inducing $p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{c})$ ; $p_{r}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{c})$ is the reference law. Here $\mathbf{x}$ is a sample in the generator’s output representation, and conditions follow $p_{c}$ independently of $\mathbf{z}$ . For differentiable positive densities, define $\mathbf{s}_{i}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{c}):=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p_{i}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{c})$ , $i\in\{r,\theta\}$ . At fixed $\mathbf{c}$ , the reverse KL is

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\theta}\|p_{r})=\int p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{c})\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{c})}{p_{r}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{c})}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.$$ (2)

Its sample-space distribution gradient is

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{c})=\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{c})-\mathbf{s}_{r}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{c}).$$ (3)

The descent direction is $-\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}=\mathbf{s}_{r}-\mathbf{s}_{\theta}$ , pointing locally toward a lower model-to-reference density ratio. It changes as the generator distribution changes. Applying the same identity to $p_{\theta,t}$ and $p_{r,t}$ gives $\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL},t}=\mathbf{s}_{\theta,t}-\mathbf{s}_{r,t}$ , the noisy field estimated in DMD. We state the identities below for clean laws under the regularity assumptions in Appendix B.1 ; the decomposition also applies to the noisy laws at each fixed $t$ , with their own means, covariances, and score residuals.

The full field $\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}=\mathbf{s}_{\theta}-\mathbf{s}_{r}$ ( left ) is the sum of a Gaussian moment component ( middle ) and a non-Gaussian residual component ( right ). Solid contours show $p_{i}$ and dashed contours its moment-matched Gaussian $q_{i}$ , for $i\in\{r,\theta\}$ . Equal means and covariances eliminate the Gaussian component but can leave a nonzero residual. All fields use the same evaluation points and arrow scale.


#### 3.2 Parameter Gradient

The field above specifies a sample update. To relate it to training, consider a discrepancy $\mathcal{D}(p_{\theta},p_{r})$ with a differentiable first-variation potential, which describes how redistributing probability mass changes $\mathcal{D}$ . Its spatial gradient specifies the field used in the sample update:

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{c}):=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\frac{\delta\mathcal{D}(p_{\theta},p_{r})}{\delta p_{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{c}).$$ (4)

Under the assumptions in Appendix B.1 ,

[Tableau] $$\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{D}(p_{\theta},p_{r})={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{z}\sim p_{z}}\left[\underbrace{[J_{\theta}G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c})]^{\top}}_{\text{Jacobian transpose}}\underbrace{\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{D}}(G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c})\mid\mathbf{c})}_{\text{distribution gradient}}\right]\,.$$ (5)

Here $J_{\theta}G_{\theta}:=\partial G_{\theta}/\partial\theta$ ; backpropagation applies its transpose, and training averages over $\mathbf{c}$ . For reverse KL, the first variation is $\log(p_{\theta}/p_{r})+1$ , recovering Eq. 3 . Thus the score difference gives the distributional direction that is passed back through the generator. This separates the distributional change that reduces the discrepancy from the parameter update that realizes this change. The decomposition below concerns the former and does not depend on a particular generator architecture.


#### 3.3 Decomposition of the Reverse-KL Field

To identify which statistics this field contains, let $p$ be a smooth positive density with finite second moment, mean $\mu_{p}$ , and positive-definite covariance $\Sigma_{p}$ . Its moment-matched Gaussian is $q_{p}:=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{p},\Sigma_{p})$ , with score $\mathbf{s}_{q_{p}}(\mathbf{x})=-\Sigma_{p}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mu_{p})$ . The score of $p$ decomposes exactly as

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})=\underbrace{\mathbf{s}_{q_{p}}(\mathbf{x})}_{=-\Sigma_{p}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mu_{p})}+\underbrace{\bm{\eta}_{p}(\mathbf{x})}_{\text{non-Gaussian score residual}}\,,$$ (6)

where $\bm{\eta}_{p}:=\mathbf{s}_{p}-\mathbf{s}_{q_{p}}$ contains the score structure beyond this Gaussian approximation ( Wang and Vastola, 2023 ; Wang and Vastola, 2024 ) . At fixed $\mathbf{c}$ , use $q_{i}:=q_{p_{i}}$ and $\bm{\eta}_{i}:=\bm{\eta}_{p_{i}}$ for the conditional laws $p_{i}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{c})$ , $i\in\{r,\theta\}$ . Subtracting their decompositions gives

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{c})=\underbrace{\left(\mathbf{s}_{q_{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{c})-\mathbf{s}_{q_{r}}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{c})\right)}_{\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{G}}:\;\text{Gaussian moment component}}+\underbrace{\left(\bm{\eta}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{c})-\bm{\eta}_{r}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{c})\right)}_{\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{nG}}:\;\text{non-Gaussian residual component}}.$$ (7)

The Gaussian component depends only on means and covariances. Two distributions can share these moments yet have different shapes, leaving a nonzero residual in Figure 3 . Replacing $p_{i}$ by $p_{i,t}$ gives the same decomposition for the noisy field used in DMD.

Under the integrability and boundary conditions in Appendix B.2.2 ,

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}_{p}[\bm{\eta}_{p}(\mathbf{x})]=\mathbf{0},\qquad\mathbb{E}_{p}[\bm{\eta}_{p}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}^{\top}]=\mathbf{0}_{d\times d}.$$ (8)

Each score residual is orthogonal to constant and linear functions under its own distribution; their difference need not be orthogonal under either distribution. The moment-matched Gaussian is used to analyze the score; the generated samples are not assumed to follow a Gaussian distribution. The residual does not identify a single higher-order moment: it collects all score structure left after subtracting the Gaussian score for the corresponding distribution.

Exact DMD already contains both components. In practice, its learned score estimates and finite training need not align all statistics accurately. The decomposition identifies moments as quantities that can be constrained explicitly, while the residual explains why moment matching alone is insufficient. We therefore keep the full DMD correction and add a loss on feature means and standard deviations. Features from the generator’s EMA copy provide a shared representation without introducing an external encoder. This is an additional feature-space objective motivated by the decomposition, not a separate estimate of its sample-space Gaussian or residual component. The ablations in Section 5.3 test whether this explicit constraint improves generation.


### 4 Intrinsic Distribution Matching

Intrinsic Distribution Matching ( IDM ) retains internal DMD supervision and adds the feature-moment constraint motivated in Section 3.3 . The DMD loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{score}}$ uses the full internal score-difference estimate; $\mathcal{L}_{\text{pop}}$ explicitly matches statistics of generator EMA features. We first define how the DMD correction supervises velocity and endpoint predictions, then describe the feature constraint, the empirical moment estimates, and the gradients used in joint training.


#### 4.1 DMD Loss

At a fixed condition $\mathbf{c}$ , let $p_{r,t}$ and $p_{\theta,t}$ denote the reference and model marginals under the linear noising construction of Section 2 , with scores $\mathbf{s}_{r,t},\mathbf{s}_{\theta,t}$ and exact marginal velocities $\mathbf{v}^{*}_{r,t},\mathbf{v}^{*}_{\theta,t}$ . Applying Eq. 1 to both at the same point $\mathbf{x}_{t}$ gives, for $t\in(0,1)$ :

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{d}_{t}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{t}):=\mathbf{v}^{*}_{\theta,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{v}^{*}_{r,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\frac{t}{1{-}t}\left(\mathbf{s}_{r,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{s}_{\theta,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right)\!,$$ (9)

so $\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL},t}:=\mathbf{s}_{\theta,t}-\mathbf{s}_{r,t}=-\frac{1-t}{t}\,\mathbf{d}_{t}^{*}$ is the reverse-KL field on these noisy marginals in Eq. 3 .

Following APEX ( Liu et al., 2026a ) , we separate the reference and model velocity branches by negating the condition vector:

[Tableau] $$\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{r,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{c}):=\bm{F}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\mathbf{c}),\qquad\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{c}):=\bm{F}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,-\mathbf{c}),$$ (10)

Here $-\mathbf{c}$ selects the model branch for condition $\mathbf{c}$ , and $\widehat{\mathbf{d}}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{c}):=\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{c})-\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{r,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{c})$ is their pointwise difference. This interpretation requires both branches to approximate their marginal velocities.

The same DMD correction can supervise velocity predictions or their corresponding clean-sample endpoints. We compare these regression forms and their combinations in Section 5.3 to select the training configuration.

For fixed input $\mathbf{x}_{t}$ at time $t\in(0,1]$ , the endpoint prediction is $\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\mathbf{c}):=\mathbf{x}_{t}-t\bm{F}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\mathbf{c})$ . At $t=1$ and $\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{z}$ , this equals $G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c})$ ; predictions at intermediate times need not follow the one-step output law $p_{\theta}$ . Let $\mathop{\operatorname{sg}}\nolimits$ denote stop-gradient. Set $\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\mathop{\operatorname{sg}}\nolimits}:=\mathop{\operatorname{sg}}\nolimits(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\mathbf{c}))$ and re-noise it as $\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{0,t^{\prime}}^{\mathop{\operatorname{sg}}\nolimits}:=(1-t^{\prime})\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\mathop{\operatorname{sg}}\nolimits}+t^{\prime}\bm{\epsilon}$ , where the re-noising time $t^{\prime}\in(0,1)$ and $\bm{\epsilon}\sim p_{z}$ are sampled independently of each other and of the regression input. Evaluate the branch difference $\widehat{\mathbf{d}}_{t^{\prime}}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{0,t^{\prime}}^{\mathop{\operatorname{sg}}\nolimits}\mid\mathbf{c})$ at this re-noised prediction. Its weighted, normalized value is detached to obtain $\widehat{\mathbf{d}}_{t^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{DMD}}$ ; without processing, the correction is $\mathop{\operatorname{sg}}\nolimits\!\left(\widehat{\mathbf{d}}_{t^{\prime}}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{0,t^{\prime}}^{\mathop{\operatorname{sg}}\nolimits}\mid\mathbf{c})\right)$ . The DMD targets are

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{t}^{\mathrm{DMD}}$ $\displaystyle:=\mathop{\operatorname{sg}}\nolimits\!\left(\bm{F}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\mathbf{c})-\widehat{\mathbf{d}}_{t^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{DMD}}\right),$ (11) $\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\mathrm{DMD}}$ $\displaystyle:=\mathop{\operatorname{sg}}\nolimits\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}-t\mathbf{v}_{t}^{\mathrm{DMD}}\right).$

Subtracting this correction from the velocity target adds $t\widehat{\mathbf{d}}_{t^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{DMD}}$ to the detached clean-sample prediction. Thus the same correction defines both targets. The combined squared regression loss, with both targets detached, averages over conditions, regression inputs and times, and re-noising draws:

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{score}}(\theta):=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\underbrace{\|\bm{F}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\mathbf{c})-\mathbf{v}_{t}^{\mathrm{DMD}}\|_{2}^{2}}_{\ell_{v}(t)}+\underbrace{\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\mathbf{c})-\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\mathrm{DMD}}\|_{2}^{2}}_{\ell_{x}(t)}\right].$$ (12)

At fixed $(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)$ , these squared losses satisfy $\ell_{x}(t)=t^{2}\ell_{v}(t)$ , so equal weights give an effective factor $1+t^{2}$ on velocity regression in Appendix B.4 . A separate discussion of conditional-mean regression and covariance appears in Appendix B.7 .

#### 4.2 Feature-Moment Loss

Alongside the learned DMD correction, we compare generated and reference samples in the feature space of the generator’s EMA copy. The EMA supplies the representation, while reference samples provide the target statistics for moment matching.

$\phi:=\phi_{\bar{\theta}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{C}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\phi}}$ mean-pools the last-layer hidden activations of the generator’s EMA copy into one feature vector per sample. Generated and reference samples use the noising construction of Section 2 at high noise levels, with noising arguments suppressed in $\phi$ . Here $\bar{\theta}$ denotes EMA parameters and $d_{\phi}$ the feature dimension; each update fixes $\bar{\theta}$ while retaining input derivatives. We compare this choice with frozen-encoder ( Yang et al., 2026 ) , teacher-feature ( Zhang et al., 2026b ) , and live self-feature alternatives in Section 5.3 .

Let $\mu_{i}^{\phi}$ and $\nu_{i,j}^{\phi}$ denote the mean and per-coordinate variance of $\phi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{c})$ under the joint law $(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{c})\sim p_{i}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{c})p_{c}$ , also averaging over feature-extraction noise, for $i\in\{r,\theta\}$ . These statistics pool across conditions rather than matching conditional distributions. We match these pooled statistics with

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{pop}}=\|\mu_{\theta}^{\phi}-\mu_{r}^{\phi}\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\phi}}\left(\sqrt{\nu_{\theta,j}^{\phi}}-\sqrt{\nu_{r,j}^{\phi}}\right)^{2}\!.$$ (13)

This is the squared 2-Wasserstein distance between the diagonal Gaussians $\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i}^{\phi},\diag(\nu_{i}^{\phi}))$ ; it is non-negative and vanishes exactly when their feature means and per-coordinate standard deviations match. At the feature level, the mean term shifts the average toward the reference mean, while the standard-deviation term adjusts the spread around that average. These roles are explicit in the empirical gradient in Prop. B.20 . Their effect on images is mediated by the fixed EMA feature Jacobian and the generator, so the objective does not prescribe a pixel-wise correction. A nonlinear feature map can capture properties not determined by raw-sample means and covariances. Matching its moments therefore constrains statistics in a learned representation. The loss still uses only pooled feature means and per-coordinate standard deviations; it does not estimate the sample-space Gaussian component or match the full feature distribution.

Queues $\mathcal{Q}_{\theta}$ and $\mathcal{Q}_{r}$ store up to $K=16384$ detached model and reference features, respectively. Model moments combine $B$ current-batch features with $\mathcal{Q}_{\theta}$ ; reference moments use $\mathcal{Q}_{r}$ , giving $B+K$ and $K$ features with full queues. These moments define $\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{pop}}$ . Queue entries come from earlier model and EMA states, so the moments approximate current population statistics. Prop. B.20 gives the exact empirical-loss gradient at fixed queues in Appendix B.6 .


#### 4.3 Training Objective

We use the RCGM loss ( Sun and Lin, 2025 ) , which supervises generation across time intervals through recursive consistent velocity-field estimation, as $\mathcal{L}_{\text{base}}$ . The main generator objective is

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{{IDM} }}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{\text{base}}(\theta)+\lambda_{\text{score}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{score}}(\theta)+\lambda_{\text{pop}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{pop}}.$$ (14)

Each iteration generates current samples and extracts their high-noise EMA features. The current features retain their computation graph and are combined with the detached queues to evaluate $\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{pop}}$ . Its gradient passes through the fixed EMA network to the current samples and generator; queued features receive no gradients. Across iterations, we refresh the queues with detached features and update the EMA as $\bar{\theta}\leftarrow 0.99\bar{\theta}+0.01\theta$ . The weights $\lambda_{\text{score}}$ and $\lambda_{\text{pop}}$ remain fixed in Section 5.1 . Feature extraction and queues are used only during training. Freezing the EMA parameters does not freeze the current features: their values change with the generated images. Detaching these features would also remove the moment-loss gradient to the generator. The detached queues provide historical statistics without retaining computation graphs from previous iterations. Auxiliary flow matching $\mathcal{L}_{\text{fake}}$ trains the internal model-velocity branch on detached generated samples and also updates the shared parameters in Appendix B.5 . One-step inference generates $\mathbf{x}_{\theta}=G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c})$ in one forward pass; the same checkpoint also supports few-step sampling.


### 5 Experiments

Our experiments assess the added value of $\mathcal{L}_{\text{pop}}$ over internal DMD, the suitability of generator EMA features relative to an external encoder, and the choice of velocity and endpoint regression. System-level comparisons evaluate the complete training setting; controlled ablations examine these individual choices under the shared data, backbone, and sampling budget. Throughout this section, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{pop}}$ refers to the empirical loss $\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{pop}}$ .

#### 5.1 Experimental Setup

We initialize IDM from Z-Image-Turbo 6B ( Team, 2025 ) and Qwen-Image 20B ( Wu et al., 2025a ) , using full-parameter tuning and LoRA with ranks 32 and 64. We denote the resulting models by IDM (Z-Image) and IDM (Qwen-Image); the former uses the Turbo checkpoint rather than the undistilled Z-Image base model.

The reference data defining $p_{r}$ combine ShareGPT-4o ( Chen et al., 2025c ) , BLIP-3o ( Chen et al., 2025a ) , and samples synthesized with Qwen-Image-2512 and Z-Image-Turbo: 600K from FLUX-Reason-6M prompts ( Fang et al., 2025 ) and 5M from text-rendering prompts. Compared with the 200K poster-prompt samples used by APEX ( Liu et al., 2026a ) , this larger text-focused collection provides more training examples for dense text rendering.

All models use BF16, FlashAttention4, and FSDP2 with global batch size 64. Training runs for 15K steps on Z-Image-Turbo 6B and 10K steps for Qwen-Image 20B LoRA, using 32 $\times$ H100 80GB and 8 $\times$ A100 80GB GPUs. The default weights are $\lambda_{\text{score}}=1$ and $\lambda_{\text{pop}}=0.5$ , fixed throughout training. Each trained checkpoint is evaluated at both 1 and 2 NFE without changing the training loss weights for either sampling budget.

We follow the same standard benchmark protocols used for the APEX comparisons, reporting each method at its stated NFE budget. Published reference results retain their training data and configuration; the internal IDM ablations use the shared configuration above. We evaluate compositional prompt following with GenEval ( Ghosh et al., 2023 ) , FID and CLIP score on MJHQ-30K ( Li et al., 2024a ) , detailed prompt following on DPG-Bench ( Hu et al., 2024 ) , and world knowledge on WISE ( Niu et al., 2025 ) . Z-Image comparisons and ablations additionally report OneIG ( Chang et al., 2026 ) and LongText-Bench ( Geng et al., 2025b ) ; LongText-CN and LongText-EN measure Chinese and English long-text rendering, respectively.


#### 5.2 Main Comparison

At 2 NFE, IDM (Z-Image) achieves 94.25% on LongText-CN and 90.20% on LongText-EN, the highest scores among the compared methods at this budget in Table 5 . These improve over Z-Image-Turbo++ by 2.63 and 0.32 percentage points and over APEX by 3.87 and 1.44 points, respectively. This directly supports the fine-grained text-rendering objective introduced above. The table places external comparisons alongside the controlled ablations, allowing the contribution of each loss to be examined.

With full-parameter tuning, IDM achieves GenEval scores of 0.78 on Z-Image and 0.91 on Qwen-Image at 2 NFE in Table 1 . On Qwen-Image under the same NFE and tuning setting, APEX scores 0.90; FID is 6.34 for IDM and 6.44 for APEX, with latencies of 0.44 and 0.45 seconds. Thus, the quality improvement comes at comparable sampling latency. At 1 NFE, Qwen-Image IDM reaches 0.90 GenEval at 0.35 seconds per sample, giving a faster sampling option with the same trained checkpoint.

The same full-parameter checkpoints improve from 1 to 2 NFE on both backbones: Z-Image FID decreases from 7.13 to 6.71 and Qwen-Image FID from 6.77 to 6.34, while each gains 0.01 GenEval. Thus the learned objectives remain useful when the sampling budget allows an additional network evaluation. With rank-64 LoRA, Qwen-Image IDM trains 0.4B parameters and reaches 0.90 GenEval at 2 NFE, compared with 0.91 under full tuning. This setting retains most of the compositional performance while updating substantially fewer parameters. DPG-Bench and WISE provide complementary evaluations of prompt following and world knowledge. Moment matching with DINOv2 or generator EMA features improves all five metrics over the internal DMD control. To select how the DMD correction supervises predictions, we compare $v$ regression, $x$ regression, and their combinations while retaining $\mathcal{L}_{\text{pop}}$ .

Both single-regression controls therefore include the added moment constraint, so this block tests how to apply DMD within the combined objective. Since $\ell_{x}(t)=t^{2}\ell_{v}(t)$ in Eq. 12 , these choices change time weighting and update magnitude. Equal-weight $v{+}x$ reaches 77.81 GenEval, versus 76.64 for $v$ only and 77.26 for $x$ only; $2x{+}v$ and $x{+}2v$ reach 77.71 and 77.59. Equal weights lead on the five aggregate metrics and are our empirical default, although $x{+}2v$ gives a higher position score, 52.00 versus 51.25. The mixed losses apply alternative weightings to the same detached correction; these results do not establish independent distributional information in the two regression terms.

IDM builds on APEX’s internal velocity-difference construction ( Liu et al., 2026a ) , adding endpoint regression and EMA feature-moment matching in Figure 5 . The published APEX reference uses a smaller training collection, so the comparison reflects the complete training settings. IDM scores 90.20 on LongText-EN versus APEX’s 88.76, while APEX leads on DPG-Bench, 88.64 versus 86.82. Within the IDM training setting, the loss-removal and feature-map controls separate these objective choices from differences in training data.

### 6 Conclusion

We presented Intrinsic Distribution Matching ( IDM ) , which builds on internal DMD and adds an explicit constraint on generator EMA feature statistics. The reverse-KL field decomposition motivates retaining full score-difference supervision while additionally matching feature means and standard deviations. Controlled ablations show gains over the internal DMD baseline from EMA feature moments, with performance close to DINOv2. On Z-Image-Turbo 6B, IDM reaches 94.25% and 90.20% on Chinese and English LongText-Bench, the highest scores among the compared two-NFE methods. Experiments on backbones up to 20B parameters support using the generator’s predictions and representations together for few-step text-to-image generation.

### Appendix A Related Work

Progressive distillation, consistency models, shortcut models, and MeanFlow reduce sampling steps by learning transitions or relations along a generative trajectory ( Salimans and Ho, 2022 ; Song et al., 2023 ; Lu and Song, 2024 ; Frans et al., 2024 ; Geng et al., 2025a ; Geng et al., 2026 ) . Duality Models jointly predict velocities and flow maps with a shared backbone ( Sun et al., 2026b ) . Distributional approaches use adversarial or score-based supervision to train generated outputs directly ( Sauer et al., 2023 ; Sauer et al., 2024 ; Yin et al., 2024b ; Yin et al., 2024a ) . rCM combines continuous-time consistency with score regularization and demonstrates their complementarity on large image and video models ( Zheng et al., 2025 ) . LLaDA-Image-Turbo supports inference in two to four steps, while Vidu S2 targets real-time interactive video generation and editing ( Chen et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2026a ) . IDM studies distributional supervision from the generator itself.

DMD estimates the score difference between noised reference and generated distributions using a reference network and an online fake-score model ( Yin et al., 2024b ) ; DMD2 adds adversarial supervision and techniques for stable few-step distillation ( Yin et al., 2024a ) . Related work connects adversarial and score-based updates through sample-space fields ( Franceschi et al., 2023 ; Weber, 2023 ) and extends distribution matching to broader divergence families ( Xu et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2026 ) . FlowCPO develops an offline forward-KL formulation for preference alignment in flow models ( Han et al., 2026b ) . TwinFlow and APEX estimate score differences through internally separated velocity branches ( Cheng et al., 2025 ; Liu et al., 2026a ) . IDM adds a feature-moment loss to internal DMD.

GMMN and MMD-GAN use moment or kernel discrepancies for generative training ( Li et al., 2015 ; Li et al., 2017 ) . FD-Loss turns a full-covariance Gaussian feature discrepancy into a training objective and uses queue or EMA estimators to connect population statistics with batch gradients ( Heusel et al., 2017 ; Yang et al., 2026 ) . Three-Body Scattering derives one-step regression targets from energy-distance interactions in frozen features ( Sun et al., 2026a ) . Drifting and W-Flow construct distributional updates through kernel interactions and batchwise optimal transport, respectively ( Deng et al., 2026 ; Han et al., 2026a ) , while Teacher-Feature Drifting uses representations from a pretrained teacher ( Zhang et al., 2026b ) . IDM instead matches feature means and per-coordinate standard deviations in the generator’s EMA feature space.

The Gaussian-plus-residual score representation of Wang and Vastola (2023) ; Wang and Vastola (2024) underlies our reverse-KL field decomposition and motivates an additional feature-moment constraint. REPA shows that diffusion-transformer hidden states contain useful semantic information and can be strengthened through external representation alignment ( Yu et al., 2024 ) . Embedded Representation Warmup adds a dedicated representation-alignment stage before generative training ( Liu et al., 2025 ) . Our EMA feature map reuses such internal representations and evolves with the generator; its utility is evaluated against external, teacher, and live self-feature alternatives in Section 5.3 .

Recent text-to-image models, including Qwen-Image and X-Omni, place increasing emphasis on accurate text rendering ( Wu et al., 2025a ; Geng et al., 2025b ) . LongText-Bench evaluates extended Chinese and English text across multiple scenarios ( Geng et al., 2025b ) , complementing the broader dimensions assessed by OneIG ( Chang et al., 2026 ) . Z-Image-Turbo++ reports LongText as a sensitive measure of text quality under a limited sampling budget ( Liu et al., 2026b ) . We focus on LongText performance at 2 NFE.


### Appendix B Theoretical Proofs

The distribution-gradient and score identities are stated at fixed condition $\mathbf{c}\sim p_{c}$ , with conditioning omitted when unambiguous; training averages the resulting conditional gradients over $\mathbf{c}$ . In contrast, feature moments pool samples from the joint law $p_{i}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{c})p_{c}(\mathbf{c})$ . A subscript $t$ denotes a noisy marginal, a star an exact marginal quantity, and a hat a learned or empirical estimate. Thus $\mathcal{L}_{\text{pop}}$ denotes the population moment loss and $\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{pop}}$ its empirical counterpart. For full queues of equal size $K$ , the model estimator uses $N_{\phi}=B+K$ features, where $B$ is the global current-batch size; the reference estimator uses the $K$ queued reference features.


#### B.1 Unified Gradient Structure


##### B.1.1 First variation form

We give regularity assumptions and prove the general identity behind ( 5 ). We use the standard first-variation calculation for functionals of a density obeying a continuity equation, in the sense of gradient flows of probability measures ( Ambrosio and Savaré, 2007 ) . The pushforward step converts the sample-space integral into an expectation over the noise prior and clarifies its dependence on generator parameters.

For a distributional discrepancy $\mathcal{D}(p_{\theta},p_{r})$ admitting a differentiable first-variation potential $\psi(\mathbf{x}):=\delta\mathcal{D}(p_{\theta},p_{r})/\delta p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})$ , the sample-space distribution gradient is

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{x}):=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\psi(\mathbf{x}).$$ (15)

Condition (b) is an assumption rather than a consequence of the generator construction. For a locally Lipschitz deterministic map $G_{\theta}(\cdot,\mathbf{c}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}$ , the area formula implies that a Jacobian $\partial G_{\theta}/\partial\mathbf{z}$ singular on a set of positive $p_{z}$ -measure produces a singular component in the image law. The full model law then has no Lebesgue density, so its clean score $\mathbf{s}_{\theta}=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p_{\theta}$ is not defined as assumed. This is not a pathological case. A generator that collapses a direction of the seed space, for instance one that has learned to ignore part of $\mathbf{z}$ , produces exactly this degeneracy. It is the same manifold-support obstruction identified by Arjovsky et al. (2017) for implicit generators, which there motivates replacing $f$ -divergences by a weaker metric; we instead retain the divergence and carry the density assumption explicitly.

The clean-distribution results in Section 3 therefore assume smooth, strictly positive densities on a common domain, together with the integrability and boundary conditions required by each result. The factorization ( 5 ), reverse-KL field ( 3 ), and decomposition ( 7 ) are exact within that setting. This restriction is distinct from the noisy-marginal setting: adding independent nondegenerate Gaussian noise gives a smooth positive density for every $t\in(0,1)$ , even when the clean law is singular. The score–velocity identity in Appendix B.3 therefore does not require a clean density; its finite-second-moment assumption suffices for the stated velocity regression. A noisy reverse-KL objective additionally requires the relevant divergence and gradient integrals to exist. The practical losses in Section 4 remain computable without evaluating clean scores. Singular clean laws invalidate the clean-field interpretation, but do not by themselves invalidate the noisy score fields or feature-moment objective.

Fix an arbitrary direction $\Delta\theta$ and write $\theta_{\epsilon}=\theta+\epsilon\Delta\theta$ . For $\mathbf{x}=G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c})$ , the generated sample has the first-order expansion:

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{x}_{\theta_{\epsilon}}=G_{\theta+\epsilon\Delta\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c})=\mathbf{x}+\epsilon\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{z}}+o(\epsilon),\qquad\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{z}}:=J_{\theta}G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c})\Delta\theta.$$ (16)

The corresponding velocity field is the conditional mean displacement of samples currently located at $\mathbf{x}$ :

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}):=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{z}}\mid G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c})=\mathbf{x}\right].$$ (17)

We first identify the density perturbation induced by this sample displacement. For any smooth compactly supported test function $\varphi$ , the pushforward definition and ( 16 ) give

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\int\varphi(\mathbf{x})p_{\theta_{\epsilon}}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}$ $\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}\left[\varphi(G_{\theta+\epsilon\Delta\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c}))\right]\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}$ (18) $\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}\left[\varphi(\mathbf{x}+\epsilon\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{z}})\right]\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}$ (19) $\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}\left[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi(G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c}))^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{z}}\right]$ (20) $\displaystyle=\int\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi(\mathbf{x})^{\top}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.$ (21)

The last equality is exactly the conditional-expectation definition of $\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})$ . Since ( 21 ) holds for all such $\varphi$ , the density path satisfies the continuity equation in the weak sense:

[Tableau] $$\partial_{\epsilon}p_{\theta_{\epsilon}}(\mathbf{x})\big|_{\epsilon=0}=-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot(p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})).$$ (22)

Now consider a distributional discrepancy $\mathcal{D}(p_{\theta},p_{r})$ with first variation

[Tableau] $$\psi(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{\delta\mathcal{D}(p_{\theta},p_{r})}{\delta p_{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}),$$ (23)

meaning that, along the density path $p_{\theta_{\epsilon}}$ ,

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\mathcal{D}(p_{\theta_{\epsilon}},p_{r})\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}$ $\displaystyle=\int\psi(\mathbf{x})\,\partial_{\epsilon}p_{\theta_{\epsilon}}(\mathbf{x})\big|_{\epsilon=0}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}$ (24) $\displaystyle=-\int\psi(\mathbf{x})\,\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot(p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}$ (25) $\displaystyle=\int\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\psi(\mathbf{x})\cdot p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},$ (26)

where the last equality is integration by parts, using the vanishing boundary term in Assump. 1 (c). By ( 17 ), any integrable vector field $\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x})$ satisfies the following pushforward-expectation identity (converting an expectation under $p_{\theta}$ to one under the noise prior):

[Tableau] $$\int p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}\left[\mathbf{w}(G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c}))^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{z}}\right].$$ (27)

Taking $\mathbf{w}=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\psi$ in ( 27 ) gives

[Tableau] $$\int p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})\,\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\psi(\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}\left[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\psi(G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c}))^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{z}}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}\left[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\psi(G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c}))^{\top}J_{\theta}G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c})\Delta\theta\right].$$ (28)

Combining ( 26 ) and ( 28 ), the directional derivative in the direction $\Delta\theta$ is

[Tableau] $$\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\mathcal{D}(p_{\theta_{\epsilon}},p_{r})\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}=\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}\left[\bigl[J_{\theta}G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c})\bigr]^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\psi(G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c}))\right],\Delta\theta\right\rangle.$$ (29)

Since $\Delta\theta$ is arbitrary,

[Tableau] $$\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{D}(p_{\theta},p_{r})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}\left[\bigl[J_{\theta}G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c})\bigr]^{\top}\underbrace{\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\psi(G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c}))}_{\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{D}}(G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c}))}\right].$$ (30)

Thus the sample-space distribution gradient in ( 5 ) is $\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{x})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\psi(\mathbf{x})$ whenever the discrepancy admits the first variation $\psi=\delta\mathcal{D}(p_{\theta},p_{r})/\delta p_{\theta}$ .


##### B.1.2 $f$ -divergence specialization

Here $\rho(\mathbf{x}):=p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})/p_{r}(\mathbf{x})$ denotes the density ratio.

The following template is the $f$ -divergence distribution-matching result of Xu et al. (2025) , whose variational form goes back to $f$ -GAN ( Nowozin et al., 2016 ) . We restate it in the present notation, and add in Appendix B.1.3 the distinction between an identified $f$ -divergence first variation and a detached optimal-discriminator update field, which the two coincide on only for particular generators.

Let $G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c})=\mathbf{x}$ be the generator at fixed $\mathbf{c}$ and $h:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}$ be a twice continuously differentiable convex divergence generator with $h(1)=0$ , and define

[Tableau] $$D_{h}(p_{\theta}\|p_{r})=\int p_{r}(\mathbf{x})h(\rho(\mathbf{x}))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},\qquad\rho(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})}{p_{r}(\mathbf{x})}.$$ (31)

Assume Assump. 1 applies to $\mathcal{L}[p_{\theta}]=D_{h}(p_{\theta}\|p_{r})$ , and assume $p_{\theta}$ and $p_{r}$ are smooth and strictly positive on an open set containing $\operatorname{supp}(p_{\theta})$ . Then the distribution gradient is

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{g}_{h}(\mathbf{x})=h^{\prime\prime}(\rho(\mathbf{x}))\,\rho(\mathbf{x})\,(\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{s}_{r}(\mathbf{x})),$$ (32)

and

[Tableau] $$\nabla_{\theta}D_{h}(p_{\theta}\|p_{r})={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{z}\sim p_{z}}\left[\bigl[J_{\theta}G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c})\bigr]^{\top}\left[w(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{s}_{r}(\mathbf{x}))\right]_{\mathbf{x}=G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c})}\right],$$ (33)

where $w(\mathbf{x})=h^{\prime\prime}(\rho(\mathbf{x}))\rho(\mathbf{x})$ .

We first compute the first variation with respect to the model density $p_{\theta}$ , while keeping $p_{r}$ fixed. Let $p_{\epsilon}=p_{\theta}+\epsilon\,\delta p$ be an admissible smooth perturbation with $\int\delta p(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=0$ , and define $\rho_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{x}):=p_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{x})/p_{r}(\mathbf{x})$ . Since $p_{r}$ is fixed, we have

[Tableau] $$\rho_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{x})=\rho(\mathbf{x})+\epsilon\,\frac{\delta p(\mathbf{x})}{p_{r}(\mathbf{x})}.$$ (34)

Therefore the derivative is

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\epsilon}D_{h}(p_{\epsilon}\|p_{r})\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}$ $\displaystyle=\int p_{r}(\mathbf{x})\,\left[\frac{d}{d\epsilon}h\left(\rho_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{x})\right)\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}\,\right]\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}$ (35) $\displaystyle=\int p_{r}(\mathbf{x})\,\left[\frac{d}{d\epsilon}h\left(\rho(\mathbf{x})+\epsilon\,\frac{\delta p(\mathbf{x})}{p_{r}(\mathbf{x})}\right)\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}\,\right]\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}$ (36) $\displaystyle=\int p_{r}(\mathbf{x})\,h^{\prime}(\rho(\mathbf{x}))\,\frac{\delta p(\mathbf{x})}{p_{r}(\mathbf{x})}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}$ (37) $\displaystyle=\int h^{\prime}(\rho(\mathbf{x}))\,\delta p(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,.$ (38)

By definition, the first-variation potential is any function $\psi_{h}$ whose pairing with every admissible density perturbation $\delta p$ gives the directional derivative:

[Tableau] $$\frac{d}{d\epsilon}D_{h}(p_{\theta}+\epsilon\,\delta p\|p_{r})\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}=\int\psi_{h}(\mathbf{x})\,\delta p(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.$$ (39)

Comparing this defining identity ( 39 ) with ( 38 ), we can identify

[Tableau] $$\psi_{h}(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{\delta D_{h}}{\delta p_{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})=h^{\prime}(\rho(\mathbf{x})).$$ (40)

This identification is unique up to an additive constant on the support of admissible perturbations, because $\int\delta p(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=0$ and

[Tableau] $$\int\bigl(\psi_{h}(\mathbf{x})+c_{0}\bigr)\delta p(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\int\psi_{h}(\mathbf{x})\delta p(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}$$ (41)

for any scalar constant $c_{0}$ (distinct from the conditioning vector $\mathbf{c}$ ).

We now compute the sample-space field. By the chain rule,

[Tableau] $$\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\psi_{h}(\mathbf{x})=h^{\prime\prime}(\rho(\mathbf{x}))\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\rho(\mathbf{x}).$$ (42)

It remains to express $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\rho$ in terms of the two scores. Since $\rho=p_{\theta}/p_{r}$ and both densities are positive,

[Tableau] $$\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\rho(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})}{p_{r}(\mathbf{x})}-\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}p_{r}(\mathbf{x})}{p_{r}(\mathbf{x})^{2}}.$$ (43)

Using $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}p_{\theta}=p_{\theta}\mathbf{s}_{\theta}$ and $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}p_{r}=p_{r}\mathbf{s}_{r}$ , this becomes

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\rho(\mathbf{x})$ $\displaystyle=\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})}{p_{r}(\mathbf{x})}-\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})p_{r}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{s}_{r}(\mathbf{x})}{p_{r}(\mathbf{x})^{2}}$ $\displaystyle=\rho(\mathbf{x})\bigl(\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{s}_{r}(\mathbf{x})\bigr).$ (44)

Substituting this identity into ( 42 ) gives

[Tableau] $$\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\psi_{h}(\mathbf{x})=h^{\prime\prime}(\rho(\mathbf{x}))\,\rho(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{s}_{r}(\mathbf{x})).$$ (45)

Therefore the distribution gradient is

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{g}_{h}(\mathbf{x})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\psi_{h}(\mathbf{x})=h^{\prime\prime}(\rho(\mathbf{x}))\,\rho(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{s}_{r}(\mathbf{x})).$$ (46)

Finally, applying the general first-variation template ( 30 ) to $\mathcal{L}[p_{\theta}]=D_{h}(p_{\theta}\|p_{r})$ yields

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}D_{h}(p_{\theta}\|p_{r})$ $\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{z}\sim p_{z}}\left[\bigl[J_{\theta}G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c})\bigr]^{\top}\mathbf{g}_{h}(G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c}))\right]$ $\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{z}\sim p_{z}}\left[\bigl[J_{\theta}G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c})\bigr]^{\top}\left[h^{\prime\prime}(\rho(\mathbf{x}))\,\rho(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{s}_{r}(\mathbf{x}))\right]_{\mathbf{x}=G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c})}\right].$ (47)

With $w(\mathbf{x})=h^{\prime\prime}(\rho(\mathbf{x}))\rho(\mathbf{x})$ , this is exactly ( 33 ).


##### B.1.3 Important special cases

The density-ratio-weighted form $w(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{s}_{\theta}-\mathbf{s}_{r})$ with $w=h^{\prime\prime}(\rho)\rho$ is the one-step $f$ -divergence template of Xu et al. (2025) , later extended to broader divergence families by Wang et al. (2026) ; reading the optimal-discriminator field of GANs as a weighted score difference follows Yi et al. (2023) .

For $h(\rho)=\rho\log\rho$ , we have $h^{\prime\prime}(\rho)=1/\rho$ , hence $w=\rho h^{\prime\prime}(\rho)=1$ . The distribution gradient is therefore

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{s}_{r}(\mathbf{x}).$$ (48)

This is the ideal reverse-KL field; the score-difference component of practical DMD estimates its noisy-marginal counterpart with approximate reference and online fake score models. It is not the complete DMD objective: the original method also uses an LPIPS regression term on pre-generated teacher pairs, and its finite-training online fake-score estimate can be lagged or biased ( Yin et al., 2024b ) . The corresponding sample-space descent direction is $-\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}=\mathbf{s}_{r}-\mathbf{s}_{\theta}$ .

Using twice the conventional equal-mixture JSD generator (the scale is chosen to match the saturating GAN field),

[Tableau] $$h_{\mathrm{JSD}}(\rho)=\rho\log\rho-(1+\rho)\log\frac{1+\rho}{2},$$ (49)

we have

[Tableau] $$h_{\mathrm{JSD}}^{\prime\prime}(\rho)=\frac{1}{\rho(1+\rho)},\qquad w_{\mathrm{JSD}}(\rho)=\frac{1}{1+\rho}=D^{*}(\mathbf{x}),$$ (50)

where $D^{*}(\mathbf{x})=p_{r}/(p_{r}+p_{\theta})$ is the optimal real-probability discriminator ( Goodfellow et al., 2014 ) (here the superscript ∗ denotes optimality, distinct from its use for exact marginal velocity fields elsewhere).

Assume smooth positive densities and an exact optimal discriminator. We hold the discriminator parameters fixed while retaining derivatives with respect to its input. Under $D^{*}(\mathbf{x})=p_{r}/(p_{r}+p_{\theta})$ , we have $1{-}D^{*}=p_{\theta}/(p_{r}+p_{\theta})$ . For the saturating objective $\ell_{\mathrm{sat}}(\mathbf{x})=\log(1{-}D^{*}(\mathbf{x}))=\log p_{\theta}-\log(p_{r}+p_{\theta})$ :

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\ell_{\mathrm{sat}}(\mathbf{x})$ $\displaystyle=\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})-\frac{p_{r}\,\mathbf{s}_{r}(\mathbf{x})+p_{\theta}\,\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})}{p_{r}+p_{\theta}}$ $\displaystyle=\mathbf{s}_{\theta}-D^{*}\mathbf{s}_{r}-(1{-}D^{*})\mathbf{s}_{\theta}=D^{*}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{s}_{r}(\mathbf{x}))\,.$ (51)

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\ell_{\mathrm{ns}}(\mathbf{x})$ $\displaystyle=\frac{p_{r}\,\mathbf{s}_{r}(\mathbf{x})+p_{\theta}\,\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})}{p_{r}+p_{\theta}}-\mathbf{s}_{r}(\mathbf{x})$ $\displaystyle=D^{*}\mathbf{s}_{r}+(1{-}D^{*})\mathbf{s}_{\theta}-\mathbf{s}_{r}=(1{-}D^{*}(\mathbf{x}))(\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{s}_{r}(\mathbf{x}))\,.$ (52)

The saturating field agrees with the distribution gradient of the scaled JSD above; the non-saturating field is instead a detached-optimal-discriminator generator update.

#### B.2 Score Decomposition of the Reverse-KL Field


##### B.2.1 Score Decomposition Identity

For any distribution $p$ over $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ with finite first and second moments and positive-definite covariance, define its moment-matched Gaussian as

[Tableau] $$q_{p}:=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{p},\Sigma_{p})\,,\qquad\mu_{p}:=\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{x}]\,,\quad\Sigma_{p}:=\operatorname{Cov}_{p}(\mathbf{x})\,.$$ (53)

For any smooth distribution $p$ satisfying Def. B.5 , with score $\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p(\mathbf{x})$ and moment-matched Gaussian $q_{p}$ , define the non-Gaussian score residual as

[Tableau] $$\bm{\eta}_{p}(\mathbf{x}):=\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{s}_{q_{p}}(\mathbf{x})\,.$$ (54)

where $\mathbf{s}_{q_{p}}(\mathbf{x})=-\Sigma_{p}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mu_{p})$ .

Therefore, based on Def. B.5 and Def. B.6 , we can decompose the score function of any distribution $p$ as

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{s}_{q_{p}}(\mathbf{x})+\bm{\eta}_{p}(\mathbf{x})\,.$$ (55)

This is the score decomposition in Eq. 6 of the main text.


##### B.2.2 Orthogonality of the Residual

We use the following scalar-test-function form of Stein’s identity ( Liu et al., 2016 , Def. 2.1 and Lem. 2.2) . If $\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}$ belongs to the Stein class of $p$ , then

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})\varphi(\mathbf{x})+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi(\mathbf{x})]=\mathbf{0}.$$ (56)

The two identities in Lem. B.7 correspond to $\varphi(\mathbf{x})=1$ and $\varphi(\mathbf{x})=x_{l}$ . We include a coordinate proof for clarity.

Let $p$ be a strictly positive smooth density on $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ with score $\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p(\mathbf{x})$ . Assume

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}_{p}[\|\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})\|]<\infty,\qquad\mathbb{E}_{p}[\|\mathbf{x}\|\,\|\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})\|]<\infty.$$ (57)

For $B_{R}=\{\mathbf{x}:\|\mathbf{x}\|\leq R\}$ , let $n_{k}$ be the $k$ -th coordinate of the outward unit normal on $\partial B_{R}$ . Assume also that the following boundary condition holds for all coordinates $(k,l)$ :

[Tableau] $$\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{\partial B_{R}}p(\mathbf{x})n_{k}\,\mathrm{d}S=0,\qquad\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{\partial B_{R}}p(\mathbf{x})x_{l}n_{k}\,\mathrm{d}S=0.$$ (58)

Then

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})]=\mathbf{0},\qquad\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}^{\top}]=-\mathbf{I}_{d}.$$ (59)

Since $p(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}p(\mathbf{x})$ , the $k$ -th coordinate of $\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})]$ is

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}_{p}[[\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})]_{k}]=\int\frac{\partial p(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{k}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.$$ (60)

By the divergence theorem on $B_{R}$ ,

[Tableau] $$\int_{B_{R}}\frac{\partial p(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{k}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{\partial B_{R}}p(\mathbf{x})n_{k}\,\mathrm{d}S.$$ (61)

By ( 57 ), the volume integrand is absolutely integrable. Taking $R\to\infty$ and using the first limit in ( 58 ) gives

[Tableau] $$\int\frac{\partial p(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{k}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=0.$$ (62)

Since this holds for every $k$ ,

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})]=\mathbf{0}.$$ (63)

For the second identity, fix coordinates $(k,l)$ . Again by the divergence theorem,

[Tableau] $$\int_{B_{R}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\!\left[p(\mathbf{x})x_{l}\right]\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{\partial B_{R}}p(\mathbf{x})x_{l}n_{k}\,\mathrm{d}S.$$ (64)

The second condition in ( 57 ) makes the expanded derivative integrable. Taking $R\to\infty$ and using the second limit in ( 58 ) gives

[Tableau] $$\int\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\!\left[p(\mathbf{x})x_{l}\right]\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=0.$$ (65)

Expanding the derivative gives

[Tableau] $$\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\!\left[p(\mathbf{x})x_{l}\right]=\frac{\partial p(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{k}}x_{l}+p(\mathbf{x})\delta_{kl}=p(\mathbf{x})[\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})]_{k}x_{l}+p(\mathbf{x})\delta_{kl},$$ (66)

where $\delta_{kl}$ is the Kronecker delta. Integrating and using $\int p(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=1$ yields

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}_{p}\!\left[[\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})]_{k}x_{l}\right]+\delta_{kl}=0.$$ (67)

Thus $\mathbb{E}_{p}[[\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})]_{k}x_{l}]=-\delta_{kl}$ for all $(k,l)$ , or in matrix form,

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}^{\top}]=-\mathbf{I}_{d}.$$ (68)

Let $p$ be a strictly positive smooth density on $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ with finite second moment and positive-definite covariance $\Sigma_{p}$ . Assume the integrability condition ( 57 ) and boundary condition ( 58 ). Finite Fisher information together with the finite second moment is sufficient for ( 57 ) by Cauchy–Schwarz. Then the non-Gaussian score residual $\bm{\eta}_{p}=\mathbf{s}_{p}-\mathbf{s}_{q_{p}}$ satisfies

- (i) $\mathbb{E}_{p}[\bm{\eta}_{p}(\mathbf{x})]=\mathbf{0}$ .

- (ii) $\mathbb{E}_{p}[\bm{\eta}_{p}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}^{\top}]=\mathbf{0}_{d\times d}$ .

Step 1: Score Stein identities. By Lem. B.7 ,

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})]=\mathbf{0},\qquad\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}^{\top}]=-\mathbf{I}_{d}.$$ (69)

Step 2: The moment-matched Gaussian has the same two identities under $p$ . By definition, the score of $q_{p}=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{p},\Sigma_{p})$ is

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{s}_{q_{p}}(\mathbf{x})=-\Sigma_{p}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mu_{p}).$$ (70)

Taking expectation under $p$ gives

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{s}_{q_{p}}(\mathbf{x})]=-\Sigma_{p}^{-1}\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{x}]-\mu_{p}\bigr)=\mathbf{0}.$$ (71)

For the first-moment identity,

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{s}_{q_{p}}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}^{\top}]$ $\displaystyle=-\Sigma_{p}^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{p}[(\mathbf{x}-\mu_{p})\mathbf{x}^{\top}]$ $\displaystyle=-\Sigma_{p}^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{p}[(\mathbf{x}-\mu_{p})((\mathbf{x}-\mu_{p})+\mu_{p})^{\top}]$ $\displaystyle=-\Sigma_{p}^{-1}\left(\mathbb{E}_{p}[(\mathbf{x}-\mu_{p})(\mathbf{x}-\mu_{p})^{\top}]+\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{x}-\mu_{p}]\mu_{p}^{\top}\right)$ $\displaystyle=-\Sigma_{p}^{-1}\Sigma_{p}=-\mathbf{I}_{d}\,.$ (72)

Step 3: Subtract the Gaussian identities from the true-score identities. Since $\bm{\eta}_{p}=\mathbf{s}_{p}-\mathbf{s}_{q_{p}}$ , the identities in Lem. B.7 and Step 2 imply

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}_{p}[\bm{\eta}_{p}(\mathbf{x})]=\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})]-\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{s}_{q_{p}}(\mathbf{x})]=\mathbf{0}.$$ (73)

Similarly,

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}_{p}[\bm{\eta}_{p}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}^{\top}]=\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}^{\top}]-\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{s}_{q_{p}}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}^{\top}]=-\mathbf{I}_{d}-(-\mathbf{I}_{d})=\mathbf{0}_{d\times d}.$$ (74)

Finally, combining (i) and (ii) yields the equivalent centered form:

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}_{p}[\bm{\eta}_{p}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{x}-\mu_{p})^{\top}]=\mathbb{E}_{p}[\bm{\eta}_{p}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}^{\top}]-\mathbb{E}_{p}[\bm{\eta}_{p}(\mathbf{x})]\mu_{p}^{\top}=\mathbf{0}_{d\times d}.$$ (75)


##### B.2.3 Reverse-KL Field Decomposition

Assume $p_{\theta}$ and $p_{r}$ are strictly positive smooth densities satisfying Def. B.5 , and set $q_{i}:=q_{p_{i}}$ and $\bm{\eta}_{i}:=\bm{\eta}_{p_{i}}$ for $i\in\{r,\theta\}$ . For the reverse-KL distribution gradient $\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{s}_{r}(\mathbf{x})$ ,

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbf{x})=\underbrace{\left(\mathbf{s}_{q_{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{s}_{q_{r}}(\mathbf{x})\right)}_{\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{G}}(\mathbf{x}):\;\text{Gaussian moment component}}+\underbrace{\left(\bm{\eta}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})-\bm{\eta}_{r}(\mathbf{x})\right)}_{\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{nG}}(\mathbf{x}):\;\text{non-Gaussian residual component}}$$ (76)

where $\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{G}}(\mathbf{x})=-\Sigma_{\theta}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mu_{\theta})+\Sigma_{r}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mu_{r})$ depends only on the first two moments, and $\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{nG}}(\mathbf{x})=\bm{\eta}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})-\bm{\eta}_{r}(\mathbf{x})$ captures the remaining score structure. This exact decomposition is stated for the unweighted reverse-KL field. For a general $f$ -divergence field $w(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{s}_{\theta}-\mathbf{s}_{r})$ , the algebraic decomposition is multiplied by $w(\mathbf{x})$ , but the residual orthogonality identities need not be preserved after weighting.

Apply the score decomposition in ( 55 ) to both $\mathbf{s}_{\theta}$ and $\mathbf{s}_{r}$ :

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{s}_{r}(\mathbf{x})$ $\displaystyle=\left[\mathbf{s}_{q_{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})+\bm{\eta}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})\right]-\left[\mathbf{s}_{q_{r}}(\mathbf{x})+\bm{\eta}_{r}(\mathbf{x})\right]$ $\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(\mathbf{s}_{q_{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{s}_{q_{r}}(\mathbf{x})\right)}_{\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{G}}}+\underbrace{\left(\bm{\eta}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})-\bm{\eta}_{r}(\mathbf{x})\right)}_{\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{nG}}}\,.$ (77)


##### B.2.4 Diagonal Form and Connection to Estimators

In the diagonal case $\Sigma_{i}=\diag(\sigma_{i,1}^{2},\ldots,\sigma_{i,d}^{2})$ , where $\sigma_{i,j}>0$ is the standard deviation of coordinate $j$ under $p_{i}$ for $i\in\{r,\theta\}$ ,

[Tableau] $$g^{\mathrm{G}}_{\mathrm{KL},j}(\mathbf{x}):=[\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{G}}(\mathbf{x})]_{j}=[-\Sigma_{\theta}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mu_{\theta})+\Sigma_{r}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mu_{r})]_{j}=-\frac{x_{j}-\mu_{\theta,j}}{\sigma_{\theta,j}^{2}}+\frac{x_{j}-\mu_{r,j}}{\sigma_{r,j}^{2}}\,.$$ (78)

Requiring $g^{\mathrm{G}}_{\mathrm{KL},j}(\mathbf{x})=0$ for all $\mathbf{x}$ forces the coefficient of $x_{j}$ to vanish:

[Tableau] $$\frac{1}{\sigma_{r,j}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\sigma_{\theta,j}^{2}}=0\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;\sigma_{\theta,j}=\sigma_{r,j}\,.$$ (79)

Substituting back into the constant term gives $\mu_{\theta,j}=\mu_{r,j}$ . In the same coordinate space, this is the fixed-point condition for diagonal Gaussian moment matching; Eq. 13 imposes the analogous condition on pooled feature moments. In $\phi$ -space, the moment-matching gradient in Prop. B.20 defines a pooled feature-space regularizer motivated by $\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{G}}$ . At fixed feature-map parameters and queues, let $\mathbf{h}_{k}=\phi_{\bar{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{c}_{k})$ be a current model feature. Its empirical sample-gradient contribution is

[Tableau] $$\frac{\partial\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{pop}}}{\partial\mathbf{x}_{k}}=\bigl[J_{\mathbf{x}}\phi_{\bar{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{c}_{k})\bigr]^{\top}\frac{\partial\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{pop}}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{k}},$$ (80)

where $J_{\mathbf{x}}\phi_{\bar{\theta}}:=\partial\phi_{\bar{\theta}}/\partial\mathbf{x}$ , and the feature derivative is given in Prop. B.20 . Thus the feature-moment loss is a pooled feature-space moment regularizer motivated by $\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{G}}$ , not an estimate of it.

The internal velocity difference in the DMD loss in Section 4.1 yields an estimate of the full noisy reverse-KL field through Eq. 9 , including both moment and residual components. The feature-moment loss adds an explicit constraint on pooled feature statistics alongside this pointwise signal. Whether this signal has lower empirical gradient variance is an experimental question, not a consequence of the decomposition.


##### B.2.5 Variational characterization via $L^{2}$ projection

The score decomposition ( 6 ) admits a variational interpretation that explains why the moment-matched Gaussian is not an arbitrary choice but rather the unique optimal affine approximation.

Assume that $p$ satisfies the regularity conditions of Prop. B.9 and additionally has finite Fisher information:

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}_{p}[\|\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}]=\mathbb{E}_{p}[\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}]<\infty.$$ (81)

This ensures that the $L^{2}(p)$ optimization problem below is well-posed.

Consider the problem of approximating the score function $\mathbf{s}_{p}$ by an affine function in $L^{2}(p)$ :

[Tableau] $$\min_{\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\;\mathbb{E}_{p}[\|\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})-(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b})\|^{2}].$$ (82)

The first-order optimality conditions (normal equations) for this least-squares problem are:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})-(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b})]$ $\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},$ (83) $\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p}[(\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})-(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}))\mathbf{x}^{\top}]$ $\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}.$ (84)

These are exactly the orthogonality conditions ( 8 ).

To solve for $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ , expand the first condition:

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})]=\mathbf{A}\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{x}]+\mathbf{b}.$$ (85)

By Stein’s identity in Lem. B.7 , $\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})]=\mathbf{0}$ and $\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{x}]=\mu_{p}$ , so

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{b}=-\mathbf{A}\mu_{p}.$$ (86)

Substituting into the second condition and expanding:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}^{\top}]$ $\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}]+\mathbf{b}\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{x}]^{\top}$ (87) $\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}]-\mathbf{A}\mu_{p}\mu_{p}^{\top}$ (88) $\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}(\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}]-\mu_{p}\mu_{p}^{\top})$ (89) $\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}\Sigma_{p}.$ (90)

Again by Stein’s identity, $\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathbf{s}_{p}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}^{\top}]=-\mathbf{I}_{d}$ , thus

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{A}=-\Sigma_{p}^{-1}.$$ (91)

Combining ( 86 ) and ( 91 ) recovers

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{s}_{q_{p}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}=-\Sigma_{p}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mu_{p}),$$ (92)

which is exactly the score of the moment-matched Gaussian $q_{p}=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{p},\Sigma_{p})$ .

Therefore, $\mathbf{s}_{q_{p}}$ is the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{s}_{p}$ onto the space of affine functions in $L^{2}(p)$ , and the residual $\bm{\eta}_{p}:=\mathbf{s}_{p}-\mathbf{s}_{q_{p}}$ is orthogonal to all affine functions. This yields a Pythagorean energy decomposition:

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}_{p}[\|\mathbf{s}_{p}\|^{2}]=\mathbb{E}_{p}[\|\mathbf{s}_{q_{p}}\|^{2}]+\mathbb{E}_{p}[\|\bm{\eta}_{p}\|^{2}].$$ (93)

This projection property holds within each distribution separately: $\bm{\eta}_{\theta}$ is the $L^{2}(p_{\theta})$ -orthogonal residual, and $\bm{\eta}_{r}$ is the $L^{2}(p_{r})$ -orthogonal residual. The Pythagorean decomposition ( 93 ) applies to each score function individually under its own measure.

However, the difference field $\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}=(\mathbf{s}_{\theta}-\mathbf{s}_{r})$ does not automatically inherit this orthogonality structure. While each residual has mean zero under its own measure, the cross-terms between $\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{G}}=(\mathbf{s}_{q_{\theta}}-\mathbf{s}_{q_{r}})$ and $\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{nG}}=(\bm{\eta}_{\theta}-\bm{\eta}_{r})$ need not vanish under either $p_{\theta}$ or $p_{r}$ . When these cross-moments exist, neither vanishing cross-term is guaranteed:

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}_{p_{\theta}}[\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{G}}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{nG}}]\;\text{may be nonzero},\quad\mathbb{E}_{p_{r}}[\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{G}}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{nG}}]\;\text{may be nonzero}.$$ (94)

The decomposition is exact algebraically but not energetically orthogonal in the combined field. The two losses therefore constrain complementary statistical structures rather than forming an exact orthogonal decomposition of gradient variance.


#### B.3 Score–Velocity Identity

This section proves the general score–velocity identity in Eq. 1 for the linear Gaussian path. The identity is a specialization of the Gaussian-path score–velocity conversion arising in stochastic-interpolant formulations and summarized pedagogically by Holderrieth and Erives ( Holderrieth and Erives, 2025 ) to the case $\alpha_{t}=1{-}t,\;\beta_{t}=t$ .

Let $\mathbf{x}\sim p$ have finite second moment, let $\bm{\epsilon}\sim p_{z}=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{d})$ be independent, and define $\mathbf{x}_{t}=(1{-}t)\mathbf{x}+t\bm{\epsilon}$ for $t\in(0,1)$ . Let $p_{t}$ denote the marginal density of $\mathbf{x}_{t}$ and let $\mathbf{s}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}):=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})$ . For fixed $t$ , the marginal velocity field is the $L^{2}(p_{t})$ -unique minimizer, up to $p_{t}$ -null sets, over measurable vector fields:

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{v}_{t}^{*}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{v}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-(\bm{\epsilon}-\mathbf{x})\|_{2}^{2}\right],\qquad\mathbf{v}_{t}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\mathbb{E}[\bm{\epsilon}-\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}].$$ (95)

Then, we have the following identity:

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{s}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=-\frac{\mathbf{x}_{t}+(1{-}t)\mathbf{v}_{t}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{t})}{t}.$$ (96)

Step 1: Rewrite as an additive Gaussian observation model. The OT interpolant is $\mathbf{x}_{t}=(1{-}t)\mathbf{x}+t\bm{\epsilon}$ . Define $\mathbf{x}^{\prime}:=(1{-}t)\mathbf{x}$ . Then $\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+t\bm{\epsilon}$ and $\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d})$ .

Step 2: Apply the Gaussian posterior-mean identity. For a Gaussian perturbation model $\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+\sigma\bm{\epsilon}$ , direct differentiation of the Gaussian convolution gives $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{y}]=\mathbf{y}+\sigma^{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\log p_{Y}(\mathbf{y})$ , where $p_{Y}$ is the observation density. In our case, $\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}_{t}$ , $\sigma=t$ , so:

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}]=\mathbf{x}_{t}+t^{2}\mathbf{s}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\,.$$ (97)

Step 3: Recover $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}]$ . Since $\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(1{-}t)\mathbf{x}$ , we have $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}]/(1{-}t)$ :

[Tableau] $$\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}]=\frac{\mathbf{x}_{t}+t^{2}\mathbf{s}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})}{1{-}t}\,.$$ (98)

Step 4: Recover $\mathbb{E}[\bm{\epsilon}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}]$ . From $\mathbf{x}_{t}=t\bm{\epsilon}+(1{-}t)\mathbf{x}$ , $\bm{\epsilon}=(\mathbf{x}_{t}-(1{-}t)\mathbf{x})/t$ . Taking conditional expectations:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\bm{\epsilon}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}]$ $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\left[\mathbf{x}_{t}-(1{-}t)\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}]\right]$ $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\left[\mathbf{x}_{t}-(1{-}t)\cdot\frac{\mathbf{x}_{t}+t^{2}\mathbf{s}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})}{1{-}t}\right]$ $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\left[\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}_{t}-t^{2}\mathbf{s}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right]$ $\displaystyle=-t\,\mathbf{s}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\,.$ (99)

Step 5: Form the marginal velocity field. This field is the conditional expectation of the target $\bm{\epsilon}-\mathbf{x}$ :

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{t}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{t})$ $\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\bm{\epsilon}-\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[\bm{\epsilon}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}]$ $\displaystyle=-t\,\mathbf{s}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\frac{\mathbf{x}_{t}+t^{2}\mathbf{s}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})}{1{-}t}\,.$ (100)

Step 6: Rearrange to obtain the identity. Multiplying both sides by $(1{-}t)$ :

[Tableau] $\displaystyle(1{-}t)\mathbf{v}_{t}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{t})$ $\displaystyle=-t(1{-}t)\mathbf{s}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{x}_{t}-t^{2}\mathbf{s}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})$ $\displaystyle=-t\mathbf{s}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\underbrace{[(1{-}t)+t]}_{=1}-\mathbf{x}_{t}$ $\displaystyle=-t\mathbf{s}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{x}_{t}\,.$ (101)

Rearranging and dividing by $-t$ gives

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{s}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=-\frac{\mathbf{x}_{t}+(1{-}t)\mathbf{v}_{t}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{t})}{t}\,.$$ (102)

At $t=1$ , $\mathbf{x}_{1}=\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{d})$ irrespective of the clean distribution $p$ , so $p_{1}=p_{z}$ and $\mathbf{s}_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})=-\mathbf{x}_{1}$ . The right-hand side gives $-(\mathbf{x}_{1}+0\cdot\mathbf{v}_{1}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{1}))/1=-\mathbf{x}_{1}$ , confirming the identity at $t=1$ .

For $i\in\{1,2\}$ , let $\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\sim p_{i}$ have finite second moment, and independent $\bm{\epsilon}^{(i)}\sim p_{z}$ , and define $\mathbf{x}_{t}^{(i)}=(1{-}t)\mathbf{x}^{(i)}+t\bm{\epsilon}^{(i)}$ for $t\in(0,1)$ . Let $p_{i,t}$ be the marginal density of $\mathbf{x}_{t}^{(i)}$ , and $\mathbf{s}_{i,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}):=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p_{i,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})$ be its marginal score. Define the corresponding marginal velocity field by $\mathbf{v}_{i,t}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{t}):=\mathbb{E}[\bm{\epsilon}^{(i)}-\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}^{(i)}=\mathbf{x}_{t}]$ . Then, for every $\mathbf{x}_{t}$ where both quantities are well defined,

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{v}_{1,t}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{v}_{2,t}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=-\frac{t}{1-t}\left(\mathbf{s}_{1,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{s}_{2,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right).$$ (103)

Applying the Score–Velocity Identity to each field separately:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathbf{s}_{1,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})$ $\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathbf{x}_{t}+(1{-}t)\mathbf{v}^{*}_{1,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})}{t}\,,$ (104) $\displaystyle\mathbf{s}_{2,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})$ $\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathbf{x}_{t}+(1{-}t)\mathbf{v}^{*}_{2,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})}{t}\,.$ (105)

Subtracting the second from the first:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathbf{s}_{1,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{s}_{2,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})$ $\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{t}\left[(\mathbf{x}_{t}+(1{-}t)\mathbf{v}^{*}_{1,t})-(\mathbf{x}_{t}+(1{-}t)\mathbf{v}^{*}_{2,t})\right]$ $\displaystyle=-\frac{1{-}t}{t}\left[\mathbf{v}^{*}_{1,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{v}^{*}_{2,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right]\,.$ (106)

Rearranging for the velocity difference:

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{v}^{*}_{1,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{v}^{*}_{2,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=-\frac{t}{1{-}t}\left(\mathbf{s}_{1,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{s}_{2,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right)\,.$$ (107)


#### B.4 DMD Loss: Score-Difference Identity and Regression

Following the conventions above, $\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL},t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}):=\mathbf{s}_{\theta,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{s}_{r,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})$ denotes the reverse-KL field of the noisy marginals $p_{\theta,t}$ and $p_{r,t}$ at noise level $t$ , the time- $t$ analog of the endpoint field $\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL}}=\mathbf{s}_{\theta}-\mathbf{s}_{r}$ .

Let $\mathbf{v}^{*}_{\theta,t}$ and $\mathbf{v}^{*}_{r,t}$ denote the marginal velocity fields of $p_{\theta,t}$ and $p_{r,t}$ , evaluated at the same $\mathbf{x}_{t}$ . Then the exact velocity difference

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{d}_{t}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{t}):=\mathbf{v}^{*}_{\theta,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{v}^{*}_{r,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})$$ (108)

is proportional to the noisy-marginal reverse-KL field:

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{d}_{t}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\frac{t}{1-t}\left(\mathbf{s}_{r,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{s}_{\theta,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right)=-\frac{t}{1-t}\,\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL},t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}),$$ (109)

where $\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL},t}:=\mathbf{s}_{\theta,t}-\mathbf{s}_{r,t}$ . Equivalently, the noisy-marginal distribution gradient can be recovered as

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL},t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=-\frac{1-t}{t}\mathbf{d}_{t}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{t}).$$ (110)

By with indices $1=\theta$ and $2=r$ ,

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathbf{d}_{t}^{*}$ $\displaystyle=\mathbf{v}^{*}_{\theta,t}-\mathbf{v}^{*}_{r,t}=-\frac{t}{1-t}(\mathbf{s}_{\theta,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{s}_{r,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}))=\frac{t}{1-t}(\mathbf{s}_{r,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{s}_{\theta,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}))\,.$ (111)

Since $\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL},t}=\mathbf{s}_{\theta,t}-\mathbf{s}_{r,t}$ , we have $\mathbf{d}_{t}^{*}=-\frac{t}{1-t}\,\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL},t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})$ . Rearranging gives ( 110 ).

Prop. B.18 is exact for marginal velocity fields and time- $t$ noisy marginals only when both fields recover their respective exact marginals at the same $(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)$ . Practical neural branches and mixed objectives do not guarantee recovery of these exact marginal velocities. TwinFlow and APEX use internally separated branches to turn this conversion into practical one-step distribution estimators ( Cheng et al., 2025 ; Liu et al., 2026a ) ; we adopt APEX-style condition separation with $\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{fake}}=-\mathbf{c}$ and use the losses defined in Section 4.1 . In practice, $\widehat{\mathbf{d}}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{c}):=\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{c})-\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{r,t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{c})$ approximates $\mathbf{d}_{t}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{t})$ . The correction queries it at $\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{0,t^{\prime}}^{\mathop{\operatorname{sg}}\nolimits}$ and detaches the result after weighting and normalization. With neither processing step, $\widehat{\mathbf{d}}_{t^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{DMD}}=\mathop{\operatorname{sg}}\nolimits\!\left(\widehat{\mathbf{d}}_{t^{\prime}}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{0,t^{\prime}}^{\mathop{\operatorname{sg}}\nolimits}\mid\mathbf{c})\right)$ . In Eq. 11 , subtracting $\widehat{\mathbf{d}}_{t^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{DMD}}$ from the velocity target adds $t\widehat{\mathbf{d}}_{t^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{DMD}}$ to the endpoint target.

For fixed $\mathbf{x}_{t}$ and $t>0$ , both targets in Eq. 11 are held constant during differentiation. Their velocity-to-endpoint conversion gives

[Tableau] $$\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}-\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\mathrm{DMD}}=-t\bigl(\bm{F}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\mathbf{c})-\mathbf{v}_{t}^{\mathrm{DMD}}\bigr),\qquad\ell_{x}(t)=t^{2}\ell_{v}(t).$$

Hence $\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{x}(t)=t^{2}\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{v}(t)$ , and the equal-weight DMD loss in Eq. 12 satisfies

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{score}}$ $\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[(1+t^{2})\ell_{v}(t)\right],$ $\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{score}}$ $\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}\!\left[(1+t^{2})[J_{\theta}\bm{F}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\mathbf{c})]^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{d}}_{t^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{DMD}}\right].$

If the correction equals the exact, unweighted velocity difference $\mathbf{d}_{t^{\prime}}^{*}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{0,t^{\prime}}^{\mathop{\operatorname{sg}}\nolimits})$ , then Eq. 109 and $J_{\theta}\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}=-tJ_{\theta}\bm{F}_{\theta}$ yield the per-example identity

[Tableau] $$\nabla_{\theta}(\ell_{v}(t)+\ell_{x}(t))=\frac{2t^{\prime}(1+t^{2})}{t(1-t^{\prime})}[J_{\theta}\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}]^{\top}\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{KL},t^{\prime}}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{0,t^{\prime}}^{\mathop{\operatorname{sg}}\nolimits}).$$

This is a pointwise pullback of the field defined by $p_{\theta,t^{\prime}}$ and $p_{r,t^{\prime}}$ . An on-policy KL interpretation additionally requires the queried prediction law to agree with the model law used to train the model-velocity branch. For the one-step law of Section 3.1 , this agreement is immediate at $t=1$ , $\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{z}$ , but is not implied for intermediate regression inputs. For the squared losses above, the two per-example gradients are collinear and their combination changes the time weighting and magnitude; at $t=1$ , $\ell_{x}=\ell_{v}$ . Separate residual-dependent normalizations can change the proportionality factor and must be included when applying this calculation to a normalized loss. With approximate branches and correction processing, Eq. 12 does not in general have the same gradient as the unweighted time-averaged noisy reverse KL.


#### B.5 Internal model-velocity training

Draw $\mathbf{z},\bm{\epsilon}\sim p_{z}$ independently, set $\mathbf{x}_{\theta}^{\mathop{\operatorname{sg}}\nolimits}:=\mathop{\operatorname{sg}}\nolimits(G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c}))$ and $\mathbf{x}_{\theta,t}^{\mathop{\operatorname{sg}}\nolimits}:=(1{-}t)\mathbf{x}_{\theta}^{\mathop{\operatorname{sg}}\nolimits}+t\bm{\epsilon}$ , and minimize

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{fake}}(\theta)={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{z},\bm{\epsilon},t,\mathbf{c}}\left[\|\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{\theta,t}(\mathbf{x}_{\theta,t}^{\mathop{\operatorname{sg}}\nolimits}\mid\mathbf{c})-(\bm{\epsilon}-\mathbf{x}_{\theta}^{\mathop{\operatorname{sg}}\nolimits})\|_{2}^{2}\right]\,,$$ (112)

Here $\mathbf{c}\sim p_{c}$ , $\mathbf{z},\bm{\epsilon}\sim p_{z}$ are independent, and $t$ is independently sampled from a distribution supported on $(0,1)$ . This auxiliary regression uses the one-step output law $p_{\theta}$ defined in Section 3.1 . Gradients pass through the model-velocity branch, while generated samples and regression targets are detached. The branches share $\theta$ , so this auxiliary regression also updates shared parameters.


#### B.6 Gradient of the Feature-Moment Loss

Within an update, the EMA feature-map parameters and queued features are held fixed, while derivatives through the current model samples are retained. The feature-moment loss therefore cannot be reduced by directly changing the feature-map parameters within that update. This removes a direct feature-collapse shortcut of one update but does not rule out slow degeneration as the EMA map evolves over training.

Let $\mathbf{h}_{n}$ , $n=1,\ldots,N_{\phi}$ , denote model features: current entries $\mathbf{h}_{n}=\phi(\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathbf{c}_{n})$ retain gradients, while stored queue entries are detached. Define the empirical means and biased empirical variances

[Tableau] $$\widehat{\mu}_{\theta}^{\phi}:=N_{\phi}^{-1}\sum_{n}\mathbf{h}_{n}\,,\qquad\widehat{\nu}_{\theta,j}^{\phi}:=N_{\phi}^{-1}\sum_{n}(h_{n,j}-\widehat{\mu}_{\theta,j}^{\phi})^{2}\,,$$

and for the queued reference features, let $\{\mathbf{h}_{k}^{r}\}_{k=1}^{K}$ denote the $K$ pre-computed feature vectors stored in $\mathcal{Q}_{r}$ (computed as $\phi(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{c}_{k})$ at enqueue time and detached), and define

[Tableau] $$\widehat{\mu}_{r}^{\phi}:=K^{-1}\!\sum_{k=1}^{K}\!\mathbf{h}_{k}^{r}\,,\qquad\widehat{\nu}_{r,j}^{\phi}:=K^{-1}\!\sum_{k=1}^{K}\!(h^{r}_{k,j}-\widehat{\mu}_{r,j}^{\phi})^{2}\,;$$

assume $\widehat{\nu}_{\theta,j}^{\phi}>0$ . These are estimators of the population quantities $\mu_{i}^{\phi},\nu_{i,j}^{\phi}$ defined in Section 4.2 . Write $\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{pop}}$ for Eq. 13 evaluated at these empirical moments. Its gradient with respect to a current feature $\mathbf{h}_{k}$ is

[Tableau] $$\frac{\partial\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{pop}}}{\partial h_{k,j}}=\frac{2}{N_{\phi}}\!\left[(\widehat{\mu}_{\theta,j}^{\phi}-\widehat{\mu}_{r,j}^{\phi})+\frac{h_{k,j}-\widehat{\mu}_{\theta,j}^{\phi}}{\sqrt{\widehat{\nu}_{\theta,j}^{\phi}}}\left(\sqrt{\widehat{\nu}_{\theta,j}^{\phi}}-\sqrt{\widehat{\nu}_{r,j}^{\phi}}\right)\right]\!.$$ (113)

This is the exact gradient of the empirical objective at fixed queues; it is not, in general, an unbiased estimate of the gradient of the population loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{pop}}$ .

The empirical loss decomposes as $\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{pop}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mu}+\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}$ , where

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mu}$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{j}(\widehat{\mu}_{\theta,j}^{\phi}-\widehat{\mu}_{r,j}^{\phi})^{2}\,,$ (114) $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{j}(\sqrt{\widehat{\nu}_{\theta,j}^{\phi}}-\sqrt{\widehat{\nu}_{r,j}^{\phi}})^{2}\,.$ (115)

(a) Mean term. Since $\partial\widehat{\mu}_{\theta,j}^{\phi}/\partial h_{k,j}=1/N_{\phi}$ ,

[Tableau] $$\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mu}}{\partial h_{k,j}}=\frac{2}{N_{\phi}}(\widehat{\mu}_{\theta,j}^{\phi}-\widehat{\mu}_{r,j}^{\phi})\,.$$ (116)

(b) Variance term. Since $\widehat{\nu}_{\theta,j}^{\phi}=N_{\phi}^{-1}\sum_{n}(h_{n,j}-\widehat{\mu}_{\theta,j}^{\phi})^{2}$ ,

[Tableau] $$\frac{\partial(h_{n,j}-\widehat{\mu}_{\theta,j}^{\phi})}{\partial h_{k,j}}=\delta_{nk}-\frac{1}{N_{\phi}}\,.$$ (117)

By the chain rule and the centered-sum identity, summing over $n$ gives

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widehat{\nu}_{\theta,j}^{\phi}}{\partial h_{k,j}}=\frac{2}{N_{\phi}}(h_{k,j}-\widehat{\mu}_{\theta,j}^{\phi})\,.$ (118)

Applying the chain rule to the standard deviation gives

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}}{\partial h_{k,j}}=\frac{2}{N_{\phi}}\frac{h_{k,j}-\widehat{\mu}_{\theta,j}^{\phi}}{\sqrt{\widehat{\nu}_{\theta,j}^{\phi}}}(\sqrt{\widehat{\nu}_{\theta,j}^{\phi}}-\sqrt{\widehat{\nu}_{r,j}^{\phi}})\,.$ (119)

Combining. Summing (a) and (b):

[Tableau] $$\frac{\partial\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{pop}}}{\partial h_{k,j}}=\frac{2}{N_{\phi}}\left[(\widehat{\mu}_{\theta,j}^{\phi}-\widehat{\mu}_{r,j}^{\phi})+\frac{h_{k,j}-\widehat{\mu}_{\theta,j}^{\phi}}{\sqrt{\widehat{\nu}_{\theta,j}^{\phi}}}\left(\sqrt{\widehat{\nu}_{\theta,j}^{\phi}}-\sqrt{\widehat{\nu}_{r,j}^{\phi}}\right)\right]\!.$$ (120)

For a stabilized version with $\sqrt{\widehat{\nu}_{i,j}^{\phi}+\delta}$ , where $\delta>0$ is a scalar stabilizer, the same derivation replaces every standard deviation in the gradient by its stabilized value, including the denominator. The displayed gradient is for the unstabilized objective under the stated positive-variance assumption.


#### B.7 Covariance Contraction Under Regression

This result shows a limitation of interpreting squared-error regression as distribution matching: a conditional-mean predictor need not preserve the target covariance. It is a separate observation about regression, not a consequence of the reverse-KL field decomposition or a claim that DMD lacks variance information.

Let $(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})$ be any square-integrable jointly distributed random vectors and let $\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{Y}):=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}\mid\mathbf{Y}]$ be the optimal squared-error predictor. Then

[Tableau] $$\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{Y}))\preceq\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{X}).$$ (121)

Moreover, $\operatorname{Tr}\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{Y}))<\operatorname{Tr}\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{X})$ whenever $\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Tr}\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{X}\mid\mathbf{Y})]>0$ .

By the law of total covariance,

[Tableau] $$\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{X}\mid\mathbf{Y})]+\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}\mid\mathbf{Y}])=\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{X}\mid\mathbf{Y})]+\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{Y})).$$ (122)

The conditional-covariance term is positive semidefinite, proving the matrix inequality. Taking traces gives the strict statement.

This proposition applies only when the trained predictor realizes the conditional mean under the chosen squared-error coupling. Strict covariance contraction occurs only when unresolved conditional variance remains. It therefore motivates possible variance suppression in ambiguous endpoint regression, but it does not imply that deterministic or one-step generators generally underestimate variance. Note that exact score differences do contain variance information: for $p_{\theta}=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\theta}^{2})$ and $p_{r}=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{r}^{2})$ ,

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(x)-\mathbf{s}_{r}(x)=-\frac{x}{\sigma_{\theta}^{2}}+\frac{x}{\sigma_{r}^{2}}\,,$$ (123)

so the descent direction $\mathbf{s}_{r}(x)-\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(x)=x(1/\sigma_{\theta}^{2}-1/\sigma_{r}^{2})$ pushes samples outward when $\sigma_{\theta}<\sigma_{r}$ . The learned pointwise estimator can nevertheless be noisy because it depends on the accuracy of the learned velocity branches and random time/noise samples. The pooled feature-moment loss provides an explicit population-statistic correction. Under an approximately independent, stationary-feature idealization with finite moments, the model- and reference-side mean-estimation variances scale as $O(1/N_{\phi})$ and $O(1/K)$ ; this does not establish that its empirical parameter-gradient variance is lower than that of the DMD loss, and queue staleness can introduce bias.
