Abstract: The automated transformation of unstructured natural language process 
descriptions into Business Process Model and Notation (BPMN) diagrams remains 
a challenging task, due to linguistic ambiguity and the difficulty to detect non-adja-
cent control-flow relationships. This paper proposes a solution for generating 
BPMN models from natural language descriptions through a multi-layered pipeline 
that integrates transformer-based architectures, Large Language Models and ontol-
ogy population. The proposed method starts with natural language descriptions and 
separates textual processing, control-flow relation extraction, and structural valida-
tion into dedicated components. Entity extraction is performed using a fine-tuned 
RoBERTa-based transformer model while a control-flow engine captures non-ad-
jacent relationships through LLM-based inference. To improve generalization un-
der limited available data, the approach uses a dynamic few-shot strategy with con-
trolled template input construction, enabling improved generalization under limited 
data availability. Additionally, an ontology population mechanism is introduced to 
represent the generated process models as semantic knowledge structures. For eval-
uation purposes we have used from the literature and industry reference processes. 
Additionally, we have validated the proposed method on a real-world heart disease 
patients transitional care process, capturing practical constraints and multi-actor 
collaboration, and serving as an expert-defined benchmark scenario. Experimental 
results demonstrate strong performance in the relation extraction task, achieving an 
accuracy of 90.7%, as well as maintaining structural alignment between the gener-
ated and reference BPMN models across multiple evaluation scenarios scenarios. 
Keywords BPMN Modeling, RoBERTa model, Large Language Models, Ontol-
ogy, Natural Language Processing 

1 Introduction 
Business Process Model and Notation (BPMN) has become the default standard 
for representing complex organizational and industrial processes in a visual and ex-
ecutable form. BPMN modelled processes can be mapped to services or components 
allowing for orchestration or choreography of tasks thus helping automating tasks 
and optimizing organization specific processes [1]. In the current AI-enabled con-
text, BPMN modelled activities can be dynamically managed by AI agents integrat-
ing learning and reasoning into the process execution [2]. However, currently 
BPMN model creation remains primarily a manual task, requiring domain experts 
to interpret unstructured natural language descriptions and translate them into struc-
tured diagrams that comply with BPMN semantics [3]. This activity is labour-in-
tensive, subject to interpretation of errors and often leads to inconsistencies across 
organizations, particularly when multiple stakeholders interpret the same textual 
specification differently. 
Automating the transformation of natural language process descriptions into for-
mal BPMN models presents several challenges [4]. Natural language specifications 
usually contain implicit dependencies, ambiguous sequencing, and branching con-
ditions that must be translated into specific BPMN control-flow structures such as 
gateways and parallel flows. Conventional relation extraction techniques usually 
assume that relationships occur between adjacent entities in text, an assumption re-
ferred to as the adjacency constraint [5]. Nevertheless, business process descriptions 
often include non-adjacent (i.e., non-linear) control-flow relationships, such as con-
ditional branching and parallel execution, where related elements may be separated 
by multiple clauses or sentences. As a result, systems that rely primarily on sequen-
tial text interpretation can fail to accurately reconstruct the correct control-flow 
structure of complex processes. 
Existing work has explored multiple approaches to address these challenges. Pro-
cess algebra has been used for modelling and verification of business models, al-
lowing for reasoning and checking the correctness and early identification of any 
logical faults in the model design phase [6]. Early attempts for rule-based natural 
language processing (NLP) pipelines combined syntactic parsing and semantic role 
labelling to identify activities and their ordering relationships [7]. Later studies ex-
tended this direction toward declarative process modelling by extracting activities 
and constraint relations from text and mapping them to declarative process tem-
plates [8]. The development and evolution of transformer-based architectures such 
as BERT [9] and RoBERTa [10] have improved token-level perception tasks, in-
cluding entity recognition, coreference resolution, and relation extraction, enabling 
more reliable detection of process elements from textual descriptions. Furthermore, 
large language models (LLMs) and foundational models are lately investigated as a 
solution to process modelling and BPMN generation tasks [11,12]. While these 
models demonstrate that they can generate syntactically valid process representa-
tions, empirical studies indicate that the overall structural correctness tends to dete-
riorate as complexity increases, particularly in the presence of branching logic and 
non-adjacent dependencies [13]. For defining knowledge that can be reused and 

integrated in such BPMN generation techniques, especially in the modern AI-based 
ones, existing BPMN ontology semantically represents elements, relationships, and 
rules [14]. To add real execution context and transforming this knowledge base from 
a static model into a representation of how processes behave requires expanding it 
with process instances. This would allow Artificial Intelligence (AI) models such 
as LLMs to better learn and improve their ability to generate context-aware insights. 
These findings highlight the need for further research and development of novel 
architectures that combine modern AI techniques with semantic knowledge repre-
sentation and explicit structural validation mechanisms capable of generating valid 
BPMN processes that can be further used for automating domain specific processes 
and even integrating AI agents into the process flow [15]. 
In this context, we propose a multi-layered approach for transforming unstruc-
tured natural language descriptions into valid BPMN models. The main contribu-
tions of this work are: 
• Design of a multi-layered pipeline for automated BPMN generation from 
natural language descriptions while separating entity identification, control-
flow extraction, and structural validation into dedicated processing layers. 
• Definition of an entity identification method that combines Longformer ar-
chitecture and a fine-tuned RoBERTa-based transformer model to classify 
tokens into BPMN primitives. 
• Definition of a relation extraction engine that combines structured candidate 
generation with few-shot LLM-based inferences to capture non-adjacent 
(non-linear) control-flow relationships in the BPMN process descriptions. 
• Definition of a validation layer that assures that the resulting BPMN struc-
ture complies with the BPMN modelling constraints before generating the 
final representation. It also integrates an ontology population mechanism 
that extends the existing BPMN ontology with a semantic knowledge repre-
sentation of the generated process model, enabling semantic persistence. 
• Evaluation and validation of the proposed method on expert defined process 
models of real-world scenarios and on literature defined process models. 
The rest of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews the state of the 
art in automated BPMN generation from natural-language descriptions, categoriz-
ing existing approaches according to their methodological approaches and high-
lighting the current research gaps. Section 3 details the proposed method. Section 4 
presents evaluation and validation results including component level and overall 
performance analysis through multiple case studies. Section 5 discusses the method 
results, analysing the strengths and limitations and their applicability in real world 
scenarios. Finally, Section 6 concludes the paper by summarizing the main contri-
butions and highlighting directions for future research. 

2 Related Work 
The automated transformation of natural language process descriptions into 
BPMN diagrams has been thoroughly studied, driven by the need to accelerate pro-
cess digitalization and reduce the specialized knowledge required for manual mod-
elling. Existing approaches in the literature can be grouped into five categories: rule-
based NLP pipelines, declarative or constraint-based extraction methods, machine 
learning (ML) and data-driven extraction approaches, modern LLM-based genera-
tion frameworks, and structured intermediate-representation approaches. In the fol-
lowing we analyse these categories and discuss their contributions and limitations. 
Early contributions focus on rule-based NLP pipelines that mapped linguistic 
structures to imperative modelling notations. [16] combined syntactic parsing, se-
mantic role labelling, and a dedicated anaphora resolution to extract activities and 
ordering relations from textual specifications. Their approach constructed an inter-
mediate “world model” before generation of BPMN diagrams, demonstrating the 
technical feasibility of systematic text-to-model transformation. Similar approaches 
remain relevant for domains where explainability and deterministic behaviour are 
required, as argued in [17] and [18]. However, rule-based solutions require exten-
sive linguistic rules and domain knowledge, making it difficult to adapt to new do-
mains or complex process descriptions. Moreover, such approaches fail to capture 
implicit relations and non-adjacent control-flow structures that are usually found in 
natural language specifications. 
Subsequent research extended process extraction beyond imperative control-
flow reconstruction toward semantically richer and more flexible representations. 
[19] introduced the first automated approach for extracting declarative process mod-
els from natural language. Instead of reconstructing explicit sequences, their ap-
proach identifies activities and constraint relations and maps them to Declare tem-
plates. This shift from sequence-oriented modelling to constraint-based extraction 
reflects a broader recognition that textual description often specifies constraints and 
obligations rather than enumerated execution sequences, as described in [20]. De-
clarative approaches focus on constraint identification and do not directly construct 
structures required for BPMN diagrams, and consequently additional transfor-
mation steps are often required for process representations. In parallel, research has 
addressed the misalignment that frequently occurs between textual description and 
process models. Techniques for automatic inconsistency detection between text and 
process diagrams have demonstrated discrepancy in ordering, missing activities and 
constraint violation are common occurrences, as presented in [7]. Furthermore, 
compliance checking approaches based on behavioural spaces explicitly model am-
biguity by representing multiple plausible interpretations of natural language spec-
ifications instead of merging into a single deterministic structure, as discussed in 
[8]. 
Methodological approaches to process extraction have evolved significantly with 
the increasing adoption of ML and deep learning techniques in NLP. [21] docu-
ments the transition from rule-based pipelines toward hybrid and learning-based 
architectures. Recent studies address this transition by decomposing text to process 

modelling into standard information extraction subtasks, such as named entity 
recognition and entity linking, by implementing these components with trainable 
models rather than handcrafted linguistic rules. [22] formulates process model ex-
traction as a text classification problem and uses hierarchical neural architecture to 
capture process relevant details across different textual levels. [23] replaces rule 
based named entity recognition and relation extraction with fully data-driven pipe-
lines, enabling faster adaptation across datasets, types and domains. Furthermore, 
structure aware NLP approaches, as described in [24], have been proposed that use 
flexible tree structured patterns over dependency parses to extract actions, roles, 
conditions, and control-flow relations, offering generalization to linguistic varia-
tion, while requiring less rule management compared to earlier handcrafted meth-
ods. While these methods show improved validity in linguistic analysis; their effec-
tiveness remains affected by the limited annotated datasets for processing extraction 
tasks. The increasing use of such methods highlighted the need for standardized 
datasets and evaluation benchmarks for processing extraction tasks. One of the first 
benchmarks of text-to-process extraction techniques is defined in [25] and high-
lighted the lack of annotated datasets as a major limitation in this field. The intro-
duction of PET in [26] represents a major step toward standardized evaluation, 
providing annotated descriptions that include activities, actors, gateways, and flow 
relations. Even so, dataset limitations remain one of the primary bottlenecks for 
such approaches. 
The recent evolution of transformer-based and large language models (LLMs) 
has reshaped research directions in business process management (BPM). [27] sys-
tematically analyses the integration of LLMs across the BPM lifecycle, identifying 
opportunities in modelling support, documenting, and conversational interaction, 
while also showcasing methodological risks related to hallucinations, evaluation 
and reliability. In the context of process modelling, [11] propose structured ap-
proaches for LLM-based model generation that incorporates abstraction choices and 
iterative improvement strategies. Furthermore, [12] presents an evaluation frame-
work and benchmark, for assessing LLM performance on business process model-
ling tasks. Their findings show deteriorates LLMs can generate syntactically valid 
models, but the structural correctness and the overall quality deteriorate as com-
plexity increases. This is a finding that is further supported by [13], which evaluated 
BPMN XML diagram generation for robotic process automation scenarios across 
different complexity levels. It indicates strong performance for basic processes but 
decreasing structural correctness and validity for more advanced processes that in-
volve complex gateways. These results suggest that basic one-shot diagram gener-
ation is insufficient for process modelling, and additional mechanisms are neces-
sary. The usage of LLMs in the context of BPM can tackle the issue of process 
model comprehension. [28] shows that the abstraction choice directly influences 
LLM performance in understanding and reasoning over process models. Represen-
tations that expose structural semantics in a machine interpretable format enhance 
their reasoning capabilities compared to graphical encodings. This suggests that 
LLMs can not only be used as generators in the area, but as interpreters that support 
validation, querying, and iterative correction within process modelling. Similarly, 
[4] introduces BPMNGen, a conversation framework that generates BPMN 2.0 pro-
cess models from natural language descriptions using a custom LLM assistant. The 

system produces intermediate JSON representations that are later converted and 
validated as BPMN XML diagrams. While such approaches demonstrate the feasi-
bility of text-to-BPMN generation, it highlights as well that many similar applica-
tions remain in conceptual prototypes due to the practical complexity of such a sys-
tem. Looking at research outside the BPMN context, process generation in the LLM 
literature provides relevant methodological insights. System such those presented 
in [22] and [23] use LLMs to generate executable processes using APIs and stages 
of prompting strategies. Hallucinations are addressed by applying validation con-
straints within the predefined function interfaces and including user feedback for 
iterative improvement. They demonstrate that decomposition and controlled ab-
straction successfully improve the generation's validity. 
Another focus of recent research is the use of intermediate representations (IRs) 
to improve the controllability and reliability of model generation. BPMN Assistant, 
presented by [29], demonstrates that generating and editing JSON representations 
before compiling them into BPMN XML improves edit success rates while main-
taining comparable similarity to ground-truth models. By moving away from the 
verbose XML syntax, JSON-based IRs reduce the cognitive and structural overhead 
position on LLMs and enable validation before the final export. Similarly, the Par-
tially Ordered Workflow Language (POWL), as shown in [30], introduces a mod-
elling formalism that integrates partial orders with hierarchical control-flow opera-
tors. By design, POWL provides soundness guarantees and offers a compact 
representation of concurrency, choice, and loops. Although not designed specifi-
cally for LLM-based pipelines, such representations are well suited as intermediate 
stages in the generation step. However, the intermediate representations alone do 
not guarantee semantic equivalence between the general workflow and the natural 
language description. Although structured model-to-text generation has been 
demonstrated in [31], current LLM-based text to BPMN systems rarely incorporate 
reverse verbalization as a validation step. Such an approach, following a text-to-
model generation, could provide a useful mechanism for verifying semantic equiv-
alence between the original specification and the generated process, such as detect-
ing ambiguities or structural inconsistencies. 
Overall, the current state of the art reflects a transition from rule-based text-to-
model transformation toward a multi-stage generation pipeline that combines se-
mantic extraction, constraint modelling, intermediate representations, and iterative 
improvement. While LLMs expand the potential for automation in process model-
ling, they still require extensive validation layers and evaluation frameworks to en-
sure reliability. The research is moving beyond whether the process models can be 
generated from text, towards how generation can be validated, controlled, and inte-
grated in complex process models.

The generation process begins with the Entity Identification Layer, a prepro-
cessing step that resolves pronominal references through coreference resolution to 
ensure semantic completeness of textual specification. This normalized input is pro-
cessed by a fine-tuned transformer-based entity recognition module, which classi-
fies tokens into BPMN primitives. The extracted entities are passed to the Relation 
Extraction Layer, that combines candidate generation with a LLM engine to infer 
dependencies between process elements. Finally, the inferred structure is processed 
within the Validation Layer where entities are assigned to BPMN lanes based on 
identified actors, then structural validation constraints based on BPMN ontology 
schema are used to detect and correct inconsistencies in the generated control-flow 
graph. Once validated, the model is serialized into a BPMN 2.0 XML format and 
the extracted entities and their relationships are instantiated as ontological individ-
uals within the BPMN ontology, enabling semantic knowledge representation and 
future enhancement of LLM-based inference. 
3.1 Entity Identification Layer 
The Entity Identification Layer (see Figure 2) aims to identify BPMN-relevant 
elements that serve as the fundamental components of a process model. Process 
descriptions frequently contain pronominal references (e.g. He, She, etc.) that hide 
the association between action and the actors performing them. The standard Named 
Entity Recognition (NER) models often process tokens independently and may fail 
to associate correctly with the appropriate process participants. To mitigate this, we 
have integrated a preprocessing module based on FastCoref, as defined in [32], a 
coreference resolution model based on the Longformer architecture, [33]. The mod-
ule scans the input text, identifies the coreference clusters and replaces all the pro-
nominal mentions with their referents. It converts ambiguous phrases such as “He 
validates it” into explicit statements as “The manager validates the invoice”. This 
transformation produces a semantically explicit representation of the description, 
which improves the reliability of the extraction and actor identification tasks. 

 
Fig. 2 Architectural workflow of the Entity Identification Layer. 
 
For Entity Recognition, first Entity extraction is performed using a fine-tuned 
RoBERTa-based transformer model [10]. The model operates as a token classifier 
using the BIO (Beginning-Inside-Outside) tagging scheme. Given input sequence, 
the model predicts a corresponding sequence of elements each belonging to the en-
tity tag set corresponding to the BPMN elements. In addition to identifying the main 
entity classes, the extraction process may capture auxiliary contextual information 
associated with the activity descriptions, such as contextual attributes. This infor-
mation is preserved as a metadata linked to the corresponding activity entity and 
can be utilized by other layers of the pipeline. Then a post-processor component is 
used to rectify common prediction errors. While Transformer architecture provides 
superior semantic mapping, they often lack a strict schema format required for fur-
ther layers. We bridge this gap by implementing two deterministic correction func-
tions: 
• Subword Merging: Transformer tokenizers frequently split words into sub-
word fragments. A reconstruction procedure scans the predicted token se-
quence and merges contiguous subword tokens that share the same entity 
label, restoring the complete lexical units. 
• Entity Splitting: In certain cases, the model incorrectly merges gateways in-
dicators with adjacent activity descriptions. A rule-based separation proce-
dure identifies predefined BPMN logic markers and separates them from 
surrounding tokens, ensuring that gateway expressions are represented as 
independent entities. 
The output of the Entity Identification Layer is the entity set E, which is passed 
to the Relation Extraction Layer, where control-flow relationships between the ex-
tracted entities are inferred.

Natural language process descriptions often contain non-adjacent control-flow 
dependencies, where related entities are separated by multiple clauses or sentences, 
especially in conditional and branching structures involving gateways. To address 


this, candidate relations are generated using a sliding window mechanism over the 
entity sequence. For each entity 𝑒𝑖 the system evaluates relations within a look-
ahead window of size k: 
𝑃= {(𝑒𝑖,𝑒𝑗) | 𝑗−𝑖≤𝑘} (10) 
 
In this work, the window size is set to k = 3, which balances the detection of non-
adjacent relations against computational complexity. This mechanism enables the 
model to evaluate both proximal and non-adjacent relations that are common in 
conditional process structures. 
The candidate relations are evaluated by the candidate pair generation, which 
performs contextual analysis to determine the most possible control-flow depend-
ency between the entities. Instead of relying only on adjacent dependencies, the 
engine analyses linguistic clues such as temporal ordering, logical connectors, and 
conditional expressions in the surrounding text. The context C represents a textual 
segment extracted from the normalized process description, containing 𝑒𝑖 and 𝑒𝑗 
along with their surrounding context, such that it captures the semantic information 
required to infer their control-flow relationship. To represent the relation extraction 
task, each candidate entity pair, together with its context C, is encoded using the 
input. 
The input is processed by a LLM inference engine [34] within the control-flow 
engine to predict a relationship between the entities. It was selected due to its pa-
rameter efficiency, large context window, and its ability to perform chain-of-
thought reasoning. Model interactions are managed using the DSPy framework, as 
described in [35], which enables prompt construction and optimization. Given a tri-
plet (𝑒𝑖, 𝑒𝑗, C) consisting of the candidate entity pair and the surrounding textual 
context C, the model predicts the most plausible relation label 𝑟𝑖𝑗 while simultane-
ously generating an intermediate tracing. This process allows the system to infer 
implicit logical dependencies that may not be explicitly expressed by syntactic pat-
terns. To overcome the limitations of the zero-shot inference, the relation extraction 
component uses a dynamic few-shot conditioning strategy that combines both static 
and dynamic prompt construction. The static component consists of a fixed template 
that defines the relation classes, the input structure, and the expected output format. 
This includes the task description, a list of rules describing each relation type, and 
a schema specifying the context, candidate entity pair, and predicted relationship. 
The dynamic component is based on programmatic selection of annotated examples 
from the training data. Each sample contains the contextual sentence, the candidate 
entity pair (𝑒𝑖, 𝑒𝑗) and the corresponding relation label. During inference, a subset 
of three of these examples is selected using a relevance sampling strategy and in-
cluded in the input sequence.

3.3 Validation Layer 
While the previous stages identified process entities and their control-flow de-
pendencies, this one ensures that the resulting structure complies with the BPMN 
modelling constraints before producing the final representation. This layer performs 
four main actions: (i) assigning activities to BPMN lanes based on the identified 
actors, (ii) validating the structure of inferred control-flow relations, (iii) serializing 
the validated structure into a BPMN 2.0 XML file, and (iv) populating a semantic 
ontology representation of the generated process model. 
Actors identified in the Entity Identification Layer define the organizational 
structure of the generated BPMN model. Each identified actor (as defined in Equa-
tion (7)) corresponds to a BPMN lane, representing the participant responsible for 
executing a subset of activities. Each activity is mapped to the lane associated with 
its corresponding actor. Activities sharing the same actor are grouped within the 
same lane, ensuring consistency between the textual process description and the 
generated BPMN model. 
Before generating the final BPMN model, the inferred control-flow relations 
are validated against a set of structural constraints derived from the BPMN speci-
fication. These ensure that the generated process is syntactically correct and execut-
able. The validation procedure verifies several structural properties: 
• Gateway correctness, ensuring that conditional branches originate from 
gateway elements. 
• Branch completeness, ensuring that gateway nodes contain the required 
outgoing flows. 
• Sequential consistency, ensuring that activities participate in valid incom-
ing and outgoing relations. 
• Parallel flow integrity, ensuring that concurrent branches are correctly de-
fined. 
When inconsistencies are detected, rule-based validations are applied to correct 
the structure before serialization. This validation step prevents the generation of 
invalid BPMN models and ensures that the resulting process diagrams follow the 
BPMN control-flow semantics. 
Following structural validation, the system serializes the validated BPMN into 
standard BPMN 2.0 representation. As an intermediary step, the extracted entities 
and relations are first converted into a Process Tree representation using the PM4Py 
library [36]. Compared to direct BPMN generation, this method ensures that the 
resulting structure consists of properly nested blocks free of deadlocks. The tree is 
then serialized into a baseline BPMN XML structure. The resulting BPMN model 
is therefore structurally valid, semantically enriched and compliant with the BPMN 
2.0 specification, enabling direct visualization, further editing, or execution within 
the standard BPMN process engines. 
Finally, a semantic ontology representation of the generated process model is 
created using Web Ontology Language (OWL). The extracted entities and inferred 
relations are instantiated as ontological individuals within the BPMN ontology. This 
process creates a semantic representation of the generated process in the form of a 

knowledge graph. Each entity 𝑒𝑖 𝜖 𝐸 is mapped to a corresponding ontology class 
instance, while each relation 𝑟𝑖𝑗 𝜖 𝑅 is represented as an object propriety linking the 
corresponding entities. This semantic representation preserves the structural and or-
ganizational relationship identified during the extraction process. Maintaining the 
process models generated within an ontology enables future semantic querying of 
process structures, facilitates knowledge reuse across related processes, and allows 
future mechanisms to refine or extend previously generated. We have used the Py-
thon framework owlready2 [37] that creates a copy of the original a previous artifact 
based on BPMN 2.0 Ontology OWL file format, which contains the definitions of 
the meta-model classes and their associated properties. For each identified entity, a 
corresponding OWL Individual is created, mapping it to its ontological class, and 
the entity’s attributes (such as name, lane assignment) are injected as Data Proper-
ties. When there is a relation between two entities, firstly the system updates the 
has_outgoing property of the source, to point to a new SequenceFlow instance, 
which is linked to the successor entity using the has_target_ref property.

4.2 LLM-based relation extraction inference engine evaluation 
For the relation extraction task, no publicly available dataset exists that maps the 
logical dependencies required for BPMN construction. Conventional relation ex-
traction models are typically trained by adjacent entity pairs which assume that se-
mantic dependencies occur sequentially in the text. However, BPMN process de-
scriptions usually contain non-adjacent control-flow dependencies, most of the time 
in gateway constructs. In such cases, the relation between a gateway and its corre-
sponding branches may be across multiple clauses. These structures often follow a 
pattern such as true branches are proximal relations, while false branches are usually 
non-adjacent control-flow dependencies. To address this, we designed a generation 
pipeline focused on capturing conditional branching patterns. We initiated the pro-
cess by extracting sentence structures that contain explicit control-flow logic from 
the PET dataset. A sliding window algorithm was then applied over the extracted 
sentences. While traditional methods assume strict adjacency, this assumption was 
relaxed in our method because gateway constructs frequently separate the positive 
and negative branched by intermediate clauses. Since these extractor pairs lacked 
explicit logical labels, we used a Zero-Shot BART model [40], to automatically 
annotate the 519 simplified relation pairs. The model was prompted with two hy-
potheses: Hypothesis A: “The step {target} represents a positive flow” and Hypoth-
esis B: “The step {target} represents a negative or rejection flow” to distinguish 
between true and false branches and annotate the dataset. 
To further ensure generalization, an evaluation dataset was constructed to in-
clude variations in domain terminology, phrasing, and control-flow structures that 
are not present in the training data. This design prevents the model from relying on 
memorized patterns and instead evaluates the ability to infer relationships in previ-
ously unseen scenarios. Each data instance follows the same representation intro-
duced in the methodology, consisting of the contextual sequence C, the candidate 
entity pair (𝑒𝑖, 𝑒𝑗), and their corresponding relation label 𝑟𝑖𝑗. The dataset was created 
using a hybrid-generation strategy. The initial samples were hand-crafted and well-
known examples that were not included in the PET dataset, with the focus on gate-
ways and implicit negation patterns. Additional samples were generated from exist-
ing PET scenarios by altering domain-specific terminology and keywords used in 

the gateway components. Moreover, we have continued to generate more data based 
on these two methodologies. The resulting benchmark is explicitly balanced for the 
branching logic, ensuring that the evaluation includes sufficient examples of all con-
trol-flow dependencies. The dataset contains: 142 Branching samples, 66 Se-
quences, 48 Condition Specification, and 46 Parallel constructs (illustrated in Figure 
4).

4.3 Real-world expert defined healthcare process generation 
The generation of processes from raw text has multiple applications in 
healthcare, ranging from the development and updating of treatment plans to the 
creation of diagnostic and triage protocols, and the organization of workflows 
within medical teams. Medicine relies on protocols and is in a constant state of evo-
lution, therefore, the continuous updating of these protocols is essential. The ability 
to generate and update protocols automatically represents a major advantage, saving 
time and improving overall efficiency. In the prehospital level, simple but continu-
ously improved protocols enable accurate patient triage by facilitating the rapid 
identification of life-threatening conditions based on presenting symptoms or vital 
parameters. Once a diagnosis is established, the initial steps: clinical decision-mak-
ing and treatment, are supported by clearly defined protocols. At the hospital level, 
monitoring patient parameters according to simplified protocols allows nursing staff 
to effectively track patient status, recognize early warning signs, and guide the ini-
tial response in emergency situations until a physician becomes available. Follow-
ing discharge, the development of protocols designed for nursing staff or even non-
medical personnel may significantly contribute to the monitoring of patients with 
chronic conditions, particularly in areas with physician shortages or among popula-
tions with limited access to medical services. 
In this context, to further validate the practical applicability of the proposed so-
lution, we applied the pipeline to a healthcare process model derived from an ongo-
ing European research project, [41]. The process represents an operational care pro-
cess describing the transitional care path of a patient with heart disease between 
home and hospital in Romania, reflecting real constraints and multi-actor collabo-
ration patterns. The process description was developed by domain experts and 
serves as a representative realistic scenario and input for the proposed method. 
The process begins when a patient develops new symptoms or experiences a deterioration 
of a pre-existing condition and depending on the severity of the symptoms, the process 
branches into two pathways. 
If it presents acute severe symptoms, the patient or their family contacts emergency ser-
vices via 112 or the patient may present directly to the emergency department, either inde-
pendently or accompanied by family. Upon arrival, the patient is evaluated to determine 
whether urgent hospitalization is necessary or if they can be discharged with appropriate 
treatment recommendations. 
If discharged, follow-up care is provided by the general practitioner, or the patient may 
be referred to for outpatient cardiology evaluation. 
For mild symptoms or known conditions, the patient visits the general practitioner, who 
adjusts the current treatment or initiates a new one, and recommends evaluation by a cardi-
ologist. Patients may also consult a cardiologist directly, but only in a private setting. 
The outpatient cardiologist performs a detailed evaluation, establishes the final diagnosis, 
prescribes treatment, and schedules periodic follow-up visits. 

For complex cases, the cardiologist recommends hospitalization for additional compre-
hensive evaluations. If the patient is unable to provide full medical information, the family 
provides the relevant medical history, otherwise the patient provides it directly. 
Based on the final diagnosis, the patient receives the appropriate treatment: medical, in-
terventional, or surgical. During hospitalization, communication with the patient’s family is 
maintained, and they are continuously informed of the patient’s progress. When the treatment 
is complete, the hospital staff prepares the discharge. 
At discharge, the hospital staff provides the patient with a discharge summary containing 
treatment recommendations and a follow-up plan. The process concludes when the patient 
submits the discharge summary to their general practitioner. 
From this description, six actors are identified, nine XOR Gateways and a Par-
allel path. Patient, Family, Emergency Department, General Practitioner, Outpatient 
Cardiologist and Hospital represent the actors, serving as primary lanes within the 
process pool. Following the onset symptoms, the first XOR Gateway evaluates the 
Patient’s condition by distinguishing between acute and mild cases, ensuring a mu-
tually exclusive branching structure. If the symptoms have a sudden onset, the pro-
cess is directed toward emergency care, either through emergency services (112) 
being directly contacted by the Patient or Family, or direct presentation to the Emer-
gency Department. Otherwise, the Patient follows the mild pathway and is directed 
to the General Practitioner or outpatient care. Upon evaluation in the Emergency 
department, another XOR Gateway determines whether the Patient must be admit-
ted or can be discharged. In the outpatient path, a further XOR Gateway evaluates 
whether the case is complex or not. If not, the Patient continues with the normal 
routine care, otherwise, it is referred for urgent hospitalization. Upon admission, 
another XOR Gateway checks the Patient’s ability to provide medical history. If it 
cannot communicate, the Family provides the medical history, otherwise, the Pa-
tient provides it directly and a final diagnosis is established. Finally, a parallel 
branch is executed during the hospital stay, where treatment (represented as the final 
XOR Gateway, which can be medical, interventional or surgical) is carried out 
while communication with Family is maintained. The process ends when the Patient 
presents the summary to the General Practitioner. This process has been manually 
translated into a standard BPMN 2.0 diagram (illustrated in Figure 6).

5 Discussions 
The proposed solution presents strong potential for automating business process 
modelling. Enabling direct transformation from unstructured natural language pro-
cess descriptions into BPMN models, the pipeline reduces the need for specialized 
modelling expertise, domain-specific knowledge, and accelerates the process design 
development. This is valuable considering that in organizational environments pro-
cess knowledge is often documented informally and needs to be manually inter-
preted. In addition to automation, this approach contributes to improved consistency 
across process representations. The integration of ontology further extends the ap-
plicability of this solution beyond model generation. Transforming process models 
into knowledge structures allows for further querying and reuse of process 
knowledge. These capabilities facilitate comparison, detection of recurring patterns, 


and incremental refinement over time, particularly in organizational environments 
where processes evolve over time. Evaluation results demonstrate that the proposed 
multi-layered pipeline can perform relation extraction and structural reconstruction 
with 90%+ accuracy. A closer analysis of the class level performance shows con-
sistently high precision and recall across all relation categories, confirming the abil-
ity to capture both sequential and non-adjacent control-flow structures. This is par-
ticularly relevant for modelling conditional and branching structures, where 
relations span across multiple clauses. The inference engine captures non-adjacent 
(non-linear) relationships, essential for capturing conditional branching. The per-
formance analysis indicates that structured prompting and controlled inference sig-
nificantly improves the relation extraction performance and has an important role 
in reducing misclassification of complex branching patterns. 
Another important observation is the ability of the proposed pipeline to handle 
real world expert defined processes descriptions even under limited data conditions. 
Despite using a relatively small number of samples, the model achieves high accu-
racy across the evaluated relation types. This indicates that the structured formula-
tion of the task is composed of a dynamic few-shot conditioning strategy, combin-
ing static and dynamic prompt construction, reduces the dependence on large 
datasets and suggests potential to generalize from limited samples.

it nevertheless suggests that the model produces a closely aligned representation. 
For the Camunda training process, the computed GED value is 6.87 and the nor-
malized edited distance (RGED) is 0.286 and a similarity score of 0.714. The rela-
tively low edit distances indicate that only a small number of transformations are 
required to convert to the reference diagram. Most of these differences appear due 
to variations in task or minor gateway placement adjustments, rather than incorrect 
control-flow. For the textbook process, GED was 9.97, closely aligned to the previ-
ous cases while RGED has a value of 0.453 and the similarity of 0.547. The major 
structural differences come from variations in the rejection-related activities and 
additional flow connections introduced in the generated model. Despite these in-
consistencies, the main evaluation logic and parallel verification structure remains 
correctly represented. This analysis shows that, generally, for simpler processes, the 
generated models align closely with the reference models. GED values are directly 
proportional to the complexity of the process, due to the additional elements and 
branching patterns. Nevertheless, the generated models consistently correctly iden-
tify the core process topology, including the actors, main execution paths, and de-
cision points. 
Despite the promising results, several limitations must be considered. First, the 
evaluation is partially based on synthetic and generated datasets from relation ex-
traction, due to the absence of publicly available datasets or benchmarks covering 
BPMN control-flow relationships. While these datasets enable controlled experi-
mentation, they may not fully capture the variability and ambiguity of real process 
descriptions, potentially leading to an overestimation of the overall performance. 
Second, although the proposed pipeline shows improvements into non-adjacent 
control-flow relationships, certain complex linguistic constructs as implicit tem-
poral relation, deeply nested conditions or domain specific terminology, remain 
challenging and can lead to oversimplification in the generated models. Third, the 
inference mechanism depends on structured templates and example selection, which 
introduces a sensitivity to the quality and representativeness of the provided exam-
ples. As a result, performance may be limited when dealing with diverse or domain 
specific descriptions. Finally, the current implementation focuses on a subset of 
BPMN elements, primarily activities, actors and control-flow constructs, and it does 
not cover the entire spectrum of BPMN ones, such as event types, message flows, 
and advanced gateway configurations. This limitation may limit the expressiveness 
of the generated models in complex scenarios. 
 

6 Conclusions 
In this paper, we introduced a multi-layered pipeline for transforming natural 
language process descriptions into BPMN process models. The proposed solution 
separates the textual processing, control-flow relationship extraction, and structural 
validation into multiple components, enabling a consistent reconstruction of the 
processes. A key contribution of this work is the identification of the limitation in 
adjacent relation extraction approaches and the introduction of an LLM-based in-
ference engine capable of capturing non-adjacent (non-linear) relationships. The 
experimental evaluation on multiple scenarios, including multi-actor complex pro-
cesses, demonstrated the practical usage of the proposed method. Beyond model 
generation, the integration of ontology population extends further the contribution, 
by providing a foundation for iterative improvement and knowledge reuse. Overall, 
the proposed pipeline contributes to the automation of business process modelling 
across diverse domains and scenarios, by bridging the gap between informal process 
description and formal process representations, while improving consistency and 
efficiency in process design. 
As for future work, several directions can be explored to further improve the 
proposed approach. First, extending the pipeline to support a broader range of 
BPMN elements, including event types, message flows, and advance gateways con-
figurations, would increase the level of detail and applicability of the generated 
models. Second, the ontology representation can be leveraged as a retrieval-aug-
mented generation (RAG) component to support more context-aware inference and 
improve the handling of complex or domain-specific process descriptions. Finally, 
integrating learning-based validation mechanisms would help the method to learn, 
detect and correct structural inconsistencies in generated process models especially 
in complex or ambiguous scenarios.
