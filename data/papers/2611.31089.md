## Orthogonally Mixed Sign Coding with Progressive Residual Refinement for Transformer Weight Compression

LLM quantization reduces storage but often degrades predictive fidelity at low bit rates. We propose a training-free, CDF-free, Gaussian-CLT-motivated progressive residual-sign quantization framework that uses randomized orthogonal mixing to promote Gaussian-like residual distributions and improve sign-based reconstruction of Transformer weights. Each stage independently normalizes residuals and analytically calibrates reconstruction, progressively recovering quantization errors. The framework enables flexible storage–fidelity trade-offs without learned codebooks, empirical CDFs, or trellis optimization. We evaluate GPT-2 Large and Llama-3-8B across attention and MLP projections. At five residual stages, joint quantization preserves near-baseline predictive fidelity, achieving perplexities of 32.16 and 12.73, compared with full-precision values of 29.89 and 11.07, respectively. Ablations further demonstrate that orthogonal mixing substantially improves reconstruction over identity-based sign coding, while activation-aware scaling improves output fidelity and reduced-precision statistics lower storage overhead with negligible perplexity degradation. Comparisons with conventional quantizers establish the method’s progressive fidelity recovery and characterize its storage–quality and runtime trade-offs. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of orthogonally mixed residual-sign coding for flexible, training-free Transformer weight compression.

Keywords: large language models, post-training quantization, orthogonal mixing, progressive residual coding.


### 1 Introduction

Transformer models Vaswani et al. (2017) , including GPT-2 and the LLaMA family Radford et al. (2019) ; Touvron et al. (2023a) ; Touvron et al. (2023b) , impose substantial weight-storage, transfer, and inference-memory costs. Post-training quantization (PTQ) reduces these costs without retraining. Established methods include outlier-aware LLM.int8() Dettmers et al. (2022) , second-order GPTQ Frantar et al. (2023) , activation-aware SmoothQuant and AWQ Xiao et al. (2023) ; Lin et al. (2024) , and rotation-based QuaRot and SpinQuant Ashkboos et al. (2024) ; Liu et al. (2025) . Yet scalar quantization does not directly exploit relationships among coordinates of a weight vector.

Vector quantization (VQ) captures such structure through joint encoding and has a long history in information theory and signal processing where given a matrix $W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ of n vectors in dimension d, is mapped to a discrete, finite subset of representative vectors called a codebook. Then, using just the codebook approximately reconstruct the original matrix. Recently, VQ has been central to AI infrastructure with techniques that employ additive codebooks Egiazarian et al. (2024) , data-aware codebooks and error compensation van Baalen et al. (2024) , structured lattice codebooks Tseng et al. (2024a) , or trellis coding Tseng et al. (2024b) . These methods motivate a question: can vector weights be compressed progressively, with explicit control over reconstruction fidelity, without learned codebooks, trellis search, or distribution-dependent Cumulative Distribution Function(CDF) computations? This question matters because a fixed low-bit representation may offer insufficient fidelity, while storing additional correction stages could recover accuracy at an added storage cost.

In this paper, we introduce a codebook-free, CDF-free, information theoretically near-optimal, Progressive Residual-sign Vector Codec where each stage centers and RMS-normalizes a weight or residual vector, applies seeded orthogonal mixing, and encodes the transformed coordinates by their signs. Compact statistics and a fitted least-squares gain support reconstruction; blockwise distortion selects among deterministic transform candidates. The codec then encodes the remaining error as successive sign-coded corrections, accepting a stage only when it decreases blockwise RMS distortion. Any accepted prefix can be decoded, making residual-stage depth an explicit storage–fidelity control rather than requiring a separately trained quantizer for each operating point. Gaussian central-limit behavior motivates mixing but is neither assumed for correctness nor evaluated through CDF or inverse-CDF operations. Activation calibration does not update model weights; no gradients, distillation, or recovery fine-tuning are required.

We evaluate GPT-2 Large and Llama-3-8B under attention-only, MLP-only, and joint attention–MLP quantization, measuring reconstruction error, perplexity, output fidelity, serialized storage, and runtime across residual depths and codec ablations. Our contributions are: (i) a CDF-free, sign-based vector codec with compact reconstruction statistics; (ii) distortion-aware transform selection and monotonic residual refinement; and (iii) a model-level characterization of progressive storage–fidelity trade-offs without retraining.

Our results show that deeper residual refinement substantially improves model fidelity, although it increases storage and reconstruction cost. Under joint attention–MLP quantization at five residual stages, perplexity reaches $32.16$ on GPT-2 Large and $12.73$ on Llama-3-8B, compared with full-precision baselines of $29.89$ and $11.07$ , respectively. Ablations identify activation-aware scaling and transform selection as useful contributors to fidelity. These findings demonstrate the potential of training-free progressive residual-sign quantization to balance compression efficiency and model fidelity through adjustable residual-stage depth.


### 2 Background and Related Work

Aggressive weight compression requires preserving useful model information within a limited storage budget, including the auxiliary information needed for reconstruction. The choice of representation is therefore central: it determines what information is retained, how weights are reconstructed, and how additional bits improve the approximation. Our work investigates a progressively refinable representation based on orthogonally mixed sign vectors. Its distinguishing contribution is the design of each residual stage, which combines distortion-based selection with reconstruction statistics and transform metadata.

Researchers have proposed different PTQ techniques for compressing Transformer weight matrices $W\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ without retraining. GPTQ uses approximate second-order reconstruction information Frantar et al. (2023) ; AWQ protects activation-salient channels Lin et al. (2024) ; SmoothQuant shifts quantization difficulty between weights and activations Xiao et al. (2023) ; and LLM.int8() treats outliers separately Dettmers et al. (2022) . In spite of their significant contribution these methods don’t explore VQ.

Whiles the scalar quantizers encode coefficients, vector quantizers encode blocks. AQLM uses additive codebooks Egiazarian et al. (2024) , and GPTVQ uses data-aware vector coding van Baalen et al. (2024) . QuIP# combines incoherence processing with structured lattice codebooks Tseng et al. (2024a) , while QTIP uses trellis-coded quantization to access high effective vector dimensions without an exponential codebook Tseng et al. (2024b) . Our codec instead stores transformed signs, reconstruction statistics, and transform metadata, providing a block representation that requires neither centroid lookup nor trellis optimization.

Orthogonal transforms preserve Euclidean norm while redistributing coordinate magnitudes. QuaRot and SpinQuant use rotations to improve quantization robustness Ashkboos et al. (2024) ; Liu et al. (2025) . TurboQuant combines random rotations with distribution-matched scalar quantization and, for inner-product estimation, a one-bit residual correction Zandieh et al. (2026) . We directly sign-code orthogonally mixed vectors and recursively encode their residuals. Our encoder is CDF-free : it requires neither empirical CDF estimation nor inverse-CDF tables.

Residual quantization constructs $\widehat{x}^{(r)}=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\widehat{e}^{(k)}$ , with $e^{(k)}=e^{(k-1)}-\widehat{e}^{(k)}$ and $e^{(0)}=x$ . RRQ applies low-bit scalar quantizers to a base and its residuals, producing multi-precision LLM weights from one checkpoint Luo et al. (2026) . Our contribution is the residual-stage codec : each correction is a distortion-selected, orthogonally mixed sign vector reconstructed from $(\mu,\sigma,\gamma)$ and transform metadata. Thus, the distinction lies in the stage representation, rather than residual refinement itself: additional stages refine the approximation through sign-vector geometry rather than increased scalar precision.

LoRA and QLoRA adapt model weights through optimization Hu et al. (2022) ; Dettmers et al. (2023) . Our quantizer requires neither model retraining nor recovery-adapter training; calibration examples supply only activation statistics. This avoids an additional training phase while retaining distortion-based selection during encoding.

Random orthogonal mixing forms weighted sums of normalized block elements. A central-limit argument suggests approximately Gaussian marginals under suitable regularity conditions, but does not guarantee Gaussianity for arbitrary weights. Gaussianity motivates mixing; the codec itself requires neither Gaussian probability evaluation nor empirical CDF mappings.


### 3 Problem Formulation

Let us assume that $\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{S}}=\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{L}\}$ denote the weights selected under quantization scope $\mathcal{S}\in\{\mathrm{Attention},\mathrm{MLP},\mathrm{Attention+MLP}\}$ , and let $Q_{r}(\cdot)$ represent progressive residual-sign quantization with $r$ stages.

[Tableau] $$\boxed{\widehat{W}_{\mathrm{Q},\ell}^{(r)}=Q_{r}(W_{\ell}),}$$

while untargeted parameters remain pretrained. Parameter distortion is

[Tableau] $$D_{\mathrm{param}}^{(r,\mathcal{S})}=\frac{\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{S}}\|W_{\ell}-\widehat{W}_{\mathrm{Q},\ell}^{(r)}\|_{F}^{2}}{\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{S}}|W_{\ell}|},$$

with RMSE, relative error, and cosine similarity as complementary measures.

For input $X$ , let $Z_{\mathrm{FP}}=f_{\mathrm{FP}}(X)$ and $Z_{r,\mathcal{S}}=f_{r,\mathcal{S}}(X)$ . Functional distortion is measured over valid, unshifted token positions:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{logit}}^{(r,\mathcal{S})}$ $\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i,t,v}a_{i,t}\bigl(Z_{\mathrm{FP},i,t,v}-Z_{r,\mathcal{S},i,t,v}\bigr)^{2}}{\displaystyle V\sum_{i,t}a_{i,t}},$ (1)

where $a_{i,t}\in\{0,1\}$ masks valid logit positions, and $V$ is the vocabulary size. Unlike the next-token target mask below, $a_{i,t}$ applies to unshifted token positions. Logit cosine similarity is also reported. Language-model quality is measured by masked next-token perplexity,

[Tableau] $$\boxed{\mathrm{PPL}=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}-1}m_{i,t}\log p(x_{i,t+1}\mid x_{i,\leq t})}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}-1}m_{i,t}}\right),}$$ (2)

where $m_{i,t}\in\{0,1\}$ identifies valid shifted target positions in evaluation sample $i$ . The same held-out samples, tokenization, and masking are used for reference and quantized models.

Let $S(r,\mathcal{S})$ denote compressed storage and $D(r,\mathcal{S})$ a quality-loss measure. We characterize the following conceptual storage–quality operating-point objectives by evaluating configured residual depths and projection scopes, rather than solving a constrained optimization problem:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\min_{r,\mathcal{S}}\;$ $\displaystyle D(r,\mathcal{S})$ $\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\quad S(r,\mathcal{S})\leq B,$ (3) $\displaystyle\min_{r,\mathcal{S}}\;$ $\displaystyle S(r,\mathcal{S})$ $\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\quad\mathrm{PPL}(r,\mathcal{S})\leq P_{\max}.$ (4)

Thus $r$ controls codec capacity and $\mathcal{S}$ the type and fraction of Transformer weights compressed.


### 4 Proposed Method


#### 4.1 Overview and Activation-Aware Calibration

The training-free pipeline selects attention, MLP, or attention+MLP projections, collects calibration activations, applies optional activation-aware equivalent scaling, and blockwise encodes selected weights with the CDF-free residual-sign codec. No model parameters are optimized.

For input-channel activation statistic $a_{j}$ and reference magnitude $a_{\mathrm{ref}}$ , we define

[Tableau] $$s_{j}=\left(\frac{a_{j}}{a_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)^{\beta},\qquad S=\operatorname{diag}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{d_{\mathrm{in}}}),$$ (5)

subject to configured bounds. The equivalent transformation is written as

[Tableau] $\displaystyle W^{\prime}$ $\displaystyle=WS,$ (6) $\displaystyle x^{\prime}$ $\displaystyle=S^{-1}x,$ (7) $\displaystyle W^{\prime}x^{\prime}$ $\displaystyle=Wx,$ (8)

which changes the weight distribution presented to the codec without changing the full-precision operation.


#### 4.2 CDF-Aware Quantization

When the cumulative distribution function (CDF) $F_{X}$ is known, a $b$ -bit quantizer can map $X$ to a uniform variable $U=F_{X}(X)$ and assign

[Tableau] $$q(X)=\min\left\{2^{b}-1,\left\lfloor 2^{b}F_{X}(X)\right\rfloor\right\}.$$ (9)

Reconstruction uses the inverse CDF:

[Tableau] $$\widehat{X}=F_{X}^{-1}\left(\frac{q(X)+1/2}{2^{b}}\right).$$ (10)

This produces equal-probability bins but does not necessarily minimize mean-squared error. It also requires CDF knowledge or estimation. In contrast, our framework avoids explicit CDF estimation through orthogonal mixing, sign encoding, and progressive residual reconstruction.


#### 4.3 CDF-Free Residual-Sign Codec

We now eliminate the need to know CDFs. For each length- $n$ residual vector $e$ in a row block, the codec computes its mean and centered RMS:

[Tableau] $$\mu=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i},\qquad\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\|e-\mu\mathbf{1}\|_{2}^{2}}.$$ (11)

Using $q\in\{16,32\}$ -bit stored statistics, let $\widetilde{\mu}=Q_{q}(\mu)$ and $\widetilde{\sigma}=Q_{q}(\max\{\sigma,\epsilon\})$ , where $Q_{q}$ denotes rounding to the specified format. The residual is normalized, orthogonally mixed, and sign-encoded:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle z$ $\displaystyle=\frac{e-\widetilde{\mu}\mathbf{1}}{\max\{\widetilde{\sigma},\epsilon\}},$ (12) $\displaystyle u$ $\displaystyle=Vz,\qquad VV^{T}=I_{m},$ (13) $\displaystyle s_{i}$ $\displaystyle=\begin{cases}+1,&u_{i}\geq 0,\\ -1,&u_{i}<0.\end{cases}$ (14)

Only the first $m_{\mathrm{eff}}\leq m$ signs are stored; the remaining transformed coordinates are zeroed during reconstruction. By default, $m_{\mathrm{eff}}=m=n$ .

Each mixed coordinate $u_{i}=\sum_{j}V_{ij}z_{j}$ may exhibit approximately Gaussian behavior under suitable weak-dependence and non-dominance conditions. This motivates orthogonal mixing but neither guarantees Gaussianity nor imposes a distributional assumption. The transform is generated by QR orthonormalization of a seeded Gaussian random matrix. Unlike QTIP Tseng et al. (2024b) , which employs trellis-coded quantization, our codec directly encodes transformed signs without Gaussian CDF or inverse-CDF evaluation.

For reconstruction, let $\bar{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}$ contain the retained signs followed by zeros. Define

[Tableau] $$b=\widetilde{\sigma}V^{T}\bar{s},\qquad t=e-\widetilde{\mu}\mathbf{1}.$$ (15)

A per-vector, per-stage least-squares gain is fitted and stored at precision $q$ :

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\gamma_{\mathrm{LS}}$ $\displaystyle=\frac{t^{T}b}{\max\{b^{T}b,\epsilon\}},$ (16) $\displaystyle\widetilde{\gamma}$ $\displaystyle=Q_{q}(\gamma_{\mathrm{LS}}),$ (17) $\displaystyle\hat{e}$ $\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mu}\mathbf{1}+\widetilde{\gamma}b.$ (18)

Nonfinite gains are replaced by the Gaussian-derived fallback $\sqrt{2/\pi}$ before rounding.

For each row block $B$ , the codec evaluates $K$ deterministic candidate transforms and selects

[Tableau] $$j^{*}=\arg\min_{1\leq j\leq K}\frac{\|B-\widehat{B}(V_{j})\|_{F}^{2}}{|B|},$$ (19)

where $\widehat{B}(V_{j})$ includes progressive residual refinement and any early termination. Only the selected transform identifier and encoded signs and statistics $(\widetilde{\mu},\widetilde{\sigma},\widetilde{\gamma})$ are retained. No empirical CDF tables, learned codebooks, or trellis paths are required.


#### 4.4 Successive Residual Refinement

Successive residual-stage quantization progressively encodes reconstruction errors through sign-coded corrections, improving weight fidelity. Its progressive representation, and near-optimality at every level, enables inference-time selection of decoding depth, providing flexible storage–fidelity trade-offs without retraining or requantization. For each row block $B$ , initialize $E^{(0)}=B$ and $\widehat{B}^{(0)}=0$ . At stage $k$ , vectorwise encoding and decoding of $E^{(k-1)}$ yields a correction $\widehat{E}^{(k)}$ and

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\widehat{B}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{cand}}$ $\displaystyle=\widehat{B}^{(k-1)}+\widehat{E}^{(k)},$ (20) $\displaystyle E^{(k)}_{\mathrm{cand}}$ $\displaystyle=B-\widehat{B}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{cand}}.$ (21)

With $\mathrm{RMS}(A)=\sqrt{\|A\|_{F}^{2}/|A|}$ , monotonic refinement accepts a stage only if

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{RMS}(E^{(k-1)})-\mathrm{RMS}(E^{(k)}_{\mathrm{cand}})>\delta,$$ (22)

where $\delta\geq 0$ (default $0$ ). Accepted candidates become $\widehat{B}^{(k)}$ and $E^{(k)}$ ; otherwise, block encoding terminates. For $r_{\mathrm{enc}}\leq r$ accepted stages,

[Tableau] $$\widehat{B}^{(r_{\mathrm{enc}})}=\sum_{k=1}^{r_{\mathrm{enc}}}\widehat{E}^{(k)},\qquad E^{(r_{\mathrm{enc}})}=B-\widehat{B}^{(r_{\mathrm{enc}})}.$$ (23)

Any accepted prefix is decodable, and acceptance is evaluated blockwise rather than vectorwise.

With i.i.d. input weights, the orthogonally mixed, normalized residual coordinates are i.i.d. $u_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ . For constant-amplitude sign reconstruction $\widehat{u}_{i}=c\,\operatorname{sign}(u_{i})$ , the expected MSE is

[Tableau] $$D(c)=1-2c\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}+c^{2}.$$ (24)

The optimal amplitude is $c^{*}=\sqrt{2/\pi}$ , yielding $D(c^{*})=1-2/\pi\approx 0.3634$ , a $63.7\%$ reduction in expected squared error within this reconstruction family.

Assuming i.i.d. input and CLT mixing, all successive stages’ residuals are conveniently also i.i.d. Gaussian, hence, successive refinement compounds distortion like so:

[Tableau] $$\frac{D_{k}}{n}=\left(1-\frac{2}{\pi n}\right)D_{k-1},\;\frac{D_{L}}{n}=\left(1-\frac{2}{\pi n}\right)^{L}D_{0}.$$ (25)

As $n\gg 1$ , $(1-1/n)^{n}\to e^{-1}$ giving $\frac{D_{L}}{n}\approx D_{0}2^{-0.9185\frac{L}{n}}$ .

On the other hand, for a memoryless (i.i.d.) Gaussian source, Shannon’s rate–distortion function is worst, giving the information theoretic distortion lower-bound of:

[Tableau] $$\frac{D_{\mathrm{RD}}(R)}{n}\geq D_{0}2^{-2R},$$ (26)

where rate $R=\tfrac{L}{n}$ denotes bits per coordinate. Thus, the predicted decay exponent $0.9185$ is approximately $45\%$ of the optimal Gaussian rate–distortion exponent, while still preserving exponential improvement with $L$ .

Our implementation uses least-squares reconstruction gains and blockwise monotonic acceptance to ensure non-increasing realized block distortion. However, if the input weights are not i.i.d., residuals are not automatically guaranteed to be Gaussian across stages – but as CLT is applicable under relatively mild conditions, this can be remedied by normalization and whitening of input weights, while stored statistics metadata may increase the effective rate.


#### 4.5 Blockwise Execution and Storage

Selected matrices are partitioned into independent row blocks with associated range, projection type, residual stream, statistics, and transform metadata. For $P$ codec devices, blocks are assigned as

[Tableau] $$B_{i}\mapsto G_{\,1+((i-1)\bmod P)},$$ (27)

enabling multi-GPU quantization. Compressed blocks are reconstructed on demand with optional caching, while both models can be evaluated on a single accelerator.

Checkpoints may store compressed projections and metadata as overlays, loading untargeted parameters from the pretrained model. Thus, selected weight payload, serialized overlay size, and complete deployment storage are reported separately, since overlay size does not represent a self-contained compressed model.

### 5 Experimental Methodology

We evaluate GPT-2 Large ( gpt2-large ) and Llama-3-8B ( meta-llama/Meta-Llama-3-8B ) under attention, MLP, and attention+MLP quantization, excluding embeddings and the language-model head. Each scope uses $r\in\{1,2,3,5\}$ , yielding $2\times 3\times 4=24$ primary conditions.

Calibration, test, and held-out subsets are constructed from WikiText-2 raw ( wikitext-2-raw-v1 ). Original splits are combined, normalized, filtered, deduplicated, and deterministically shuffled (seed 42); the first 512 samples form the calibration pool, the next 256 the test set, and the final 256 the held-out set. Calibration only estimates activation statistics; runs use at most 32 calibration samples, and manuscript metrics use the held-out set. The supplied Llama-3-8B evaluation JSON identifies data/evaluation.txt , a limit of 256 evaluation samples, a maximum sequence length of 256, and 36,153 evaluated tokens. The JSON alone does not independently verify the provenance of the evaluation file.

##### 6.1.1 Comparison Across Quantization Scopes

Figure 1 and Table 2 show a consistent progressive recovery trend across all three scopes. Parameter MSE and relative error decrease similarly with $r$ . At $r=5$ , parameter MSE is approximately $5.3\text{--}5.5\times 10^{-5}$ and relative error is approximately $0.149$ across all scopes. The main differences therefore appear in model-level fidelity rather than in the local weight-reconstruction trend.

Attention-only quantization is the least disruptive scope at low and moderate residual depth. At $r=1$ , its perplexity is $429.36$ , compared with $5765.20$ for MLP-only and $6768.31$ for joint attention+MLP quantization. The gap narrows rapidly with additional residual stages: at $r=3$ , perplexity is $34.64$ , $36.94$ , and $41.09$ for attention, MLP, and attention+MLP, respectively. At $r=5$ , all three configurations are close to the full-precision baseline, with perplexities of $31.44$ , $31.15$ , and $32.16$ , respectively.

Output fidelity also converges. At $r=5$ , attention-only quantization gives the strongest overall logit fidelity, with logit MSE $0.0836$ , cosine similarity $0.9990$ , KL divergence $0.0342$ , next-token agreement $88.86\%$ , and top- $k$ overlap $88.91\%$ . MLP-only and joint quantization also recover high fidelity, with next-token agreement of $80.68\%$ and $80.05\%$ , respectively. MLP-only quantization obtains the lowest perplexity at this retained maximum stage count, $31.15$ , while attention-only quantization retains stronger token-level and logit-level agreement.

Storage and runtime scale primarily with the count of quantized parameters. Attention-only quantizes approximately $236$ million parameters, MLP-only approximately $472$ million, and the joint scope approximately $708$ million. Consequently, at $r=5$ , checkpoint size increases from $287.32$ MiB for attention to $570.18$ MiB for MLP and $857.25$ MiB for attention+MLP. Streaming forward time follows the same ordering: approximately $2.91$ s, $4.28$ s, and $7.43$ s. These results show that residual depth controls fidelity within a scope, while scope size determines the dominant storage and runtime cost.

Overall, $r=5$ , the largest residual depth retained in this study, provides near-baseline perplexity for all three scopes. The results therefore support a clear rate–distortion interpretation: increasing residual depth through the evaluated range recovers model quality, whereas quantizing a larger subsystem increases the cost required to reach a comparable fidelity level.

##### 6.2.1 Comparison Across Quantization Scopes

Figure 2 and Table 3 demonstrate progressive recovery at every scope. At $r=1$ , perplexity is $2076.92$ for attention, $327947.62$ for MLP, and $1.21\times 10^{6}$ for joint quantization. Thus, a single residual stage does not preserve usable language-model fidelity under the present settings, particularly when MLP weights are quantized. Next-token agreement is $9.62\%$ , $0.05\%$ , and $0.00\%$ , respectively.

At $r=2$ , perplexity decreases to $32.09$ , $35.70$ , and $123.44$ for attention, MLP, and joint scopes. At $r=3$ , it reaches $13.75$ , $16.04$ , and $17.29$ . With five residual stages, the corresponding perplexities are $11.90$ , $12.55$ , and $12.73$ , compared with $11.07$ for full precision. The relative increases at $r=5$ are approximately $7.5\%$ , $13.3\%$ , and $15.0\%$ , respectively.

Output fidelity improves alongside perplexity. At $r=5$ , the attention-only setting attains logit cosine similarity $0.9840$ , KL divergence $0.0789$ , next-token agreement $87.06\%$ , and top- $k$ overlap $85.49\%$ . MLP-only obtains cosine similarity $0.9738$ , agreement $83.11\%$ , and top- $k$ overlap $81.06\%$ . Joint quantization obtains cosine similarity $0.9692$ , agreement $81.61\%$ , and top- $k$ overlap $79.64\%$ . Parameter MSE decreases with stage depth within each scope: from $3.278\times 10^{-4}$ to $2.247\times 10^{-5}$ for attention, $5.228\times 10^{-5}$ to $3.152\times 10^{-6}$ for MLP, and $1.053\times 10^{-4}$ to $6.867\times 10^{-6}$ for joint quantization.

At $r=5$ , serialized overlay checkpoints occupy $1610.37$ MiB, $6737.66$ MiB, and $8347.85$ MiB for attention, MLP, and joint scopes, respectively. Mean streaming forward times are $8.07$ s, $43.17$ s, and $50.06$ s, respectively. The streaming path is substantially slower than the full-precision reference (approximately 35 ms per forward pass); these measurements do not demonstrate accelerated inference. The checkpoints also retain dependencies on the unchanged base-model weights and should not be interpreted as self-contained deployment artifacts.


#### 6.3 Cross-Model Analysis

GPT-2 Large and Llama-3-8B exhibit the same broad trend: increasing residual depth reduces parameter distortion and improves language-model fidelity for attention-only, MLP-only, and joint quantization. However, their baseline perplexities and quantized parameter counts differ, so absolute perplexities and raw checkpoint sizes should not be interpreted as matching comparisons. GPT-2 Large has a full-precision perplexity of $29.89$ , and Llama-3-8B has $11.07$ .

At $r=5$ , GPT-2 Large perplexity is $31.44$ , $31.15$ , and $32.16$ for attention, MLP, and joint scopes, corresponding to approximately $5.2\%$ , $4.2\%$ , and $7.6\%$ above baseline. For Llama-3-8B the corresponding perplexities are $11.90$ , $12.55$ , and $12.73$ , or $7.5\%$ , $13.3\%$ , and $15.0\%$ above baseline. Attention-only quantization is the least disruptive scope at $r=1$ for both models, but differences among scopes remain visible at $r=5$ , particularly for Llama-3-8B.

The larger Llama-3-8B quantized scopes also incur substantially larger serialized overlays and longer streaming reconstruction times. These figures characterize the measured prototype, not a normalized compression advantage or hardware speedup. The results support residual depth as a useful fidelity control while underscoring the need for scope-aware and implementation-aware evaluation.


#### 6.4 Orthogonal-Mixing Comparison

A separate joint-scope benchmark evaluates FP16 reconstructed dense weights on 4,095 scored tokens; its results are not directly comparable to the scope-specific streaming runs. Figure 3 shows that orthogonal mixing lowers GPT-2 Large perplexity and selected-weight MSE at all tested depths. On Llama-3-8B, MSE improves at every depth and perplexity at $r=2,3,5$ ; the omitted $r=1$ perplexity is worse with orthogonal mixing ( $2.42\times 10^{6}$ versus $9.03\times 10^{5}$ ). Estimated payloads are nearly matched between the two sign variants.

The orthogonal sign codec does not dominate conventional quantizers in perplexity per selected-weight payload. At $r=5$ , GPT-2 Large sign coding yields PPL $39.73$ at $6.618$ estimated BPW, whereas scalar 4-bit coding at $r=1$ yields $37.59$ at $3.658$ BPW and residual VQ ( $K=256$ , $r=5$ ) yields $36.98$ at $4.995$ BPW. ### 7 Ablation Studies

Both models use joint attention+MLP quantization at $r=5$ with $n=m=256$ , 32-bit statistics, 128 block rows, block batch size 4, $K=4$ , activation-aware scaling (AAS), 32 calibration samples, monotonic stage acceptance, and seed 42 unless otherwise stated. Detailed measurements appear in Appendix A.

For GPT-2 Large, AAS reduces logit MSE from $0.3001$ to $0.2543$ and perplexity increase from $9.79\%$ to $7.63\%$ , despite higher parameter MSE. Four transform candidates outperform one; calibration-size and block-row effects are modest. Using 16-bit rather than 32-bit statistics increases compression from $4.77\times$ to $5.53\times$ with negligible fidelity change. Vector dimension controls the observed storage–fidelity trade-off.

For Llama-3-8B, AAS reduces logit MSE from $1.0983$ to $0.6920$ and PPL from $13.9445$ to $12.5854$ , although parameter MSE rises from $3.379\times 10^{-6}$ to $6.867\times 10^{-6}$ . Sixteen-bit statistics reduce the overlay from $8347.85$ to $7220.71$ MiB with comparable PPL. Increasing $K$ from 1 to 4 reduces PPL from $12.6102$ to $12.5854$ but increases encoding time from about $100.3$ to $175.6$ s. Other tested settings have smaller or model-dependent effects. No Llama-3-8B vector-dimension or early-stop-rate ablation is available. These results favor evaluating functional fidelity alongside weight error.


### 8 Discussion

Our proposed framework combines distortion-selected orthogonal mixing, analytical sign reconstruction, and progressive residual refinement without requiring learned codebooks or CDF estimation. Increasing $r$ trades storage for fidelity: from $r=1$ to $r=5$ , joint-scope PPL drops from $6768.31$ to $32.16$ (GPT-2 Large) and $1.21\times 10^{6}$ to $12.73$ (Llama-3-8B), versus full-precision $29.89$ and $11.07$ . Attention-only coding is less disruptive at $r=1$ ; all scopes approach baseline at $r=5$ (Section 6).

Weight MSE does not reliably predict functional quality. At $r=5$ , Llama-3-8B attention-only and joint coding yield MSEs of $2.247\times 10^{-5}$ and $6.867\times 10^{-6}$ but PPLs of $11.90$ and $12.73$ , respectively; their MSEs cover different parameter sets. GPT-2 Large ablations similarly show AAS benefits despite higher weight error. On Llama-3-8B, AAS raises weight MSE from $3.379\times 10^{-6}$ to $6.867\times 10^{-6}$ but lowers logit MSE from $1.0983$ to $0.6920$ and PPL from $13.9445$ to $12.5854$ .

Both models improve with depth but differ in low-depth sensitivity. Llama-3-8B uses AAS $\beta=0.5$ and four transform candidates. One-factor ablations cover AAS, candidate and calibration counts, statistics precision, block rows, and batch size, but not interactions, vector dimensions, or seed generalization. Lower $r$ and larger vectors favor storage; higher $r$ and smaller vectors favor fidelity. Across both models, $r=3$ restores substantial quality and $r=5$ approaches baseline; 16-bit statistics further reduce storage. Increasing depth or scope raises storage and reconstruction time. Streaming remains slower than full precision, while overlay size excludes self-contained deployment costs. Cross-scope rates therefore require selected-weight BPW or compression ratios.

Appendix B compares identity/orthogonal sign coding, scalar quantization, and residual VQ at estimated selected-weight rates. Mixing often improves sign coding, although scalar quantization or VQ can achieve lower PPL at lower BPW; codebook-free coding guarantees neither inference speedup nor universal rate–distortion superiority. Finally, the CLT motivates mixing without assuming Gaussianity: reconstruction uses least-squares-fitted gains, not Gaussian CDF evaluation.


### 9 Limitations

AAS relies on limited calibration data, with domain-shift robustness untested. Global residual depth ignores layer-specific sensitivity. Quantization scopes differ in parameter count, making overlay sizes incomparable without normalization and distinct from self-contained deployment storage. Streaming weight reconstruction is slower than full precision, while multi-GPU scaling depends on hardware and transfer overhead.

Evaluation is limited to GPT-2 Large and Llama-3-8B, primarily using perplexity and short-horizon fidelity; downstream, long-context, instruction-following, and generative robustness remain untested. Although Llama-3-8B includes scope/depth sweeps and component ablations, vector-dimension, early-stop, repeated-seed, and matched-rate baseline analyses are absent. One-factor ablations omit hyperparameter interactions, and broad hardware-matched PTQ comparisons remain unavailable. Finally, Gaussian-CLT behavior is a motivation rather than a verified result; orthogonality alone guarantees neither Gaussianity nor independence.


### 10 Conclusion and Future Work

We introduced training-free, CDF-free, Gaussian-CLT-motivated residual-sign quantization with orthogonal mixing and compact stage statistics. Progressive reconstruction follows

[Tableau] $$\boxed{\hat{x}^{(r)}=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\hat{e}^{(k)}},$$

where residual depth $r$ controls the rate–distortion trade-off without learned codebooks, empirical CDFs, or trellis optimization.

Progressive refinement improves fidelity across attention-only, MLP-only, and joint quantization. At $r=5$ , their respective perplexities are $31.44$ , $31.15$ , and $32.16$ for GPT-2 Large (full precision: $29.89$ ), and $11.90$ , $12.55$ , and $12.73$ for Llama-3-8B (full precision: $11.07$ ), with scope-dependent fidelity gaps.

Llama-3-8B ablations (Section 7 ; Appendix A) show that activation-aware scaling improves perplexity from $13.9445$ to $12.5854$ and next-token agreement from $75.68\%$ to $81.75\%$ , despite increased parameter MSE. Reducing statistics precision from 32 to 16 bits shrinks checkpoints from $8347.85$ to $7220.71$ MiB, with perplexities of $12.5854$ and $12.5803$ , respectively. Four versus one transform candidate modestly improves MSE and perplexity at higher encoding cost, while calibration size and block-row/batch sizes minimally affect perplexity within tested ranges. These ablations exclude orthogonal-versus-identity comparisons and adaptive early stopping.

A separate matched-configuration joint-scope benchmark (Appendix B) shows that, at $r=5$ , orthogonal mixing reduces perplexity from $45.81$ to $39.73$ on GPT-2 Large and from approximately $6.22\times 10^{4}$ to $18.02$ on Llama-3-8B, relative to identity sign coding at similar estimated selected-weight payloads. However, scalar quantization achieves lower perplexity at some higher payload rates, precluding claims of universal rate–distortion superiority.

Streaming reconstruction preserves compactness and fidelity but incurs substantial decode-and-forward overhead. Future work includes sensitivity- or residual-energy-guided layer- and projection-specific stage allocation,

fused decoding/matrix-multiplication kernels, improved transform selection, self-contained compressed checkpoints, broader downstream and long-context evaluations, additional model families, and matched post-training quantization baselines.
