Abstract
For large language models (LLMs) and mul-
timodal large language models (MLLMs),
model-reported self-confidence is often mis-
calibrated, yet it may still be useful for efficient
evaluation. At the same time, LLM-as-a-judge
offers a scalable alternative to direct human
auditing, but its practical benefit depends on
calibration quality and inference cost.
This paper develops a unified statistical frame-
work for both evaluation routes under alter-
native sampling strategies. For direct audit-
ing, confidence-stratified sampling is used to
recover population accuracy from partially ver-
ified outputs without requiring calibrated confi-
dence. Finite-sample uncertainty is character-
ized, and the analysis shows how highly uneven
confidence strata affect audit efficiency and la-
bel allocation. For judge-assisted evaluation,
the judge is modeled through sensitivity and
false-positive rate, yielding an error-corrected
estimator with calibration uncertainty. The two
routes are then compared through human-label
and total-cost efficiency boundaries.
Experiments on MMLU-Pro and MMMU-Pro
show that miscalibrated self-confidence can
still support statistically valid stratified audit-
ing. Under highly imbalanced confidence dis-
tributions, proportional allocation substantially
reduces estimation error relative to equal-per-
stratum allocation. For judge-assisted evalua-
tion, only a small subset of target–verifier pairs
satisfies the idealized human-label efficiency
condition, and automated inference costs can
further limit potential savings. These findings
clarify the statistical and economic conditions
under which confidence-guided auditing and
automated verification are useful.
1 Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) and multimodal
large language models (MLLMs) are increasingly
evaluated at scales that make exhaustive human ver-
ification impractical (Liang et al., 2022; Lee et al.,
2024; Chiang et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024). Since
obtaining reliable reference labels can constitute
a substantial fraction of evaluation cost (Kossen
et al., 2021; Boyeau et al., 2025), only a subset of
model outputs may be inspected in practice. Evalu-
ation then becomes a statistical inference problem:
population-level accuracy must be recovered from
partial verification, together with uncertainty that
reflects the limited labeling budget.
Self-confidence can guide which outputs to audit,
but its relationship with correctness varies across
models and tasks (Tian et al., 2023; Xiong et al.,
2024). Confidence is often miscalibrated, and dis-
proportionately auditing low-confidence outputs
can underrepresent errors among confident predic-
tions. Moreover, confidence strata can differ sub-
stantially in size, making audit allocation impor-
tant for estimation precision. Thus, confidence can
guide sampling, but population accuracy must be
estimated using the sampling design and stratum
sizes.
LLM-as-a-judge provides an alternative evalua-
tion route in which a second LLM evaluates outputs
at scale (Zheng et al., 2023). Because the judge
is imperfect, human labels are still needed to es-
timate and correct its error rates, and calibration
uncertainty propagates into the final accuracy es-
timate (Lee et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026). The
evaluation budget can therefore support either di-
rect auditing or judge calibration. Their relative
efficiency depends on judge discrimination, cali-
bration requirements, and the costs of automated
inference and human review.
In this work, confidence-guided auditing and
judge-assisted evaluation are unified within a com-
mon statistical framework. For direct auditing,
model confidence is used as a stratification variable
rather than a trusted correctness probability, en-
abling population accuracy and finite-sample uncer-
tainty to be estimated from partial verification. For
judge-assisted evaluation, the judge is character-

2 Related Work
Confidence, calibration, and selective evaluation.
Model confidence has long been studied as a sig-
nal of predictive uncertainty. Calibration measures
whether reported probabilities agree with empiri-
cal correctness, with methods such as temperature
scaling used to correct systematic errors on labeled
validation data (Guo et al., 2017). For language
models, uncertainty can be elicited directly through
verbalized confidence or inferred from model out-
puts and response consistency (Lin et al., 2022;
Kadavath et al., 2022; Tian et al., 2023; Kuhn et al.,
2023). Empirical studies nevertheless show sub-
stantial miscalibration across models, tasks, and
modalities (Xiong et al., 2024; Jang et al., 2026;
Xuan et al., 2025). Selective prediction uses uncer-
tainty to trade coverage for error control (Geifman
and El-Yaniv, 2017). Population evaluation dif-
fers because confidence-guided review determines
which correctness labels become observable. Esti-
mating overall accuracy from this selected subset
therefore raises a verification-bias problem (Begg
and Greenes, 1983), requiring the sampling mecha-
nism to be accounted for explicitly.
Label-efficient statistical evaluation. Auxiliary
model information can improve evaluation through
both label acquisition and estimation. Active test-
ing and Active Surrogate Estimators use adaptive
sampling and learned surrogates to reduce uncer-
tainty in estimated model performance (Kossen
et al., 2021, 2022). Prediction-powered infer-
ence (PPI) uses a small labeled sample to cor-
rect estimates based on abundant model predic-
tions (Angelopoulos et al., 2023a), a principle ap-
plied to model evaluation by AutoEval (Boyeau
et al., 2025). StratPPI combines this correction
with stratified sampling and label allocation to ex-
ploit differences in automatic-label quality across
subpopulations (Fisch et al., 2024). These ap-
proaches make both the use of auxiliary predictions
and the allocation of labels part of the evaluation de-
sign. PPI++ and Adaptive AutoEval adjust reliance
on predictions according to their usefulness (An-
gelopoulos et al., 2023b; Park et al., 2025). With
few labels, estimating prediction weights can it-
self introduce substantial uncertainty, as shown by
studies of post-hoc regression and finite-sample
PPI++ behavior (Eyre and Madras, 2025; Mani
et al., 2025). Label savings therefore depend on
the available budget as well as the auxiliary signal.
We examine this design problem for confidence-
stratified direct auditing, characterizing estimation
precision under fixed allocations and allocations
learned from pilot audits.
LLM-as-a-judge and noisy verification. LLM-
based evaluators have been studied extensively as
scalable substitutes for human assessment, includ-
ing G-Eval, MT-Bench, and learned judge mod-
els (Liu et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2023; Zhu et al.,
2025). However, systematic effects such as posi-
tion, self-preference, knowledge, and format bias
can substantially influence their decisions (Pan-
ickssery et al., 2024). For binary correctness,
judge-assisted evaluation can instead be viewed
as prevalence estimation under an imperfect screen-
ing test (Rogan and Gladen, 1978). A judge’s
sensitivity and false-positive rate determine how
its acceptance rate maps to target-model accuracy,
while finite calibration data introduce additional
uncertainty. Recent work has incorporated such
calibration into statistical estimation and hypoth-
esis testing (Lee et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2026;
Chen et al., 2026). Building on this literature, we
compare direct auditing and judge-assisted estima-
tion at a common target precision and derive both
human-label and total-cost efficiency boundaries.

rectly auditing target outputs and calibrating an
automated judge. We first establish accuracy esti-
mation and uncertainty for each route, then com-

direct random auditing.

4 Experiments and Results
4.1 Experimental Setup
Benchmarks and scope. We evaluate whether
confidence and cross-model verification can reduce
human labeling in model-accuracy audits. MMLU-
Pro (Wang et al., 2024) and MMMU-Pro (Yue et al.,
2025) provide exact multiple-choice ground truth
for text and multimodal evaluation, respectively.
We use 8,000 MMLU-Pro questions and a fixed
1,000-question MMMU-Pro subset, covering ten
and eight model runs; primary MMLU-Pro com-
parisons require at least 80% coverage. Full model
and preprocessing details are given in Appendix B.
Audit simulation. Complete benchmark ground
truth is used to simulate partial human verification.
Outputs are stratified by confidence, and sampling
without replacement is repeated 5,000 times across
five audit budgets. Only sampled labels are used
for estimation; full ground truth is used to assess
error and construct oracle baselines.
Verifier evaluation. Each of eight models is eval-
uated against the other seven, yielding 56 directed
target–verifier pairs per benchmark. We report
judgment accuracy, sensitivity, false-positive rate,
discrimination gap, and label efficiency. The pri-
mary analysis uses binary judgments; full details
are given in Appendix B.
Cost model. We estimate each verifier’s per-
judgment cost from its observed input and out-
put tokens and standard API prices on October
10, 2026. Original answer-generation costs are
excluded because they are common to all audit
strategies. Estimates also exclude retries, discounts,
taxes, and surcharges.

4.2 Self-Confidence Is Informative but
Miscalibrated Across Benchmarks
Self-reported confidence generally tracks accuracy
but is not a calibrated probability of correctness.
On MMLU-Pro (Fig. 2), higher-confidence outputs
are typically more accurate, yet panels a and b
show substantial, model-dependent miscalibration.
Claude Sonnet 5 is the best calibrated, while several
models remain strongly overconfident; panels c
and d confirm persistent confidence–accuracy gaps.
The same pattern appears on MMMU-Pro (Ap-
pendix Fig. 6). All eight models are overconfi-
dent on average; even Claude Sonnet 5 retains a
10.1-point confidence–accuracy gap and an ECE of
0.108. Across models, accuracy ranges from 27.2%
to 63.5%, with gaps reaching 59.6 points. Thus,
confidence is informative across both benchmarks
but unreliable as a direct probability of correctness.
This distinction motivates confidence-stratified
auditing. Rather than inferring correctness from
confidence, we use it only to partition outputs and
estimate population accuracy from representative
samples within each stratum.
4.3 Partial Audits Reliably Recover
Population Accuracy
Figure 3 shows that confidence-stratified auditing
reliably recovers GPT-5.5’s MMLU-Pro accuracy
of 80.54%. Across budgets and allocation strate-
gies, bias remains near zero (panela), while estima-
tion error decreases with additional human labels
(panel c).
Allocation primarily affects precision. Uni-
form allocation performs worse at small budgets,
whereas proportional and oracle Neyman alloca-
tions are nearly identical. With 200 labels, RMSE
decreases from 4.56 percentage points under uni-
form allocation to 2.46 and 2.41 percentage points
under proportional and oracle Neyman allocation,
respectively. This reflects the long-tailed confi-
dence distribution: equal allocation oversamples
sparse strata and undersamples dominant ones,
while the π2j /nj term favors larger strata.
Panels b and d show that observed failure proba-
bilities remain below the corresponding Hoeffding
bounds. The bounds are conservative but provide
valid worst-case guarantees. MMMU-Pro shows
the same overall pattern, with greater variability at
small budgets (Appendix Fig. 7).

4.4 Verifier Efficiency across Benchmarks
Figure 4 shows that verifier accuracy, discrimi-
nation, and label efficiency are distinct. Panel a
reports generally high judgment accuracy, while
panel b shows much larger variation in the discrim-
ination gap s − αv. Panel c translates these oper-
ating characteristics into human-label efficiency:
despite strong raw accuracy, only 6 of 56 MMLU-
Pro target–verifier pairs achieve Bdirect/Bv > 1.
Panel d explains this pattern: most verifiers have
high sensitivity, but several exhibit substantial false-
positive rates, weakening discrimination. GPT-5.5
provides the strongest pooled operating point.
This distinction is important because similar
judgment accuracy can imply very different statis-
tical value. For example, Claude Sonnet 5 achieves
0.84 judgment accuracy when verifying GPT-5.5
but only 0.25 label efficiency, whereas GPT-5.5
verifying Llama-3.1-8B achieves 0.89 accuracy
and the highest efficiency ratio, 1.45. Thus, ef-
ficient population-accuracy recovery depends more
directly on separating correct from incorrect an-
swers than on overall agreement.
The pattern is more pronounced on MMMU-
Pro (Appendix Fig. 8). Across 413,562 MMLU-
Pro judgments and 55,971 MMMU-Pro judgments,
no MMMU-Pro pair exceeds the label-efficiency
threshold. GPT-5.5, for example, reaches 0.700
judgment accuracy and 0.844 sensitivity, but its
false-positive rate of 0.402 limits the discrimination
gap to 0.442.
Figure 5 adds inference cost. Its six finite break-
even cells correspond exactly to the six MMLU-
Pro pairs with Bdirect/Bv > 1. The most favor-
able case is GPT-5.5 verifying Llama-3.1-8B, with
c⋆H ≈ $0.09, while the remaining thresholds are ap-
proximately $0.34, $0.75, $0.80, $4.33, and $21.55.
Pairs only slightly above the efficiency boundary
can therefore require very high human-label costs
before their statistical advantage offsets inference
cost.
Overall, verifier discrimination determines the
human-calibration burden, while inference cost de-
termines how cheaply automated-audit uncertainty
can be reduced. Cheaper LLM inference improves
precision by enabling more verifier judgments, but
it cannot remove the calibration floor.

Limitations
This study focuses on multiple-choice
LLM/MLLM benchmarks with known refer-
ences and simulated partial auditing, assuming
representative sampling and stable verifier behavior.
Human-label noise, distribution shift, open-ended
evaluation, and continuous verifier confidence are
not fully modeled. The cost analysis relies on
observed token use and current API-price proxies,
while actual human and inference costs may vary;
thus, the derived efficiency and break-even bound-
aries should be interpreted as analytical guidance
rather than universal deployment guarantees.

B.2 Fixed and Adaptive Audits
The initial strata are
[0, 0.2], (0.2, 0.4], (0.4, 0.6], (0.6, 0.8], and
(0.8, 1]. Adjacent strata are merged until each

retained stratum contains at least 25 outputs.
Integer allocations obey 1 ≤ nj ≤ Nj and sum to
the budget. Uniform allocation distributes counts
as evenly as capacities allow. Proportional and
oracle Neyman allocations assign each remaining
audit to the stratum giving the greatest decrease in
their respective convex objectives,Pj π2j /nj andP

Appendix C gives the allocation formulas, pilot

B.3 Verifier Judgments
A verifier received the question, answer options,
and the target model’s selected answer and returned
a correct/incorrect judgment together with an esti-
mated probability that the selected target answer
was correct. The primary analysis uses the bi-
nary judgment. On MMLU-Pro, 19 of 413,581 re-
quested non-self judgments were unparseable, leav-
ing 413,562 valid judgments. MMMU-Pro target–
verifier pairs contain 997–1,000 usable judgments,
yielding 55,971 valid judgments in total. Rates
are computed separately for each target–verifier
pair; pooled operating points aggregate eligible
confusion-matrix counts. The full available pair
population is used for the efficiency calculation
rather than a selected high-confidence subset.
Sensitivity is the fraction of correct target an-
swers accepted; the false-positive rate is the frac-
tion of incorrect answers accepted. Judgment ac-
curacy counts both correct acceptance and correct
rejection. Complete-sample estimates are substi-
tuted into Eq. (7). This is a descriptive comparison,
with neither a held-out calibration pool nor an im-
plemented sequential class-enrichment policy.

D.5 Practical Interpretation
Three regimes are distinguished. If human error is
negligible, the main-text analysis applies directly.
If human sensitivity and false-positive rates can
be estimated from adjudicated examples, Eqs.(33)–
(37) provide an error-corrected extension. If human
reliability is unknown, however, population accu-
racy is generally not point identified from a single
noisy human label per output without additional
assumptions or repeated/adjudicated annotations.
Accordingly, the perfect-human assumption in
the main analysis should be interpreted as defining
the statistical value of a trusted reference label.
In practical deployments, disagreement resolution,
repeated annotation, or expert adjudication can be
used to approximate this reference process, with
their additional costs incorporated into cH.
