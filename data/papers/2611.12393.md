## AutoBuddy: Evaluating Proactive, Always-On Agents on Task Discovery and Execution in a Simulated Workplace

As large language model (LLM) agents become more autonomous, they are increasingly expected to run as always-on assistants on a user’s behalf, identifying work that needs to be done and completing it without being asked. Yet existing benchmarks usually give the agent the task to perform, whereas in knowledge work the requests sit in the user’s workspace and the agent must first recognise which obligations exist. We study proactive task discovery and execution, in which an agent must infer a user’s pending tasks from the state of their workspace and complete them in the systems where the work lives. This setting poses two challenges. For the agent, the tasks are buried in a large volume of mail, chat, documents and records. For evaluation, a benchmark needs a simulated organisation whose people, events and documents are mutually consistent, so that tasks arise realistically and their correct outcomes are known. We introduce AutoBuddy-Bench , a simulator that addresses these gaps and challenges. It models an organisation’s employees, projects, business systems and event history as a temporal graph, renders it into grounded, realistic workspace artefacts, derives ground-truth tasks from the events that create obligations, and computes each task’s outcome from the world state; execution is judged by the agent’s changes to the workspace. To evaluate current LLMs, we build AutoBuddy , a reference agent with interchangeable backbone LLMs: it identifies tasks from the user’s workspace, grounding each in the records it came from, and carries out each in a separate session with write access. Varying only the backbone, we find a systematic shift in where tasks are lost: weaker models fail mainly at discovery, intermediate models identify tasks but often stop short of carrying them out, and the strongest models fail mainly during execution, most often on business-system operations. Discovery precision is only weakly related to execution quality, suggesting that the two are distinct capabilities.


### 1. Introduction

LLM agents ( Yao et al., 2023 ; Schick et al., 2023 ; Wang et al., 2024 ) are increasingly expected to run as always-on assistants: rather than waiting for each task to be stated, an agent should identify the user’s pending tasks and complete them on the user’s behalf. In an enterprise, such tasks span human resources, finance, research and engineering, and they reach the user through emails, chats, calendar, documents and business records, for example, an email asking for a quarterly report by Friday. This setting poses two challenges. The first lies with the agent: a workspace holds a large volume of such data, most of which carries no task for the user, such as requests already answered, requests addressed to colleagues and background material. An agent must identify the user’s tasks in this context, determine what needs to be done, and then carry out multi-step execution grounded in the tools and data of the applications involved. The second lies with evaluation: a benchmark needs a simulated organisation whose people, events, documents and records are mutually consistent, so that tasks arise realistically and their correct outcomes are known.

Existing benchmarks evaluate important parts of this problem but not holistically (Section 2 ). For example, execution-based benchmarks for web, coding and enterprise agents ( Zhou et al., 2024 ; Jimenez et al., 2024 ; Merrill et al., 2026 ; Drouin et al., 2024 ; Huang et al., 2024 ; Yao et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2025 ; Patwardhan et al., 2025 ) measure how well an agent carries out a task it is given, without evaluating task discovery. Proactive-assistance benchmarks study when and how agents can choose to offer help ( Lu et al., 2025 ; Tang et al., 2026 ) , infer goals from what a user is already doing ( Nathani et al., 2026 ) , recover unstated requirements of a user-initiated task ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) , or maintain a timetable from chats ( Li et al., 2026 ) ; none targets discovering and completing a user’s outstanding workplace responsibilities. Most closely related, PROBE ( Pasternak et al., 2025 ) also requires agents to find problems in workplace data without instructions, but evaluates the discovery of a predefined bottleneck and the choice of a single resolving action, rather than the execution of complex, multi-step tasks evaluated on the resulting state.

We introduce AutoBuddy-Bench , a benchmark that addresses these gaps and challenges. From a short company specification, it generates a simulated organisation: its employees, projects, business systems and a dated history of events, linked in a temporal graph. Tasks are derived from this history, and the expected outcome of each task is derived from the world’s own records and rules rather than inferred by a separate LLM judge ( Zheng et al., 2023 ) (Figure 2 ). The graph is then rendered into realistic workspace artefacts grounded in it: the organisation is laid out onto mailboxes, chat channels, calendars and file libraries, and a language model authors its documents. For each employee and date, the benchmark renders a snapshot of that person’s mail, chat, calendar, files and business-system state, in which the ground-truth tasks are mixed with other records; the correct outcomes are never rendered. The agent acts in this environment through tools: it reads the workspace to identify the tasks to be done, then carries them out by sending mail and chat messages, editing files and operating the business systems. We evaluate task discovery by whether the agent finds the ground-truth tasks correctly, and evaluate task execution by what it actually changes in messages, documents and business records. The two scores are reported separately as well as combined end to end, so every lost task is attributed to the stage that lost it: never found, or found but not completed.

To study this setting end to end, we also present AutoBuddy , a proactive, autonomous agent which can carry out task identification and execution (Figure 3 ). To start with, AutoBuddy builds a work profile of the user, establishing their role and projects from dated workspace signals. It then identifies tasks from the user’s emails, chats, calendar and files, grounding each in the records it came from, and executes each task in a separate session with write access to the relevant systems. We evaluate AutoBuddy with 17 backbone LLMs on 120 tasks. End-to-end task discovery and execution scores range from 0.23 to 0.70 (Figure 1 ). Where tasks are lost shifts with model strength: weaker models fail mainly at discovery, intermediate models identify tasks but often stop short of carrying them out, and the strongest models fail mainly during execution, most often on business-system operations. Discovery precision is only weakly related to execution quality, indicating that recognising outstanding work and fulfilling it are distinct capabilities.

In summary, this paper makes the following contributions:

- (1) AutoBuddy-Bench , a benchmark for proactive discovery and execution of users’ outstanding workplace tasks in a simulated organisation (Section 3 ).

- (2) AutoBuddy , a proactive, autonomous agent that profiles the user’s work, discovers evidence-grounded tasks from workspace data and carries out multi-step task execution in the corresponding systems (Section 4 ).

- (3) An evaluation of 17 LLMs and detailed analyses of their performance and failure modes (Section 5 ).


### 2. Related Work


##### Instruction-driven agent execution.

Most agent benchmarks evaluate the execution of explicitly specified tasks: on the web and computer ( Zhou et al., 2024 ; Deng et al., 2023a ; Koh et al., 2024 ; Xie et al., 2024 ; Yoran et al., 2024 ; Wei et al., 2025 ) , in software engineering ( Jimenez et al., 2024 ; Merrill et al., 2026 ) , with tools ( Liu et al., 2024 ; Mialon et al., 2024 ; Qin et al., 2024 ; Luo et al., 2025 ) , and in enterprise and office applications ( Drouin et al., 2024 ; Boisvert et al., 2024 ; Styles et al., 2024 ; Trivedi et al., 2024 ; Yao et al., 2024 ; Barres et al., 2025 ; Huang et al., 2024 ; Huang et al., 2025 ; Xu et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2025 ; Patwardhan et al., 2025 ) , often checking the resulting environment state. Some simulate people, such as users and colleagues ( Park et al., 2023 ; Barres et al., 2025 ; Xu et al., 2024 ) , or asynchronous events ( Froger et al., 2025 ) . Yet execution always begins because the agent is instructed to perform a specific task. In AutoBuddy-Bench , the agent is never invoked for a task: the requests sit in the workspace it reads, and it must decide by itself which work the user owes and then carry it out.


##### Proactive assistance and intent inference.

Proactivity has recently been studied in mixed-initiative interfaces and dialogue systems ( Horvitz, 1999 ; Deng et al., 2023b ) . ProactiveBench ( Lu et al., 2025 ) and ProAgentBench ( Tang et al., 2026 ) evaluate when agents should offer help from observed user activity, ContextAgent ( Yang et al., 2025 ) whether proactive help calls the right tools, and FingerTip 20K ( Yang et al., 2026 ) task suggestions on a phone. PARE ( Nathani et al., 2026 ) infers goals from a simulated user’s activity across stateful apps and verifies its interventions, and UniClawBench ( Chen et al., 2026 ) evaluates proactive agents on live real-world tasks. Other work tests whether agents recover unstated intentions, preferences or constraints ( Qian et al., 2024 ; Wu et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2026 ) ; each task, however, begins with a user’s request, so the challenge is to recover the implicit requirements of an existing task rather than to discover which tasks need doing. ProEvent ( Li et al., 2026 ) maintains a user’s timetable from instant-messaging conversations, focusing on event tracking rather than heterogeneous workplace tasks. Most closely related, PROBE ( Pasternak et al., 2025 ) has agents search a persona’s data for an unstated bottleneck and select a resolving action; each instance is built around a predefined bottleneck, and resolution is judged by the selected action rather than by executing the work and checking the resulting state. In contrast, AutoBuddy-Bench requires an agent both to discover a user’s outstanding tasks from their emails, chats, meetings and documents and to complete them.

AutoBuddy-Bench has two parts: a generator that builds a simulated organisation and the ground-truth tasks in it (Section 3.1 ), and an evaluation pipeline that renders one employee’s workspace, runs an agent in it and scores what the agent does (Section 3.2 ).

#### 3.1. Simulating the organisation

The generator builds an organisation in four steps (Figure 2 ; more detailed stages in Appendix A ). Company scope: an LLM expands a specification of the business, workforce size, simulation period and systems into a grounding profile, from which an exact population plan is compiled. Positions: the generator creates dated employment histories, roles, accounts, projects and external parties; access rights follow this history. Events: experiments, business transactions, ledger calculations, reports and collaboration episodes (emails, messages, etc) populate a temporal graph whose validity periods support queries as of any date. Workspace artefacts: the organisation is rendered into mail, chat, calendar and file surfaces with access lists, and a language model authors documents with revisions and audiences. Every task-referenced document is bound to an exact revision and its underlying report data; unresolved bindings reject the build. The graph and ground-truth outcomes are rebuilt over these bindings.


##### Domains, tools and ground-truth tasks.

Agents read and change the workspace only through tools (Table 4 in Appendix A shows a list of tools used). Collaboration tools cover mail, chat, calendar and files; research tools mock Weights & Biases, Kubernetes, an issue tracker and a code-analysis sandbox; business tools adopt AutomationBench’s ( Shepard and Salimans, 2026 ) simulated customer-support, sales, finance and human-resources services and schemas ( Shepard and Salimans, 2026 ) . Ground-truth tasks arise from events in these domains and are kept only when anchored to a fact of the world. Each fixes the responsible employee, date and readable evidence. Expected outcomes are computed from the world’s records and governing rules: required facts, delivery target, audience and, for business tasks, each named record’s final state. The agent observes evidence and colleagues’ request, but not the ground truth.


##### World validation.

Before any agent is run, an automatic acceptance check (Figure 2 ) verifies that the task set is large and covers every task type, that every task has a required output, and that every request states what its ground truth depends on (such as the recipients or the document to update). The evaluation world passes these checks.

The pipeline evaluates an agent on one actor-day at a time, the workspace of one employee on one observation date with every ground-truth task that employee has on that date, and evaluates finding and carrying out the tasks separately (step 5 in Figure 3 ). It applies to any agent that uses the benchmark’s tools; ours is described in Section 4 .


##### Evaluating task discovery.

The agent first lists the tasks it has identified, and each listed task is matched one-to-one to the ground-truth task it refers to. Matching is deterministic: a listed task matches a ground-truth task when it cites that task’s request record as evidence. No language model is used in matching or in grading. Recall is the fraction of ground-truth tasks matched. Mistakes are judged by labels hidden on every rendered mail, chat, calendar and file record: treating an answered request or a colleague’s work as open, or creating work out of background, is a mistake. Precision is the number of matched tasks divided by matched tasks plus mistakes; listing an answered request as already done is not a mistake and is not counted. An empty list finds no tasks.


##### Evaluating execution.

Each matched task is then executed in a fresh session with write access, and the workspace before and after the session is diffed to recover what the agent actually changed: a sent email counts only if it reached the recipients’ mailboxes, a document only if its content or audience changed, and business-system state is compared record by record. The observed changes, not the agent’s own account, are compared with the ground-truth outcome: whether the required action happened, on the right target and with exactly the required audience, with the required content; and for business-system tasks, whether every named record ends in the required state with no unrelated record changed, and the requester is told by email. Business-system, headcount-review and research tasks are evaluated as pass or fail, except three tasks that ask the agent to restart failed jobs and report back. Those three and the 54 document tasks receive a weighted score of content coverage (0.5), completion (0.2), evidence (0.2) and answer status (0.1). Content coverage matches the required facts in the delivered text; evidence is the share of the records the outcome depends on that the agent’s tool trace shows it read.


##### End-to-end score.

The end-to-end score averages the execution score over all ground-truth tasks, counting a task never found as zero. A business-system task whose starting records another task on the same account has already changed, so that they no longer support its ground-truth outcome, and which the agent executed without passing, is ungradable and excluded. A harness failure that stops a whole actor-day before any task is scored leaves its tasks out of the run; a harness failure during an execution counts as zero.


### 4. The AutoBuddy Agent

AutoBuddy mines a list of tasks from the user’s workspace and carries them out: on each actor-day, it builds a work profile, discovers tasks and executes them (Figure 3 ).


#### 4.1. Discovering tasks


##### Work profile.

In a first read-only session (step 1 in Figure 3 ), the agent establishes who the employee is and what they work on. It collects a bounded set of recent, dated signals about the employee’s role and projects, clusters them into projects, and is instructed to merge two project names only when the evidence states that they are the same or two independent signals agree. The resulting profile gives discovery a project vocabulary for grouping tasks.


##### Task mining.

Discovery runs in a second read-only session over a time window (steps 2 and 3); under evaluation, the window runs from the earliest event to the latest request of the actor-day’s tasks. The agent navigates the workspace itself with tools that list, search and open records, plus a natural-language query that returns ranked matches across surfaces; it is instructed to query mail, chat, and calendar with files separately, and to open every human message in the window before classifying it. It can also read, but not change, the business systems and research tools. It extracts direct requests, commitments the employee made, follow-ups and meeting action items, and excludes updates with no expected action, tasks clearly assigned only to someone else, and automated messages that create no real obligation; a direct request is never excluded only because another record makes it look like a duplicate. Workspace content is treated as evidence, never as instructions. The output is a structured task list. Each task has a title and summary, a type, its requester and assignee, the evidence it rests on (source record, author, timestamp and excerpt), a due date flagged as stated or inferred, urgency and impact scores, a project, a stable key identifying the underlying obligation, a status, and a first-pass brief with the objective and acceptance criteria. The list can also propose status changes for tasks already known, such as a request that a later reply has answered. Tasks with no evidence inside the window are dropped.


##### Organising the task list.

The organiser ingests the list in its deterministic mode, without model calls. A task’s identity is derived from its stable key, so a later sighting of the same obligation adds evidence to the existing task instead of creating a duplicate. A recurring obligation that fires again reopens as a new occurrence, and a completed task reopens only on a newer explicit request. Proposed status changes, such as a completion, are held for the user to confirm, and tasks are scored by urgency, impact and effort and ranked to order the user’s board. Under evaluation, each actor-day starts from an empty state and runs discovery once, so only some of these features take effect: duplicates are merged within the pass and tasks the agent lists as already done are recorded as such (Section 3.2 ), whereas reopening, confirmation and ranking have no effect, since there are no earlier tasks and every matched task is executed.


#### 4.2. Executing tasks

Each matched task is executed once, in a fresh session with write tools (step 4 in Figure 3 ); tasks of one actor-day run in sequence on the same workspace. The session receives only the identifier of the task as the agent itself listed it and reads that task’s record, with the summary, evidence and brief written during discovery; neither the ground-truth task nor its request text is given to it. The brief is a starting point rather than a specification: the agent re-reads the records the task was mined from and follows them to related mail, chat, meetings, documents and system records, takes stock of the target before writing, and distinguishes completing the requested piece of work from completing the project it belongs to. All writes are pre-authorised in the simulated workspace. The agent ends by recording an outcome (done, blocked, needs confirmation or failed); evaluation relies on the observed changes, and the recorded outcome enters only the answer-status component of the execution score (Section 3.2 ). The grader’s verdict is recorded on the task after scoring and is never returned to the agent for another attempt. After an execution that the grader scores as fully correct, AutoBuddy may distil a reusable skill from it for later tasks; in the runs reported here this happened in 13 of 157 run reports, each time after the actor-day’s last task, so no scored execution could use such a skill.

##### Finding 1: main results.

Within each provider with several models, the lighter tier scores lower: claude-haiku-4.5 below claude-sonnet-5 below the claude-opus models, gpt-5.4-mini below gpt-5.4 , each luna model below the sol model of its generation, and DeepSeek-V4-Flash below DeepSeek-V4-Pro . ##### Finding 2: the stronger the model, the later in the job it fails.

The models with the weakest discovery lose tasks before they start: claude-haiku-4.5 , gpt-5.4-mini and DeepSeek-V4-Flash miss 60, 36 and 32 of the 120 tasks per run, which is $65\%$ , $44\%$ and $41\%$ of all the credit they lose. Models in the middle of the table find most tasks but often stop short of carrying them out: in only $72$ to $78\%$ of the executions of tasks they find do claude-opus-4.8 , gpt-5.4 , DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash , claude-sonnet-5 and gpt-5.6-luna make a change of the required kind (for example, sending the email or updating the record), and stopping to ask accounts for $25$ to $32\%$ of the credit they lose. Kimi-K2.6 , also in the middle of the table, is the exception: it makes the required kind of change for $92\%$ of the tasks it finds and loses its credit to misses ( $38\%$ ) and wrong or incomplete content ( $32\%$ ). The five strongest models miss three to nine tasks per run ( $7$ to $20\%$ of their lost credit) and make the required kind of change for $83$ to $92\%$ of what they find, so what they lose, they lose while doing the task: wrong or incomplete content ( $22$ to $44\%$ of lost credit), stopping to ask ( $19$ to $44\%$ ) and the wrong target ( $7$ to $25\%$ ). The two gpt-6 models break this ordering: they find tasks well ( gpt-6.1-sol has the highest recall, $0.989$ ) but make the required kind of change for only $65\%$ and $53\%$ of them, and stopping to ask accounts for $71\%$ and $57\%$ of the credit they lose (Section 5.3 ).


##### Finding 3: the strongest models lose most on business-system work.

For each of the five strongest models, business-system tasks are about $40\%$ of the tasks but $48$ to $56\%$ of the credit lost. These models earn $0.45$ to $0.56$ per business-system task, against $0.67$ to $0.75$ per collaboration task. In business systems the agent must change exactly the records a policy selects and leave every other record untouched, and the most common execution failure there is to stop and ask (Section 5.3 ).

##### Finding 4: task mining quality is distinct from execution.

Task lists differ in completeness , ownership and closure (Table 2 ). Misses range from one or two per run for gpt-6.1-sol to sixty for claude-haiku-4.5 . Three of the five strongest models raise no colleague’s request, yet strong executors still reopen finished work: gpt-5.6-sol and gpt-5.5 treat about one in ten closed items they list as open, against $88\%$ for grok-4.7 and $99\%$ for DeepSeek-V4-Pro . These are mostly errors of judgement, not fabricated evidence: $99.6\%$ of cited records exist in the employee’s workspace, and every quoted excerpt is verbatim on $84\%$ of listed tasks.

Across 17 models, discovery precision correlates with execution at $r=0.27$ and recall at $r=0.65$ ; excluding the three weakest discoverers ( $F_{1}<0.70$ ), these become $-0.30$ and $0.50$ . The coefficients are noisy with so few models, but the rankings also diverge: grok-4.7 and DeepSeek-V4-Pro rank fourteenth and thirteenth by discovery $F_{1}$ , yet sixth and third by execution. Recognising what work is owed and completing it are distinct capabilities; Section 5.3 examines the errors behind this gap.

##### Discovery: false positives are mostly old or someone else’s requests.

Over all 51 runs, the agents raised 1,663 false positives (Figure 4 a). Each one cites the records it came from, and the workspace records what those records are. Of the 1,663, $60\%$ re-opened a request that the workspace shows was already answered on an earlier day, $28\%$ took on a request addressed to a colleague, and $13\%$ cite only background material, with no request behind them. The agents also raised 1,313 closed items correctly, as work already done; of the 2,304 closed items raised in all, $43\%$ were treated as open. The two error types fall on different models. Re-opening closed work accounts for almost all of the low precision of grok-4.7 (68 per run, against 2 colleague requests) and DeepSeek-V4-Pro (43 per run). Both models still score high end to end, because the redundant work costs them nothing on the graded tasks. Taking a colleague’s request is concentrated in claude-haiku-4.5 (71 per run) and gpt-5.4-mini (35), which also miss the most tasks (60 and 36 per run). It also explains much of claude-opus-5 ’s pairing of near-top recall (0.975) with low precision (0.691): 17 of its 53 false positives per run are requests addressed to someone else.


##### Discovery: misses are single tasks, not whole workspaces.

Of the 907 misses in the same runs, 732 occurred on an actor-day on which the agent found other tasks. Only 175 came from an actor-day on which it listed no task at all. The typical miss is therefore one request overlooked or misread among several found ones, not a failure to engage with the workspace.


##### Execution: half of discovered tasks are completed outright.

Across all runs, the agents executed 4,943 discovered tasks (Figure 4 b). $49.8\%$ received full credit. Of the rest, $17.9\%$ stopped to ask or reported themselves blocked, $15.0\%$ acted on the right target with wrong or incomplete content, $10.2\%$ acted on the wrong record, audience or channel, $5.3\%$ claimed the work was done without a gradable change, and $1.9\%$ misjudged answerability or used the wrong format. Failures are rarely total: 1,351 of the 2,483 failed executions still earned partial credit.


##### Execution: each model has a characteristic failure.

- • Stopping to ask separates the gpt-6 models from everything else. It ends $55.1\%$ of gpt-6-luna ’s executions and $41.0\%$ of gpt-6.1-sol ’s, against $7.3\%$ for claude-opus-5.5 and $11.3\%$ for gpt-5.6-sol . This is why gpt-6.1-sol has the best discovery recall in the table (0.989) yet ranks seventh end to end: it finds nearly every task and then declines to finish many of them.

- • Claiming completion without acting is the signature of gpt-5.4-mini , at $27.5\%$ of its executions; the next highest is DeepSeek-V4-Flash at $14.8\%$ .

- • Wrong target is most frequent for claude-haiku-4.5 , at $20.3\%$ .


##### Execution: the failure mode depends on the domain.

In business systems, stopping to ask takes $28.9\%$ of executions: two same-day requests often concern the same account, and models read them as conflicting rules and ask which to apply, although each is a separate task with its own expected change. In collaboration, wrong-target errors take $20.7\%$ : an audience left incomplete, a reply on the wrong channel, or an existing document updated where a new one was asked for. In research, wrong or incomplete content is the main failure ( $27.8\%$ ), for example a promotion decision that names the wrong run. Headcount review is the most reliably completed domain ( $69.6\%$ full credit). The failures in Table 3 are not unanswerable tasks. On the three risk-ledger tasks, gpt-6.1-sol stopped to ask in every run (0.2 each), while claude-opus-5.5 scored 1.0 in all nine attempts; it also sent the failed-jobs report by email and scored 1.0. Appendix C follows three failures step by step.


### 6. Conclusion

We presented AutoBuddy-Bench , a benchmark for proactive, always-on agents that must find a user’s outstanding tasks in their workspace and complete them. It models an organisation as a temporal graph, renders it into grounded workspace artefacts, derives each task and its outcome from the world state, and grades execution by the changes the agent makes. With 17 backbones, AutoBuddy scores $0.23$ to $0.70$ end to end. Weaker models fail mainly at discovery, intermediate models find tasks but often stop short of carrying them out, and the strongest fail mainly during execution, most often on business-system operations. Discovery precision is only weakly related to execution quality, so the two must be measured separately.
