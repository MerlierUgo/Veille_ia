## SLA: Scaling Spatial-Language-Action Model via Continual Pre-training

Reliable robotic manipulation requires both semantic understanding and precise control in three-dimensional space. However, vision-language-action (VLA) foundations largely inherit visual representations from 2D vision-language pre-training, while spatial supervision is often introduced only during task-specific adaptation. Developing spatial understanding as a transferable foundation-model capability therefore calls for learning from broader robot experience. We present SLA, a Spatial-Language-Action Model that scales implicit spatial alignment to continual pre-training and maintains it during downstream post-training. Initialized from a pretrained $\pi_{0.5}$ policy, SLA jointly optimizes action prediction and alignment between intermediate visual embeddings and spatial representations from a frozen 3D perception model. We curate and unify heterogeneous robot datasets to support spatial learning across diverse embodiments, tasks, and scenes. To accommodate this diversity, we introduce feature-dependent gates that adaptively aggregate visual embeddings from multiple VLA layers for spatial supervision. The teacher further exploits partially available depth and camera calibration alongside RGB observations, enabling effective use of heterogeneous geometric annotations. Probabilistic state dropout encourages reliance on visual cues and mitigates overfitting to demonstration trajectories. All auxiliary components are used only during training, allowing direct reuse of pretrained VLA weights and preserving the original inference architecture. Extensive evaluations demonstrate that SLA achieves state-of-the-art performance on RoboDojo-Real, RoboTwin 2.0 under the Clean2Random setting, RoboCasa-Panda, and LIBERO-Plus, highlighting its strong generalization and downstream data efficiency. Across eight real-world deployment conditions, SLA further improves the average success rate over $\pi_{0.5}$ by 24.0 percentage points.

### 1 Introduction

The development of robotic manipulation hinges on integrating semantic reasoning with accurate physical control in the 3D real world. Recently, vision-language-action (VLA) models ( Black et al., 2025 ; Intelligence, 2025 ; Yuan et al., 2026a ; Team et al., 2026b ; Liu et al., 2026b ; Bu et al., 2025 ; Song et al., 2026 ) , capable of instruction following and robotic action execution, have attracted significant attention. By combining the pretrained vision-language model (VLM) with action experts learning on diverse robot demonstrations, these models offer a promising foundation for general-purpose manipulation. However, successful manipulation requires not only recognizing objects and understanding instructions but also executing precise actions in the 3D physical world. Typical 2D VLA foundation models inherit knowledge from VLMs pretrained on 2D image–text pairs and learn robot control without explicitly incorporating 3D physical knowledge during pre-training. This limits their reliability in scenes that demand fine-grained spatial reasoning.

A direct approach to improving spatial understanding is to introduce 3D modalities during post-training on top of a pretrained 2D VLA foundation model. Several approaches ( Li et al., 2026a ; Sun et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2026c ) use depth maps or point clouds obtained from depth cameras or other 3D sensors, encode their geometric information, and feed it into the VLM backbone or action head. However, some limitations make it difficult to develop a universal and scalable 3D strategy for this paradigm: (1) Reliable 3D sensor data is hard to acquire, as measured depth maps and point clouds can be noisy or incomplete. (2) Robotic sensors vary in types, positions, and calibration status, which introduces substantial heterogeneity into the data. (3) Portions of existing large-scale robot datasets ( O’Neill et al., 2024 ; Wu et al., 2025b ; Lee et al., 2026 ; Walke et al., 2023 ; Brohan et al., 2022 ) do not include depth information, which limits the potential of scaling.

To address these questions, Spatial Forcing ( Li et al., 2025a ) has propose a implicit 3D representation alignment strategy to enbow 2D VLA with spatial intelligence without explicit demand of depth information. However, Spatial Forcing still concentrate on post-training, leaving spatial competence as a task-specific add-on and limiting the development of scalable 3D VLA foundation models with transferable spatial understanding.

Recently, continual pre-training has been widely adopted as a means of developing specific capabilities at scale while preserving the knowledge acquired during foundation model pre-training ( Yu et al., 2026 ; Guo et al., 2026b ) . Motivated by this approach, we propose to build on the strong 2D foundation model and develop spatial understanding through continual pre-training, laying the foundation for subsequent adaptation. We therefore propose SLA , a robotic foundation model with generalized spatial understanding capabilities developed through implicit representation alignment during both continual pre-training and post-training. Intuitively, action generation is conditioned on visual and linguistic representations, making the spatial content of visual tokens directly relevant to action. Higher-quality visual tokens that contain richer spatial information can facilitate generating more precise manipulation actions. Inspired by this, we align the intermediate visual embeddings of the VLA with external spatial representations extracted from a frozen 3D perception foundation model. This spatial alignment objective complements the standard action-learning objective, encouraging the policy to internalize geometric information in the representations used for action prediction. Crucially, this supervision requires no changes to the pretrained VLA architecture or the action-generation head. We can therefore initialize the policy directly from the pretrained $\pi_{0.5}$ foundation ( Intelligence, 2025 ) and continue training from its learned robotic knowledges. This architectural compatibility allows spatial supervision to shape the foundation model while retaining access to the general knowledge encoded in its pretrained weights.

Technically, to support continual pre-training across heterogeneous robot data, we combines a unified training corpus. We curate multiple large-scale public datasets covering diverse embodiments, tasks, scenes, and visual appearances, and unify their state and action annotations through consistent end-effector representations and coordinate conventions. This processing provides a common basis for learning how different robots act in space. The resulting visual diversity also makes a single manually chosen alignment layer restrictive: a certain intermediate visual layer of VLA models suited to one domain data may not be equally suitable for another. We therefore develop adaptive layer selection mechanism, using feature-dependent gates to aggregate visual embeddings from multiple VLA layers for spatial supervision. In addition, we adopt OmniVGGT ( Peng et al., 2025 ) as the external 3D perception foundation to exploit partially available camera calibrations and depth annotations alongside RGB images. We further introduce probabilistic state dropout during training to mitigate overfitting to demonstration trajectories and encourage the policy to make better use of visual cues, thereby improving generalization. After continual pre-training, we retain spatial alignment during post-training to adapt the resulting foundation model to specific down-streaming tasks. The teacher, alignment projector, and layer-selection gates are used only during training. At deployment, the policy retains its original inference architecture without requiring the external perception model, alignment projector, or layer-selection gates.

Extensive evaluations across three public simulation benchmarks, one public real-world benchmark, and eight real-world deployment conditions are conducted to demonstrate the effectiveness of our SLA. On LIBERO-Plus ( Fei et al., 2025 ) , it achieves an overall success rate of 87.4% under diverse observation, language, and scene perturbations, outperforming the compared baselines. On RoboTwin 2.0 ( Chen et al., 2025a ) , training on clean demonstrations yields a 72.3% success rate in the Clean2Random setting and a 79.2% average across Clean2Clean and Clean2Random, demonstrating generalization beyond the training conditions. On RoboCasa-Panda ( Nasiriany et al., 2024 ) , SLA achieves 68.4% success with only 50 downstream demonstrations per task, exceeding compared policies trained with 300–3,000 demonstrations per task. On RoboDojo-Real ( Chen et al., 2026b ) , a single policy adapted across three embodiments achieves 33.9% overall success, compared with 12.8% for $\pi_{0.5}$ , supporting its applicability to real robot manipulation. Our eight deployment conditions further examine bimanual coordination, spatial and dynamic generalization, few-shot cross-embodiment adaptation, and instruction following. SLA achieves the highest success rate among the compared methods in every condition, averaging 83.8% and improving over $\pi_{0.5}$ by 24.0 percentage points. Controlled data-scaling experiments further show that applying spatial alignment in both training stages outperforms either single-stage alternative at every tested data proportion. Together, these results support developing spatial understanding during continual pre-training and stengthen it during downstream adaptation.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

- • We introduce SLA, a robotic foundation model that implicitly enforces the integration of visual embeddings in VLAs with external spatial representations.

- • We extend SLA to continual pre-training on a carefully curated and unified corpus of public robot datasets, developing a spatially informed foundation model.

- • We apply SLA training principle during downstream post-training and demonstrate strong benchmark performance, together with bimanual coordination, spatial and dynamic generalization, few-shot cross-embodiment adaptation, and instruction following.


### 2 Related Work


#### 2.1 VLA Foundation Models

VLA foundation models learn generalist manipulation policies from large, diverse datasets and adapt them to downstream tasks. Cross-embodiment datasets ( O’Neill et al., 2024 ; Walke et al., 2023 ; Brohan et al., 2022 ; Wu et al., 2024 ; Fang et al., 2024 ; Allshire et al., 2026 ; Jiang et al., 2026 ) have accelerated this development. OpenVLA ( Kim et al., 2024 ) , $\pi_{0}$ ( Black et al., 2025 ) , RDT-1B ( Liu et al., 2024 ) , and GR00T N1 ( Bjorck et al., 2025 ) learn language-conditioned action prediction from robot demonstrations, while GR-2 ( Cheang et al., 2024 ) combines large-scale video pre-training with policy learning on robot trajectories. To improve task understanding, RT-H ( Belkhale et al., 2024 ) and $\pi_{0.5}$ ( Intelligence, 2025 ) adopt language-conditioned task hierarchies, whereas MolmoAct ( Lee et al., 2026 ) and MolmoAct2 ( Fang et al., 2026 ) incorporate action reasoning into robot control. RT-2 ( Brohan et al., 2023 ) , $\pi_{0.5}$ , and Xiaomi-Robotics-0 ( Cai et al., 2026b ) also co-train on robot and vision-language data to support semantic generalization and instruction following. The scarcity of teleoperated robot data has further motivated alternative sources of supervision. HumanScale ( Ma et al., 2026 ) investigates pre-training on egocentric human videos followed by adaptation with robot data. Separately, the Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI) ( Chi et al., 2024 ) enables portable demonstration collection using handheld grippers without requiring robots at collection sites. RDT2 ( Liu et al., 2026a ) and Xiaomi-Robotics-1 ( Team et al., 2026b ) exploit UMI data to expand training scale and coverage of environments and tasks. These advances support generalist action learning, but data scaling alone does not specify how geometric representations should be supervised during pre-training. Our complementary goal is to develop 3D spatial understanding in an existing VLA foundation model through spatially supervised continual pre-training.


#### 2.2 3D VLA Models

Existing 3D VLA methods introduce geometry through explicit inputs, feature fusion, or representation supervision. PointVLA ( Li et al., 2026a ) and GeoVLA ( Sun et al., 2025 ) integrate point cloud features into action prediction. BridgeVLA ( Li et al., 2026c ) projects 3D observations into multi-view images; and FALCON ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) extracts geometric tokens from spatial foundation models for fusion within the action head. These approaches still require additional geometric input extraction at deployment. Another works use training-time supervision to encode spatial structure in internal policy representations. ROCKET ( Sun et al., 2026 ) explores multilayer spatial alignment, VEGA ( Wang et al., 2026a ) supervises visual encoder outputs, and Mind-VLA ( Ding et al., 2026 ) introduces instruction-aware alignment of target-object representations. Such approaches emphasize adding 3D capabilities during post-training on top of generic 2D VLA pre-training ( Intelligence, 2025 ; Bu et al., 2025 ; Kim et al., 2025 ) , leaving spatial understanding as a supplementary capability rather than establishing a scalable 3D-aware foundation. Recent studies incorporate geometry into pre-training through egocentric spatial embeddings ( Qu et al., 2025 ) , egocentric 3D annotations ( Feng et al., 2026 ) , and object pose estimation ( Lin et al., 2026 ) . However, these 3D-aware pre-training efforts remain relatively limited in scale compared with large-scale 2D VLA pre-training. In contrast, we build on pretrained 2D VLA through continual pre-training to develop generalizable 3D-aware foundation models.


#### 2.3 Continual Pre-training

Continual pre-training extends the domain capabilities of existing foundation models through further large-scale pre-training, providing a basis for downstream adaptation. Early studies ( Lee et al., 2020 ; Alsentzer et al., 2019 ; Araci, 2019 ; Chalkidis et al., 2020 ; Gururangan et al., 2020 ) focus on adapting to domain-specific data distributions. Llemma ( Azerbayev et al., 2024 ) strengthens mathematical reasoning through continual pre-training on mathematical text and code. AgentFounder ( Su et al., 2026b ) uses diverse reasoning and action data to establish a foundation for agent capabilities before post-training. Recent efforts extend this paradigm to robotics: ET-VLA ( Li and Peng, 2025 ) uses synthetic data for continual pre-training to transfer existing VLAs to new embodiments, whereas AXIS ( Zhao et al., 2026 ) investigates how policy performance scales with data. These VLA efforts primarily focus on training data sources. In contrast, we use implicit spatial alignment framework during continual pre-training to develop generalizable 3D spatial understanding while preserving the pretrained VLA backbone architecture.


### 3 Methodology

SLA aims to develop a scalable 3D VLA foundation model through continual pre-training. Starting from a pretrained 2D VLA backbone ( Intelligence, 2025 ) , we use external spatial representation supervision to shape its visual representations while retaining its backbone and action-generation architecture. Section 3.1 motivates this approach, Section 3.2 presents the model and training mechanisms, and Section 3.3 describes the continual-pre-training corpus and its unified processing.


#### 3.1 Motivation

VLA models are built upon pretrained VLMs and generate actions through specialized designs for action experts. A $\theta$ -parameterized VLM model employs multiple causal attention layers in an auto-regressive manner to generate the next tokens, formed as $\bm{x}_{t}\sim p_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{<t}),$ where $\{\bm{x}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}$ denotes a sequence of tokens.

When adapted to VLA models, three modalities of information will be processed: vision, text, and action. The vision modality consists of multi-view images captured by robots, which are transformed into $N$ visual tokens $\{\bm{x}_{t}^{\mathcal{V}}\}_{t=1}^{N}$ through pretrained visual encoders such as DINOv2 ( Oquab et al., 2023 ) or SigLIP ( Zhai et al., 2023 ) . The text modality consists of task instructions, which are converted into $M$ linguistic tokens $\{\bm{x}_{t}^{\mathcal{L}}\}_{t=1}^{M}$ by a text tokenizer. Then, the VLA models generate $K$ action tokens $\{\bm{x}_{t}^{\mathcal{A}}\}_{t=1}^{K}$ conditioned on the preceding visual and linguistic tokens:

[Tableau] $$\bm{x}_{t}^{\mathcal{A}}\sim p_{\theta}\big(\bm{x}_{t}^{\mathcal{A}}\mid\{\bm{x}_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}\}_{i=1}^{N},\{\bm{x}_{j}^{\mathcal{L}}\}_{j=1}^{M},\bm{x}_{<t}^{\mathcal{A}}\big),$$ (1)

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{action}}=\mathcal{L}[\mathcal{G}(\{\bm{x}_{t}^{\mathcal{A}}\}_{t=1}^{K}),A_{gt}],$ (2)

where $\mathcal{L}[\cdot,\cdot]$ denotes the training loss ( e.g. L1, L2, or cross-entropy loss), $\mathcal{G}$ denotes the trainable action expert ( e.g. multi-layer MLP or flow-matching head ( Lipman et al., 2022 ) ). Equation 1 illustrates that the visual tokens as intermediate scene representations play a crucial role in generating action tokens and should be supervised properly.

Since VLA models must capture 3D information and reason about the relative positions between robots and objects to generate precise spatial movements, we hypothesize that the 3D information is implicitly embedded within the visual embeddings of VLAs. Such embeddings would allow action tokens to acquire 3D cues through the auto-regressive mechanism during inference.

To evaluate this hypothesis, we follow linear probing ( He et al., 2020 ; Wu et al., 2025a ) to conduct a lightweight depth probing experiment. Specifically, we freeze all the parameters of a VLA model ( Kim et al., 2025 ) and only train a DPT head ( Ranftl et al., 2021 ) to transform the visual embeddings of VLA to depth maps. This enables us to quantify the richness of spatial information embedded in the VLA representation space. As shown in Figure 2 , the probing results show that visual embeddings learned solely from 2D images do not produce meaningful spatial structures, suggesting a limited capacity of the model to encode 3D structure information without explicit spatial input or guidance.

> Figure 2 : Depth probing of the visual embeddings of VLAs. Embeddings learned solely from 2D images without alignment do not produce meaningful spatial structures. The aligned embeddings inherently contain rich spatial information, leading to better performance in depth probing.

However, bridging this 3D physical gap through additional sensor inputs or depth-estimation modules would change the architecture of the original 2D VLA foundation model and may disrupt the general knowledge already acquired during its pre-training. We therefore seek a training strategy that reuses the existing VLA architecture and pretrained parameters to unlock its generalizable understanding of the 3D physical world through continual pre-training.


#### 3.2 Model


##### 3.2.1 Model Architecture

Our main philosophy is to employ external supervision signals to supervise the visual tokens $\bm{x}^{\mathcal{V}}$ . To provide the signals with rich spatial information, we first input a set of multi-view images $\mathcal{I}$ into a frozen external 3D perception foundation model $f^{\mathrm{3D}}$ , which outputs pixel-level 3D representations $f^{\mathrm{3D}}(\mathcal{I})$ for each image. Available geometric annotations like camera parameters and depth images are also provided to this external 3D foundation model, as described in Section 3.2.2 . Additionally, these spatial representations are augmented with positional embeddings $E$ to ensure that the supervised tokens preserve the critical spatial position order during supervised training. We extract the final-layer representations of $f^{\mathrm{3D}}(\mathcal{I})$ and bilinearly resample the feature grids with positional embeddings to the VLA token resolution, obtaining a spatial target $\mathbf{y}_{i}$ at each visual token location:

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{y}=\mathcal{R}\!\left[f^{\mathrm{3D}}(\mathcal{I})+E\right],$$ (3)

where $\mathcal{R}\left[\cdot\right]$ denotes bilinear resampling to the VLA visual token resolution. For brevity, the available geometric annotations like camera parameters are implicit in $\mathcal{I}$ .

To align the VLA’s per-pixel visual tokens with this external 3D perception foundation model, we first process each VLA visual token $\bm{x}^{\mathcal{V}}$ with layer normalization $\mathrm{LN}$ followed by a two-layer MLP to ensure compatibility in feature dimension. Let ${}^{(\ell)}\bm{x}^{\mathcal{V}}$ denote the VLA visual tokens after attention layer $\ell$ of the VLA’s vision-language backbone. The normalization and MLP parameters are shared across layers. As there are multiple causal attention layers in the VLA model, visual tokens can be obtained after different layers. We adaptively aggregate their projected features into $\mathbf{z}$ :

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathbf{z}$ $\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}\!\left[\left\{{}^{(\ell)}\bm{x}^{\mathcal{V}}\right\}_{\ell=1}^{L}\right]$ (4) $\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}{}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{g}\odot\mathrm{MLP}\!\left[\mathrm{LN}\!\left({}^{(\ell)}\bm{x}^{\mathcal{V}}\right)\right],$

where $L$ is the number of VLA attention layers, ${}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{g}_{i}$ is a token- and channel-wise gate, and $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication. This fusion operator $\mathcal{F}\left[\cdot\right]$ denotes the adaptive layer selection strategy, which is detailed in Section 3.2.3 .

Then we employ a cosine similarity score to maximize the alignment between the visual tokens of the VLA and the external spatial representation signals. The alignment loss is

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{align}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[1-\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i})\right],$$ (5)

where $\mathcal{S}\left[\cdot,\cdot\right]$ denotes cosine similarity and $N$ is the number of visual tokens across views. The target $\mathbf{y}_{i}$ corresponds to the same view and image location as visual token $\mathbf{z}_{i}$ . Gradients update the VLA, MLP projector, and aggregation gates, while the external 3D perception model $f^{\mathrm{3D}}$ remains frozen.

The final training objective combines both the standard training loss for action generation and the 3D foundation model alignment loss with a weighting factor $\alpha$ :

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{action}}+\alpha\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{align}},$$ (6)

where $\alpha$ controls the spatial alignment strength. We instantiate SLA with the pretrained $\pi_{0.5}$ policy ( Intelligence, 2025 ) , whose action expert generates continuous action chunks through flow matching.

During inference, the VLA model trained in the SLA manner operates identically to the original VLA, introducing no additional structures or computational overhead. The frozen 3D foundation model, alignment projector, and adaptive layer gates are used only during training and are discarded at inference. Neither depth maps nor camera calibration are required as additional policy inputs for inference.


##### 3.2.2 External Spatial Supervision

The external 3D perception foundation model $f^{\mathrm{3D}}$ provides the spatial representations as alignment signals. Apart from RGB image observations, some source datasets provide calibrated camera parameters and depth images, while others provide only partial geometric annotations or none at all. Discarding these annotations would leave useful geometric constraints unused, whereas requiring complete annotations would exclude otherwise valuable demonstrations. Therefore, the external model should accommodate the uneven availability of geometric annotations across the continual-pre-training corpus.

We adopt OmniVGGT ( Peng et al., 2025 ) as the external spatial model. Built on VGGT ( Wang et al., 2025 ) , it alternates frame-wise and cross-view attention to integrate local structure with relationships across images. Its GeoAdapter encodes available camera intrinsics, extrinsics, and depth maps through separate camera and depth branches, using placeholder tokens when annotations are missing. Stochastic multimodal fusion exposes the model to different combinations of geometric inputs, including RGB-only observations. These mechanisms combine visual evidence and arbitrary available geometric constraints in a unified latent representation. We therefore argue that its backbone features contain sufficiently rich spatial information to serve as external supervision for the VLA’s visual tokens.


##### 3.2.3 Adaptive Layer Selection

A heterogeneous pre-training corpus makes a fixed alignment layer a restrictive choice. Manually selecting a single layer requires separate training experiments, and a layer selected for one data domain need not offer the same balance of visual detail and task context in another. Differences in scenes, textures, and embodiments motivate a mechanism that can adapt the contribution of each layer to the observed visual content.

Inspired by Gated Attention ( Qiu et al., 2025 ) , we learn a feature-dependent gate for every visual token at every VLA transformer layer. Given the unprojected embedding ${}^{(\ell)}\bm{x}_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}$ , the gate is

[Tableau] $${}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{g}_{i}=\sigma\!\left(W_{g}\,{}^{(\ell)}\bm{x}_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}+\mathbf{b}_{g}\right),$$ (7)

where $W_{g}$ and $\mathbf{b}_{g}$ are the learnable weight matrix and bias vector of the gate, respectively, and $\sigma$ is the element-wise sigmoid. These gate parameters are shared across layers. The fusion operator $\mathcal{F}$ in Equation 4 uses these gates to weight the projected features and sums them across layers to obtain $\mathbf{z}_{i}$ .

Through this mechanism, multi-layer VLA visual tokens can be supervised simultaneously, allowing different visual cues like depth and channel informations contribute to a unified spatial representation. The gates belong exclusively to the auxiliary training branch and introduce no additional policy computation at deployment.


##### 3.2.4 State Dropout

Spatial supervision is useful only when the policy learns to rely on visual evidence for control. Robot states provide precise proprioceptive information, but can become shortcuts: a policy may associate particular states with demonstrated trajectories instead of responding to visual changes in the scene ( Lu et al., 2026 ; Zhao et al., 2025 ) . We apply state dropout to discourage this shortcut during both training stages. Independently for each training sample, we randomly omit the entire state-token segment during tokenization; the instruction and image tokens are retained. Together, our external spatial alignment and state dropout encourage the model to encode scene geometry and to use visual observations when generating actions. This dropout is disabled at inference when the normal state input is available.


#### 3.3 Data

The quality and diversity of training data are fundamental to the generalization ability of VLAs. To build a VLA model that generalizes well across embodiments, tasks, and environments, we carefully curate a large-scale corpus for continual pre-training. Furthermore, we apply a unified data curation and preprocessing pipeline to all sources to ensure consistent robot state and action annotations.

##### 3.3.2 Data Processing

Different robot morphologies induce incompatible joint-state and joint-action spaces, making joint coordinates a difficult basis for shared control representations. We instead express action through end-effector (EEF) poses, which describe the spatial effect of an action independently of the number and arrangement of arm joints. We use absolute EEF states as observations and chunk-wise delta EEF actions as prediction targets.

We first express all poses in a consistent robot-centered EEF coordinate frame. We unified the coordinate origin to the arm base for single-arm robots and the midpoint between the two arm bases for bimanual robots. Besides, we unified its axes point to forward ( $+x$ ), left ( $+y$ ), and upward ( $+z$ ). A rigid coordinate transformation maps each source data’s EEF positions and orientations into this convention. Thus, the numerical axes of the shared action representation have consistent physical meanings across embodiments.

Rotation format is equally important for consistent action targets. Common choices include Euler angles, continuous 6D representations, rotation vectors, and quaternions. Directly subtracting Euler angles as delta actions may convert a small physical rotation across an angular wrap boundary into a large numerical displacement. We observe such wrap-induced outliers in the pre-training action distributions shown in Figure 3 . Our controlled comparisons motivate representing orientation with rotation vectors while computing relative rotations through $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ composition.

Specifically, for one arm, let $(\mathbf{p}_{0},R_{0})$ be its current absolute EEF pose and $(\mathbf{p}_{t},R_{t})$ the absolute target pose of action $t\in\{1,\ldots,H\}$ within a chunk of $H$ actions. We construct

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\Delta\mathbf{p}_{t}$ $\displaystyle=\mathbf{p}_{t}-\mathbf{p}_{0},$ (8) $\displaystyle\Delta R_{t}$ $\displaystyle=R_{t}R_{0}^{\top},$ (9) $\displaystyle\Delta\mathbf{r}_{t}$ $\displaystyle=\operatorname{Log}(\Delta R_{t})^{\vee},$ (10)

where $\operatorname{Log}(\cdot)^{\vee}$ maps a relative rotation matrix to its rotation-vector representation. Every action in the chunk uses the same current pose as its reference, rather than the preceding action.

> Figure 3 : Comparison of rotational action distributions. (a) Euler-angle representations with direct subtraction for relative-rotation targets. (b) Rotation-vector representations with relative rotations computed through $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ composition. In (a), angular wrapping produces outliers near $\pm 2\pi$ , which widen the interval between the 1st and 99th percentiles. This severely compresses the range of typical action values after normalization, hindering training.

A rotation vector has a unified physical meaning through its direction and magnitude, making it natural to normalize its three channels using a shared scale. However, our controlled experiments show that channel-wise normalization yields substantially better performance, and we therefore adopt this approach in our experiments.

Large numbers of similar trajectories can dominate a mixed training corpus without adding comparable behavioral diversity. This is particularly relevant to scripted simulation data, where repeated executions may follow highly regular motion patterns. We reduce such redundancy by subsampling complete episodes within tasks, preserving the original temporal sampling rate of every retained trajectory.

For example, for InternData-A1, we retain 3% of episodes for selected tasks with highly repetitive trajectories, and 10% for the other tasks. For the pre-training subsets of RoboCasa365, we retain 0.5% of the MimicGen episodes and 15% of the composite episodes. These source-dependent and task-dependent retention rates bring episode counts closer to a comparable order of magnitude, reducing the dominance of a small number of scenes or tasks.

We focus on manipulation with two-finger grippers and exclude dexterous-hand data and trajectories involving robot-base motion. We additionally use action statistics to identify and remove data with anomalous arm-motion speeds. Where existing annotations are available ( Ye et al., 2026 ) , we remove idle frames in which both the robot and the scene remain stationary. Together, these filters keep the corpus aligned with the intended control setting and reduce uninformative or inconsistent supervision.


### 4 Experiments


#### 4.1 Experimental Setup

We evaluate SLA through benchmark-specific post-training on three simulation benchmarks, one real-world benchmark, and plenty real-world deployments. These evaluations cover robustness to visual and language perturbations, manipulation under scene variation, and applicability across robot embodiments.

LIBERO-Plus ( Fei et al., 2025 ) tests robustness along seven perturbation dimensions: camera, robot, language, lighting, background, observation noise, and object layout. We post-train on the standard LIBERO demonstrations and evaluate the perturbed environments without additional training on LIBERO-Plus. Evaluation comprises 10,030 rollouts; the overall SR weights each dimension by its number of evaluation episodes.

RoboTwin 2.0 ( Chen et al., 2025a ) contains 50 bimanual manipulation tasks. We jointly post-train on 50 clean demonstrations per task and evaluate each task for 100 trials in both the Clean2Clean and Clean2Random settings. Clean2Random introduces domain randomization to test generalization beyond the clean training conditions. We report the task-averaged SR for each setting and their equal-weight average.

RoboCasa-Panda ( Nasiriany et al., 2024 ) contains 24-task kitchen manipulation benchmark covering pick-and-place, articulated-object interaction, and appliance operation with a Panda robot. Our post-training uses 50 demonstrations per task. Each task is evaluated for 50 trials under each of three evaluation seeds, yielding 3,600 trials in total. Results are averaged over seeds and tasks.

RoboDojo-Real ( Chen et al., 2026b ) covers 18 tasks across ARX X5, Piper, and Piper X, with six tasks per embodiment. We jointly post-train a single model on demonstrations from all three embodiments. We report scores on a 0–100 scale to measure partial task progress and success rates to measure complete task success. Aggregation equally weights tasks within each embodiment and then the three embodiments.

Apart from these benchmark evaluation, we additionally conduct real-world deployment experiments under eight private deployment conditions A–H on a bimanual Piper platform and a single-arm Franka robot. Piper platform uses one main camera and two wrist cameras while Franka uses one main camera and one wrist camera. The type of All cameras is Realsense D435. For each method, we post-train one policy on 100 demonstrations of A and reuse it for A–C evaluation. For D, we jointly post-train on the 100 Piper demonstrations of A and 10 additional Franka demonstrations. For task E–H, one policy is post-trained on 100 demonstrations collected in the E scene configuration and evaluated across E–H. We report object-level successs rates over 50 evaluated object instances per method and condition. For A, B, and D, these instances come from 10 rollouts with five objects per rollout, and each object is scored separately. C and E–H each comprise 50 single-object rollouts. Success requires complete manipulation sequence for the evaluated object.


#### 4.2 Implementation Details

We initialize SLA from the pretrained $\pi_{0.5}$ policy ( Intelligence, 2025 ) to conduct continual pre-training, followed by post-training for specific tasks.

The continual pre-training runs for 36k optimizer steps with an effective global batch size of 4,608, obtained by accumulating gradients over two microsteps with a global batch size of 2,304 each. The learning rate warms up to $5\times 10^{-5}$ over the first 1k steps, follows cosine decay to $1\times 10^{-5}$ at step 30k, and remains constant thereafter. Continual pre-training takes approximately 110 hours on 48 NVIDIA H100 GPUs.

As for post-training, LIBERO-Plus takes 512 batch size and 30k steps, RoboTwin takes 256 batch size and 30k steps, RoboCasa-Panda takes 64 batch size and 60k steps, RoboDojo-Real takes 512 batch size and 40k steps, and all real-world deployment experiments take 64 batch size and 20k steps.

A key question for continual pre-training is whether additional training data translate into better downstream performance. To examine this relationship, we sample demonstrations within each task of the full pre-training mixture to construct subsets containing 5%, 25%, 50%, and 100% of the original data. For each subset, we perform continual pre-training with and without SLA alignment to obtain separate checkpoints. We then independently enable or disable alignment during post-training to examine how spatial supervision in the two stages interacts, yielding the four configurations shown in Table 6 and Figure 4 .

We report RoboTwin 2.0 Clean2Random SR averaged over all 50 tasks. With SLA alignment in both stages, SR increases from 49.2% at 5% data to 72.3% at full data, a gain of 23.1 percentage points. This configuration performs best at every tested proportion. Without alignment in either stage, SR changes from 43.8% to 48.2%, with a small decline between 25% and 50% data. Increasing data alone therefore provides a much smaller benefit in this comparison.

The two single-stage variants further clarify the role of spatial supervision. At full data, SLA alignment only during continual pre-training reaches 63.2%, compared with 57.1% when applied only during post-training. Applying alignment in both stages improves on these variants by 9.1 and 15.2 percentage points, respectively. Overall, the consistently stronger joint configuration supports using spatial supervision both to shape the pretrained representation and to guide its downstream adaptation.

State dropout is intended to discourage shortcuts based on associations between robot states and demonstrated trajectories, encouraging the policy to encode scene geometry and use visual observations for action generation. ### 5 Conclusion

In this work, we presented SLA, a Spatial-Language-Action Model that develops transferable spatial understanding through continual pre-training and downstream post-training. By aligning intermediate visual representations with a frozen 3D perception model, our approach integrates geometric supervision into action learning while directly reusing pretrained VLA weights. A unified robot-data corpus and adaptive layer selection support training across heterogeneous embodiments and scenes, while available depth and calibration annotations enrich the spatial supervision. The teacher and alignment modules are required only during training. Extensive evaluations demonstrate state-of-the-art performance on RoboDojo-Real, RoboTwin 2.0 under the Clean2Random setting, RoboCasa-Panda, and LIBERO-Plus, alongside improved spatial and dynamic generalization, downstream data efficiency, and few-shot cross-embodiment adaptation. Controlled experiments further show that applying spatial alignment during both training stages consistently outperforms either single-stage alternative across the tested data scales. These findings support developing spatial understanding during continual pre-training and reinforcing it during downstream adaptation, offering a practical path toward spatially informed robotic foundation models.

### Appendix B Memory Mechanism

As illustrated in Section 4.3.4 , we utilize a memory mechanism for RoboDojo-Real post-training stage. Several recent VLA models incorporate memory through dedicated modules ( Shi et al., 2026b ; Shi et al., 2026a ; Zeng et al., 2026 ; Sridhar et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2026b ) . Our memory mechanism instead supplies selected past frames of the main camera to the policy as additional image inputs, which are processed by the same vision-language backbone as the current views.


#### B.1 Memory Construction

Let $I_{k}$ denote the $k$ -th main-camera frame of the current episode, where $k=0$ is the first frame, and let $f$ be the recording frequency of the embodiment ( $f=25$ for Piper X and $f=30$ for ARX X5 and Piper). When generating an action chunk at frame $t$ , the memory context consists of nine main-camera frames:

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{M}_{t}=\big[\,I_{0},\;I_{\max(t-8f,\,0)},\;I_{\max(t-7f,\,0)},\;\ldots,\;I_{\max(t-f,\,0)}\,\big],$$ (11)

that is, the episode-initial frame followed by one frame per second over the preceding eight seconds, ordered from the oldest to the most recent. The initial frame records the scene layout before manipulation and remains available throughout the episode, while the recent frames capture short-term progress and motion that a single frame cannot convey. Within the first eight seconds of an episode, the missing recent frames are replaced by the initial frame. Memory frames are taken only from the main camera, while the wrist cameras contribute only their current frames.


#### B.2 Memory Encoding

Each memory frame is resized to the resolution of the current views and encoded by the same SigLIP encoder ( Zhai et al., 2023 ) into 256 visual tokens. Within the prefix, the image tokens are arranged in temporal order, namely the initial frame, the recent frames from the oldest to the most recent, and the three current views, followed by the language tokens. This order is conveyed only by the token positions, without additional temporal embeddings. Like the current-view tokens, the memory tokens interact with the other prefix tokens through full attention and are accessible to the action expert. The memory frames are excluded from the spatial alignment branch: they are not passed to OmniVGGT, and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{align}}$ in Equation 5 is computed only over the current-view tokens.


#### B.3 Training and Inference

During post-training, the data loader retrieves the memory frames of each training sample according to Equation 11 .During deployment, an online buffer records the main-camera frame at the camera frequency, including the control steps at which no new action chunk is requested, and retains the initial frame together with the frames of the most recent eight seconds. Whenever a new action chunk is requested, the buffer assembles $\mathcal{M}_{t}$ following Equation 11 , so that the memory observed at inference matches the one constructed during training. The buffer is cleared at the start of each episode to prevent leakage across episodes.
