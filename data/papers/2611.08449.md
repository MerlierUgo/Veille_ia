## VFlash: Fast, Accurate Visual Planning via Flash Primitive Decoding

Models for physical AI tasks, such as robotics and autonomous driving, need not reason solely in language. They can use visual plans—points, bounding boxes, and trajectories—to represent spatial goals and anticipate motion. However, autoregressive vision-language models generate these plans one coordinate token at a time, making richer predictions increasingly costly. We introduce VFlash, a method for fast, accurate visual planning through Flash Primitive Decoding. VFlash exploits the known structure of visual primitives to predict their coordinates jointly, using a lightweight drafter that generates an entire primitive in one forward pass. The layout adapts to the primitive’s type, dimensionality, and length, supporting compact boxes and long, variable-length trajectories. Crucially, spatial accuracy need not require reproducing an autoregressive model’s exact token sequence. VFlash therefore trains on annotated primitives and directly accepts structurally valid predictions without target-token agreement. Across the evaluated 2D and 3D robot trajectory tasks, VFlash consistently reduces prediction errors relative to autoregressive decoding. Preliminary single-rollout measurements indicate approximately $25\times$ faster generation for 3D point motion forecasting. A world action model conditioned on VFlash plans achieves 70.2% average success across four MolmoSpaces task suites, compared with 62.6% for the baseline.

### 1 Introduction

Vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly used as high-level brains for physical AI systems, interpreting visual observations and task instructions to guide behavior in robotics and autonomous driving ( Gemini Robotics Team, 2025 ; Tian et al., 2025 ; NVIDIA et al., 2025 ) . Their reasoning need not be expressed solely in language. VLMs can formulate intermediate predictions as visual plans: points and bounding boxes identify relevant locations or objects ( Yuan et al., 2025 ; Jiang et al., 2025 ) , while trajectories describe intended motion or forecast how a scene will evolve ( Gu et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2026a ) . Recent work uses spatial traces and visual primitives to guide action generation ( Li et al., 2025a ; Lee et al., 2025 ; Guo et al., 2026 ) . These plans make spatial goals and anticipated motion explicit before action. Each visual primitive has identifiable boundaries and an organized coordinate layout.

Generating such plans efficiently becomes increasingly important as their spatial detail grows. Longer trajectories, additional objects, and joint 2D image and 3D predictions require more coordinate tokens, which autoregressive VLMs decode sequentially ( Chen et al., 2022 ; Bai et al., 2023 ; Zhu et al., 2025 ; Bai et al., 2025 ) . For physical agents that update their plans as new observations arrive, sequential decoding limits spatial detail and update frequency under a fixed latency budget. This motivates decoding methods that can generate complete visual plans with fewer sequential forward passes while preserving their spatial accuracy.

Parallel decoding offers a promising route to reducing this sequential cost, particularly for latency-sensitive physical AI systems operating at batch size one. Existing approaches include speculative decoding with parallel verification ( Leviathan et al., 2023 ; Li et al., 2025b ) and diffusion language models (dLLMs) with parallel denoising ( Nie et al., 2025 ; Arriola et al., 2025 ) . However, dLLMs often exhibit a speed–quality trade-off relative to autoregressive models ( Wu et al., 2025 ; Liu et al., 2025 ) . DFlash combines these approaches using a lightweight block diffusion draft model to predict draft tokens in a single forward pass, followed by target verification that preserves the autoregressive model’s output distribution ( Chen et al., 2026 ) . For visual outputs, LocateAnything exploits spatial structure through parallel box decoding ( Wang et al., 2026 ) .

In this work, we introduce VFlash , a generalist decoding framework for physical AI (Figure 1 ) that accommodates both text (e.g., sub-task plans) and diverse visual primitives, such as bounding boxes, variable-length trajectories, and dense 3D motion predictions. Our study reveals an interesting observation: a lightweight diffusion drafter can even surpass its autoregressive target in spatial accuracy of the visual plans while decoding much faster. The structure of visual primitives helps explain this finding: once a primitive’s type, dimensionality, and length are specified, its boundaries and coordinate layout are known. Aligning draft blocks with these boundaries allows an entire geometric object to be predicted jointly, with tens to hundreds of tokens decoded in a single forward pass. These observations motivate aligning training and decoding with complete primitive boundaries and directly accepting structurally valid drafts without requiring token-level agreement with the target, while retaining verified decoding for text.

At the center of VFlash is Flash Primitive Decoding (FPD), which uses the requested output structure to organize both drafter training and inference. A tool call specifies the size of a variable-length prediction, while the primitive type determines its coordinate layout. Conditioned on features from a frozen target VLM, a lightweight diffusion drafter learns to predict complete primitives from annotated spatial data. At inference, the decoder applies layout-specific constraints ( Willard and Louf, 2023 ; Geng et al., 2023 ) and accepts valid primitives in full. The target VLM supplies context for subsequent predictions, and ordinary text retains speculative decoding with target verification.

We evaluate VFlash on 2D and 3D robot trajectories, bounding boxes, and dense 3D point motion forecasting, and study its use in downstream manipulation and driving. VFlash consistently reduces prediction errors relative to its autoregressive target across the evaluated robot trajectory tasks. In the downstream robot manipulation pipeline shown in Figure 1 , it reduces inference latency by about 80% on both Jetson Thor and DGX Spark. These visual plans also provide useful conditioning for action models: on MolmoSpaces ( Kim et al., 2026b ) , a world action model ( Ye et al., 2026 ; NVIDIA, 2026c ) conditioned on VFlash plans achieves 70.2% average success across four task suites, compared with 62.6% for the baseline.

Beyond acceleration, our work suggests a broader role for parallel decoding in multimodal systems. Lossless speculative decoding preserves the autoregressive target’s output distribution ( Leviathan et al., 2023 ; Chen et al., 2023a ) , whereas structured visual planning instead permits predictions to be judged by their spatial accuracy and usefulness for action, in the spirit of relaxed acceptance criteria ( Bachmann et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2025c ) . VFlash enables a System-1 mode ( Kahneman, 2011 ) of visual planning in VLMs, producing fast, structured spatial predictions through joint coordinate decoding. This perspective interestingly connects to recent System-1 models such as Jev, which emphasize fast, typed decisions ( Almeida, 2026 ; Yu and Yao, 2026 ) . VFlash brings this perspective to complete geometric predictions, exploiting structure within each primitive. The resulting gains in both speed and spatial accuracy suggest that autoregressive decoding need not define the quality ceiling for visual outputs.


### 2 Method

This section presents the generalist decoding framework of VFlash for physical AI, covering both the preliminaries of DFlash and the detailed design of Flash Primitive Decoding (Figure 2 ).


#### 2.1 Preliminaries

In speculative decoding, a draft model $q_{\phi}$ proposes $\gamma$ tokens that the target model $p_{\theta}$ verifies in parallel, giving an average per-token latency of

[Tableau] $$L=\frac{T_{\mathrm{draft}}+T_{\mathrm{verify}}}{\tau},$$ (1)

where $\tau$ is the expected number of tokens accepted per cycle ( Chen et al., 2026 ) . DFlash reduces the numerator with a lightweight block diffusion drafter. Features $H_{\theta}(x)$ from selected target layers are fused and injected as keys and values into each draft layer. Given a start token $a_{0}$ , the drafter predicts $\gamma$ masked tokens in one bidirectional forward pass, yielding $T_{\mathrm{draft}}=t_{\mathrm{par}}\ll\gamma\,t_{\mathrm{step}}$ , where $t_{\mathrm{step}}$ is the sequential drafting cost per token. Longer drafts, however, need not increase $\tau$ : greedy verification accepts tokens only up to the first mismatch with the target:

[Tableau] $$\tau_{\mathrm{ver}}=1+\max\Big\{k\leq\gamma:\;a_{j}=\argmax_{v}\,p_{\theta}\!\left(v\mid x,a_{<j}\right)\ \text{for all}\ j\leq k\Big\}.$$ (2)

$\tau_{\mathrm{ver}}$ often saturates well below $\gamma$ . Thus enlarging a block does not necessarily enlarge its final throughput. For text generation, the goal is to preserve the target distribution ( Leviathan et al., 2023 ) via verification. But token-level agreement need not reflect spatial accuracy. VFlash retains the drafting architecture and replaces Eq. 2 with direct acceptance of structurally valid visual primitives.


#### 2.2 Flash primitive decoding

Given visual observations and a task instruction, the VLM predicts spatial objects such as boxes, robot trajectories, or future point positions. Each prediction is a visual primitive with a schema $s$ specifying its type, coordinate dimensions, and extent. This fixes each coordinate’s role while leaving its value to be inferred from the input. A trajectory’s 2D image coordinates and 3D spatial coordinates can form one primitive and be predicted jointly. For variable-length predictions, a tool call declares the extent, which together with the primitive type determines the layout; for fixed-size predictions such as boxes, the type alone suffices. The structure is thus known before coordinate prediction, and each decoding block covers a complete primitive.

The schema defines a block of length $B(s)$ : an observed start token $a_{0}$ followed by $B(s)-1$ masked tokens. An autoregressive target factorizes the blocks as $\prod_{j}p_{\theta}(a_{j}\mid x,a_{<j})$ and needs $B(s)-1$ sequential passes. Given the layout and the target context, VFlash instead predicts all positions conditionally independently,

[Tableau] $$q_{\phi}\!\left(a_{1:B(s)-1}\mid H_{\theta}(x),a_{0}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{B(s)-1}q_{\phi,j}\!\left(a_{j}\mid H_{\theta}(x),a_{0},\mathrm{MASK}^{B(s)-1}\right),$$ (3)

in a single forward pass. The complete primitive is thus the unit of parallel prediction, with a speculation budget $\gamma=B(s)-1$ set by the request rather than by a fixed window. The same principle covers targets that emit coordinates as ordinary text: we align their numeric fields in a fixed-width layout and render the predictions back into the target’s text format. Appendix A gives the coordinate conventions and serialization formats of each task.

By adapting the prediction block to each primitive’s structure, VFlash supports different spatial formats and lengths without requiring token-level agreement with the VLM.


#### 2.3 Token representations

Visual plans often require large, variable-length draft blocks. For example, NAVSIM requires eight waypoints over four seconds, while Waymo requires twenty over five seconds. Drafting such blocks introduces challenges in format following and spatial accuracy. We find that two design principles help address these challenges:

Boundary alignment decides what is predicted together: each draft block covers one complete primitive, avoiding the splits caused by fixed-length windows. The tool call declares the block length, from which the waypoint count $P$ follows, and a shared layout template accommodates different lengths, allowing the whole primitive to be drafted in one pass.

Position alignment decides what each position predicts: every slot has a known role—a waypoint, axis, or delimiter—before decoding. This supports parallel prediction with a consistent layout and per-position constraints at inference.

Maintaining this layout requires each coordinate to occupy a fixed number of positions. Recent VLMs normalize image coordinates to 0–1,000 and write them as ordinary text ( Bai et al., 2023 ; Zhu et al., 2025 ) ; models built around visual primitives often use special tokens ( Jiang et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2026 ) . A special coordinate token occupies one position, whereas text tokenization can vary with the value and shift subsequent coordinates. VFlash supports both representations.

For our base model (Section 3 ), typed start and end tokens mark primitive boundaries and transitions between text and primitive decoding. Each coordinate uses one of 1,001 special tokens, allowing prediction to be restricted to its legal vocabulary. For text-tokenized targets, VFlash gives each coordinate a sign and five digit positions, with unused high-order positions filled by the mask token. This fixed-width grid preserves position roles and is rendered back into the target’s text format, whose delimiters mark block boundaries. Table 1 and Appendix A give the formats details.


#### 2.4 Training

We adapt an autoregressive VLM to the spatial response format with next-token supervision, then freeze it and train a shallow, non-causal drafter on its features. For an annotated block $a$ , we minimize the weighted cross-entropy of Eq. 3 ,

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prim}}=-\sum_{j=1}^{B(s)-1}w_{j}\log q_{\phi,j}\!\left(a_{j}\mid H_{\theta}(x),a_{0},\mathrm{MASK}^{B(s)-1}\right),$$ (4)

with all logits computed jointly in one forward pass. Primitive positions use uniform $w_{j}$ , unlike the decaying weights that favor early positions under Eq. 2 . Crucially, labels $a_{j}$ come from annotated coordinates or rendered grids rather than the target’s continuation, optimizing the drafter for spatial accuracy instead of token-level agreement.

Each annotated primitive contributes one complete block. Where the response also contains text, sampled short blocks train the ordinary drafting path, so one drafter serves both the verified text path and the direct primitive path. Packed attention keeps examples and independent blocks separate while preserving attention within each primitive. Backbones, data, validity weights, and adaptation recipes are given in Appendix B .


#### 2.5 Inference

Decoding runs 16-position DFlash blocks until a primitive-start token is accepted; the decoder then resolves the declared schema and drafts the complete block of length $B(s)$ in one pass. Per-position constraints restrict coordinates and delimiters to their legal vocabularies, and cross-position rules pair out-of-frame coordinates; both act on logits already computed and add no neural evaluation.

Let $\hat{a}$ be the resulting prediction and $A_{s}$ its acceptance test: the schema validator, plus for boxes the spatial-confidence guard of LocateAnything ( Wang et al., 2026 ) . VFlash replaces Eq. 2 with

[Tableau] $$\tau_{\mathrm{dir}}=\begin{cases}B(s),&A_{s}(\hat{a})=1,\\[2.0pt] 1+r,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}$$ (5)

where the first $r$ draft slots are retained ( $r=0$ for trajectories; for boxes, those before the first uncertain coordinate) and the rest are regenerated by constrained target AR. An accepted primitive is committed without token-level agreement; one target pass updates the cache and context features and emits the next token. Text still follows Eq. 2 ; Appendix C gives both procedures.

Substituting Eq. 5 into Eq. 1 , a valid primitive costs

[Tableau] $$L_{\mathrm{dir}}=\frac{t_{\mathrm{par}}+t_{\mathrm{target}}}{B(s)},$$ (6)

against one target forward $t_{\mathrm{target}}$ per coordinate under autoregressive decoding, up to prefix and continuation overhead. Both passes grow mildly with $B(s)$ , but their number does not, so a more detailed plan adds no sequential decoding steps.


### 3 Experiment

Base VLM. Most experiments use a customized embodied VLM combining Qwen3-4B ( Yang et al., 2025 ) , a native-resolution MoonViT encoder ( Kimi Team, 2025 ) , and an MLP connector. It is trained progressively on general vision-language tasks, grounding ( Wang et al., 2026 ) , embodied spatial understanding, and joint 2D/3D trajectory prediction. Coordinates for bounding box and trajectory use special tokens, with 2D image coordinates normalized to 0–1,000 and 3D coordinates uniformly quantized within predefined ranges. For dense motion prediction, we use the released MolmoMotion-AR model ( Zhang et al., 2026a ) , retaining its text-based coordinate representation.

Per-task training. For clean ablation studies, we fine-tune the AR target on task-specific data, then freeze it and train a five-layer VFlash drafter on the same data. For MolmoMotion, we train only the drafter on the frozen released target. Detailed per-task datasets, training mixtures, and optimization settings are provided in Appendix B .

Visual-plan conditioning. Despite differences in DiT/WAM architectures and their interfaces with the VLM, a commonality is that the action models cross-attend to VLM features encoding the generated visual plans. These features are obtained by processing VFlash’s accepted primitives with the frozen target VLM. Appendix D details the architectures and conditioning mechanisms.

#### 3.1 Visual primitive prediction

2D trajectory prediction. On ShareRobot-Bench ( Ji et al., 2025 ) and VABench-V ( Yuan et al., 2026a ) , VFlash lowers all six errors of its AR target (Table 3 ), by 7% on HD and 3–6% on DFD and RMSE across both benchmarks. On VABench-V, VFlash cuts every error by roughly 40% relative to the best prior model, LoHo-Manip ( Liu et al., 2026b ) , with our AR target already outperforming the listed baselines; on ShareRobot-Bench, VFlash is second only to RoboBrain-2.0 ( BAAI RoboBrain Team, 2025 ) .

2D bounding box prediction. On COCO ( Lin et al., 2014 ) and Dense200 ( Jiang et al., 2025 ) , VFlash with AR fallback matches AR on mean F1 while decoding 2.6–2.8 $\times$ faster (Table 3 ), with higher F1 at IoU 0.50, especially on the crowded Dense200 scenes, but lower F1 at IoU 0.95. Without fallback, mean F1 drops by about 20 points on Dense200.

3D trajectory prediction. On InternA1 ( Tian et al., 2026 ) , AgiBot ( Bu et al., 2025 ) , and MolmoBot ( Deshpande et al., 2026 ) , VFlash improves all four metrics for all three target variants (Table 4 ). ADE falls by 18–49% with the 3D-only target and by 28–61% with the 2D+3D target; waypoint accuracy at 5 cm on AgiBot rises from 50% to 73% and from 40% to 74%, respectively. With joint 2D+3D outputs, AR’s 3D ADE is 15–51% higher than with 3D-only outputs, while VFlash maintains similar accuracy by predicting both segments in one parallel pass. The wrist-camera training variant further reduces VFlash’s ADE by 3–12%, with evaluation still using only the primary camera.

Dense 3D motion prediction. On PointMotionBench ( Zhang et al., 2026a ) , we freeze the backbone of MolmoMotion-AR and draft each future frame of query points as one block with a fixed-width digit layout (Appendix A ). As shown in Table 5 , VFlash reduces DAVIS ADE and FDE by 27% and 19%, respectively, and HOT3D FDE by 8%. Its ADE ranks second on HOT3D and WorldTrack, behind AR but ahead of the flow-matching variant, although WorldTrack FDE increases by 15% and PWT is 7–12% lower across the three datasets. These results extend VFlash to dense motion prediction with text-tokenized coordinates, with gains over AR on several error metrics and approximately $25\times$ faster single-rollout generation (Appendix F.3 ).

Autonomous driving. For NAVSIM ( Dauner et al., 2024 ) and Waymo ( Xu et al., 2026b ) the policy is a ReCogDrive-derived diffusion transformer ( Li et al., 2026 ) that reads the VLM’s final-layer states over the prompt and the generated plan through cross-attention, alongside ego history and vehicle state, and outputs a continuous trajectory. The VLM is frozen and generates a 2D+3D trajectory on each dataset’s native grid; separate heads are trained on Waymo, NAVSIM, and Alpamayo-derived data. RL then fine-tunes the head alone with PDMS and RFS as rewards, so the plans are identical in the SFT and RL rows.

As shown in Table 8 , VFlash achieves competitive driving performance with a frozen planning VLM and a small action head. With supervised training, it reaches 87.1 PDMS on NAVSIM, above ReCogDrive (86.8), and 7.79 RFS on Waymo, comparable to Qwen-Drive-1.0-SFT (7.78). These gains require no updates to the VLM or its generated plans. Together, the results show that VFlash supports competitive plan-conditioned driving while substantially reducing decoding latency (Section 3.3 ).

#### 3.3 Throughput analysis

Figure 4 splits per-step latency into visual encoding, prefill, decoding, and the action model at batch size one on Jetson Thor, DGX Spark, and H100. Thor is an embedded platform for robots and vehicles and Spark a desktop-class system, typical on-device hardware for the System-1 visual planning of Section 1 ; H100 is the server reference. Appendix E gives the timing boundary and the full table. Across devices, VFlash cuts per-step latency 5.1–5.2 $\times$ on MolmoSpaces on Thor and Spark and 3.6 $\times$ on H100, and 3.4–3.6 $\times$ and 2.6 $\times$ on NAVSIM; on Waymo (Figure 5 ) the H100 reduction is 4.5 $\times$ . The gain comes almost entirely from decoding: under AR it takes 75–87% of each step on Thor, and VFlash cuts it 12–42 $\times$ while the other stages are unchanged.

> Figure 4: VFlash latency across different downstream tasks on Jetson Thor, DGX Spark and H100.

AR decoding runs one 4B VLM pass per token (35–39 ms on Thor, 33–40 ms on Spark, 4.7–6.0 ms on H100), so decoding time grows roughly linearly with token count. Drafting predicts the remaining tokens of a 36–84-token primitive in one pass, taking 13–23 ms on Thor and 1–3 ms on H100. Verification still needs a target pass per block, so its benefit depends on the tokens committed per iteration. On MolmoSpaces, DFlash commits 5.5 tokens per iteration on subtask text but only 2.2 within trajectory blocks: a coordinate one bin off the target’s choice fails Eq. 2 even with small geometric error. DFlash thus only about halves decoding time. VFlash skips token-level verification for visual primitives, using one drafter pass and one target pass per primitive; the target pass updates the cache and extracts hidden states for the action head, amortized over the block. On Thor, decoding takes 72–78 ms as the driving primitive grows from 36 to 84 tokens, so denser plans add little latency over this range.


### 4 Related Work

Embodied VLMs and visual plans. Embodied VLMs are increasingly used as high-level modules that turn visual observations and language instructions into plans, affordances, and trajectories ( Ji et al., 2025 ; BAAI RoboBrain Team, 2025 ; Gemini Robotics Team, 2025 ; Yuan et al., 2026b ; Dang et al., 2026 ) . Spatial prediction is central to these models: points and boxes localize objects or affordances ( Yuan et al., 2025 ; Zhou et al., 2025a ; Deitke et al., 2025 ; Jiang et al., 2025 ) , while traces describe intended or predicted motion ( Zhou et al., 2026a ; Wen et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2026a ) . These visual plans can condition manipulation policies ( Gu et al., 2024 ; Li et al., 2025a ; Liu et al., 2026b ; Tan et al., 2026 ; Huang et al., 2025 ) or be fed back as prompts ( Zheng et al., 2025 ) . Other work reasons spatially before acting ( Lee et al., 2025 ; Fang et al., 2026 ; Guo et al., 2026 ) , predicts future states ( Kim et al., 2026a ; Ye et al., 2026 ) , or plans driving trajectories from scene understanding ( Tian et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2025b ; NVIDIA et al., 2025 ) . While this literature asks what embodied models should predict, we ask how fast such spatial predictions can be produced. Token-by-token coordinate generation makes every extra waypoint, object, or dimension an additional decoding step.

Parallel and speculative decoding. Speculative decoding accelerates LLM inference by verifying multiple drafted tokens in parallel ( Stern et al., 2018 ; Leviathan et al., 2023 ; Chen et al., 2023a ) . Medusa, EAGLE, TIDAR, and DFlash explore different ways to produce these drafts ( Cai et al., 2024 ; Li et al., 2024a ; Liu et al., 2026c ; Chen et al., 2026 ) . For visual outputs, LocateAnything decodes points and boxes as units, but is limited to fixed-size 2D primitives ( Wang et al., 2026 ) . Closest to this work, Spec-VLA accelerates OpenVLA by accepting draft action tokens whose bins lie near the target’s ( Wang et al., 2025c ) . However, it still verifies each action token against the target and commits only $2.10$ – $2.94$ tokens per pass. Based on our finding that, for visual trajectory prediction, one-pass drafts are more accurate than the autoregressive target, VFlash instead drafts the entire visual primitive in one pass rather than by agreement with the target.

Embodied and System-1 models. In a deployed perception-action loop, VLM latency directly limits how quickly the agent can react. Prior efficiency works mainly accelerate the action execution pipeline, for example via action compression, parallel action chunks, or flow matching ( Pertsch et al., 2025 ; Kim et al., 2025 ; Song et al., 2025 ; Black et al., 2024 ) . Prior VLM-based planning, however, still often rely on highly autoregressive token decoding. System-1 models such as Jev make fast typed decisions that downstream applications can use directly ( Almeida, 2026 ) , and Visual Jev answers many independent visual questions in parallel after one image encoding ( Yu and Yao, 2026 ) . Our work can be seen as a relevant effort that enables System-1 VLM spatial prediction. Within this context, our work studies optimized token representation and decoding schemes systematically, supporting both dedicated special tokens ( Chen et al., 2022 ) and plain numbers ( Chen et al., 2023b ; Bai et al., 2023 ; Zhu et al., 2025 ) .

In summary, VFlash uses DFlash-style drafting without target-token agreement, extending fast and accurate visual plan generation from LocateAnything’s parallel box decoding to richer and more general visual primitives that are useful for downstream embodied applications. It also enables more efficient decoding parallelism via systematic studies, in contrast to relevant or concurrent works.


### 5 Conclusion

We presented VFlash, which uses the known structure of visual primitives to replace sequential coordinate generation with parallel prediction. Our results show that useful spatial predictions need not reproduce an autoregressive model’s output: direct drafts reduce robot trajectory errors, while single-rollout timing estimates indicate approximately $25\times$ faster generation for 3D object point forecasting. Plan-conditioned manipulation further demonstrates how these predictions support downstream action generation. Future work will examine whether generating multiple complementary primitives from shared observations improves control within a fixed latency budget.

Decoding enforces the same rules by restricting each position to its legal tokens (Appendix C ).

Dense motion on a text-tokenized target. The MolmoMotion target keeps its own tokenizer and text format: coordinates are signed millimeter integers relative to the first query point at the current frame, and future frames are separated by a fixed separator. One block covers one future frame of $P$ query points and has $19P+3$ positions: the frame separator serves as the start token, two positions hold the timestamp, and each point gets one position for its index followed by three coordinates of a sign and five digits. Unused high-order digits, and every position of a point the target would omit, are filled with the target’s mask token, so an omitted point is signaled by a masked index rather than by a shorter frame. The completed block is rendered back into the target’s text by inserting its fixed punctuation; the mask token never appears in the rendered answer. The target’s vocabulary is unchanged. Following MolmoMotion ( Zhang et al., 2026a ) , we use $P=8$ and five-digit coordinates in all experiments (Appendix B ).


### Appendix B Training Details

Base VLM. The visual encoder is MoonViT-SO-400M ( Kimi Team, 2025 ) with 27 layers of width 1,152, 14-pixel patches, and $2\times 2$ spatial merging; a two-layer MLP connects it to Qwen3-4B ( Yang et al., 2025 ) , whose decoder has 36 layers of width 2,560. It is trained on image and video question answering, grounding, pointing, and spatial reasoning data. Every task-specific AR target in Section 3 is fully fine-tuned from this checkpoint. The dense-motion experiment instead uses the released MolmoMotion-4B-H3-F30 ( Zhang et al., 2026a ) target without fine-tuning.

Drafter architecture. Following DFlash ( Chen et al., 2026 ) , the drafter has five full-width Qwen3 transformer layers with 32 query heads, eight key/value heads, 128-dimensional heads, and a 9,728-dimensional feed-forward layer. Target layers 1, 9, 17, 25, and 33 (zero-based) provide context features; their concatenated 12,800-dimensional states are projected to 2,560 dimensions and RMS-normalized. The drafter shares the frozen target’s input embedding and output projection and has 537M trainable parameters. Positions inside a block attend to each other and to the target context before the block; target features are computed online with gradients disabled.

Loss weights. We minimize Eq. 4 over the annotated sequence, with the target encoding the clean sequence and the drafter reconstructing the masked positions. Primitive positions have unit weight. Ordinary-text positions decay as $w_{j}=\exp(-(j-1)/7)$ with distance from the anchor, following DFlash. Out-of-frame labels are downweighted by 0.1 in both the target’s fine-tuning loss and the drafter loss. Anchors and padding are excluded. No soft-logit distillation or hidden-state regression is used.

Block sampling and packing. Each annotated primitive contributes one complete block. The 2D, 2D+3D, and wrist-camera drafters additionally sample up to 512 ordinary-text anchors per example, shared across assistant turns, so that one drafter serves both the text and the primitive path; text blocks stop at primitive boundaries. The 3D-only and box drafters train on primitive blocks only. Packed examples keep independent position indices and attention masks: each block sees only its own positions and the target positions of its own example before its anchor, so schemas and lengths mix freely in a batch without leaking labels across blocks or examples.

### Appendix C Decoding Details

Algorithm 1 summarizes one generation. Text follows the DFlash procedure and primitives follow VFlash; the two share the drafter, the target, and the KV cache.

> Algorithm 1 Flash primitive decoding for one response

Text and routing. After multimodal prefill, ordinary text is generated in DFlash blocks of 16 positions: one accepted anchor and up to 15 proposals. The target verifies the block in one pass and keeps the longest matching prefix plus its own next token. The tool call is generated on this path. When an accepted prefix reaches a primitive-start token, any speculative tokens after it are discarded and the declared schema and block length take over. A block whose length exceeds the remaining budget is rejected before drafting.

Primitive prediction and acceptance. The drafter processes the start token and all masked positions in one pass. Delimiter positions are fixed, coordinate positions are restricted to number tokens, and coupled rules such as the pairing of out-of-frame markers and the ordering of box corners are resolved by a small selection loop over the logits already computed, with no further network evaluation. The target then processes the completed block once to update its cache, supply context features for the next block, and predict the continuation. A schema-valid block is accepted in full without token-level agreement. If validation fails, the target cache is rolled back to the anchor and the block is regenerated by constrained target AR from the first invalid position ( $r=0$ for trajectories); the drafter cache is reset afterwards. The DFlash baseline in Section 3.3 instead applies 16-position blocks and longest-prefix acceptance throughout the response, including inside primitives.

Dense motion continuation. For the MolmoMotion target the drafter fills the $19P+2$ positions of one frame. The rendered frame receives one target pass, which supplies the next frame’s features and chooses between the frame separator and the closing delimiter; the target writes the answer’s opening and closing text autoregressively. Missing or non-increasing timestamps are set to the previous timestamp plus one, and a frame whose points are all masked keeps its highest-margin point. Malformed frames fall back to target AR. Generation continues for at most 30 frames, so parallelism is across the points and coordinates of one frame while frames remain sequential.


### Appendix D Downstream Action Models


#### D.1 MolmoSpaces

Plan conditioning. The manipulation policy is the world action model (WAM) of MolmoSpaces ( Kim et al., 2026b ) , obtained by post-training Cosmos-Predict2.5 ( NVIDIA, 2026c ) for robot control. Unless otherwise specified, MolmoSpaces uses the primary-view 2D+3D robot target–drafter pair described in Appendix B . We replace its text-encoder context with states from the frozen 4B planning VLM. For every retained position, each of the VLM’s 36 layer outputs is standardized along its 2,560 channels and the results are concatenated; a newly initialized linear projection followed by GELU maps this vector to the WAM’s context width. The resulting 512 context positions enter the WAM through its existing text cross-attention path. The trajectory reaches the WAM only through these states; there is no separate numeric trajectory encoder. The VLM stays frozen throughout.

Post-training. All five configurations start from the same pretrained WAM checkpoint and are evaluated after 100,000 post-training updates. Post-training uses AdamW with learning rate $10^{-4}$ , weight decay 0.01, gradient-norm clipping at 1.0, BF16, and global batch size 512 (32 GPUs, four examples per device, four accumulation steps); the schedule warms up for 15,000 steps and stays at its peak through the evaluated checkpoint, for 51.2 million samples per model. Training uses seed 24, and every configuration is evaluated at the same update count rather than at its own best checkpoint. The post-training data are 172.5 million frames of simulated Franka demonstrations in six task splits covering picking, placing, and opening

Execution and scoring. The WAM executes 20-action chunks. A new plan is generated synchronously every three chunks, or 60 control steps; the simulator pauses during inference. Evaluation uses seed 42 and all 3,915 benchmark episodes: 1,000 each for Pick, Pick & Place, and Open, and all 915 Close episodes. Horizons are 455 control steps for Pick, Open, and Close and 682 for Pick & Place. An episode succeeds if the goal is reached at any step; episodes run to the horizon. Suite success is aggregated before rounding, and the mean is the unweighted average of the four suites. Each row is one training seed and one evaluation seed.


#### D.2 LIBERO

Action head. The LIBERO policies use the PIv3 flow-matching action head of StarVLA ( StarVLA Community, 2026 ) . The LIBERO experiments use the wrist-camera-trained 2D+3D robot target–drafter pair described in Appendix B . A LayerNorm and linear projection map each of the frozen VLM’s 36 hidden-state layers from 2,560 to 1,024 dimensions, and action-transformer layer $i$ cross-attends to projected VLM layer $i$ . The 36-layer head and projections contain about 634 million trainable parameters. Both primary and wrist images are rotated by $180^{\circ}$ , resized to $224\times 224$ , and included in the VLM sequence; no proprioceptive input is used. The head predicts eight seven-dimensional actions (end-effector increments and a gripper command) with four flow-matching steps. VFlash generates a fresh subtask and plan before every action chunk. The two context variants retain the prompt through the generated subtask, or the prompt through the full generated answer. The baseline is the same head on a frozen Qwen3-VL-4B that encodes only the images and the instruction.

Training and evaluation. All policies train on 273,463 demonstration steps from the same 1,693 episodes. #### D.3 Autonomous Driving


##### D.3.1 Action Model Training

Conditioning on the target VLM.

The action head conditions on selected 2,560-dimensional final-layer states of the frozen target VLM. States at generated trajectory positions are obtained by processing the accepted primitive with the target VLM. We define conditioning sequence as prompt and plan. Variable-length BF16 states are projected to 384 dimensions, with padding masked in pooling and cross-attention.

Ego motion inputs. The head uses 16 history entries, each containing position and heading $(x,y,\psi)$ , three availability indicators, and a relative timestamp. The resulting 112-dimensional vector is standardized using training-set statistics, with unavailable values zeroed and shorter histories left-padded. The 16-dimensional current-state vector contains four command indicators, four motion measurements, and eight availability indicators. Separate MLPs with 1,024 hidden units and LayerNorm map the history and current state to 384-dimensional embeddings.

Trajectory head. The ReCogDrive-derived head alternates eight self-attention and eight cross-attention blocks, with eight heads of width 48, adaptive normalization, and SwiGLU feed-forward layers. Each noisy waypoint is embedded with a learned index and a sinusoidal encoding of its physical future time; the index table supports up to 64 waypoints. Concatenating the waypoint embedding with the broadcast history embedding and masked mean VLM context yields 1,152 channels, which are projected to 384. The current-state and diffusion-timestep embeddings condition the transformer blocks. A 512-dimensional output projection followed by an MLP with 1,024 hidden units predicts three noise components per waypoint, for $(x,y,\psi)$ on NAVSIM and $(x,y)$ on Waymo, where the heading channel is masked. NAVSIM and Waymo use the same architecture with separately trained weights.

Diffusion objective and sampling. For normalized recorded future waypoints $\widetilde{Y}$ , the head $\epsilon_{\theta}$ minimizes masked noise-prediction error:

[Tableau] $$Y_{s}=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{s}}\,\widetilde{Y}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{s}}\,\epsilon,\qquad\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{drive}}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{\sum_{h,c}m_{hc}\bigl(\epsilon_{\theta,hc}(Y_{s},s;Z,u,\tau)-\epsilon_{hc}\bigr)^{2}}{\sum_{h,c}m_{hc}}\right].$$ (8)

Here $\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ , $s$ is the diffusion timestep, $\bar{\alpha}_{s}$ is the cumulative noise schedule, $Z$ denotes VLM states, $u$ contains ego history and state, and $\tau$ contains future timestamps. The mask $m_{hc}$ is one for valid coordinates and zero otherwise; the heading channel is masked out on Waymo. The schedule has 100 training timesteps. Five deterministic DDIM steps ( Song et al., 2020 ) , at indices 80, 60, 40, 20, and 0, produce the continuous trajectory. During SFT, only the action head and its input projections receive gradients.

Normalization and training configuration. Each channel is mapped affinely to $[-1,1]$ , $z_{\mathrm{norm}}=2(z-z_{\min})/(z_{\max}-z_{\min})-1$ , with bounds set per dataset. The NAVSIM head inherits the fixed ReCogDrive bounds: X in $[-1.57,65.17]$ m, Y in $[-19.68,22.32]$ m, and $\psi$ in $[-1.67,1.86]$ rad; targets are not clipped, while denoised predictions and the final normalized actions are clipped to $[-1,1]$ . The Waymo head uses X in $[-46.98,138.77]$ m and Y in $[-18.14,17.81]$ m, fitted on training trajectories as the smaller of zero and the minimum to the 99th percentile for X and the 0.5th to 99.5th percentiles for Y, with no clipping. Both heads are initialized from scratch and trained for 200 epochs with AdamW at learning rate $10^{-4}$ , weight decay $10^{-4}$ , $(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(0.9,0.95)$ , and gradient-norm clipping at 1.0; three warmup epochs are followed by cosine decay to $10^{-6}$ . Training uses batch size 64 per GPU on eight GPUs with FP16 mixed precision.

Reinforcement learning. After SFT, each head is fine-tuned with DiffGRPO ( Li et al., 2026 ) , which treats the five-step DDIM chain as a policy rollout. For every training scene, the head samples eight trajectories, scores them with a dataset-specific reward, and updates with group-standardized advantages. A behavior-cloning loss with weight 0.1 regularizes the update. It is the negative log-likelihood under the current head of denoising chains sampled from the frozen SFT head. No KL penalty is used. Both heads use an initial learning rate of $10^{-5}$ with cosine decay and no warmup. On NAVSIM the reward is PDMS from the non-reactive simulator ( Dauner et al., 2024 ) on the training split, with ego progress, time-to-collision within bound (TTC), and comfort weighted 10/5/2 during training against the official 5/5/2 used for evaluation. Training runs 10 epochs (6,650 updates) at batch size 16 per GPU on eight GPUs. The checkpoint is selected by PDMS on an internal validation split held out from navtrain by log (18,179 clips, Appendix F.5 ). On Waymo the reward is RFS from the official implementation on the 479 validation scenes. Training runs 20 epochs (300 updates) at batch size 4 per GPU on eight GPUs, and the checkpoint is selected on the same 479 scenes, so the selection score is in-sample. These settings are shared by all layout and context configurations. The VLM, its plans, and the conditioning states stay fixed, so the SFT and RL rows of Table 8 consume identical plans and differ only in head weights. Reported scores use the held-out test splits, which take no part in SFT, RL, or checkpoint selection.


### Appendix E Latency Evaluation


#### E.1 Deployment and Configuration

> Figure 5: Waymo planning latency on H100 (ms, batch size one). AR, DFlash, and VFlash share the VLM, primitive, and action head and differ only in the decoder. Qwen-Drive-1.0 is measured on the same hardware; the Fast-dDrive value is from its paper and its AR baseline is not the AR bar shown here.

Hardware and runtime. All measurements use batch size one and one request at a time on NVIDIA Jetson AGX Thor (SM110), DGX Spark (GB10, SM121), and a single H100 80 GB (SM90). Our models run in a locally modified TensorRT-Edge-LLM ( NVIDIA, 2026b ) C++ runtime: vision encoder, target VLM, and drafter are exported through ONNX and compiled into TensorRT engines, TensorRT selects kernels for the exported operators, and Edge-LLM plugins supply attention (CuTe DSL FMHA for vision and prefill, XQA for decoding and speculative verification), cache updates, and positional encoding. Engines and kernel artifacts are built separately for each architecture. The stacks are CUDA 13.0 and TensorRT 10.15.2.7 on Thor, CUDA 13.0.88 and TensorRT 10.14.1.48 on Spark, and CUDA 12.9.86 and TensorRT 10.11.0.33 on H100. All Thor runs use a modified Edge-LLM 0.9.0 tree; all Spark runs and the H100 MolmoSpaces runs use Edge-LLM 0.10.1; the H100 driving runs use the 0.9.0 tree with an SM90 attention bridge. Vision, target, and draft engines run in FP16 with FP16 KV caches; no weight or KV quantization is used.

Decoders. AR performs target-only single-token decoding. DFlash drafts 16-token blocks and accepts the longest prefix that matches the target’s greedy choice exactly, plus the correction token; numeric coordinates receive no tolerance. VFlash decodes text with the same verified blocks, then drafts the whole primitive in one unverified block (54 tokens for MolmoSpaces, 36 for NAVSIM, 84 for Waymo) and runs one cached target prefill over the accepted block, the EOS token, and end-of-text padding to produce the hidden states the action model consumes. This prefill is counted as decoding. For MolmoSpaces the prefill retains 512 non-image positions from all 36 target layers; for driving it exports the final layer only, padded to 2,200 positions on NAVSIM. In both pipelines the fixed tool-call JSON is supplied rather than generated; on MolmoSpaces it is committed inside the text verification pass, and on driving the think-and-tool prefix precedes generation, so decoding covers exactly the primitive. All decoders use the same supplied tool-call JSON and the same primitive length. AR and DFlash reuse the target states computed during generation and need no separate feature pass.

Action models. The MolmoSpaces WAM runs one WAN encoder call and four DiT calls (joint, action, joint, action) inside a CUDA graph, with BF16 DiT weights and FP16 attention through a custom head-dimension-128 CuTe DSL kernel on Thor and Spark; the H100 baseline uses native TensorRT attention. The ReCogDrive head is a BF16 TensorRT engine containing feature encoding and the DDIM sampler. Latency profiles unroll ten DDIM steps to match the ten-step Qwen-Drive planner; the quality results of Table 8 use five steps (Appendix F.5 ).

Qwen-Drive-1.0. We run the released checkpoint in its reasoning planning mode with greedy decoding. On Thor and Spark it uses a customized Edge-LLM 0.9.0 runtime with CuTe DSL Gated DeltaNet kernels for its 24 linear-attention layers and FMHA/XQA for its eight full-attention layers; on H100 it uses the TensorRT-LLM backend with FlashInfer kernels. Its 1.04B flow-matching planner is a separate TensorRT engine with BF16 weights, FP16 attention, and ten Euler steps in a CUDA graph. Inputs are one 1600 $\times$ 896 front image on NAVSIM (1,400 visual tokens; 1600 $\times$ 800 in the Thor runs) and three 800 $\times$ 896 images on Waymo (2,100 tokens). The Fast-dDrive value in Figure 5 is taken from its paper and was not measured here.

Timing protocol. Each request starts from fresh vision computation and model state. CUDA events bracket each stage (preprocessing and vision encoding, prefill, decoding, action model) and are summed over all calls within a request; these intervals include host launch gaps and are not kernel-only durations. Model loading, engine construction, graph capture, warmup, image loading and CPU preprocessing outside the instrumented modules, and output detokenization are excluded. Repeat counts differ by run: the Thor MolmoSpaces pipeline uses 24 records and ten passes after five warmups; the Spark and H100 robotics runs one pass over the same 24 records after five warmups; driving runs five records and ten passes after five warmups; the Qwen-Drive H100 run five scenes twice after five warmups. Reported per-step latency is the sum of stage means. Within each device and task, the vision, prefill, and action-model means are shared across decoders; decoding is profiled separately. The MolmoSpaces AR decoding time comes from an earlier standalone profile with an output-only target engine, whereas the VFlash decoding time includes the all-layer feature completion described above; the driving decoders share one target-engine configuration.

### Appendix F Evaluation Protocols


#### F.1 2D Trajectories

We evaluate 200 ShareRobot-Bench queries and 300 VABench-V queries with the same 4B target and its drafter. Coordinates are on the 0–1,000 image scale. Predictions are linearly interpolated by waypoint index to the reference point count before computing discrete Fréchet distance, symmetric Hausdorff distance, and RMSE, the square root of the mean squared Euclidean waypoint error; Table 3 divides each distance by 1,000. All AR and VFlash outputs parse and enter the means.


#### F.2 3D Robot Trajectories

Inputs and references. Every decoder is evaluated on the same 1,000 InternA1, 999 AgiBot, and 4,221 MolmoBot records. InternA1 uses the current image; AgiBot contains 499 image and 500 video records, MolmoBot 2,757 and 1,464. Video records sample six frames from the preceding three seconds and exclude the current frame. Each record supplies only the primary camera, including for the model trained with wrist-camera views. References contain ten waypoints per gripper, uniformly resampled by 3D arc length over a nominal five-second window that may end early at an episode or action boundary; they describe path geometry rather than fixed-time control samples. Coordinates are in meters in the current primary camera frame and lie on a 1 cm grid inherited from the annotation export.

Metrics. The scorer requires the expected arm identities and exactly ten points per arm, without interpolation or repair; all reported configurations return valid trajectories for every record. ADE averages Euclidean waypoint error and FDE the final-waypoint error over the evaluated arms. SR@ $\tau$ is the fraction of waypoints with error below $\tau$ , averaged within a record and then across records, for $\tau=5$ and 10 cm. An arm is active when the sum of its XYZ standard deviations is at least 5 mm; if any reference arm is active, only active arms are scored. InternA1 evaluation episodes are absent from training; MolmoBot uses five held-out validation configurations covering pick, place, and opening tasks.


#### F.3 Dense Motion

Inputs and selection. Each record gives three observed frames, the caption, and the observed XYZ coordinates of eight query points, and asks for up to 30 future frames. We use PointMotionBench with one fixed point selection shared by all local evaluations: points must be visible with finite coordinates in all three observed frames; at most 24 are sampled per clip with seed zero and split into groups of eight, discarding incomplete groups; HOT3D uses one fifth of its clips. This gives 491/144/88 clips and 1,473/412/257 records for HOT3D/WorldTrack/DAVIS. Frames are consecutive at the source rate, so the horizon is 1.0 s for HOT3D and WorldTrack and 1.25 s for DAVIS; shorter clips keep their available frames. WorldTrack uses the provided world coordinates.

Speed annotation. The $25\times$ in Table 5 compares one VFlash rollout with the target’s AR rollout on the same selections, both on one H100 at batch size one: 3.21/3.86/3.78 s against 79.8/90.6/99.6 s on HOT3D/WorldTrack/DAVIS, or 24.8/23.5/26.4 $\times$ .


#### F.4 Box Generation

Queries and generation. Evaluation uses 4,952 COCO val2017 images and 200 Dense200 images. The prompt asks for every instance of a given category in the primitive format. COCO queries each ground-truth-present category as one turn of a multi-turn conversation over the image; Dense200 has one category per image. AR and VFlash share the image, query, and reference records, decode greedily with neutral repetition penalty, and apply no resizing before the model’s image processor. The output budget is 512 new tokens per COCO turn and 4,096 per Dense200 query; only complete boxes are returned when the budget runs out. Boxes are mapped back to pixels and merged per image.

Direct and AR fallback. Direct accepts every schema-valid box. AR fallback adds the spatial-confidence criterion of LocateAnything ( Wang et al., 2026 ) : for each coordinate, the numeric tokens among the five most probable full-vocabulary predictions are examined, and the box is regenerated by constrained target AR if this set is empty, or if the top numeric token has probability below 0.9 and at least two numeric candidates span more than 60 bins. Boxes before the first uncertain coordinate are kept (Eq. 5 ). On COCO a third consecutive identical box ends the list, keeping the first two copies; Dense200 uses the guard without this rule.

Metrics. COCO F1 follows the inherited evaluator: every prediction receives confidence 0.2, and per-category precision and recall are aggregated into F1 at each IoU threshold; this is distinct from COCO AP. Dense200 greedily matches ground-truth boxes to unused predictions at each $\tau\in\{0.50,0.55,\ldots,0.95\}$ , averages per-image precision and recall, and computes $F_{1,\tau}=2\overline{P}_{\tau}\overline{R}_{\tau}/(\overline{P}_{\tau}+\overline{R}_{\tau})$ ; mean F1 averages the ten thresholds. Boxes per second (BPS) divides the number of returned boxes by generation time on one H100 at batch size one, where generation time covers all queries of an image including prefill, target passes, and fallback, and excludes model loading.


#### F.5 Autonomous Driving

Splits and data separation. NAVSIM training uses navtrain, divided by log into 85,109 training and 18,179 internal validation clips; SFT and RL fit on the former, and checkpoints are selected on the latter by PDMS. Test scores use all 12,146 navtest scenes. Waymo SFT uses the official training split. The 479 validation scenes provide the RL reward and are used to select both SFT and RL checkpoints by RFS. Test scores come from the official test split. Neither test split enters SFT, RL, or checkpoint selection. The upstream VLM was fine-tuned on all of navtrain and on the Waymo training split. The NAVSIM internal validation clips are therefore held out from action-head fitting but not from VLM fine-tuning. The Waymo validation scenes and both test splits are excluded from VLM fine-tuning.

Scoring. Each scene is evaluated once: the initial Gaussian noise is seeded from the clip identifier, and five deterministic DDIM steps produce the trajectory. On NAVSIM the head’s eight $(x,y,\psi)$ waypoints at 2 Hz are scored by the official non-reactive PDM simulator ( Dauner et al., 2024 ) with the standard 5/5/2 weights for ego progress, time-to-collision within bound, and comfort. On Waymo the head’s twenty $(x,y)$ waypoints at 4 Hz are scored by the official RFS implementation, and ADE at 3 s and 5 s averages Euclidean position error over the horizon; both require a complete prediction through the horizon.

Compared methods. DiffusionLTF is the DiffusionDrive-based policy released with Open X-AV ( Nguyen et al., 2025 ) ; its scores are taken from the WOD-E2E leaderboard. Qwen-Drive-1.0 ( Zhou et al., 2026b ) couples Qwen3.5-4B with a flow-matching planner conditioned on VLM representations of the scene and an autoregressively generated text reasoning trace; its RL stage, like ours, optimizes only the planner with PDMS and RFS rewards. Fast-dDrive ( Zhang et al., 2026b ) is a block-diffusion dLLM that generates reasoning and trajectory as structured text and reports Waymo results only. Both reason in text where VFlash uses a visual plan. Latency measurement conditions for these methods, including our own measurement of Qwen-Drive-1.0, are given in Appendix E .
