## SOLVE: Sparse, Ordered Keyposes Make LLM Agents Versatile and Efficient Robot Manipulators

LLM agents have made remarkable progress in robot manipulation, yet they suffer from expensive inference, dense step-by-step control, and tool bottlenecks. We introduce SOLVE, a training-free, versatile and efficient agent policy: a multimodal LLM agent writes a sparse, ordered sequence of 6-DoF end-effector keyposes per call, observes the result, and writes the next, while a motion planner interpolates between them. The agent decides the number and spacing of keyposes for both fast long-range motion and fine-grained manipulation. This granularity matches the sparsity of teleoperated manipulation: most demonstrations in DROID can be compressed into a few keyposes instead of dense actions. SOLVE grounds keyposes with sparse depth queries answered by noisy sources, such as multi-view triangulation, stereo, or monocular depth, and copes with that noise itself. It also improves from execution feedback, reflecting on its own rollouts and distilling a few rules per task. SOLVE reaches state-of-the-art performance of 74.5% on RoboDojo and 84.5% on RoboLab, with only $1.7 per episode on average, 12 $\times$ less than previous dense-control agent policies. On a real robot, the same agent achieves an 88% success rate (75 of 85 episodes) over 16 challenging zero-shot tasks. Our project page is at https://serenos.github.io/solve-website .

### 1 Introduction

Robot manipulation policies have progressed from small task-specific networks trained with reinforcement or imitation learning [ 51 , 26 , 71 , 14 ] to vision-language-action (VLA) models [ 3 , 22 , 35 , 1 ] and world-action models (WAMs) [ 27 , 7 , 25 ] , yet all remain data-hungry: they need large teleoperation corpora for each robot and benchmark, and transfer poorly beyond them. Frontier multimodal LLMs offer a training-free alternative and can now control robots directly [ 64 , 56 , 69 , 32 ] , but current agents sit at one of two extremes. Step-wise agents [ 69 , 56 , 64 ] issue one small step per model call, so every step costs seconds of latency and money, and errors compound. Code-as-policy agents [ 40 , 55 , 19 ] instead generate programs over a primitive library, which becomes a bottleneck: uncovered tasks require new primitives, written by hand or searched offline [ 19 , 39 ] , and feedback arrives only when the program returns, too late to correct a failed step.

This raises a key question: what action granularity is appropriate for an agent policy? We compress 57,534 successful DROID episodes [ 34 ] into end-effector keyposes joined by straight lines ( Fig. 1 b), and find that over 91% of the travel lies on segments longer than 5 cm, and that a grasp or release needs only 6 keyposes instead of 96 recorded actions. We therefore hypothesize that, for an agent policy, a sequence of sparse end-effector keyposes suffices for manipulation, in place of a dense stream of actions.

We present SOLVE, a training-free framework in which a multimodal LLM agent writes sparse, ordered end-effector keyposes, and a motion planner [ 57 ] executes straight-line motions between them ( Fig. 1 c). Each keypose is an absolute 6-DoF grasp-point pose with a gripper command; one call specifies up to five keyposes, roughly one primitive such as approach, grasp, and lift, and the gripper actuates only upon arrival. The agent decides how many keyposes to use, how far apart, and how fast: a single keypose for a long free-space motion, and short, slow ones near contact. After each sequence, the agent inspects the outcome before predicting the next one, so visual feedback enters its reasoning at every primitive rather than only at the checks a program anticipates.

Because each sparse keypose is an absolute commitment, it must be metrically accurate. SOLVE therefore grounds keyposes through sparse metric queries : the agent specifies the few pixels on which its next keypose depends, and a tool returns their 3D positions, each annotated with its measurement modality. In RoboDojo and RoboLab simulation, which provide no stereo cameras, the tool triangulates each queried pixel across multiple views by epipolar matching, and falls back to monocular depth estimated by Depth Anything 3 [ 42 ] , scaled by the known table height, when no multi-view match is found; on the real robot, it directly uses depth from FoundationStereo [ 58 ] on a stereo infrared camera. Over multiple rounds of keypose reasoning, the agent adapts to the resulting depth noise by itself. On difficult tasks, the agent further improves from its own execution feedback without training: it reflects on rollouts from layouts disjoint from evaluation, and consolidates the notes from several rounds into at most five bounded rules per task, which are provided to it at test time.

Since no component of SOLVE is trained, and keyposes live in end-effector space while the planner resolves embodiment-specific execution, the same agent deploys on different robots without modification. SOLVE achieves 74.5% on the dual-arm RoboDojo under the leaderboard’s metric ( Tab. 1 ) and 84.5% on the single-arm Franka RoboLab [ 63 ] . On a real UR12e, it completes 75 of 85 episodes across 16 challenging zero-shot tasks ( Tab. 4 ), including table cleaning, peg-in-hole, sketch drawing, and playing Gomoku against a human. It does so at $1.7 per episode on average, with one quarter of the model calls and 12 $\times$ lower cost than a step-wise agent ( Fig. 1 d).

Our contributions are as follows:

- • We show that teleoperated demonstrations compress into a small number of keyposes and, building on this finding, propose SOLVE, a framework in which an LLM agent writes sparse, ordered keyposes for direct manipulation.

- • We propose sparse metric queries, combining cross-view epipolar matching with depth estimation to ground keyposes accurately and efficiently in 3D from RGB in simulation, and use stereo depth on the real robot.

- • We enable the agent to improve from rollouts, and show that SOLVE transfers without modification across robots and benchmarks, achieving state-of-the-art results on RoboDojo, RoboLab, and a real robot at 12 $\times$ lower cost.

### 2 Related Work

Vision-language-action (VLA) models [ 4 , 3 , 22 , 35 , 2 , 1 , 44 , 36 , 50 , 5 , 53 , 59 , 72 , 48 , 20 ] and imitation policies that predict action chunks [ 71 , 14 ] are trained on large teleoperation corpora to emit dense, high-frequency actions; recent work improves their spatial representations [ 50 , 73 , 62 ] and action tokenization [ 49 ] . World-action models (WAMs) [ 17 , 60 , 8 , 27 , 38 , 74 , 31 , 7 , 41 , 37 , 46 , 66 , 6 , 25 ] additionally learn from video to predict future observations and derive actions from them. On RoboDojo [ 12 ] , the best entries of both families reach 35% on the leaderboard and at most 23% on the Open dimension, whose tasks come without demonstrations. SOLVE instead acts through sparse keyposes and requires no robot-specific training.

A growing line of work builds policies around frontier LLMs with little or no training. Code-as-policy methods let the model write programs over perception and control primitives [ 40 ] ; recent systems benchmark coding agents [ 18 ] , structure programs as graphs rehearsed in simulation [ 9 ] , grow skill libraries by evolutionary search [ 45 ] , or rewrite the training code of neural policies [ 61 ] . Agent as Policy [ 32 ] similarly writes programs at test time on a real arm. Harness methods wrap a model with tools, memory and recovery logic, steering a frozen VLA as a retryable or interruptible tool [ 70 , 10 , 15 ] or calling semantic tools such as grasp and align [ 43 ] . Finally, some works use the model directly as a policy , issuing one end-effector target per call [ 69 , 56 ] , several absolute poses each executed for a single control step [ 64 ] , discrete motion units of a few centimeters [ 13 ] , or batches of relative, axis-aligned motion commands. SOLVE is a direct policy in that every call observes the scene and outputs motion targets. Unlike other direct policies, a call outputs an ordered sequence of absolute 6-DoF keyposes, placed with metric 3D measurements, that can span a whole primitive.

Sparsity itself is not new. Keyframe policies predict the next gripper pose rather than a dense trajectory [ 30 , 54 , 23 , 21 , 24 , 33 ] , and bimanual policies condition on keyposes or skills [ 67 , 75 , 65 ] . These methods learn from task demonstrations, and they extract keyframes with heuristics such as gripper state changes and near-zero joint velocities [ 30 , 54 ] , implicitly assuming that a few poses suffice; our analysis quantifies how many are needed per primitive and where this assumption fails. VLM-based methods such as VoxPoser [ 28 ] , ReKep [ 29 ] , and PIVOT [ 47 ] are also training-free, but the model typically produces object-centric keypoints, constraints or value maps once, and a separate optimizer turns them into motion. In SOLVE, the model itself writes end-effector keyposes at every call (hence keypose rather than keypoint).


### 3 Method


#### 3.1 Overview

A robot with arms $\mathcal{A}$ is required to complete a task, given as a language instruction $\ell$ , within $t_{\max}$ environment steps. At step $t$ it observes $o_{t}$ : RGB images from cameras $\mathcal{C}$ and, for each arm, the grasp-point pose $T^{a}_{t}\in SE(3)$ and gripper opening $g^{a}_{t}\in[0,1]$ . No depth is observed, but every camera pose follows from $o_{t}$ and a fixed calibration. The agent is a frozen multimodal LLM $\pi$ ( Sec. 4.1 ) that emits exactly one tool call per model call $m$ ,

[Tableau] $$z_{m}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid\ell,\,G,\,E,\,h_{m}),\qquad z_{m}\in\mathcal{Z},$$ (1)

given an optional task guide $G$ and experience notes $E$ ( Sec. 3.4 ) and the history $h_{m}$ of earlier calls and results, in which only the latest two observations keep their images. Of the tools $\mathcal{Z}$ ( Tab. 2 ), move_eef hands a keypose sequence $\mathcal{K}$ to the executor $\Psi$ , which plays it and returns the next observation with execution feedback $e_{m}$ ; query_points passes a few pixels $Q=\{(c_{j},u_{j})\}_{j}$ , pixel $u_{j}$ of camera $c_{j}$ , to the query tool $\Phi$ , which returns their world coordinates without advancing the environment,

[Tableau] $$\Phi(Q;\,o_{t})=\{(X_{j},\,\sigma_{j})\}_{j},\qquad X_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},$$ (2)

where $X_{j}$ is the world point of pixel $j$ and $\sigma_{j}$ its source, triangulated, monocular or stereo depth, which tells the agent how far to trust it; wait lets the scene evolve, and give_up ends the episode.


#### 3.2 Sparse, ordered keyposes

We first measure how far demonstrations compress into keyposes joined by straight lines. Keeping the first and last poses and every gripper event (a grasp or a release), we simplify position (interpolated linearly) and orientation (by slerp) jointly with Ramer–Douglas–Peucker [ 16 ] , adding the farthest frame as a keypose until every frame lies within a tolerance. We apply it to RoboDojo’s teleoperated demonstrations and to 57,534 successful DROID episodes [ 34 ] , which human operators teleoperated on real Franka arms.

> Figure 3 : Demonstrations compress into a few straight-line keyposes , in simulation (RoboDojo) and on real robots (DROID).

Both compress alike ( Fig. 3 ). A grasp or release spans a median of 56 recorded actions on RoboDojo (25 Hz) and 96 on DROID (15 Hz), as many as a policy imitating them at the recording rate must output; at 2 cm / 10 ∘ it needs only 4.9 and 6.0 keyposes, 11 $\times$ and 16 $\times$ fewer, and a DROID episode shrinks to a median of 13 keyposes, about one per second. Over 91% of the travel lies on segments longer than 5 cm, and on DROID this share stays within 88–95% for every instruction verb. The segments are genuinely straight: at 1 cm, 92.6% of DROID travel lies on segments whose path is at most 1.11 times their chord. A short sequence of absolute targets thus describes most manipulation, and the dense motion can be left to the planner.

RoboDojo-Sim tests precision, long horizons and memory with two ARX-X5 arms, RoboLab tests unseen objects and instructions on a single Franka without demonstrations, and the real UR12e robot tests transfer to hardware on 16 unseen tasks. The same agent, tools and executor run in all three, with only the robot’s constants changed.

A keypose of arm $a\in\mathcal{A}$ is a target for its observed state $(T^{a}_{t},g^{a}_{t})$ , $k^{a}=(\mathbf{p}^{a},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{a},g^{a})$ : the world-frame position $\mathbf{p}^{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$ of the grasp point, its orientation $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{a}=(\text{pitch},\text{roll},\text{yaw})$ , and the gripper opening $g^{a}\in[0,1]$ . The agent writes only the dimensions it wants to change; all others hold their current value in $T^{a}_{t}$ and $g^{a}_{t}$ , and naming dimensions of several arms moves them together. One move_eef call carries an ordered sequence $\mathcal{K}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})$ with $n\leq n_{\max}$ , a cap sized to one primitive, such as approach; descend and close; lift, which our demonstration analysis puts at five to six keyposes per grasp or release. Three choices make the interface easy for an LLM to use. Absolute targets: each keypose is the pose the arm should reach, not an increment, so the agent never has to plan intermediate waypoints of a long move. Arrive, then actuate: a gripper command takes effect only after the arm reaches its keypose, so “go there and grasp” is a single keypose. Agent-set speed: each keypose may carry a ramp $r$ , the number of environment steps used to accelerate toward it and to brake at it; the agent chooses a long ramp to carry liquid or set an object down gently, and a short one to strike or knock something over. The agent thus decides the number, spacing and speed of keyposes: one keypose at full speed for a long free-space move, several short and slow ones near contact. The executor $\Psi$ turns a keypose sequence $\mathcal{K}$ into joint commands without any learning. The move to each keypose is split into short segments of bounded translation and rotation, each solved for a joint path by a motion planner [ 57 ] and chained. If a segment cannot be reached, the sequence stops before it and the feedback $e_{m}$ reports which keypose failed, so the agent can replan.


#### 3.3 Sparse metric queries

Placing keyposes requires metric 3D grounding, but only at a few task-relevant points, not dense scene depth. Through query_points , the agent selects pixels $Q=\{(c_{j},u_{j})\}_{j}$ needed for its next keypose, such as a cup rim or a socket hole, and $\Phi$ returns their world points $X_{j}$ ( Eq. 2 ). The tool uses calibrated RGB views in simulation with no stereo cameras, as described below, and additionally the stereo depth of the wrist camera on hardware ( Sec. 4.1 ).

We triangulate each query pixel by calibrated two-view matching along its epipolar line. To accommodate the large viewpoint and scale differences between head and wrist cameras, we evaluate depth hypotheses using plane-induced patch warping and normalized cross-correlation rather than comparing fixed image windows. This directly matches the agent-selected pixel without requiring it to be a detected keypoint or computing a dense disparity map. We first try the other current cameras, then recent wrist frames with stricter acceptance thresholds to account for scene motion. A point is accepted only when the match passes confidence, uniqueness and texture checks; otherwise, we use the depth model fallback below. Implementation details and comparisons with feature matching are provided in the supplementary material.

When triangulation fails, e.g . , on textureless surfaces or points visible in only one camera, the tool uses depth predicted by Depth Anything 3 [ 42 ] from all current views and their camera calibration. We correct the scale per view using reference depths obtained by intersecting table-pixel rays with the known table plane. The model runs once per observation $o_{t}$ , shared by all queries; its depth is accurate to a centimeter but smooths edges and thin parts.

Each returned point $X_{j}$ carries its source $\sigma_{j}$ ( Eq. 2 ), triangulated, monocular or, on hardware, stereo; triangulated points additionally carry the match confidence. The agent uses it to decide whether to act on a reading or to measure again, e.g . , by querying a more distinctive pixel or the same point in a closer wrist view before a contact-critical keypose.

#### 3.4 Self-evolution with experience

The keypose interface, the executor and the queries are shared by all tasks and robots. Task-specific knowledge enters only through the two optional inputs of Eq. 1 , the guide $G$ and the experience notes $E$ .

Where a benchmark provides demonstrations, a guide $G$ explains one demonstration of the task in text and keyframes. The LLM writes it offline from the demonstration’s grasp-point trajectory and camera frames, following a fixed structure: the demonstration’s stages and its hard parts, and then for each stage its goal, the moves as performed in move_eef values, the grasp geometry, a check that tells when the stage is done, and head and wrist keyframes where the text cites them. Every statement is tagged as general, such as which arm grasps which part of an object and in what order, or as specific to the demonstrated layout, such as its coordinates; A guide thus conveys a strategy in the agent’s own action space rather than a trajectory to replay, since object poses differ in every evaluated layout. Tasks without demonstrations, such as those of RoboLab and the real robot, run without a guide ( $G=\varnothing$ ).

Experience notes $E$ let the agent improve on a task from its own execution feedback, without parameter updates. On layouts that do not overlap with the evaluation layouts, the agent runs $R$ rollouts of a task one after another. After each one, with its history $h_{m}$ still in context, it is told whether it succeeded, shown the task’s current notes, and answers with one more tool call $z_{m}$ , update_experience : a short summary of why the rollout succeeded or failed, and operations that add, update or delete notes. The prompt asks only for lessons that decided the outcome of the task or of a sub-step, stated with the situation in which they hold rather than with coordinates, and requires any lesson that adds verification, retries or extra motion to bound it in attempts or steps, since the step budget $t_{\max}$ ends most failed episodes. A note set $\mathcal{N}$ holds a bounded number of short notes, each with a few frames.

Raw notes vary from one writing run to the next, and their effect depends on what each run happens to write ( Sec. 4.3 ); notes that urge the agent to verify or to be careful make it run out of steps. We therefore write $S$ note sets $\mathcal{N}^{(1)},\dots,\mathcal{N}^{(S)}$ independently, each from its own $R$ rollouts, and merge them with one LLM call $\mathcal{M}$ per task,

[Tableau] $$E_{\text{task}}=\mathcal{M}\big(\mathcal{N}^{(1)},\dots,\mathcal{N}^{(S)}\big),\qquad|E_{\text{task}}|\leq L,$$ (3)

where $\mathcal{M}$ sees each set’s final notes together with the outcome and summary of all $RS$ rollouts, and keeps only lessons that at least two rollouts support, citing them. Each rule takes the form “When situation : action ” under a word limit, with a step cost or limit wherever it spends steps; advice to verify without a bound and numbers specific to one layout are not allowed, and rules carry no frames. A final call distills general rules $E_{\text{gen}}$ that recur across many tasks, on measurement, grasping, carrying, reachability, arm allocation, placing, merging moves and step budgeting. At test time the agent reads $E=E_{\text{task}}\cup E_{\text{gen}}$ .


### 4 Experiments


#### 4.1 Setup

All experiments share the agent code, tools and executor; only the robot’s constants change (kinematic model, camera calibration, robot description in the prompt). RoboDojo uses guides (except on Open) and self-evolution, RoboLab only self-evolution, and the real robot neither: each episode is a single zero-shot attempt.

RoboDojo-Sim [ 12 ] comprises 42 tasks across five dimensions on two ARX-X5 arms, with demonstrations except for Open tasks. We use only RGB observations, follow the official evaluation protocol, and report success rate (SR) and partial-credit score.

RoboLab [ 63 ] comprises 120 tasks on a Franka; we use its external and wrist RGB views and follow the official protocol, without guides.

We also run SOLVE on a single-arm UR12e with a wrist stereo camera and a fixed third-person camera; query_points additionally reads the wrist camera’s stereo depth (details in the supplementary material). We design 16 tasks ( Tab. 4 ) along the Long-Horizon, Precision, Memory and Open dimensions and beyond them: in-context imitation (rebuilding a structure that a person builds once in front of the robot), playing Gomoku against a person, and folding a shirt. Several are hard: peg-in-hole insertion leaves only 1 mm of clearance, and in drawing the agent picks up the pen itself, so the offset from the grasp point to the pen tip is unknown. No task has pre-collected demonstrations, guides, notes or task-specific code. We run five episodes per task and ten of shirt folding, 85 in all.

The prompt describes the robot, the keypose convention and the tools, then gives the instruction, $G$ and $E$ ; each later turn adds the current images, the arms’ state and the last call’s feedback. It again sets the keypose density itself: three or four for a grasp and place, up to 30 per call when it draws. In context, it rebuilds a six-cup pyramid and a five-part block castle after watching a person build each once; when the person has built the castle but not yet scattered the blocks, it keeps watching until they do. It also wins all ten Gomoku games against a person. When an attempt falls short, it adjusts the next one from the images rather than repeating it: after a broken test stroke it lowers the pen by 1.5 mm, and after a failed top-down grasp of a plate it tilts the gripper to grasp the rim. No task-specific code prompts these corrections (reasoning quoted in the supplementary material). Its ten failures come from a shirt sliding along with the gripper, an insertion released before both pegs are seated and then blocked by a protective stop, or a drawing that would drive the wrist camera into the table.

#### 4.4 Analysis

> Figure 5 : Keyposes written by SOLVE vs . keyposes a demonstration needs , for the 34 RoboDojo tasks with demonstrations.

Figure 5 compares the keyposes SOLVE writes per episode with those a teleoperated demonstration of the task needs. The two counts agree closely: medians of 39.5 vs . 40.3 per task, a rank correlation of 0.90 over 34 tasks. This is not merely because both grow with the number of objects: with the demonstration’s grasps and releases held fixed, the partial rank correlation remains 0.69.

> Figure 6 : Keyposes per call. (a) Success rate and (b) model calls per episode against the keyposes allowed per move_eef call.

We cap a move_eef call at one, three or five keyposes and change nothing else ( Fig. 6 ). Success is unchanged (69.8, 67.8 and 68.2%), but model calls per episode fall from 56.6 to 36.2 and 33.7, 40% fewer. A higher cap would save little more in simulation: even with five allowed, the agent sends one to three keyposes in 89% of its calls and five in only 3%, as it often splits a primitive across calls to look in between. Drawing needs many more: on the real robot, a cap of 30 lets the agent draw a portrait’s 305 keyposes in 15 move_eef calls instead of at least 61, each saving seconds of model latency.

> Figure 7 : Evolving the notes over more rounds. Bold: SR per five rounds; dashed and dotted: our merged rules and no notes.

We let the agent keep evolving its notes for 20 rounds, each running one new layout per RoboDojo task ( Fig. 7 ). Starting from 59.8% without notes, success reaches 66.0, 68.4, 69.0 and 65.6% after 5, 10, 15 and 20 rounds: it peaks near our frozen merged rules (68.3%) and then declines. It is also unstable: single rounds swing between 57.1% and 76.2%, while three runs with the frozen merged rules average 68.6%. The notes explain both: as they fill up, the agent stops adding and rewrites them around the latest episode, so that most notes end up describing one particular episode and earlier lessons are overwritten. Merging avoids this: it keeps only layout-free lessons that recur across independent runs and freezes them, reaching the peak from the start and holding it across runs.


### 5 Conclusion

An LLM agent does not need dense, step-by-step control to manipulate. Teleoperated demonstrations compress to a few keyposes per grasp or release, and SOLVE lets a multimodal LLM agent write such keyposes directly, grounds them with sparse metric queries, and refines them with notes from its own rollouts, without training. SOLVE reaches 74.5% on RoboDojo and 84.5% on RoboLab, ahead of every leaderboard entry, at $1.7 per episode, and 88% over 16 zero-shot real-world tasks. Sparse, ordered keyposes are an effective and economical interface between LLMs and robot control.

Some real tasks require force or continuous feedback that keyposes cannot provide, and each model call takes seconds; a next step is to combine keyposes with a learned low-level policy for contact-rich segments.

The agent is called through an OpenAI-compatible Responses API and must answer every turn with exactly one tool call; if a call has invalid arguments, such as a sequence of more than five keyposes, it is rejected with an explanation and the agent is prompted again. Each call carries a note , one or two sentences on what the agent sees and why it acts, which is logged and kept in the history ( Sec. E.2.1 quotes such notes). Only the latest two observations keep their images ( Sec. 3.1 ); older images are replaced by placeholders.

After a move_eef call, the next observation contains the current camera images, each arm’s grasp-point pose and gripper opening, and the feedback $e_{m}$ : for each arm, the distance from each commanded keypose; any slip (which stops that arm and skips its remaining keyposes) or fully closed grasp (which is reported without stopping); the keypose at which the planner refused a sequence, if any; and the remaining environment steps. A move that would take more than 10 s is refused and has to be split. wait returns camera keyframes recorded while it held the arms, and can end early once the scene has stopped moving.

On both RoboDojo and RoboLab, experience notes are written only on layouts that differ from the evaluation layouts, then merged and frozen before evaluation, so no evaluation layout is seen while writing notes. The first rollout on RoboLab ( Sec. 4.2 ) is the evaluation without notes; the self-evolved notes are written on other layouts and then used to evaluate the same evaluation layouts again.

#### C.1 Keypose compression of demonstrations

This section details the analysis of Sec. 3.2 .

RoboDojo provides 100 teleoperated demonstrations for each of its 34 tasks with demonstrations, recorded at 25 Hz on two ARX-X5 arms; we convert each arm to the grasp point used by move_eef , count the keyposes of both arms together, and skip an arm that moves less than 2 cm and 5 ∘ . For DROID [ 34 ] we take all 57,639 successful episodes (15 Hz, Franka with a Robotiq 2F-85) and drop those that move less than 2 cm and 5 ∘ or last under one second, leaving 57,534 episodes and 18.7M frames, without subsampling. We use DROID’s recorded end-effector pose, detect gripper events with hysteresis (closed above 0.4, open below 0.2, ignoring flips shorter than three frames), and smooth positions over about 0.2 s before measuring path lengths, which changes the shares of long segments by less than 0.3 points.

On RoboDojo, every task has at least 80% of its travel on straight segments longer than 5 cm, the lowest being push T (80.2%) and pour liquid into cup (80.4%). The 13,144 failed DROID episodes compress similarly: 88.9% of their travel lies on long segments, against 91.3% for successes. Moving DROID’s recorded pose 15 cm along the tool axis, in case it is the flange rather than the fingertips, raises the median keyposes per episode from 13 to 15 and leaves the share at 91.2%. The analysis measures geometric compressibility only: neither the DROID nor the RoboDojo demonstrations record the states of the objects, so the compressed trajectories cannot be replayed to measure their success.


#### C.2 Epipolar matching of query pixels

query_points must return the 3D point of the pixel the agent chose, often the center of a hole or a point on a rim, from cameras that differ greatly in direction and scale. We compare our epipolar matching ( Sec. 3.3 ) with the alternatives offline, on pixels that the agent actually queried, using as reference the 3D point measured on simulator depth for the same pixel.

Depth hypotheses lie on the query ray from 5 mm below to 36 cm above the table, 1.5 mm apart. At each one, the $15{\times}15$ gray-level window around the query pixel is warped into the second view through a horizontal plane or a plane facing the query camera, whichever matches better, and compared by normalized cross-correlation with Gaussian weights, so that the pixels nearest the query decide. A match is accepted if its score $s$ is high, if it stands out from the best score elsewhere on the ray, $s_{r}$ , by the ratio $\rho=(1-s_{r})/(1-s)$ , which rejects repeated structures, and if the window has texture (a gray-level spread of at least 4). We require $s\geq 0.85$ and $\rho\geq 1.5$ for the current cameras and the previous observation, and $s\geq 0.9$ and $\rho\geq 2$ for older wrist frames (up to six observations back), since objects may have moved; a second view must be at least 2 cm or $8^{\circ}$ away from the query camera.

Triangulating from feature matches between two views is a natural alternative, but it suits our queries poorly: the head and wrist cameras see the scene from very different directions and at very different scales, so local features often fail to match between them, and the pixel the agent queries, such as the center of a hole, need not carry a matchable feature at all, so its depth would have to be borrowed from matches nearby. Our epipolar matching instead searches along the ray of the queried pixel itself and warps the window by the plane-induced homography, which absorbs the differences in viewpoint and scale.

On all 1,182 query pixels of the Precision tasks ( Tab. 7 ), epipolar matching answers 37% of the pixels, using the other cameras and the wrist frames of the last six observations, at a median error of 4.6 mm; two thirds of its answers lie within 1 cm. This is about four times more accurate than measuring object silhouettes (20.0 mm) and ten times more than intersecting the pixel’s ray with the table (51.6 mm). The gap is largest on holes, where a silhouette has no surface to measure: 2.8 vs . 36.4 mm on deposit coin . Past wrist frames raise the coverage from 24% to 37% at no loss of accuracy, since a moving wrist camera provides new baselines for free.

Pixels on textureless, slanted or specular surfaces have no reliable match; there the tool reads the depth estimated by Depth Anything 3, accurate to about a centimeter, or the table plane, and tags each answer with its source. The agent therefore gets an answer for every pixel and knows which ones to trust to millimeters: the full chain answers all 1,182 pixels, not only the 37% that epipolar matching accepts, at a median error of 5.4 mm, close to that of epipolar matching alone, and puts 64% of them within 1 cm, against 36% for object silhouettes and 10% for the table plane ( Tab. 7 ).

#### C.3 Evolving notes over more rounds

In the experiment of Fig. 7 , the agent keeps evolving its notes over 20 additional rounds instead of merging and freezing them ( Sec. 4.4 ), using layouts that differ from the evaluation layouts. The resulting notes explain why performance stops improving and fluctuates: each task keeps at most ten notes, and once full, the agent mostly overwrites existing notes rather than adding new ones, replacing general lessons with accounts of the latest episode. For example, a play stacking toy note on grasping square pieces between occupied pegs ends up reading “This success placed all three squares between a tall ring stack and a blue block…”. Merging keeps only lessons that several independent rollouts support, stated without layout details, and freezing stops the latest episode from rewriting them, which is why SOLVE merges and freezes its notes.

##### E.2.1 The agent’s reasoning

Every tool call carries the agent’s note on what it sees and why it acts ( Appendix B ). We quote notes where the agent does more than the instruction spells out.

In the Gomoku endgame, before its first move, the agent plans two lines at once:

“Black has the diagonal three C3, D4, E5 as well as F4 and F5; I choose F6 to make a diagonal four and a vertical threat.”

The person blocks the diagonal at G7, and the agent turns to the second line:

“White’s new move at G7 confirmed; black draws an x on the empty F3, so that F3–F6 form a four open at both ends.”

An open four cannot be blocked at both ends; the person blocks F7 and the agent wins at F2. In the full game it adapts in the same way: “White blocked the three on the diagonal at its lower-right end. I switch to the horizontal line”, and at the end it races the person: “White’s latest move forms a four above, still not five. It is black’s turn: I draw an x at the measured, still empty right end, completing five horizontally.”

In the castle task, the person first built the castle but did not yet scatter the blocks. Rather than start rebuilding, the agent keeps watching:

“The first demonstration showed the finished castle but lacked the scattering step that the task requires; keep the arm still and watch this part as well.”

Folding the shirt, the gripper reports an empty grasp on the thin hem. The agent does not retry blindly:

“The thin hem may be held even though the gripper reports nothing. I will make a small vertical lift and check whether the fabric follows before retrying.” “The images confirm that the hem lifted despite the empty-grasp report. I will carry the lower hem in an arc over the shirt’s midpoint to fold the lower half upward.”

In every drawing task the agent first draws a short test stroke, looks at it, and adjusts the pen height by millimeters:

“The top view shows the lower half of the C touched too lightly and the line is broken. Lower the pen by only 1.5 mm and complete these three straight strokes, without adding lateral pressure.”

Copying the rocket, it measures each marker separately after each regrasp: “The red nib is about 2.4 mm shorter below the grasp than the black nib. I will draw the two straight red nose edges at the corresponding contact height”, and later “The blue nib is another 1.5 mm shorter at this grasp.” For the portrait it respects the cap of the instruction exactly, noting that its last drawing call “has exactly 30 keyposes”.

In the shell game the agent states its goal before the person starts, “observe which cup the cap is hidden under, and continuously track that cup’s position during the shuffle”, and confirms it on lifting the first cup it chose: “The red cap has appeared from under this cup, confirming that the tracking was correct.”

In insertion trial 1 the agent lowered the workpiece 4 mm into the holes, released it and lifted; the arm then entered a protective stop. It measures the result, gives up rather than waiting for a correction it cannot make, and states why and what it should have done:

“The pin workpiece is upright on the receiving workpiece and the gripper is open and clear, but the final height measurements indicate that the pegs are not fully seated. The robot is now in protective-stop safety mode, so I cannot safely correct the insertion.” Hindsight: “The insertion should have been checked for actual downward movement of the workpiece at the first contact step. A better alignment check and verified peg engagement were needed before releasing.”

In the two successful trials it stops at first contact and lets gravity seat the workpiece:

“The pin is now supported at the hole block, and its small movement relative to the jaws suggests contact. I will not push farther: I will release it to settle naturally.” “I will lower another 7 mm to establish roughly 1 cm of engagement, then release rather than press the shoulder against the receiver.”

This is the kind of lesson that the experience notes of Sec. 3.4 distill on RoboDojo.
