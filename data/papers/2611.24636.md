## Clover-35B-A3B-Preview Technical Report

We introduce Clover-35B-A3B-Preview (Clover), a mixture-of-experts diffusion language model with 35 billion total parameters and 3 billion activated parameters, supporting thinking-mode generation. Clover adapts Gated Delta Networks to block diffusion by combining recurrent memory of previously generated context with bidirectional softmax attention inside the current block. Historical memory readout competes with current-block tokens in a shared softmax, with learnable block-size calibration and a separate, zero-initialized right-context convolution branch. Clover leads the seven evaluated agent metrics, including 73.6 on SWE-bench Verified and 51.7 on Terminal-Bench 2.1. On internal agent workloads, it reaches a reported generation speed of 680 tokens/s using one NVIDIA GB300 GPU at BF16 precision and batch size one. We organize the evaluation around model quality, drafting and verification costs, and deployment under constrained memory. We also describe a decision-model variant that generates answers to related questions in one denoising pass. ### 1 Introduction

Diffusion language models (DLMs) offer a different route to language generation: reconstructing sequences through iterative denoising, with multiple tokens predicted in parallel. This formulation connects flexible generation order with the prospect of efficient inference. Progress in diffusion formulations, model scaling, and decoding has brought DLMs from specialized text-generation settings to increasingly capable general-purpose models. The next step is to translate these advances into strong performance and practical efficiency across demanding applications.

Continuous text diffusion models established generation through denoising learned token representations. Diffusion-LM explored controllable generation, while DiffuSeq and DiffuSeq-v2 developed sequence-to-sequence diffusion and a bridge between discrete corruption and continuous representations [ 27 , 16 , 17 ] . Alongside these models, D3PM formalized structured categorical corruption [ 3 ] . Reparameterized Discrete Diffusion Models (RDM) exposed a routing interpretation of backward sampling [ 67 ] ; SEDD and MDLM further developed discrete score modeling and masked-token reconstruction [ 31 , 47 ] .

Beyond text reconstruction, Diffusion of Thought explored denoising explicit reasoning trajectories [ 61 ] . Ye et al. [62] investigated discrete diffusion for planning and constraint-solving tasks, using multi-granularity learning and adaptive generation order. These studies established capability directions that complement the development of general-purpose DLMs.

Scaling made these ideas accessible through larger models. Adapting pretrained autoregressive checkpoints provided one route to diffusion language modeling [ 18 ] ; LLaDA and Dream 7B broadened the publicly available model ecosystem [ 35 , 64 ] , and code-focused models such as Dream-Coder 7B extended masked diffusion to code generation and reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards [ 58 , 19 ] . Block diffusion combines sequential progress across blocks with parallel denoising inside each block [ 2 ] . Fast-dLLM and Fast-dLLM v2 developed complementary inference and adaptation techniques based on caching and confidence-aware parallel decoding [ 56 , 55 ] . DreamReasoner further extends block diffusion to long chain-of-thought reasoning through block-size curriculum learning [ 57 ] .

Industrial development now spans commercial systems such as Mercury and publicly released models such as DiffusionGemma and LLaDA2.2 [ 22 , 11 , 23 ] . These systems have expanded the scope and deployment options of diffusion language modeling.

As the model ecosystem matures, agentic applications provide a demanding test of this progress: they require both capable tool use and efficient generation through repeated interactions with an environment. Strong results on isolated reasoning or code-generation tasks do not by themselves establish reliable repository-level problem solving. Competitive agent performance remains a challenge for open DLMs. Recent models already include agent evaluations, but the reproduced comparison shows that LLaDA2.2 flash and DiffusionGemma still trail the evaluated Qwen baselines on both Terminal-Bench 2.1 [ 32 ] and SWE-bench Verified [ 9 ] (Table 1 ). Advancing agent capability while retaining efficient parallel generation is a central motivation for Clover.

To this end, we present Clover-35B-A3B-Preview, an efficient and capable diffusion language model designed for agentic applications. It combines a 35B-total, 3B-active mixture-of-experts architecture with a bidirectional adaptation of Gated Delta Networks. Completed context is represented by recurrent memory, while tokens in the active block interact through softmax attention. A shared softmax combines historical and block-local information, with learnable block-size calibration and a separate right-context convolution branch. Our evaluation emphasizes agentic coding and tool use alongside general capabilities, and examines the cost of turning parallel predictions into accepted output tokens.

The report first introduces the modeling and decoding background, then presents Clover’s architecture, benchmark results, and runtime analysis. Separate sections examine on-device deployment and a decision-model variant that generates related answers jointly.

We release model weights, supervised fine-tuning code, and the inference engine, with particular emphasis on dedicated infrastructure for running Clover locally on consumer devices. We will continue to improve inference speed and model performance, expand multimodal capabilities, and contribute to the continued development of diffusion language models.


### 2 Preliminaries


#### 2.1 Masked diffusion and block generation

Let $x_{0}=(x_{0}^{1},\ldots,x_{0}^{L})$ be a clean token sequence and $m$ be the mask token. In an absorbing-mask forward process [ 3 ] , each position is retained with probability $\alpha_{t}$ and replaced by $m$ otherwise:

[Tableau] $$q(x_{t}^{i}\mid x_{0}^{i})=\alpha_{t}\,\delta_{x_{0}^{i}}(x_{t}^{i})+(1-\alpha_{t})\,\delta_{m}(x_{t}^{i}),\qquad\alpha_{0}=1,\quad\alpha_{1}=0.$$ (1)

Here $\delta$ denotes a point mass. A masked diffusion model (MDM) predicts clean tokens from the corrupted sequence. For a differentiable decreasing schedule, the standard continuous-time masked reconstruction objective [ 47 , 49 ] can be written as

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MDM}}=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1),\,x_{0},\,x_{t}}\left[w(t)\sum_{i:x_{t}^{i}=m}\bigl(-\log p_{\theta}(x_{0}^{i}\mid x_{t},t)\bigr)\right],\qquad w(t)=-\frac{\alpha_{t}^{\prime}}{1-\alpha_{t}}.$$ (2)

Fast-dLLM accelerates masked diffusion through approximate KV reuse and confidence-aware parallel decoding, while Fast-dLLM v2 develops block-level caching [ 56 , 55 ] . In block diffusion [ 2 ] , completed blocks supply context for subsequent blocks. We divide the output into blocks $B_{1},\ldots,B_{K}$ and let $c_{k}$ contain the prompt and completed blocks preceding $B_{k}$ . The model denoises the current block conditional on $c_{k}$ , with bidirectional access to its partially reconstructed contents. Block size, the number of denoising passes, and the token selection rule together determine how much parallelism is exposed. See Section 3.4 for Clover’s training setup.


#### 2.2 Self-speculative decoding

Speculative decoding accelerates autoregressive generation by verifying multiple draft tokens in a single target-model pass [ 5 , 28 ] . Recently, diffusion language models offer a natural drafting mechanism through parallel block prediction, as demonstrated by DFlash [ 7 ] and DFlash 2 [ 24 ] . Self-speculative decoding integrates these roles within the same model: diffusion-style block prediction supplies candidate tokens, and autoregressive verification determines which prefix can be committed. NVIDIA’s Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion supports autoregressive, diffusion, and self-speculation decoding within one model [ 15 ] .


#### 2.3 Gated Delta Networks

Gated Delta Networks combine recurrent state decay with a delta-rule memory update [ 60 ] . With column-vector keys $k_{i}$ , values $v_{i}$ , and queries $q_{i}$ , one conventional orientation of the recurrence is

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\widetilde{S}_{i}$ $\displaystyle=\gamma_{i}S_{i-1},$ (3) $\displaystyle S_{i}$ $\displaystyle=\widetilde{S}_{i}+\beta_{i}(v_{i}-\widetilde{S}_{i}k_{i})k_{i}^{\top},\qquad o_{i}=S_{i}q_{i}.$ (4)

Here $S_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}\times d_{k}}$ stores the recurrent memory, while $\gamma_{i}$ and $\beta_{i}$ control retention and the update. The delta rule writes the residual between a new value and the value retrieved with its key. Unlike a token-wise attention cache, this state has a fixed shape for a fixed head configuration. ( 4 ) suppresses head indices, feature normalization, and surrounding projections.

A causal recurrence summarizes information to the left of a token. Clover’s adaptation retains this role for completed context and introduces bidirectional interactions for tokens being denoised together. The distinction is between completed history and the active block ; bidirectionality does not expose unseen future blocks.


### 3 Clover-35B-A3B-Preview: Architecture and Evaluation


#### 3.1 Model overview

Clover-35B-A3B-Preview adapts the text backbone of Qwen3.6-35B-A3B [ 45 ] into a block diffusion language model with approximately 35B total parameters and 3B activated parameters per token. Its hybrid backbone combines recurrent sequence mixing, full attention, and sparse expert computation. We adapt two components to block denoising: Block-diffusion Gated DeltaNet (BD-GDN) combines compressed clean history with bidirectional interactions inside the current block, while dMoE coordinates expert selection across that block. Figure 1 summarizes the architecture.

(b) Pink boxes are linear projections; the $Q$ , $K$ , $V$ , and $Z$ paths carry query, key, value, and output-gate features. (c) dMoE combines token-level top-eight routing within a block coreset with a shared expert. Solid lines carry features, dashed arrows show routing, and gray experts are inactive. For one token, $p_{i}$ is the normalized weight of routed expert $i$ , and $g_{s}$ is the shared expert’s sigmoid gate.


##### Hybrid backbone.

The model has 40 decoder layers with hidden width $D=2{,}048$ . A group of three BD-GDN layers followed by one full-attention layer is repeated ten times, giving 30 recurrent mixers and 10 full-attention mixers. Each mixer is followed by a dMoE [ 14 ] module, with pre-normalization and residual connections:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle U^{\ell}$ $\displaystyle=H^{\ell}+\operatorname{Mixer}_{\ell}(\operatorname{RMSNorm}(H^{\ell})),$ (5) $\displaystyle H^{\ell+1}$ $\displaystyle=U^{\ell}+\operatorname{dMoE}_{\ell}(\operatorname{RMSNorm}(U^{\ell})).$

Here $\ell$ indexes decoder layers, $H^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times D}$ is the input hidden-state sequence of length $L$ , and $U^{\ell}$ is the intermediate state after sequence mixing. $\operatorname{Mixer}_{\ell}$ is either BD-GDN or full attention; RMSNorm [ 65 ] normalizes each token over its feature dimension with a learned feature-wise scale.

BD-GDN uses $H_{k}=16$ query/key heads and $H_{v}=32$ value heads, with key and value dimensions $d_{k}=d_{v}=128$ . Each query/key head serves two value heads. Full-attention layers use 16 query heads, two K/V heads, and head dimension 256, with query/key normalization, partial rotary position embeddings [ 51 ] , and a sigmoid output gate. The model supports 131,072 tokens across the prompt and generated response, with untied input/output embeddings and a vocabulary of 248,320 entries.

Our architectural exploration led us to prioritize bidirectional interaction within Clover’s current active block. By enabling tokens to use context from both directions as fully as possible, we aim to preserve and fully exploit the bidirectional modeling capability of diffusion, while retaining efficient access to the completed prefix.


#### 3.2 Block-diffusion Gated DeltaNet

BD-GDN separates writing completed context from reading it during denoising . The clean path retains the gated delta-rule recurrence of Section 2.3 [ 60 ] . The noisy path reads the resulting prefix state and combines it with bidirectional attention over the current block. This prefix state stays fixed throughout the block’s denoising passes.


##### Projected features and notation.

Let $h_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$ be the RMS-normalized input to the BD-GDN at token position $i$ . Linear projections produce query, key, and value features; these pass through the short convolution and SiLU activation described below. The resulting per-head vectors are $q_{i},k_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}$ and $v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}$ . Queries and keys then undergo

[Tableau] $$\widehat{q}_{i}=\frac{q_{i}}{\sqrt{\lVert q_{i}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\epsilon}},\qquad\widehat{k}_{i}=\frac{k_{i}}{\sqrt{\lVert k_{i}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\epsilon}},\qquad\epsilon=10^{-6}.$$ (6)

Here $\lVert\cdot\rVert_{2}$ is the Euclidean norm over one head’s features; the hats denote the normalized vectors. We describe one value head below, omitting layer and head indices on its vectors and recurrent state. Query/key heads are repeated to match the 32 value heads.


##### Clean memory update.

For a clean token, the learned retention and write gates are

[Tableau] $$\alpha_{i}=\exp\!\left[-\exp(a_{\log})\operatorname{softplus}(w_{a}^{\top}h_{i}+b_{\Delta})\right],\qquad\beta_{i}=\sigma(w_{\beta}^{\top}h_{i}).$$ (7)

The vectors $w_{a},w_{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$ project the token to scalar gates; $a_{\log}$ and $b_{\Delta}$ are learned scalars for this head. We use $\sigma(u)=1/(1+e^{-u})$ and $\operatorname{softplus}(u)=\log(1+e^{u})$ . Thus $\alpha_{i}$ controls retention of the previous memory and $\beta_{i}$ controls the strength of the new write. The update is

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\widetilde{S}_{i}$ $\displaystyle=\alpha_{i}S_{i-1},$ (8) $\displaystyle S_{i}$ $\displaystyle=\widetilde{S}_{i}+\beta_{i}(v_{i}-\widetilde{S}_{i}\widehat{k}_{i})\widehat{k}_{i}^{\top},$ $\displaystyle o_{i}^{\mathrm{clean}}$ $\displaystyle=S_{i}\widehat{q}_{i}/\sqrt{d_{k}}.$

The memory $S_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}\times d_{k}}$ stores completed context, and $\widetilde{S}_{i}$ is its decayed value before the write. The residual $v_{i}-\widetilde{S}_{i}\widehat{k}_{i}$ corrects the value retrieved with the current key; $o_{i}^{\mathrm{clean}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}$ is the clean output. Each packed example starts from a zero state; padding neither writes nor decays the memory.


##### Combining history and the current block.

Let $b$ index the block being denoised, $S_{b}$ denote its clean prefix state, and $\mathcal{A}_{b}$ be the set of valid token positions in that block. For query position $j\in\mathcal{A}_{b}$ , the historical readout is

[Tableau] $$r_{j}=\frac{S_{b}\widehat{q}_{j}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}},\qquad r_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}.$$ (9)

Here $S_{b}$ is the state at the block boundary , whereas $S_{i}$ in ( 8 ) is indexed by a clean token. The readout retains the $1/\sqrt{d_{k}}$ scaling of the clean GDN output. It does not apply the noisy query’s decay gate: $\alpha$ and $\beta$ govern clean memory updates.

BD-GDN treats $r_{j}$ as one information unit alongside the block’s token values. For each layer and value head $h$ , we learn a local logit scale $s_{h}$ , a history bias $b_{h}$ , and a block-size coefficient $c_{h}$ . With $N_{b}=|\mathcal{A}_{b}|$ and reference size $N_{\mathrm{ref}}=32$ , the logits are

[Tableau] $$\ell_{ji}=s_{h}\widehat{q}_{j}^{\top}\widehat{k}_{i}\quad(i\in\mathcal{A}_{b}),\qquad\ell_{j,\mathrm{hist}}=b_{h}+c_{h}\log(N_{b}/N_{\mathrm{ref}}).$$ (10)

The index $i$ runs over current-block keys, while $j$ identifies the query. The subscript $h$ indexes value heads, not token positions. One softmax normalizes the $N_{b}$ local logits and the history logit together:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle(\{p_{ji}\}_{i\in\mathcal{A}_{b}},p_{j,\mathrm{hist}})$ $\displaystyle=\operatorname{softmax}(\{\ell_{ji}\}_{i\in\mathcal{A}_{b}},\ell_{j,\mathrm{hist}}),$ (11) $\displaystyle o_{j}$ $\displaystyle=p_{j,\mathrm{hist}}r_{j}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}_{b}}p_{ji}v_{i}.$

The weights $p_{ji}$ and $p_{j,\mathrm{hist}}$ sum to one for each query and head; $o_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}$ is their weighted output. A query can therefore use both the compressed prefix and individual current-block tokens. Local logits use the learned scale $s_{h}$ on normalized Q/K; the $1/\sqrt{d_{k}}$ factor scales the historical value , not these logits. All valid block positions participate, including the query itself and unresolved mask tokens. Padding is excluded from $\mathcal{A}_{b}$ .


##### Learnable block-size calibration.

History occupies one softmax entry, while the number of local entries grows with block size. The term $c_{h}\log(N_{b}/N_{\mathrm{ref}})$ compensates for this change in the number of competitors. To see the effect, suppose all local logits equal a common value $\mu$ and $c_{h}=1$ . Then

[Tableau] $$p_{j,\mathrm{hist}}=\frac{\exp(b_{h})}{\exp(b_{h})+N_{\mathrm{ref}}\exp(\mu)},$$ (12)

which is independent of $N_{b}$ . Learning $c_{h}$ lets each head adjust this compensation as its local logit distribution changes. We initialize $(s_{h},b_{h},c_{h})=(10,5,1)$ . The count $N_{b}$ includes unresolved mask positions and excludes padding, so partial blocks use their actual valid length.


##### Independent bidirectional short convolution.

The query, key, and value projections form a tensor with $C=2H_{k}d_{k}+H_{v}d_{v}=8{,}192$ channels. Let $u_{c,i}$ be channel $c$ of this tensor at token position $i$ , before convolution. We combine a causal depthwise kernel of width $W=4$ with an independent right-context kernel:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle a_{c,i}$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{W-1}w^{\leftarrow}_{c,r}u_{c,i-(W-1-r)}+\sum_{m=1}^{W-1}w^{\rightarrow}_{c,m}u_{c,i+m},$ (13) $\displaystyle\bar{u}_{c,i}$ $\displaystyle=\operatorname{SiLU}(a_{c,i}).$

Here $r$ indexes causal taps, $m$ is the rightward token offset, and $w^{\leftarrow}$ and $w^{\rightarrow}$ are the two learned kernel weights. Their outputs are summed before one activation $\operatorname{SiLU}(u)=u\sigma(u)$ ; $\bar{u}$ is then split into Q/K/V. The convolution is bias-free and operates independently on each channel. Left inputs come from committed convolution history and earlier positions of the current block. Right inputs are restricted to valid successors in that block; missing inputs contribute zero. Thus no right tap crosses a block or packed-example boundary (Figure 2 ).

We retain the pretrained causal kernel and initialize all three right taps to zero. This preserves the causal convolution at initialization while allowing the added taps to learn local right-context features. Clean prefill and causal commit use only the causal kernel. Across 30 BD-GDN layers, the right kernels add 737,280 parameters and the three per-head softmax scalars add 2,880, for a total of 740,160 new parameters.

> Figure 2 : The two block-diffusion adaptations in BD-GDN. (a) The query-specific history readout $r_{j}$ from ( 9 ) competes with $N_{b}$ current-block values in one softmax. Its logit includes learnable block-size calibration. (b) Independent causal and right-context depthwise kernels are summed before SiLU. The right taps start at zero and read only valid successors in the current block; clean prefill and commit use the causal kernel.


##### Output gating and cache updates.

Each value head applies RMSNorm over its $d_{v}$ output features and a SiLU output gate. Concatenating all heads gives the mixer output

[Tableau] $$z_{j}=W_{z}h_{j},\qquad y_{j}=W_{o}\operatorname{Concat}_{h}\left[\operatorname{RMSNorm}(o_{j,h})\odot\operatorname{SiLU}(z_{j,h})\right].$$ (14)

The learned projection $W_{z}$ produces $H_{v}d_{v}$ gate features, split into per-head vectors $z_{j,h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}$ ; $W_{o}$ maps the concatenated features back to width $D$ . The symbol $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication, and $y_{j}$ enters the mixer residual in ( 5 ). Every denoising pass reads the same committed recurrent and convolution caches. Once the block is resolved, causal replay recomputes its clean activations and advances these caches. Full-attention preview K/V are likewise temporary and become persistent only through the clean path.


#### 3.3 Block-aware expert routing

Parallel block prediction can spread token-level routing across many experts. Following dMoE [ 14 ] , we coordinate these choices by first selecting a shared expert pool for the block, then routing individual tokens within that pool (Figure 1 c). Each module contains 256 routed experts and one shared expert, all using SwiGLU [ 48 ] with intermediate width 512.

For the RMS-normalized dMoE input $f_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$ , a learned router $W_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{256\times D}$ produces probabilities $a_{je}=\operatorname{softmax}(W_{r}f_{j})_{e}$ over routed experts $e\in\{1,\ldots,256\}$ . We first identify each valid block token’s top-eight experts and sum their probabilities $a_{je}$ by expert across the block. Sorting these aggregate scores, we retain the smallest expert prefix covering a fraction $\tau$ of their total mass. This is the block’s expert coreset ; the released configuration uses $\tau=0.9$ . Each layer and denoising pass computes its own coreset. Invalid padding does not contribute to its scores.

We mask router logits outside the coreset and rerun token-level top-eight selection. Let $\mathcal{T}_{j}$ contain token $j$ ’s selected experts with nonzero weight, and let $p_{je}=a_{je}/\sum_{e^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{j}}a_{je^{\prime}}$ for $e\in\mathcal{T}_{j}$ . The module output is

[Tableau] $$\operatorname{dMoE}(f_{j})=g_{s}(f_{j})E_{s}(f_{j})+\sum_{e\in\mathcal{T}_{j}}p_{je}E_{e}(f_{j}),\qquad g_{s}(f_{j})=\sigma(w_{s}^{\top}f_{j}).$$ (15)

Here $E_{e}$ is routed expert $e$ and $E_{s}$ is the shared expert, each mapping width $D$ back to width $D$ ; $w_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$ is the shared gate’s learned projection. The shared expert is always evaluated and is outside the coreset selection. This block-level restriction encourages expert reuse across parallel predictions while retaining token-specific weights. Clean causal processing uses ordinary token-level routing, so its expert choices do not depend on later tokens.


#### 3.4 Training recipe

We extend Megatron-Core [ 34 ] and Megatron Bridge [ 37 ] to train the hybrid backbone with block diffusion. The implementation carries the same clean/noisy distinction through sequence packing, attention masks, recurrent state, expert routing, and distributed execution.


##### Two-stream training and causal isolation.

For each example $s$ , we construct a corrupted copy $x_{t}^{(s)}$ and a clean copy $x_{0}^{(s)}$ , where $t$ denotes the corruption level introduced in Section 2 . The copies share semantic token positions and are stored as $[x_{t}^{(1)}\mid x_{0}^{(1)}\mid x_{t}^{(2)}\mid x_{0}^{(2)}\mid\cdots]$ . Clean queries are token-causal. A noisy query can read clean tokens strictly before its block and noisy tokens within that block, but cannot read its clean target block or other noisy blocks. Explicit example, stream, and block identities enforce this visibility after packing. Padding inserted for alignment neither advances clean memory nor becomes a supervision target. For each microbatch, we uniformly sample the block size from $\{4,8,16,32\}$ and use the resulting boundaries consistently for attention, BD-GDN, right-context convolution, and expert routing.


##### Denoising and auxiliary causal supervision.

The denoising objective uses no-shift alignment: logits at position $i$ predict $x_{0}^{i}$ , with corruption, labels, and loss masks aligned to the same position. The auxiliary clean-stream objective uses shifted next-token prediction, where position $i$ predicts $x_{0}^{i+1}$ . For a sampled corruption, let $\widetilde{x}_{b}$ denote noisy block $b$ , and $c_{b}$ its clean prefix, including the prompt. Let $\mathcal{M}$ be the corrupted positions eligible for training and $\mathcal{T}$ the clean positions with a valid, supervised next-token target in the same example. The supported joint objective is

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BD}}$ $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}w(t_{i})[-\log p_{\theta}(x_{0}^{i}\mid\widetilde{x}_{b(i)},c_{b(i)})],$ (16) $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{AR}}$ $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}}[-\log p_{\theta}(x_{0}^{i+1}\mid x_{0}^{\leq i})],$ $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}$ $\displaystyle=\lambda_{\mathrm{BD}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BD}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{AR}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{AR}}.$

Here $\theta$ denotes model parameters and $p_{\theta}$ its predicted token distribution. The index $i$ runs over semantic token positions, and $b(i)$ identifies the block containing position $i$ . The noise level associated with position $i$ is $t_{i}$ , and $w(t_{i})$ is its configured weight; $w=1$ gives unweighted masked cross-entropy. The coefficients $\lambda_{\mathrm{BD}}$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{AR}}$ weight the two losses. An empty selection contributes zero. In the causal term, $i+1$ means the next real token, skipping alignment padding. Its target loss mask determines eligibility, allowing the last prompt token to predict the first assistant token. Both objectives share one backbone pass and one output projection over the union of selected positions, followed by one fused cross-entropy computation and separate reductions.


##### Long-context sparse attention.

The two streams double a 128K-token semantic sequence to approximately 256K stored positions. We describe permitted interactions with structured metadata derived from example boundaries, stream roles, semantic positions, and block identities. FlexAttention [ 12 ] uses this metadata to execute permitted attention tiles. The same identities guide BD-GDN, convolution, and expert routing and are reused during activation recomputation, keeping visibility consistent across the hybrid layers.


##### Parallelism for heterogeneous layers.

Context parallelism shards a sequence across devices, but the required communication differs by mixer. Full-attention layers keep local queries and gather and reorder K/V. BD-GDN supports either a Ulysses sequence-to-head transpose [ 25 ] or recurrent-state propagation using the context-parallel implementation in Flash Linear Attention (FLA) 1 1 https://github.com/fla-org/flash-linear-attention/blob/main/fla/ops/cp [ 59 ] , which we refer to as FLA-KCP. Ulysses redistributes projected features before convolution and restores the sequence-sharded layout before output projection; FLA-KCP preserves ordered state dependencies across sequence shards. Expert parallelism partitions routed experts across devices. Block metadata follows each layout transformation, and sampled noise and block identities are kept consistent across ranks and pipeline stages processing the same example.


#### 3.5 Decoding recipe


##### Expert routing.

We use the block-level expert coreset described in Section 3.3 for diffusion decoding or drafting, while causal verification retains token-level routing.


##### Confidence-aware decoding.

We decode the response block by block using a predict-and-commit strategy [ 56 , 23 , 8 , 57 ] . Given the committed prefix $x_{\leq t}$ , we append a block of $B$ mask tokens and predict all $B$ masked positions in parallel. We commit the predictions whose confidence exceeds a predefined threshold and remask the remaining positions, which can be refined in the next diffusion step. Once all tokens are decoded, we update the clean memory and move to the next block.


##### Self-speculative decoding.

Rather than committing tokens solely based on a fixed confidence threshold, we use the causal GDN to verify the draft block generated by a single diffusion step. We consider two acceptance rules: exact verification and entropy-based verification . For each draft token $d$ , let $p$ denote its corresponding causal verification distribution. Exact verification accepts $d$ only when it matches the causal encoder’s greedy prediction [ 26 , 15 , 7 ] , i.e., $d=\arg\max_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p(v)$ . In contrast, entropy-based verification adapts the acceptance threshold based on the uncertainty of prediction [ 21 , 5 ] . Specifically, let $H=-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p(v)\log p(v)$ denote the entropy of $p$ . The draft token is accepted if

[Tableau] $$p(d)\geq\delta\exp(-H),$$ (17)

where $\delta$ is a predefined hyperparameter. Under both rules, draft tokens are verified sequentially until the first rejection, and only the accepted prefix of the draft block is committed. All subsequent evaluations of Clover-35B-A3B-Preview use entropy-based self-speculative decoding.


##### Decoding penalties.

To mitigate repetition occasionally observed in diffusion decoding, we apply history-aware presence and repetition penalties to both confidence-aware and self-speculative decoding, with coefficients of $0.9$ and $1.05$ , respectively. We additionally use length-dependent positive logit biases for stop tokens and the </think> token in thinking mode to encourage response termination and the transition from reasoning to the final answer.

#### 3.7 Generation speed of self-speculative decoding


##### Evaluation setup and workloads.

We compare Clover-35B-A3B-Preview with Qwen3.6-35B-A3B accelerated by DFlash 2 [ 24 ] on a single NVIDIA H200 GPU. All experiments use BF16 precision, a batch size of one, a block size of 16 and greedy decoding. Clover uses entropy-based self-speculative decoding with $\delta=0.6$ , defined in Section 3.5 . We select three representative workloads for evaluation: MATH-500, agent replay, and daily chat.

The agent replay corpus contains 64 prefixes from historical trajectories generated by Qwen3.6-35B-A3B. We select one prefix per trajectory in sorted path order, alternating targets of 8K and 16K input tokens. Eligible prefixes end in a user message, contain 8K–24K historically recorded input tokens, and precede a historical response of at least 1,024 tokens. Thus, corpus construction is deterministic and targets long-context generation. At evaluation time, the model generates a new continuation; commands are not executed. Replay measures inference speed, not a complete agent rollout or task success. The daily chat prompts cover everyday and explanatory topics, including diffusion, sleep, memory, weather, batteries, refrigerators, the Internet, GPS, seasons, bread, music, language learning, habits, stars, plants, and photography. Each prompt requests a 1,200–1,600-word English response, without imposing a minimum token count.


##### Measurement definitions.

For request $i$ , let $C_{i}$ be the completion-token count, $F_{i}$ the token count in its first streamed event, $L_{i}$ its elapsed latency, $T_{i}$ its time to first token (TTFT), and $V_{i}$ its number of verification rounds. We report aggregate committed tokens per round and decode throughput as

[Tableau] $$A=\frac{\sum_{i}C_{i}}{\sum_{i}V_{i}},\qquad\mathrm{TPS}_{\mathrm{decode}}=\frac{\sum_{i}(C_{i}-F_{i})}{\sum_{i}(L_{i}-T_{i})}.$$ (18)

The accounting for $A$ includes bonus and boundary tokens and the first token emitted by prefill. Decode TPS excludes prefill and the wait before the first event, but includes thinking, answer text, and client-side streaming overhead. It is a ratio of cumulative tokens to cumulative time, not an average of per-request rates. We do not separately report TTFT against autoregressive decoding because Clover uses the same prefill computation; TTFT is therefore largely unchanged, and we focus on the decoding phase.

Table 2 shows that Clover sustains high committed-token yields across all three workloads, exceeding DFlash 2 in tokens committed per verification round. On MATH-500, however, its higher drafting cost offsets this advantage, resulting in lower decode throughput than DFlash 2. On agent replay and daily chat, Clover achieves both higher committed-token yields and higher throughput. We attribute this advantage to Clover’s drafting capability developing alongside the model throughout training. In contrast, methods such as DFlash train a separate lightweight drafter on target-generated responses over a selected prompt distribution, which may not cover the full range of workloads supported by the target. Expanding training data can improve coverage, but the drafter’s limited capacity may still constrain how well it captures the target’s behavior across workloads. These results highlight the importance of both draft cost and workload coverage when translating speculative progress into speed.

##### Routing-informed expert placement.

Expert placement strongly affects hybrid execution because CPU expert computation is slower and can dominate generation time. We use routing statistics from mathematical tasks to place frequently used experts on the GPU and less frequently used experts on the CPU, reducing CPU computation without changing the number of experts resident on either device. With top-eight verification, this improves decode throughput from 66.64 to 97.52 tokens/s, with 97.6% reviewed accuracy. Reducing verification to six active experts per token further increases throughput to 106.25 tokens/s at 96.0% accuracy, offering a faster operating point through approximate computation. These results highlight the value of matching expert placement to workload demand alongside decoding optimization.


##### Draft sparsity and acceptance.

We also tested a lower draft coreset threshold of $\tau=0.6$ . A smaller coreset is intended to reduce the number of experts used during drafting on both GPU and CPU. However, this setting also reduced the accepted draft length during verification, and the resulting throughput was lower than with $\tau=0.9$ . Thus, reducing draft computation alone does not necessarily improve speed: the saved work must be balanced against the number of tokens committed per verification round.

Workload-aware expert placement and self-speculative decoding enable fast Clover inference on a 16 GB consumer GPU. Expert placement, draft sparsity, and verification cost provide complementary controls for balancing generation speed and model quality.


### 5 Clover Decision Model

Human decision-making is often described in terms of fast, intuitive System 1 thinking and slower, deliberate System 2 reasoning. Drawing on this distinction, Jev, a commercial model from TypeSafe AI, targets fast, structured decisions that software can use directly [ 1 ] . This motivates the Clover decision model: we use block diffusion to produce related judgments jointly in a single denoising pass, combining fast response with the ability to share information across decisions.


#### 5.1 Joint decisions in one denoising step

The Clover decision model is a variant for fast, structured judgment tasks. Given shared material and a set of questions, it predicts up to eight answers together in a single denoising pass after context prefill. This variant is planned for a subsequent release.

Let $c$ denote the shared material and $q_{1},\ldots,q_{M}$ the associated questions, with $M\leq 8$ . We place these in the input and provide a fixed answer template in one diffusion block. Each question has one masked answer position, while the surrounding format is given. One denoising pass predicts all answer positions, and each prediction is read over that question’s candidate labels. Independent mode instead invokes the same model separately for each $(c,q_{j})$ .

All answer positions share the input and can exchange intermediate features within the block. This provides a way to use related evidence across decisions: for example, several judgments about a contract may depend on the same disclosure exception. The mechanism supports coordinated prediction, although a single pass does not guarantee that the resulting answers satisfy every constraint.

We compare joint and independent prediction using the same decision-model checkpoint on an internal set of 500 groups with 2,420 questions and a set of 50 partially completed $4\times 4$ Sudoku boards with 364 missing entries. Alongside accuracy over individual questions or cells, we report the fraction of groups or boards for which every answer is correct.

#### 5.3 Efficiency of joint prediction

Joint mode amortizes shared context processing and model invocation across questions while reading all answers in one denoising pass. Figure 5 compares it with sequential and parallel independent calls under the same hardware, checkpoint, and runtime configuration. With eight questions, mean group latency is 164 ms for joint prediction, compared with 1,243 ms for sequential independent calls and 249 ms for parallel independent calls, corresponding to speedups of $7.58\times$ and $1.52\times$ , respectively. The parallel comparison allows up to eight independent requests at once. Even when independent requests are batched, each retains its own context and answer block, whereas joint prediction processes the shared material once and places all answers in one block.

### 6 Conclusion

We presented Clover-35B-A3B-Preview, a 35B-total, 3B-active mixture-of-experts diffusion language model built around a bidirectional adaptation of Gated Delta Networks. Its architecture combines recurrent historical memory, selective interactions within the current block, learnable block-size calibration, and bidirectional local features. The benchmark results show a consistent lead across the evaluated agent metrics alongside competitive general capabilities, with opportunities for further improvement in knowledge and mathematics. Quantized deployment offers a further direction for studying this architecture under memory constraints. Our decision-model results additionally suggest that jointly generating related answers can improve the correctness of a complete set of judgments.

The next priorities are to further reduce latency, characterize the quality–latency trade-off under matched evaluation conditions, and strengthen quantized deployment. We are also extending Clover toward multimodal capabilities, informed by Dream-VL and Dream-VLA work on vision-language understanding and robotic action modeling [ 63 ] , and extending the context length.
