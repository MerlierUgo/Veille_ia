## Can LLM Agents Be Forward-Deployed Engineers? Requirement Discovery and Delivery for Enterprise Text-to-SQL

LLM coding agents can increasingly automate model post-training, but whether they can act as forward-deployed engineer (FDE) agents, discovering customer requirements and turning them into deployment decisions, remains unclear. We study this on a real enterprise text-to-SQL engagement: a simulated customer, grounded in the real use case’s requirements, starts with an ambiguous request. An agent must discover acceptance criteria, data permissions, and hardware constraints in the discovery phase, then plan and execute a post-training recipe in the delivery phase. Across 15 models, the strongest discover over 80% of withheld requirements and open-weight models discover 40-50%; in the delivery phase, only 25% of runs across eight coding-agent configurations pass all acceptance criteria. We identify four bottlenecks in this engagement: (1) incomplete discovery, with 40% of withheld requirements missing or misrepresented; (2) unreliable execution; (3) underused compute budgets and a lack of systematic comparisons across base models and post-training methods; and (4) benchmark accuracy does not guarantee production readiness. We suggest human review of discovered requirements, monitoring of the agent’s plan during execution, and an explicit experiment-environment specification covering hardware, budget scope, and production checks.


### 1 Introduction

Turning a capable language model into a useful enterprise system requires understanding customer workflows, identifying operational constraints, and making engineering decisions that connect model behavior to business needs. Forward-deployed engineers (FDEs) perform this work by embedding with customer teams and taking responsibility for discovery, technical design, implementation, and delivery ( Vartabedian and Chernova, 2025 ) . Pioneered at Palantir, the role combines hands-on engineering with the judgment needed to turn an initially incomplete business problem into a working solution.

Recent industry investment highlights the importance of this function. In May 2026, OpenAI announced its Deployment Company with more than $4 billion in initial investment to help organizations build and operate AI systems ( OpenAI, 2026d ) . In October 2026, Anthropic announced a $100 million commitment to train 10,000 Frontier Deployed Engineers by the end of 2027, emphasizing the expertise required to move AI “from an idea to a system in production ( Anthropic, 2026a ) .”

This motivates three research questions: RQ1: Can LLM FDE agents automate the forward deployment engineering work required to discover customer requirements and deliver a system that satisfies them? RQ2: What are the bottlenecks, and when does human intervention help? RQ3: Which practices improve delivery reliability and efficiency?

> Figure 1: A forward deployment agent: discovery, then delivery. In discovery , a discovery agent turns the ambiguous request of a customer simulated from a real engagement into identified requirements (1)–(4), establishes by experiment what the customer cannot answer (the SQL dialect), and gets sign-off. In delivery , a planner agent turns the identified requirements into an experiment plan and an execution agent runs it, profiling on the target hardware and evaluating on (1)–(4), returning model artifacts and an accuracy, profiling and cost report.

To investigate these questions, we study a production use case of forward-deployed engineering for enterprise text-to-SQL, grounded in a completed engagement: a human forward deployment engineer’s delivery of a commercial text-to-SQL system to an internal stakeholder, including its requirement discussions, experiment log, and shipped model. From this record, we annotate the customer’s stated requirements and the operational facts into standardized customer knowledge.

As shown in Figure 1 , our FDE agent delivers the solution in two phases: discovery and delivery. In the discovery phase, the simulated customer begins with an ambiguous request, and the FDE agent must ask follow-up questions to clarify the requirements. The simulated customer answers the FDE agent’s follow-up questions using annotated customer knowledge derived from the ground-truth engagement. Finally, the FDE agent consolidates the information gathered into a set of discovered requirements. In the delivery phase, the FDE agent can either plan and execute the solution end-to-end or separate planning from execution. It first takes the requirements gathered during discovery and the available data to produce a detailed plan covering model baselining, post-training, evaluation, profiling and checkpoint selection. Finally, it refines and executes the plan until the goal is achieved.

Our contribution is a deployment case study of FDE agents in a real enterprise text-to-SQL engagement that (1) uses annotated customer requirements to ground a customer simulator and the human FDE’s shipped system as a delivery baseline; (2) evaluates requirement discovery across 15 LLM agents from seven model families, measuring requirement discovery, elicitation through dialogue, and question cost; and (3) compares autonomous (end-to-end) and decoupled delivery workflows across four models, identifying bottlenecks, failure cases, and required human interventions.


### 2 Related Work


##### LLM agents as forward-deployed engineers.

Ding and Zhan (2026) compare agent and human-plus-assistant delivery of post-training services across ten oracle-scored stages from ticket to deployment under shared ground truth; FDE-Bench ( Ding et al., 2026 ) and DeployBench ( Wang et al., 2026 ) also evaluate deployment. All of these studies begin with gathered requirements indicated in written tickets. We begin before ticket creation: the agent defines acceptance criteria and operational constraints through dialogue and experiments to establish facts unknown to customers. Our reference is a completed enterprise engagement with a human trajectory and a model serving in production, rather than a synthetic scenario on public datasets. Our fact-sheet based discovery simulation design could be generalized into other enterprise use cases.


##### Agentic post-training and ML engineering.

A 2026 line of benchmarks asks whether agents can post-train models autonomously, including PostTrainBench ( Rank et al., 2026 ) , Agent 2 RL-Bench ( Chen et al., 2026 ) , FT-Dojo ( Li et al., 2026 ) , continuing earlier ML-engineering evaluations such as MLE-bench ( Chan et al., 2025 ) , MLAgentBench ( Huang et al., 2023 ) , and RE-Bench ( Wijk et al., 2024 ) . Each supplies a complete task specification and rewards raising a held-out metric. We treat post-training as one stage of a delivery whose acceptance criteria are the customer’s. On top of that, our work involves iterative requirement gathering, technical solution discovery given the practical constraint before autonomous post-training efforts.


##### Dialogue-driven and under-specified agent tasks.

AmbigSWE ( Vijayvargiya et al., 2026 ) evaluates clarifying questions over an incomplete specification; Dialogue-SWEBench ( King and Flanigan, 2026 ) removes the specification entirely and evaluates coding agents through dialogue with a persona-grounded, self-revising user simulator; $\tau$ -bench ( Yao et al., 2025 ) evaluates tool-use agents against simulated users under domain policies. We adopt user simulation but shift the focus from clarifying code changes to establishing deployment acceptance criteria and operational constraints, including those the customer cannot directly resolve and the agent must determine experimentally.


##### Enterprise text-to-SQL.

Spider, BIRD, and Spider 2.0 track Text-to-SQL progress ( Yu et al., 2018 ; Li et al., 2023 ; Lei et al., 2025 ) , with execution-grounded post-training driving recent gains ( Zhai et al., 2025 ; Yao et al., 2026 ) ; SOMA-SQL ( Somayajula et al., 2026 ) tackles query and schema-level ambiguity through synthetic logs and execution probing; PLSQLBench extends executable evaluation to Oracle PL/SQL ( Liu et al., 2026 ) . Deployment accuracy also depends on dialect, schema, prompt format, quantization, and serving path, with several effects documented in our reference engagement. We choose this domain for its concrete production constraints and executable evaluation.


### 3 Method

We study forward deployment as two phases: discovery, in which an FDE agent turns an incomplete customer brief into requirements, and delivery, in which the requirements are turned into a post-trained, served system. Both phases are grounded in a completed engagement.


##### Reference engagement.

Our reference is a four-month deployment of an Oracle-dialect text-to-SQL model by one human FDE to a production stakeholder inside a large enterprise, recorded as established requirements, 100+ LoRA adapters and 200 evaluations, and the shipped Ministral-3 8B system with a scaled standalone adapter and engineered prompt. We use this record to ground the customer’s knowledge and the deployment constraints and baseline the FDE agent’s performance.


##### Discovery phase.

Real customers rarely arrive with complete requirements. To mirror this, we annotate 17 core business, data, and evaluation requirements from the reference engagement, plus six decisions the customer had explicitly left open, such as the latency timing boundary (Appendix B ). An agent should elicit the 17 requirements and record the six decisions as open rather than guessing. This human-validated specification grounds the simulated customer and is the ground truth for discovery evaluation. The agent receives an ambiguous customer opening and must elicit requirements through dialogue; we test openings at low, moderate, and extreme ambiguity (Appendix A ).


##### Delivery.

The delivery agent receives the discovered requirements and the data, and must deliver a post-trained text-to-SQL system that meets them. We compare two workflows over these inputs. In the decoupled workflow, a planner agent produces a structured plan (base model, data processing, post-training, evaluation, profiling) that is validated and frozen before an execution agent carries it out. In the autonomous workflow, a single coding agent plans and executes within one session.


### 4 Experiments


#### 4.1 Experimental Setup


##### Discovery settings.

We test three customer-brief settings with low, moderate, and extreme ambiguity levels. Gemini 2.5 Pro ( Google DeepMind, 2025 ) plays the simulated customer with access to the fact sheet. The discovery agent runs in a simple agentic loop with four actions (ask, request an artifact, inspect, submit) and ends by submitting the discovered requirements. The discovery agent receives the brief and interacts with this customer using the protocol in Appendix A .


##### Delivery settings.

We evaluate the decoupled and autonomous workflows using OpenCode 1.18.4 ( OpenCode, 2025 ) under same resource and data constraints (Appendix E ). We additionally run one autonomous workflow with Codex CLI 0.144.6 ( OpenAI, 2025b ) to isolate harness effects.


##### Models.

For discovery, we evaluated fifteen models across seven families: GPT-5.4 Mini, GPT-5.6 Sol, GPT-6.1 Sol, GPT-OSS-120B ( OpenAI, 2026a ; OpenAI, 2026b ; OpenAI, 2026c ; OpenAI, 2025a ) ; Claude Opus 5, Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5, Fable 5 ( Anthropic, 2026d ; Anthropic, 2026c ; Anthropic, 2026e ; Anthropic, 2026b ) ; Grok 4.7 and Grok 4.20 (reasoning) ( xAI, 2026b ; xAI, 2026a ) ; Llama 3.3 70B ( Meta AI, 2024 ) ; Gemini 2.5 Flash and Gemma 4 31B ( Google DeepMind, 2025 ; Google DeepMind, 2026 ) ; Command A ( Cohere, 2025 ) ; GLM 5.3 Flash ( Z.ai, 2026 ) . We perform 10 independent discovery runs per model. For delivery, we evaluate GPT-5.6 Sol, GPT-6.1 Sol, Grok 4.7, and GLM 5.3 Flash in both decoupled and autonomous delivery.

##### Data.

Training data includes Oracle-dialect variants ( Oracle, 2025c ; Oracle, 2025a ; Oracle, 2025b ) of Spider, BIRD, and SParC ( Yu et al., 2018 ; Li et al., 2023 ; Yu et al., 2019 ) , with SParC flattened to question–SQL pairs. Spider-dev and BIRD-dev support evaluation and selection only. We also include 100 production questions obtained from the enterprise customer.


##### Discovery metrics.

We evaluate discovery using recovery rate, elicitation count, and ask turns. To compute them, Gemini 2.5 Flash first acts as an LLM judge ( Zheng et al., 2023 ) , labeling each withheld fact as consistently captured in the submitted requirements and as disclosed through customer dialogue, against the human-validated fact sheet. Recovery rate is the fraction of withheld facts correctly submitted; elicitation count is the number of withheld facts both disclosed in dialogue and correctly submitted; ask turns counts clarification actions, each of which may bundle several questions. A human audit of discovery results from 3 models agrees with the LLM judge on 70.6% of recovery and 82.4% of disclosure labels (Appendix C ), providing support for the annotations.


##### Delivery metrics.

We report execution accuracy (EX) on Spider-dev and BIRD-dev, production SQL compilation failures, and p90 inference latency on the production SQLs in the specified CPU-only environment. The targets are Spider-dev execution accuracy $\geq 60\%$ , $\leq 10\%$ compilation failures on production SQLs, and p90 latency $<15$ s. p90 latency is profiled over 5 independent runs. BIRD-dev accuracy is reported for reference only. Compilation measures SQL validity.

##### Frontier closed models recover most withheld requirements; open-weight models recover about half.

Claude Opus 5.5 and 5 lead recovery at 81.7% and 81.1%, while GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-5.6 Sol recover 75.2% and 68.4% of withheld facts. Closed models generally outperform open-weight models. Open-weight models recover 41.8–49.4% of withheld requirements, led by Gemma 4 31B at 49.4%, followed by GLM-5.3-Flash at 46.2%.


##### Targeted clarification matters more than a detailed customer opening.

We measure questioning by ask turns (agent turns requesting clarification) and elicited facts (requirements uncovered through dialogue). Models with over 70% recovery rate elicit 9–11 facts, while those below 50% average only 1.7–3.6 ask turns and 3.8–5.4 facts. Grok 4.20 Reasoning, with 1.8 ask turns and 5.1 facts, illustrates limited clarification.

A more detailed opening does not consistently improve recovery. Opus 5.5 performs best under moderate ambiguity (84.4%), versus low (77.1%) and extreme (83.5%). Across 14 consistently hidden facts, Claude models elicit 53.6% with the most detailed opening question versus 71.4% with the most ambiguous one, while other models remain near 30%. Questioning efficiency also varies: Opus 5.5 matches Opus 5 (81.7 vs 81.1) with 5.5 versus 9.0 ask turns, while GPT-6.1 Sol exceeds GPT-5.6 Sol by 6.8 points with 4.6 versus 7.1. Effective discovery depends on targeted clarification rather than more questions or a detailed opening question.


##### Discovery surfaces requirement gaps the customer never resolved, as an experienced human FDE would.

In the extreme-ambiguity GPT-5.6 Sol trajectory, the agent raised questions the customer had never specified: which Oracle-SQL operation defines a successful compilation, what database privileges the system needs, where the latency timer starts and stops, and which evaluation protocol is acceptable. The customer acknowledges the ambiguity, and the agent recorded them as undecided. This mirrors real engagements, where customers often cannot answer until they see results: the FDE records the ambiguity, proceeds with a best guess, and, if the delivered numbers disappoint, traces back to the open decision and realigns with the customer (see Appendix D ).


#### 4.3 Delivery Agent

Table 2 reports results from decoupled and autonomous delivery. We present the findings:


##### Only two of eight delivery runs meet all acceptance targets, and both perform worse than the human FDE.

Decoupled GPT-5.6 Sol (76.11% Spider, nine production failures, 11.24 s p90) and autonomous GPT-6.1 Sol (71.57%, six failures, 12.62 s) are the only two runs (25%) to satisfy the Spider, production compilation, and latency targets together, one in each workflow; the other six miss at least one target. Even the two that pass fall short of the human FDE’s shipped system, which reaches 79.90% Spider accuracy with six production failures: the best agent run is about four points behind on accuracy, and none matches the human on accuracy and production validity at once.


##### Passing the development benchmark does not mean passing production.

Production compilation is the most frequently missed target: four of eight runs exceed the failure limit, against two below the Spider target and two above the latency limit. Autonomous Grok 4.7 reaches 75.82% Spider and 44.29% BIRD, the best accuracy in the table, yet fails 13 of 100 production queries; autonomous GLM clears 67% Spider at 3s latency yet fails 23. Development sets and the production workload differ in schema, dialect idioms, and question style, so a recipe tuned to benchmark accuracy can still emit SQL the customer’s database rejects. When FDE agents perform model post training, it should consider production accuracy as an important dimension for the release decision.


##### Autonomous delivery improves several measured outcomes, but does not improve the overall pass rate.

For three of four models, autonomous delivery improves Spider and BIRD accuracy and CPU latency. Grok improves all four metrics; GPT-6.1 Sol clears the Spider target (55.90% to 71.57%); and GLM gains 11 Spider points while reducing latency from 14.51 s to 3.04 s. GPT-5.6 Sol is the exception, with decoupled delivery outperforming autonomy on every metric. These results suggest that adapting plans based on evaluation feedback can improve performance. However, production compilation failure rate does not consistently drop: GLM’s failures increase from 11 to 23.

##### OpenCode delivery exhibits limited reliability.

Across three independent runs per GPT-6.1 Sol workflow, only three (3/6 overall) completed the training and one (1/6 overall) met all acceptance criteria. See Appendix F for failure analysis.


##### A stronger coding harness helps with the delivery.

With model, budget, and evaluator fixed, Codex’s autonomous GPT-5.6 Sol run meets all three targets (Table 3 ), while OpenCode did not pass the targets due to the p90 latency failing at 16.2s, 1.2s slower than the target. The gain is from harness quantization choices, consistent with Codex’s greater maturity for its own models.


#### 4.4 Limitations of LLM FDE Agent

LLM FDE agent remains unreliable: only 25% of runs passed in Table 2 . Below, we discuss the failure patterns and mitigation strategies:

Requirement discovery is incomplete, and what is discovered is not always handed over. Across 15 models and 10 runs (Table 7 ), agents recover 4,227 of 7,050 withheld facts (60.0%), leaving 40% missing or misrepresented. The loss is not only in asking: of 4,122 facts the customer disclosed, 874 (21.2%) never reached the final document, and Grok 4.7’s extreme-ambiguity run recovers 82% of facts but omits the latency constraints, which is crucial to the key delivery success (Appendix C ).

Execution failures occur across multiple stages of the workflow. Failures include database connectivity issues, unsuccessful credential retrieval, data preprocessing errors, and incorrect training implementations (Appendix G ). These failures show that reliable autonomous execution is challenging.

GPU budget management is poorly coordinated across planning, execution, and evaluation. We observed two types of budget-management failures. First, the agent underutilizes the available budget: given 12 hours of GPU time for post-training, the planner schedules 10 hours of experiments, but the actual execution takes less than 5 hours. The remainder is left idle rather than spent on the comparisons (Appendix G.3 ). Second, the pipeline misinterprets the scope of the budget: in the GLM Decoupled experiments, the GPU runner applied the 12-hour post-training limit to final inference, causing the executor to skip BIRD evaluation after saving the checkpoint (Appendix G.1 ).

Experimental choices lack systematic comparative validation. Base models vary across runs without comparison, while all runs in Table 2 use LoRA SFT, never exploring GRPO despite available compute and execution-based rewards. Agents compare serving configurations (4-bit vs. BF16 bases, 8-bit vs. 16-bit adapters) to optimize CPU latency, but neglect comparable evaluation of base models and training methods, leaving their choices insufficiently justified.

Failure Mitigation. We also suggest human interventions below to further improve end to end pass rate: (1) Explicit environment setup. Providing all required credentials and connection details helps the agent to execute the planned workflow. Appendix G.2 shows a failure caused by missing credentials. (2) Human review of the requirements during discovery and plan during execution. Eliminating ambiguities in discovered requirements reduces misunderstandings in goals (Appendix G.1 ); and reviewing and refining the plan helps with a sound experimental design and realistic resource estimates (Appendix G.3 ).


### 5 Conclusion

We study whether LLM agents can act as forward-deployed engineers on a real enterprise text-to-SQL engagement, introducing an evaluation grounded in human-validated requirements and a shipped system. Across 15 models, the strongest recover over 80% of withheld requirements and open-weight models about half, but only 25% of delivery runs satisfy all three acceptance criteria. Four bottlenecks emerge: incomplete requirement discovery, unreliable autonomous execution, underused compute with limited comparisons, and a benchmark-to-production gap. We recommend human review of requirements and plan, and explicit specifications for hardware, budgets, and production checks.


### Limitations

The findings reflect a bounded enterprise case study and may not represent the diversity of customer needs, communication styles, or organizational contexts. Broader applications should consider whose requirements are represented and whether the resulting systems serve the interests of affected stakeholders.

### Appendix A Experimental Protocol


##### Information availability.

The annotation fact set contains 17 known core facts derived from the delivered engagements, comprising 15 hard requirements and two preferences. Six explicitly unresolved questions are excluded from recovery denominators. Low, moderate, and extreme openings withhold 14, 16, and 17 core facts, respectively. Low-ambiguity openings state accuracy and latency targets and a conditional CPU preference; moderate openings retain the CPU preference; extreme openings convey the broad task. Detailed measurement conditions remain subjects for clarification. These labels denote initial information availability, not an independently validated difficulty scale. All known facts remain available to the simulator throughout the dialogue. Candidate replies are checked against the private specification and revised when inconsistent, adapting the validation-and-revision approach of King and Flanigan (2026) .


##### Interaction and model settings.

Discovery allows up to 28 agent steps, including at most 15 clarification actions before submission. Agents can request and inspect released artifacts. Sampling uses model defaults, with temperature 1.0 explicitly set for Gemini. Reasoning is disabled where supported; GPT-OSS-120B, Grok 4.7, and Gemini use low reasoning, and Grok 4.20 Reasoning retains its reasoning mode. Llama and Command A use nonreasoning configurations. Llama 3.3 retains a fixed 2,048-token allowance. The thin planners request high reasoning where supported, and Codex planning and execution use maximum reasoning effort. These model-specific settings do not constitute a matched reasoning-compute budget.


##### Opening preparation.

The short-opening protocol uses explicit nested disclosure profiles (Table 4 ). Gemini 2.5 Pro generates a conversational opening from the broad engagement intent and the permitted core projections. A separate audit call, also using Gemini 2.5 Pro in the opening-preparation configuration, checks intent, required disclosure, leakage, unsupported claims, style, and length against the private ground truth. Preparation permits up to three audited drafts; a profile that does not pass is rejected. Accepted openings are frozen with fact and preparation-design fingerprints; Table 5 shows one sample opening per ambiguity level. The low profile permits three core projections, moderate permits one, and extreme permits none. Nested acceptance details remain for dialogue even when a broad target is initially disclosed. These levels manipulate initial specificity; they are not validated difficulty levels.

##### Customer loop.

On each ask action, Gemini 2.5 Pro receives all currently available customer knowledge, the customer–engineer dialogue history, and the latest question, and generates a candidate reply. Questions determine relevance, not which facts the model is allowed to know. Replies should answer known requested details naturally, preserve unknowns, avoid unrelated requirement dumps, and distinguish engineering proposals from customer agreement. There is no persona sampling. The customer has dialogue access only: it cannot run code, inspect repository files, evaluate a model, or claim engineering results. Files are offered by permitted identifiers and released separately through the artifact actions.

Gemini 2.5 Flash reviews the candidate for grounding and disclosure violations and annotates question-target and disclosed-fact IDs. These diagnostic labels do not filter the generation knowledge. The implementation also checks for private annotation markers and certain unavailable-answer/file-block disclosures. A flagged candidate receives one targeted rewrite by Gemini 2.5 Pro and one further review. A remaining blocking violation yields a fixed fallback with no newly credited fact disclosure. Incompleteness, concision, character, and immersion flags alone are non-blocking after revision; an accurate but incomplete reply is retained with its flags. Malformed factual-review output invalidates the trajectory rather than being shown as customer uncertainty. Invalid diagnostic labels receive one labels-only repair; failure of that repair also invalidates the trajectory. Only the delivered final reply is returned to the agent; drafts, review labels, generation-source metadata, and reviewer identities remain in researcher logs.


##### Agent loop and access boundary.

At conversation turn, the harness outputs one final action block: ask , request , inspect , or submit . The prompt requests exactly one final block; the parser selects the last action block and requires it to terminate the response. Questions outside the dispatched ask payload are not independently delivered. The limits are 28 outer turns and 15 initial ask actions, not 15 individual interrogative sentences; an ask action can bundle related questions. The current runner stops on submission and does not use the configured reserve-question or delivery-feedback hooks for a second discovery round.


##### Readback and recovery.

At submission, Gemini 2.5 Flash compares each logical submitted line against currently available customer facts. The program renders Confirm for supported comparisons with no unsupported substantive content; Correct when a line contradicts a known fact, returning the annotated answer and acceptance details; and Can’t Say otherwise. An explicit acknowledgment of an annotated unknown can be consistent, whereas inventing its value is unsupported. A contradiction anywhere in the submission removes that fact from pre-readback coverage, even if another line states it correctly. The loop ends without post-readback agent repair. The handoff records corrections as superseding corresponding submitted claims and preserves unresolved decisions.

Let $W$ be the known core facts withheld from the selected opening, $S$ the facts judged consistent in the submitted criteria before corrections and not contradicted elsewhere, and $A$ the facts first disclosed through an ask response. Recovery rate is $|W\cap S|/|W|$ , and the elicited-and-submitted count is $|W\cap S\cap A|$ . A correctly submitted fact without dialogue disclosure can receive recovery credit but not elicitation credit. Readback corrections are tracked separately and do not increase pre-readback recovery. Coverage concerns core fact IDs rather than exhaustive clause-level correctness.


##### Model settings.

Models are accessed through configured hosted inference interfaces, including remote hosted-model endpoints where applicable. The standard max new tokens bases are 2,048 tokens for the agent, 2,048 for customer generation/revision, and 8,192 for the judge. On detected truncation only, the standard ladders retry at 4,096/8,192/16,384, 4,096/8,192, and 16,384/32,768 respectively.

For trajectory $\tau$ , let $W$ contain the known facts omitted from its opening, $C$ the facts consistently represented in its submission, and $A$ the facts first disclosed in customer clarification replies. Facts contradicted elsewhere in the submission are excluded from $C$ . We compute

Ask turns count clarification actions, with multiple questions in one action counting once. Recovery measures core-fact coverage, not satisfaction of every clause in a compound requirement. Unknown facts receive no credit and are excluded from denominators; partial credit is not used. Failed or absent submissions remain unscored, and their counts accompany valid-submission means. A supplementary elicitation rate uses the 14 facts omitted at all three levels and models with valid submissions at every level. Gemini 2.5 Flash serves as both evaluator and one agent condition, which may introduce evaluator bias. ### Appendix D Discovery Case Study: Surfacing Unresolved Requirement Gaps

Recovery rate evaluates whether an agent captures known customer facts that were withheld from the opening. A discovery conversation can also provide value in a different way: it can identify consequential decisions for which the customer has no established answer. We examine this behavior in the extreme-ambiguity GPT-5.6 Sol trajectory. The evidence supports the narrower claim that the customer had not specified these decisions and did not resolve them during the conversation; it does not establish whether the customer had previously considered them.


##### Trajectory evidence.

Table 9 reproduces excerpts from the actual trajectory before the finalized requirements and interprets what each exchange establishes. In each case, the agent distinguished an unresolved decision from an established requirement instead of silently converting a proposed default into customer policy. Ellipses remove adjacent material without changing the quoted claim.

The finalized requirements contained eleven open-decision entries. Besides the four groups above, it identified unresolved input/output serialization, tokenizer choice, Oracle version and provisioning, privacy and residency constraints, production-data provenance, third-party-service approval, the final API contract, and production execution behavior. Some entries group multiple related questions, so eleven is a count of open-decision entries rather than a count of independent latent requirements.


##### Traceability into delivery.

The later delivery execution illustrates why preserving these gaps matters. To make progress, the study adopted explicit provisional rules: Oracle cursor-parse as the compilation check, a study-defined result-comparison policy, read-only admission, and an HTTP request boundary for latency. The latency profile timed the complete HTTP completion request but excluded model startup, precomputed tokenization, warm-up, and Oracle execution. It measured five complete passes over the 100-question production workload and obtained an all-500 nearest-rank p90 of 16.156 seconds. The delivery report did not present this operational choice as a resolved customer requirement: it reported the measured result under the operator-defined boundary while continuing to mark the customer timing boundary as unresolved. The same distinction was retained for compilation semantics, result equivalence, serialization, tokenizer choice, and read-only policy.

This provenance creates a useful path for later customer realignment. If an acceptance result changes after the customer selects a different compilation operation, timing boundary, or equivalence rule, the change can be attributed to a previously recorded open decision rather than mistaken for an unexplained model regression. The saved trajectory demonstrates this traceability, though it does not contain a subsequent customer decision or a completed realignment cycle.


##### Relation to recovery rate.

This behavior is deliberately outside the recovery-rate metric. The recovery rate counts only known facts in the withheld set $W$ that are represented consistently in the pre-readback submission. The six explicitly unresolved reference decisions are excluded from $W$ , and newly surfaced open questions do not earn recovery-rate credit. The case therefore illustrates a complementary dimension of discovery quality: whether the agent recognizes material underspecification, separates unknowns from requirements, and preserves the decisions needed for a reproducible downstream handoff.


### Appendix E Delivery Workflows


##### Starting inputs and separation of roles.

Our delivery agent implements decoupled and autonomous settings. Each condition reads its requirements from the finalized discovery handoff of the corresponding extreme-ambiguity trajectory: the submitted acceptance criteria, the customer’s open decisions, and the readback corrections, normalized and injected into the planner and executor prompts as the primary requirements. Each condition also receives the released artifact grants and resource constraints. Discovery annotations and private simulator/judge traces are not delivery inputs. A versioned manifest pins the source workspace inventories. Preparation verifies those inventories, preserves the released evidence, and creates a fresh workspace; preparation alone does not launch planning, training, database evaluation, or profiling. The selected plans retain their own provenance and research settings; the current planner’s default configuration must not be substituted for the configuration that produced a frozen plan.


##### Decoupled setting.

An OpenCode-based planner uses a controlled tool interface to inspect granted artifacts and produce a structured experiment plan. Its planning interface permits evidence inspection, draft construction, validation, and submission; it does not permit shell execution, model training, SQL execution, or performance measurement. The completed plans selected for delivery agent used static research inputs. The planning implementation also supports controlled live literature tools for new runs, but that capability does not imply that the selected plans used them. This setting uses the OpenCode planning baseline rather than the earlier thin action-loop planner.

The accepted plan is frozen before a fresh OpenCode execution session begins. The executor implements the plan, resolves prerequisites, and records repairs, deviations, and measured outcomes. Frozen training choices remain the starting prescription; bounded implementation changes must be documented so their effect on interpretation can be assessed. Planning and execution are separate sessions, and the executor does not inherit an earlier session’s implementation or results.


##### Autonomous setting.

A fresh OpenCode session first writes its own execution plan from the finalized discovery requirements, resources, and grants, then implements and revises that plan in the same session. No training recipe or training hyperparameters are supplied. The agent must select and justify its methods, hyperparameters, finite experiment strategy, evaluation rules, and cumulative budget, and record revisions and outcomes under its workspace’s work/ directory. Conditions remain independent; another condition’s implementation, checkpoints, or results are not inputs. We additionally conduct ablation experiment with same settings using Codex CLI.

Eligible checkpoints are the pinned revisions of meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and mistralai/Ministral-3-8B-Instruct-2512-BF16 , to ensure fair comparison with the human FDE delivered model. Decoupled executions follow their frozen plan’s selection; autonomous agents choose and justify their selection within the same eligibility constraints. A budget is a ceiling, not a requirement to use the full allocation.


##### Separate CPU profiling.

Profiling follows checkpoint export on a separate allocation with 96 CPU cores, 32 GiB RAM, zero GPUs, and exactly one profiling thread. The delivery agent execution policy permits dual AMD EPYC 9J14 or dual AMD EPYC 9J45 systems. Executors must verify actual allocation and instruction support, record the observed hardware, and use matched hardware for before/after comparisons; the two CPU models are not assumed to have identical performance. This execution-specific policy is distinct from the historical discovery hardware annotation. CPU profiling is outside the post-training GPU deadline.


##### Data permissions and interpretation.

Released artifact availability does not expand training permission. Development examples, reference labels, and per-example execution feedback may support authorized evaluation and model selection, but not supervised training, preference training, RL rewards, training-data synthesis, or quantization calibration. Executors retain split manifests and record the provenance of training and calibration data. Runtime Oracle connection material remains outside model prompts and logs. A frozen plan, successful input verification, or completed preparation is not evidence of measured accuracy, latency, or acceptance; such claims require the corresponding execution and evaluation artifacts.


##### Data and downstream evaluation.

Training resources include 8,644 Spider examples, 9,055 BIRD examples, and 8,039 optional SParC turns. SParC turns are flattened without conversation history or rewriting, so some questions remain context-dependent; this is a modified setup rather than evaluation on the original conversational task. Training inputs pair questions with schema DDL. Execution accuracy compares predicted and reference query results on the target database. The deployment compilation-error denominator retains all 100 questions, including invalid or missing model outputs. Benchmark latencies are reported separately from deployment-workload acceptance. Downstream comparisons require a common timing boundary, warm-up procedure, decoding configuration, and timeout policy, with measurements tied to the final served checkpoint.
