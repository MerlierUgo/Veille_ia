## Learning Video Foresight Reasoning from Unlabeled Videos via On-Policy Self-Distillation

Video foresight reasoning requires vision-language models to anticipate future events or next actions from currently observed videos. Existing methods fit future captions, rationales, or full visual states, but face two limitations. First, they rely on annotations or external large models for supervision, limiting scalability. We address this with an on-policy self-distillation framework that turns past–future video pairs into a natural self-supervised signal without extra annotations or LLM supervision. Second, they treat the whole future clip as equally valuable, although most of it is irrelevant context information. We address this by defining a foresight score , from which we obtain a contrastive privilege advantage that drives the model to learn the most valuable future information. Since the score is intractable, we optimize a tractable lower-bound surrogate that interpolates this advantage, computed between the true and a random reference future, with a teacher–student distillation advantage; together, they amplify decision-relevant content information and suppress irrelevant context information. We call the resulting framework Future-OPSD. On open-ended prediction and closed-ended planning, Future-OPSD improves base models by up to 14.2 and 15.9 points without compromising general video understanding. Code will be available at https://jingchensun.github.io/future-opsd/ .


### 1 Introduction

> Figure 1 : Not all of the future is equally important. To answer what will I do next? from what is seen now, only a small part of the future clip carries content information ( rinse the knife ); most of it is context information (tap, dish rack, sink, sponge) that neither answers the question nor reflects the expected action and trajectory of the ongoing event.

Video foresight reasoning aims to infer plausible future events or states from an observed video by reasoning over its visual dynamics, causal relations, and agent intentions. This ability has been studied through diverse task formulations, including activity forecasting and action anticipation [ 29 , 10 , 22 , 17 , 19 , 62 ] , trajectory forecasting [ 2 , 23 , 43 ] , future frame and representation prediction [ 54 , 39 , 14 ] , and video-language future event prediction [ 59 , 31 ] . With the advent of vision-language models (VLMs), recent work recasts foresight as high-level reasoning, with dedicated benchmarks [ 21 , 6 , 46 , 30 ] and reasoning-oriented training. Foresight underpins world models for planning and control [ 24 , 25 , 12 , 3 ] , and is essential for autonomous driving [ 26 ] , embodied robotics [ 14 , 12 , 3 ] , and proactive egocentric assistants [ 10 , 22 ] .

Recent VLM-based methods have begun to explicitly train foresight reasoning. Merlin [ 60 ] injects foresight through trajectory-based pre-training and instruction tuning. Next-Event Prediction [ 55 ] learns from captions of future segments and text-only LLM rationales, and further applies reinforcement learning with multiple-choice answers. Another line grounds reasoning in future visual states: TwiFF [ 35 ] reasons with explicit future frames, FUTURE-L1 [ 27 ] aligns latent reasoning with future-frame embeddings before answer-based reinforcement learning with an LLM judge, and IVT [ 65 ] adds next-embedding prediction to fine-tuning on annotated event descriptions.

Despite this progress, these methods share two limitations. First, their supervision relies on ground-truth answers, annotated descriptions, or external large models such as LLM rationale generators and judges [ 55 , 48 , 27 , 65 ] , which limits scalability. Second, they treat the future clip as a uniformly valuable target and fit its full textual or visual content [ 55 , 35 , 27 , 65 ] , yet future information is not equally useful for a given question. As shown in Fig. 1 , to predict what to do next, only rinse the knife is event-relevant content information, while most of the scene ( e.g . , tap, dish rack, sink) is irrelevant context information.

To address the first limitation, we adopt an on-policy self-distillation framework to turn naturally occurring past–future video pairs into a self-supervised learning signal. We split each video into a past clip and its future clip (the paired future ). The student sees only the past and generates a reasoning-and-answer response, while a frozen copy of the initial student that additionally sees the paired future acts as the teacher and scores each token of that response. As supervision comes from the video’s own future, this framework needs no annotations or external large models and thus scales well.

To address the second limitation, we define a foresight score to teach the model to identify the most valuable future information. It measures how well a student response matches the true future. Directly optimizing this score is intractable, as it averages over all possible futures. We therefore derive a tractable surrogate objective that provably lower-bounds the foresight score. The surrogate interpolates between a contrastive privilege advantage , which compares the teacher’s scores of the same response under the paired true future and an unrelated reference future, and a teacher–student distillation advantage toward the paired-future teacher. The joint interpolated objective suppresses shared context information and amplifies content information specific to the paired future ( Fig. 2 ). We refer to the framework combining these two designs as Future-OPSD.

We evaluate Future-OPSD on Qwen3-VL and Qwen3.5 across two video foresight tasks: open-ended prediction and closed-ended planning. Future-OPSD improves the foresight reasoning of the base models by up to 14.2 and 15.9 points, respectively, and consistently outperforms Naive OPSD, which does not optimize the foresight score ( Tabs. 1 and 2 ). The gains transfer across tasks and largely preserve general video understanding. Stronger teachers and more data bring further gains. Token-level analyses and discussions show that the interpolated objective suppresses context information and amplifies content information.

Our contributions are:

- • We propose Future-OPSD, which is, to our knowledge, the first on-policy self-distillation framework for video foresight reasoning. It turns naturally occurring past–future video pairs into a self-supervised signal, requiring no extra annotations.

- • We introduce a foresight score and derive its tractable lower bound as a token-level training objective, which enables the model to automatically identify and learn the most valuable future information.

- • Experiments on Qwen3-VL and Qwen3.5 show gains of up to 14.2 and 15.9 points on open-ended prediction and closed-ended planning, with strong cross-task transfer, no compromise in general video understanding, and gains that grow with stronger teachers and more data.


### 2 Related Work

Video Foresight Reasoning. Early studies on video foresight focus on action anticipation with recurrent and transformer models [ 54 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 38 ] , future event prediction in natural language [ 31 ] , and LLM-based long-term action planning [ 62 , 28 ] . With the rise of VLMs, benchmarks such as FutureBench, TwiFF-Bench, EgoPlan-Bench, and SEED-Bench-R1 evaluate future-event reasoning and next-action planning [ 55 , 35 , 8 , 7 ] , while CausalVQA, FutureOmni, and RIVER extend evaluation to physical, audio-visual, and streaming settings [ 15 , 6 , 46 ] . Existing training methods fall into three lines: (i) supervised learning of textual future targets and rationales [ 60 , 55 ] ; (ii) modeling explicit or latent future visual states, or predictive video representations [ 35 , 27 , 65 , 50 , 3 , 49 ] ; and (iii) reinforcement learning with answer-, event-, or generation-based rewards [ 55 , 48 , 9 ] . These methods rely on annotations or external large models for supervision, or fit the whole future clip uniformly. In contrast, Future-OPSD does not fit the future: it uses future clips as privileged evidence to score the student’s own responses, without annotations or an extra large LLM as a judge to provide supervision signals.

On-Policy Self-Distillation. Learning using privileged information trains a student with information available only during training [ 52 , 37 ] . On-policy distillation has a teacher score student-generated trajectories to reduce exposure bias [ 1 ] , and OPSD uses the same model, conditioned on privileged context, as its own teacher [ 63 ] . Multimodal variants use temporal, high-resolution, or localized visual evidence for video understanding [ 32 , 64 , 57 , 56 , 53 , 58 ] , and related ideas extend to world-action models and video generators [ 13 , 34 ] . Recent analyses show that privileged context can shift the teacher in task-irrelevant ways, termed privilege illusions [ 61 , 47 ] ; RLCSD contrasts textual hints within outcome-reward RL [ 40 ] . In contrast, Future-OPSD is, to our knowledge, the first OPSD framework for video foresight reasoning, and differs from prior contrastive distillation in two ways. (1) Data : it contrasts the true future clip of the same video with a future clip from an unrelated video, rather than textual hints [ 40 ] . (2) Objective : grounded in a foresight score and its KL-regularized lower bound ( Theorem 2 ), it interpolates contrastive and distillation advantages rather than relying on contrast alone.


### 3 Future-OPSD

We propose Future-OPSD, whose overview is shown in Fig. 2 . We first set up the problem ( Sec. 3.1 ) and define the foresight score ( Sec. 3.2 ). We then derive a tractable contrastive surrogate ( Sec. 3.3 ), control its gap with KL regularization ( Sec. 3.4 ), and turn the objective into token-level PPO updates ( Sec. 3.5 ).

#### 3.1 Problem Setup and Notation

For paired data $(X,Z)\sim p$ , $X$ denotes past frames and a question, and $Z$ the paired future, i.e . , the subsequent clip recorded in the same video. The student $\pi_{\theta}$ generates a reasoning-and-answer response $Y$ from $X$ alone; the frozen teacher $\pi_{0}$ scores it given $(X,Z)$ . Thus, $Y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid X)$ and $p_{\theta}(y\mid x,z)=\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ . This gives $Y\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\perp}Z\mid X$ and the Markov chain $Z\to X\to Y$ . We sample a reference future $\tilde{z}\sim p_{Z}$ from another video, independently of $(X,Z,Y)$ , where $p_{Z}$ is the future marginal. Training requires no answer or rationale labels. Inference uses only the student. We assume finite past, future, and response spaces and positive teacher probabilities for all allowed responses. Full assumptions and proofs appear in .


#### 3.2 Defining the Foresight Score

How to measure video foresight? A general prediction may be compatible with many futures. Inspired by InfoNCE [ 51 , 41 ] , we measure how specifically the paired future supports a response by comparing its teacher probability with the average under reference futures. We define the foresight score

[Tableau] $$\Phi(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,z)\sim p\\ y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\end{subarray}}\left[\log\frac{\pi_{0}(y\mid x,z)}{\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{z}\sim p_{Z}}\pi_{0}(y\mid x,\tilde{z})}\right].$$ (1)

Normalizing the teacher likelihoods defines a verifier distribution $q(z\mid x,y)\propto p_{Z}(z)\pi_{0}(y\mid x,z)$ . Since the teacher is frozen, $\theta$ affects the foresight score only through the student’s sampling distribution $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ : different parameters generate different responses, which receive different relative support. The score corresponds to the limit of normalized InfoNCE as the number of reference futures tends to infinity; the connection is detailed in .

Under the conditions in , a past-only student satisfies

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\Phi(\theta)$ $\displaystyle=I(X;Z)-D(\theta)\leq I(X;Z),$ (2) $\displaystyle D(\theta)$ $\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x,y}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p(\cdot\mid x)\,\middle\|\,q(\cdot\mid x,y)\right).$

Here, $D(\theta)$ is the average KL divergence from the true conditional future distribution $p(z\mid x)$ to the teacher-defined verifier $q(z\mid x,y)$ . Since $I(X;Z)$ is fixed by the data, maximizing $\Phi$ is equivalent to reducing this discrepancy. Training thus favors responses that better align the verifier with the true future distribution. The proof is provided in .

However, evaluating $\Phi$ requires the normalizer of $q$ , an expectation over the full future distribution that is generally intractable to compute exactly. We therefore introduce a surrogate that can be estimated from sampled reference futures and decomposes over tokens.


#### 3.3 Comparing Scores across Futures

How to optimize $\Phi(\theta)$ token by token? We compare the teacher’s log-probabilities of the same response under paired and reference futures. Their difference decomposes over tokens, giving the surrogate:

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,z,y,\tilde{z}}\left[\log\pi_{0}(y\mid x,z)-\log\pi_{0}(y\mid x,\tilde{z})\right].$$ (3)

Both teacher evaluations use the same past, response, and input format, so shared additive score components cancel. Log-probabilities add over tokens, enabling token-level supervision. However, the contrast includes an extra nonnegative Jensen gap:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta)$ $\displaystyle=\Phi(\theta)+\Gamma(\theta),$ (4) $\displaystyle\Gamma(\theta)$ $\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x,y}\big[\log\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{z}}\pi_{0}(y\mid x,\tilde{z})$ $\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{z}}\log\pi_{0}(y\mid x,\tilde{z})\big]\geq 0.$

The student may increase $\Gamma$ by favoring responses with larger score differences across reference futures, raising the contrastive score without improving foresight. We therefore regularize this objective.


#### 3.4 Controlling the Contrastive Gap

How to control the gap between $\mathcal{L}$ and $\Phi$ ? We encourage future discrimination while using a KL penalty to limit the student’s deviation from the paired-future teacher. The sequence-level penalty is

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{R}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,z}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\middle\|\,\pi_{0}(\cdot\mid x,z)\right).$$ (5)

We maximize the regularized objective

[Tableau] $$J_{\lambda}(\theta)=\lambda\mathcal{L}(\theta)-(1-\lambda)\mathcal{R}(\theta),\qquad\lambda\in[0,1].$$ (6)

For $0<\lambda<1$ , set $\beta=\lambda/(1-\lambda)>0$ . A change-of-measure inequality gives $\Gamma(\theta)\leq\mathcal{R}(\theta)/\beta+C_{\beta}$ (see for the derivation and proof). Substituting this into $\Phi=\mathcal{L}-\Gamma$ yields a lower bound.

Under the conditions in , fix $0<\lambda<1$ and $\beta=\lambda/(1-\lambda)$ . There is a finite constant $C_{\beta}$ , depending only on the data, frozen teacher, and $\beta$ , such that

[Tableau] $$\Phi(\theta)\geq\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\frac{\mathcal{R}(\theta)}{\beta}-C_{\beta}=\frac{J_{\lambda}(\theta)}{\lambda}-C_{\beta}.$$ (7)

For fixed $\lambda$ , maximizing $J_{\lambda}$ maximizes this lower bound. Since $C_{\beta}$ is independent of $\theta$ , training need not compute it. A higher bound need not imply a higher $\Phi$ .

The weight $\lambda$ balances the contrastive privilege and teacher–student distillation advantages. At $\lambda=0$ , the objective reduces to naive distillation toward the paired-future teacher; at $\lambda=1$ , it retains only the contrast without teacher regularization, which may lead to training collapse ( Fig. 5 ). We next derive token-level updates for this sequence objective.

#### 3.5 Updating the Student Policy

How to turn $J_{\lambda}$ into a practical update? We use immediate token advantages and clipped PPO updates. At each iteration, we fix a rollout policy $\pi_{\mathrm{old}}$ , sample responses, and draw one reference future per response. Let $h_{t}=(x,y_{<t})$ , $\ell_{t}^{u}=\log\pi_{0}(y_{t}\mid h_{t},u)$ for $u\in\{z,\tilde{z}\}$ , and $\ell_{t}^{S}=\log\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(y_{t}\mid h_{t})$ . We approximate the policy gradient by ignoring later rewards and using each token’s immediate reward under $\pi_{\mathrm{old}}$ as its advantage ( ):

[Tableau] $$a_{t}=\lambda\underbrace{(\ell_{t}^{z}-\ell_{t}^{\tilde{z}})}_{\text{contrastive privilege}}+(1-\lambda)\underbrace{(\ell_{t}^{z}-\ell_{t}^{S})}_{\text{teacher--student distillation}}.$$ (8)

One independent reference gives an unbiased estimate of the reference-averaged contrast before clipping. The two terms provide the contrastive privilege advantage and the teacher–student distillation advantage, respectively.

We clip advantages to $[-c,c]$ and center them over the set $\mathcal{T}$ of valid batch-token pairs $(i,t)$ , where $i$ indexes responses:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\bar{a}$ $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{(i,t)\in\mathcal{T}}\clip(a_{i,t},-c,c),$ (9) $\displaystyle A_{i,t}$ $\displaystyle=\clip(a_{i,t},-c,c)-\bar{a}.$

Let $\rho_{i,t}=\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid h_{i,t})/\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid h_{i,t})$ , with $h_{i,t}=(x_{i},y_{i,<t})$ , and $\bar{\rho}_{i,t}=\clip(\rho_{i,t},1-\epsilon_{l},1+\epsilon_{h})$ . Holding $A_{i,t}$ fixed, we minimize the token-averaged PPO loss [ 44 ] :

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{(i,t)\in\mathcal{T}}\min\!\big(\rho_{i,t}A_{i,t},\bar{\rho}_{i,t}A_{i,t}\big).$$ (10)

Here, $\epsilon_{l}$ and $\epsilon_{h}$ set the clipping range. Only the student is updated; the teacher, rollout policy, and advantages remain fixed. Since $a_{t}$ includes the KL penalty, no separate KL loss is needed. The algorithm flowchart and pseudocode are provided in .


### 4 Experiments

We organize our experiments around three research questions (RQs) and three discussions (DSs). RQ1: Does Future-OPSD improve VLMs’ video foresight reasoning? RQ2: Do the performance gains persist with stronger teachers or more training data? RQ3: How does Future-OPSD work? DS1: How does Future-OPSD compare with GRPO-based methods? DS2: Which design choices in Future-OPSD matter? DS3: What is the computational cost?


#### 4.1 Main Results (RQ1)


##### Models and datasets.

We use two open VLM families, Qwen3-VL [ 4 ] and Qwen3.5 [ 42 ] , with Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct and Qwen3.5-9B as the students in RQ1. Both use reason-then-answer prompting, with native thinking disabled for Qwen3.5-9B. We consider two video foresight tasks: (a) Open-ended prediction : we train on Future-L1 [ 27 ] (50K clips, one open-ended question per clip) and evaluate in-domain on TwiFF-Bench [ 35 ] (1,078 questions). (b) Closed-ended planning : we train on EgoPlan-IT [ 8 ] (43,294 four-way next-action questions on 30.5K progress clips from 610 EPIC-KITCHENS-100 videos [ 11 ] ) and evaluate in-domain on SEED-Bench-R1 [ 7 ] (L1–L3).


##### Training and evaluation.

We fine-tune all student parameters for 100 steps (256 queries, one rollout each), with learning rate $10^{-6}$ , temperature $1.0$ , $c=5$ , $\lambda=0.75$ , and no separate KL loss ( Sec. 3.4 ). Teachers remain frozen at initialization and receive up to three future frames; reference futures use the same format from other videos. Following Future-L1 [ 27 ] , a Qwen3.6-27B judge scores open-ended reasoning and answers separately on a 1–5 scale, rescaled to 0–100; planning uses A–D accuracy (%). We also report accuracy (%) on TempCompass [ 36 ] , TemporalBench [ 5 ] , Video-MME [ 16 ] , and MVBench [ 33 ] under standard settings.


##### Baselines.

(i) Base : pretrained models without task-specific fine-tuning. (ii) SFT [ 55 ] : cross-entropy prediction of future states and answers from current observations, using textual targets generated from future frames by a stronger external model. (iii) Naive OPSD [ 63 ] : token-level distillation from a frozen teacher with future context to a past-only student, without future contrast. (iv) TTRL [ 66 ] : label-free RL with majority-vote pseudo-rewards from eight rollouts (closed-ended planning only).

Future-OPSD improves video foresight reasoning in both open-ended prediction and closed-ended planning while preserving general video understanding. Table 1 evaluates Qwen3-VL-8B and Qwen3.5-9B trained on Future-L1, using TwiFF-Bench for open-ended prediction. Table 2 uses the same students trained on EgoPlan-IT, with SEED-Bench-R1 for closed-ended planning. Both show consistent gains over Base and Naive OPSD without answer labels, spanning reasoning and answer quality as well as planning across all three generalization levels. The gains also transfer across foresight tasks while retaining general video understanding.

#### 4.4 Comparison with GRPO (DS1)

GRPO is less effective than Future-OPSD for open-ended prediction. We compare Qwen3-VL (2B/8B) and Qwen3.5 (2B/9B) trained on Future-L1. GRPO [ 45 ] runs for 100 steps with eight rollouts per query, using the student’s frozen base model as its reward judge. It underperforms on both in-domain TwiFF-Bench and cross-domain SEED-Bench-R1 ( Tab. 4 ). We see two possible causes: (1) open-ended answers lack verifiable rewards, and the LLM judge cannot provide accurate reward signals; (2) even with accurate rewards, reinforcing only the student’s own rollouts may not yield cross-domain generalization.


#### 4.5 Which Design Matters? (DS2)

Images outperform text. On Future-L1, $z=\text{text}$ comprises descriptions generated by Qwen3.5-27B from the frames used as $z=\text{image}$ . Both methods achieve higher scores with images ( Tab. 5 ). A possible explanation is that captions compress the future before teacher scoring, whereas frames let the teacher select event evidence conditioned on the prediction question. The Qwen3-VL-8B image advantage is consistent with retaining cues omitted by question-agnostic captions, although the score comparison alone does not establish this mechanism.

Our search identifies $\lambda=0.75$ as the best overall setting. We sweep $\lambda\in\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\}$ for both students on EgoPlan-IT and Future-L1 ( Fig. 5 ). Combining contrastive and distillation signals improves performance, while pure contrast ( $\lambda=1$ ) collapses training on Future-L1. This is consistent with Sec. 3.4 : at $\lambda=1$ , the KL anchor to the frozen future-conditioned teacher disappears, allowing policy drift and a larger surrogate gap without improved foresight.

A frozen teacher and a trainable vision encoder work best. #### 4.6 What Is the Computational Cost? (DS3)

Future-OPSD adds only constant-factor overhead to Naive OPSD. Let $U$ denote student forward/backward optimization, $R$ rollout generation and shared policy scoring, and $F$ one frozen-teacher forward pass. For matched update budgets, SFT costs $U$ , Naive OPSD $R+U+F$ , and Future-OPSD $R+U+2F$ per query. The additional scoring pass reuses the teacher without extra generation, backpropagation, or optimizer states, preserving asymptotic complexity. ### 5 Conclusion

Existing methods for video foresight reasoning depend on annotations or external large models, and learn from the entire future clip even though not all of it is equally important. To address both limitations, we proposed Future-OPSD. It adopts on-policy self-distillation to turn naturally occurring past–future video pairs into a self-supervised signal, and optimizes a tractable lower bound of a foresight score that interpolates a contrastive privilege advantage with a teacher–student distillation advantage, helping the student focus on the most valuable future information. Experiments on Qwen3-VL and Qwen3.5 show that Future-OPSD improves video foresight reasoning without compromising general video understanding, and scales with stronger teachers and more data. Token-level analyses confirm that it amplifies content information and suppresses context information. Although our conclusions are limited to the datasets and models studied here, we believe our findings provide useful guidance for improving VLM foresight reasoning.
