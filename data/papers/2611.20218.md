## SWE-CoWork: Benchmarking Coding Agents as Digital Employees in Organizations

A coding agent working within a software organization must establish what to build, collaborate with colleagues, and verify what it delivers. We introduce SWE-CoWork , a benchmark comprising 101 development assignments across 101 real repositories, 11 programming languages, and 12 application domains. Each assignment places an agent in a simulated engineering organization with 26 colleagues who have distinct responsibilities, bounded knowledge, and controlled collaboration behavior. Starting from a brief handoff, agents must recover requirements distributed across colleagues and workplace records, reconcile conflicting evidence, and implement changes evaluated by private executable tests.

We evaluate eleven models and find a substantial gap between partial implementation and complete task delivery. The two leading models satisfy approximately 63% of fail-to-pass (F2P) tests but complete fewer than 8% of assignments. Providing complete requirements upfront improves F2P by approximately 16 percentage points for Seed Evolving and GPT-6 Astra, yet leaves complete task success unchanged. Requirement-level trajectory analysis reveals failures throughout the delivery process: Claude-Opus-5.5 directs a mean of 93% of first questions about queried cards to their accountable owners across runs but obtains only 64% of colleague-held requirements. Deferred questions go unfollowed, implementations remain incomplete even after requirements are acquired, and 31% of requirements reported as completed fail their tests.

These findings highlight sustained requirement tracking, from clarification through implementation, verification, and handoff, as a key challenge for reliable autonomous software engineering.


### 1 Introduction

Real-world software development is inherently a collaborative human endeavor rather than an isolated coding exercise. On one hand, requirements originate from human stakeholders and evolve continuously: as intermediate milestones are reached or runtime traces surface, engineers and clients routinely refine their assumptions and introduce follow-up demands. On the other hand, professional software engineering cannot be accomplished from a single static specification alone; it requires close coordination across diverse roles, where asking the right clarifying questions is indispensable ( Ko et al., 2007 ; LaToza et al., 2006 ; Sillito et al., 2008 ; Xia et al., 2018 ) . Throughout this process, developers must navigate a sprawling web of project boards, versioned design documents, chat logs, merge requests, and CI histories—artifacts that frequently disagree with one another. A design document may describe an approach that has since been superseded; a passing CI run may correspond to an outdated revision; a colleague may know a local compatibility constraint without knowing which downstream component depends on it; and a trace uncovered during implementation may invalidate an initial diagnosis. For decades, software engineers have borne the heavy cognitive burden of checking evidence provenance, reconciling conflicting accounts, and aligning shifting requirements across the organization.

Recent advances in large language model (LLM) coding agents have achieved remarkable success on software engineering tasks, driving standard benchmarks from single-digit scores to near saturation within two years ( Jimenez et al., 2024 ; The Anthropic Institute, 2026 ) . In frontier industrial workflows, AI now accounts for the vast majority of code production: Anthropic reports that as of May 2026, over 80% of the code merged into its internal codebase was authored by Claude; in the second quarter of 2026, the typical engineer merged $8\times$ as many lines of code per day as in 2024 ( The Anthropic Institute, 2026 ) . Yet scaling raw code generation does not resolve the broader organizational challenges of software delivery. Existing coding benchmarks ( Jimenez et al., 2024 ; Huang et al., 2026 ; Raghavendra et al., 2026 ; Kondra et al., 2026 ; Cursor, 2026 ; Das et al., 2026 ) typically evaluate isolated, pre-scoped task descriptions. Studies of underspecification vary the information in a fixed initial request ( Kim et al., 2026 ) or let agents query a designated simulated user who holds the missing requirements ( Pu et al., 2026 ; Edwards and Schuster, 2026 ) . Consequently, today’s coding agents largely function as passive “spec executors” : they accelerate low-level implementation, but still require humans to curate organizational context, resolve conflicting historical records, and translate evolving stakeholder needs into self-contained specifications.

To further reduce human involvement in low-level execution and elevate human engineers to high-level direction setting, prioritization, and final acceptance, coding agents must evolve from passive “spec executors” into true digital software engineers possessing four core organizational capabilities:

- • Continuous: Interacting with colleagues across familiar workplace interfaces (e.g., IM chat, project boards, and code reviews) while maintaining coherent context across multiple sources and evolving revisions—such as adapting execution when an early draft proposal is superseded in a later document version or when new runtime evidence arrives mid-implementation.

- • Proactive: Operating without step-by-step human prompting from a brief handoff (e.g., “finish the 2026.09 upgrade; the previous task owner is on leave”), actively identifying and consulting accountable owners with concrete code context, while exercising restraint against scope distractors such as canceled cards or neighboring projects.

- • Efficient: Communicating with colleagues autonomously within a bounded working budget and advancing multiple complex requirements in parallel—continuing productive work on other modules rather than blocking when a colleague requests a reproduction or defers an answer.

- • Persistent: Remaining embedded in a realistic workplace environment over long execution horizons and context compactions, continuously tracking the state of every requirement from initial clarification through implementation, verification, and an accurate final handoff report.

Because current evaluation paradigms lack tasks that measure these organizational capabilities end to end, we introduce SWE-CoWork to benchmark coding agents as digital employees inside software organizations. Each SWE-CoWork task combines a real open-source repository with an authored workplace populated by 26 digital employees —rule-governed, LLM-backed models of colleagues with distinct responsibilities, bounded local knowledge, and controlled collaboration behavior (e.g., disclosing a held requirement only when given sufficient code context, requesting a reproduction, or referring the agent to another owner), yielding 2,626 employee profiles across the benchmark. Figure 1 contrasts a single Click requirement presented directly as an issue against the same requirement embedded within an ongoing SWE-CoWork assignment, while a typical full assignment distributes requirements across a median of six accountable owners (such as module maintainers, compatibility liaisons, and release coordinators). In total, SWE-CoWork comprises 101 cumulative development assignments across 101 upstream repositories, 11 languages, and 12 application domains, where a median task adds 2,955 production lines across 60 files and is graded by 32 executable verifier test nodes . Starting from a short onboarding note, a frozen repository, and a workplace containing project boards, versioned documents, merge-request histories, CI records, and 40,836 chat messages across the benchmark (Figure 4 ), the agent must inspect records, consult colleagues, reconcile conflicting evidence, implement and verify changes, and deliver a final handoff document.

Each scored requirement is linked to an accountable owner, a discovery route through workplace records or colleague interactions, and private executable F2P/P2P tests. This linkage allows us to trace requirements from initial discovery to verified delivery. We further introduce a paired Oracle-Spec condition that provides complete requirements upfront while holding the repository, tests, scaffold, and execution budget constant. Together with agent trajectories, this comparison helps distinguish failures in requirement acquisition from those that persist during implementation, verification, and handoff.

We evaluate eleven models on 101 tasks and report F2P, P2P, and complete task pass rates (Table 2 ). Claude-Opus-5.5 and GPT-6 Astra pass 63.84% and 62.91% of F2P test nodes but complete only 6.93% and 7.92% of tasks; the remaining nine models reach at most 39.09% F2P and 0.99% complete task success. Supplying complete requirements upfront in Oracle-Spec raises pooled F2P from 39.09% to 55.76% for Seed Evolving and from 62.91% to 78.84% for GPT-6 Astra (Table 3 ), yet complete task pass rates remain unchanged (0.99% and 7.92%). Requirement-level trajectory analysis reveals why: weaker models remain trapped in passive spec-executor behavior (e.g., DeepSeek V4 Pro sends a median of 0 colleague messages per run and obtains only 1.8% of colleague-held requirements), whereas frontier models find the right people but fail at persistent requirement tracking. Specifically, Claude-Opus-5.5 directs 93% of first questions to the accountable owner and obtains 64% of colleague-held requirements, with only 3% belonging to uncontacted owners. However, failures persist after initial contact: relevant cards go unraised with already-contacted owners, deferred questions go unfollowed (153 of 450 deferred threads are abandoned with a median of 7.1 hours of budget remaining), disclosed requirements still account for 56% of failed linked tests, and 31% of requirements reported as “done” in final handoffs fail their tests.

In summary, we make the following contributions:

- • The first benchmark for organizational software engineering intelligence. SWE-CoWork provides 101 development assignments across 101 repositories, 11 languages, and 12 domains, shifting evaluation from isolated spec execution to autonomous collaboration inside software organizations. In each assignment, requirements are distributed across rule-governed colleagues with bounded responsibilities and evolving project records, with every requirement tied to a fixed F2P/P2P test contract.

- • A paired evaluation of organizational overhead and implementation bottlenecks. The CoWork and Oracle-Spec conditions share the repository, tests, scaffold, and execution budget, allowing us to jointly examine the organizational overhead of establishing requirements through colleagues and records alongside downstream implementation bottlenecks. We report this comparison for Seed Evolving and GPT-6 Astra together with CoWork results across eleven models.

- • A requirement-level account of where sustained tracking fails. By tracing each requirement across colleague conversations, code changes, verification, and handoff notes to individual tests, we show that weaker models struggle to proactively obtain requirements from the organization. Frontier models such as Claude-Opus-5.5 reliably reach the right owners, yet fail at sustained requirement tracking at later stages—through unraised cards, abandoned deferrals, unsatisfied disclosed requirements, premature stops, and completion claims contradicted by tests.

Recent software engineering benchmarks evaluate a growing range of development activities in realistic repositories. DeepSWE ( Huang et al., 2026 ; Datacurve, 2026 ) evaluates original tasks in real repositories; SWE Atlas ( Raghavendra et al., 2026 ) covers codebase questions, test writing, and refactoring; FrontierSWE v2 ( Kondra et al., 2026 ) examines implementation, optimization, and research tasks under long interaction budgets; CursorBench 4.0 ( Cursor, 2026 ; Jain, 2026 ) derives tasks from real developer activity; and Real-SWE ( Das et al., 2026 ) extends evaluation to private production codebases and business tooling. These benchmarks expand the scope, complexity, and execution horizon of coding tasks, but generally provide agents with an identified development objective. In SWE-CoWork , agents instead join an ongoing project with a brief handoff and must establish its current requirements from organizational records, colleagues, and repository history before implementing and verifying the changes.

Several benchmarks investigate how coding agents handle incomplete or ambiguous requirements. RealSWE ( Kim et al., 2026 ) varies the information provided for a fixed task, while LHAW ( Pu et al., 2026 ) systematically removes goals, constraints, inputs, and contextual information. Ask or Assume ( Edwards and Schuster, 2026 ) and Dialogue SWE-Bench ( King and Flanigan, 2026 ) introduce interactive users who answer clarification questions, and ICAE-Bench ( Peng et al., 2026 ) grounds user responses in withheld product constraints. $\tau^{\tau}$ -Bench ( Shi et al., 2026 ) requires developer agents to recover requirements through business artifacts and client interactions. These works demonstrate the importance of information acquisition, typically within a task whose scope is predefined or whose missing requirements can be clarified through a designated user or client.

SWE-CoWork examines requirement recovery within a broader engineering organization. Requirements are distributed among multiple colleagues with distinct responsibilities and bounded knowledge, as well as project boards, versioned documents, conversations, reviews, and execution records. Agents must identify the appropriate owners, ask questions with sufficient context, follow up on deferred answers, and reconcile information from different sources. Historical records may contain superseded decisions or observations tied to earlier revisions, while new evidence encountered during implementation may require revisiting an existing solution. These interactions require agents to establish both the content and the authority of requirements as the work progresses.

Workplace-agent benchmarks evaluate professional tasks involving organizational records, applications, and collaboration with colleagues. TheAgentCompany ( Xu et al., 2025 ) places agents in a simulated company with coworkers and workplace services to evaluate their ability to complete consequential professional tasks. Recent system efforts also explore more autonomous software development workflows. DeepSeek’s Super-agent prototype ( DeepSeek AI, 2026a ) , for example, connects coding agents to GitHub and team messaging, using event-driven task dispatch and dedicated execution sessions to support ongoing engineering work.

SWE-CoWork provides an evaluation environment for coding agents operating within such organizational contexts. Each task combines a real software repository with an authored engineering workplace populated by digital employees whose knowledge and responsibilities are grounded in the project. Agents must collaborate with these employees, recover relevant decisions from organizational records, and implement the required changes. Each requirement is linked to a fixed executable test contract, allowing evaluation of both requirement acquisition and verified delivery.

The reliability of simulated colleagues is also important for interactive evaluation. Evaluation outcomes can depend on the user simulator, and simulated interactions may differ systematically from interactions with human users ( Seshadri et al., 2026 ) . In SWE-CoWork , fixed knowledge boundaries and disclosure rules constrain colleague interactions across runs (Section 3.1 ).

Prior work also investigates the processes through which coding agents complete engineering tasks. SWE-RPG ( Zhou et al., 2026 ) provides representations for studying planning and clarification in software agent trajectories, while $\tau$ -bench ( Yao et al., 2024 ) examines reliability across repeated interactive executions. Coding-agent frameworks such as SWE-agent, OpenHands, and Agentless ( Yang et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2025 ; Xia et al., 2024 ) further demonstrate the influence of agent scaffolding on execution behavior and task outcomes.

SWE-CoWork enables requirement-level analysis of the full engineering workflow. By linking individual requirements to colleague interactions, workplace evidence, executable tests, and final delivery reports, we can identify where requirements are lost between discovery, clarification, implementation, verification, and handoff. This distinguishes failures to acquire necessary information from failures to implement requirements that the agent has already obtained, and reveals discrepancies between reported completion and verified outcomes.

### 3 Engineering Organizations of Digital Employees

Each SWE-CoWork task places a coding agent in an engineering organization consisting of a real software repository, a shared workplace, and a population of digital employees. Starting from a brief handoff, the agent establishes requirements through workplace records and interactions with colleagues, then implements, verifies, and delivers the requested changes. To evaluate this process, each task defines a fixed executable contract through fail-to-pass (F2P) and pass-to-pass (P2P) tests. For every scored requirement, the design connects the colleague or record that holds it, the responsibility boundary that determines who can answer for it, and the test nodes that check it. These links let us trace a requirement from the first question about it through the delivered code to the final handoff note.


#### 3.1 Digital Employees

We construct an organization of 26 modeled colleagues around each repository, yielding 2,626 employee profiles across the 101 tasks. Each digital employee represents a named employee with a defined role, responsibilities, local knowledge, and rules governing interactions with the agent, with responsibilities and knowledge grounded in the repository’s files, declarations, and modules. The organization spans one target project and four neighboring projects that provide additional context; within this population, a median of six employees hold requirements that determine the task’s executable outcome (Table 6 ).

Each digital employee has three properties: responsibility, local knowledge, and collaboration behavior. Responsibility identifies the employee accountable for a module or decision. Local knowledge defines which requirements the employee can explain and which questions require referral to another colleague. Collaboration behavior governs how the employee responds. An owner may request a reproduction or caller path, address one topic at a time, or defer an answer until the agent follows up. The environment controls which information can be disclosed at each step, while a language model generates the employee’s response.

The digital employees share project records that connect people to engineering work. Board cards identify owners, documents record decisions, and chat and review threads preserve earlier discussions. Information obtained from one employee may need to be checked against another employee’s constraints or a more recent repository revision, requiring the agent to identify appropriate owners and resolve outstanding questions as implementation proceeds.

The agent interacts with the organization through a dedicated tool interface. It can search the staff directory, inspect the project board, open versioned documents, search and read chat, message colleagues, inspect merge requests and CI records, and check for new activity. Digital employees are accessible through the same workplace that contains their decisions and work history.

The agent joins an engineering assignment already in progress. It begins with a short onboarding note that states the development objective and identifies workplace resources relevant to the current project state. The note specifies restrictions on modifying existing tests, excludes neighboring projects from the assignment, and identifies historical terminology that may differ from the current repository. The remaining acceptance criteria are distributed across workplace records and colleagues.

The agent decides which records to inspect, which references to follow, and which colleagues to contact. It interprets the available evidence against the current repository state and resolves questions that affect the implementation. At completion, it produces a delivery note describing the requirements established, the changes made, the validation performed, and any unresolved questions.

SWE-CoWork contains 101 tasks drawn from 101 upstream repositories across 11 programming languages and 12 application domains, including web frameworks, compilers, games, payments, and scientific software (Figure 2 ; Appendix C ). 11 tasks involve substantive changes in more than one programming language.

The reference solutions involve substantial engineering work. For each task, we maintain a complete Oracle-Spec document that enumerates the requirements encoded by the same executable contract used to evaluate the workplace assignment. Across the 101 tasks, these contracts contain 2,699 F2P test nodes and 562 P2P test nodes, giving 3,261 executable checks in total.


#### 3.3 Shared Records and Organizational History

Each assignment is situated within the records of a wider engineering organization. Across the benchmark release, these organizations contain 10,329 board cards, 5,097 versioned documents, 40,836 chat messages, 1,194 merge requests, and 2,626 colleague profiles. Whereas the initial onboarding note contains a median of 206 words, the authored document corpus contains a median of 5,453 words, with requirements and supporting evidence distributed across these records and their histories.

The agent determines which artifacts to inspect, which historical decisions still apply, which execution records correspond to the current revision, and which colleagues can provide missing information. We keep the overall organization size roughly comparable across tasks to limit variation in the volume of available workplace material.

The workplace records how projects develop over time. Versioned documents, board histories, review threads, CI records, and conversations contain intermediate diagnoses, superseded plans, rejected approaches, and observations tied to particular revisions. Historical records may use interface, module, or protocol names that differ from those in the current codebase. The agent establishes which claims remain applicable and reconciles them with subsequent decisions and the current repository.

Access follows the organization’s information boundaries. Private groups and direct messages that do not include the agent are inaccessible. Records from neighboring projects provide organizational context, while those projects remain outside the scope of the assignment.


#### 3.4 Scoring

We report three outcome metrics: F2P , the proportion of fail-to-pass verifier test nodes satisfied by the submitted implementation; P2P , the proportion of pass-to-pass test nodes preserved; and Pass rate , the proportion of tasks for which every F2P and P2P test node passes in the same run. F2P and P2P are computed by pooling passed test nodes and total test nodes across tasks.

Because each task contains multiple independently testable behaviors, F2P captures partial delivery, while Pass rate requires completion of the full executable contract. Section 5 provides the aggregation formulas and describes the trajectory diagnostics.


### 4 Working a SWE-CoWork Task

In a SWE-CoWork task, the agent works with the digital employees introduced in Section 3.1 . Starting from a brief handoff, it establishes the requirements through repository evidence, workplace records, and conversations with colleagues. The agent then implements and verifies the requested changes, revisiting earlier decisions as new information becomes available. Requirement discovery and implementation are interleaved: an owner’s clarification can change the behavior that a patch must satisfy.


#### 4.1 Working with the Digital Employees

Agents continuously move between investigating requirements, consulting colleagues, and implementing changes. Starting from a brief handoff, an agent explores the repository and workplace records to establish the current scope, identify unresolved requirements, and locate responsible colleagues. It must reconcile conflicting evidence, trace references across historical revisions, and ask questions grounded in the available code and project context. A typical run involves the following activities:

- 1. Orient. Read the onboarding note, inspect the repository, identify the target project, and establish the initial scope and relevant contacts.

- 2. Investigate. Search project boards, documents, chat, and engineering history for requirements and implementation constraints. Check record provenance, reconcile version differences, and trace references to renamed interfaces.

- 3. Ask. Identify the colleague responsible for an unresolved decision and ask a question supported by the available evidence. Follow referrals and respond to requests for additional context.

- 4. Build and verify. Implement the required changes, run relevant tests, and check for regressions in existing functionality and neighboring projects.

- 5. Revise. Examine new messages, traces, and corrections. Confirm their implications with the responsible colleague and update the implementation when necessary.

- 6. Deliver. Document the confirmed requirements, implementation changes, and verification results in the delivery note.

These activities are interleaved throughout execution. A colleague may request additional evidence, refer the agent to another owner, or introduce a constraint that requires revising an existing implementation. While some questions remain unresolved, the agent can continue working on other requirements, validate its changes, and revisit earlier decisions as new information becomes available. At handoff, it reports the implemented changes, verification results, and outstanding issues.


#### 4.2 Inside a Run: An Owner Clarification Changes the Patch

Task 094 involves a cumulative upgrade of Kimai, a PHP time-tracking application. One requirement, KIM-210, concerns exporting timesheet durations in decimal hours. The agent initially implemented the numerical conversion and verified that a 5,400-second entry produced 1.5 hours. However, the design document did not specify how the feature should be activated (Figure 3 ).

The agent then contacted the export owner to clarify the intended behavior:

“My XLSX cell and total calculate to 1.5. Please confirm whether that is the intended decimal behavior…” — agent

“…when the current user’s timesheet.export_decimal preference is true, the value is 1.5 hours… The preference is evaluated on every export, even when one renderer is reused for different users.” — export owner

The clarification revealed two requirements absent from the initial implementation: decimal formatting must follow the current user’s preference, and that preference must be evaluated on every export. The agent revised the renderer accordingly and extended its tests to cover consecutive exports by different users sharing the same renderer.

This interaction illustrates how a seemingly correct implementation can remain incomplete until a colleague clarifies the intended behavior. Across the full assignment, the agent satisfied 17 of 21 new behaviors while preserving all 15 existing behaviors.

#### 4.3 Workplace Information Challenges

Because requirements are distributed across records and colleagues, establishing what a record means requires checking when it became available, where it came from, and who has authority over the decision. Figure 4 groups these information challenges into five situations and shows the engineering judgment required in each. These situations affect how the agent establishes the requirements, with each misleading record accompanied by an accessible correction or a responsible colleague who can establish which evidence applies.

> Figure 4 : Five workplace information challenges and the judgments they require, organized by when evidence becomes available or is superseded, which run or project it belongs to, and who can resolve the outstanding decision. A recovered trace, corrected version label, or message from another team may arrive after implementation begins. In the schematic example, a successful browser record is followed by an SDK observation and a request to confirm the client version. The agent must follow this lead to the responsible owner, establish which behavior it concerns, and reassess the affected code and tests. Owners remain available for consultation before a scheduled notice appears.

An earlier proposal can remain searchable after a published correction. For Stencil’s AUR-202 , the draft of DOC-13 proposes an empty object when no source map is generated. The published third version instead requires the returned map field to remain undefined , while retaining the chunk’s other output information. The agent must compare revisions and publication states to identify the applicable rule.

A log’s title, contents, and surrounding discussion may refer to different executions. In the controller-runtime example, the attachment title names RUN-98752 , but the copied output identifies RUN-20547 and states that it came from a previous tab. The agent must align the output with its input, run, and code revision before using it to diagnose a failure.

Canceled cards, duplicates, rejected approaches, and neighboring projects remain visible alongside the current assignment. Similar wording can therefore lead to a record outside the required change. Status histories, project membership, and review outcomes establish which card is current and which approaches have already been rejected. The agent uses these distinctions to recover the target scope.

A colleague may know an adjacent interface boundary yet lack authority over the unresolved decision. A referral leads to the accountable owner, who may request a reproduction, caller path, or version before answering. Clarification also proceeds topic by topic: resolving one question can leave others open. The agent supplies the requested context and tracks which decisions each exchange establishes before carrying them into the implementation.

These situations make investigation and coding interdependent. A newly located record can prompt another question, whose answer changes the patch or its tests. ### 5 Evaluation

We evaluate eleven coding models in the CoWork setting using executable outcomes and collaboration records. A paired comparison with Oracle-Spec measures the performance of Seed Evolving and GPT-6 Astra when complete requirements are supplied at the start. To examine how requirements are obtained, incorporated into code, verified, and reported at handoff, we analyze Claude-Opus-5.5 trajectories.


#### 5.1 Primary metrics

We report F2P , P2P , and Pass rate on $N=101$ tasks. For task $i$ , let $f_{i}$ and $p_{i}$ denote the numbers of passing F2P and P2P test nodes, and let $F_{i}$ and $P_{i}$ denote their respective totals. All metrics are expressed as percentages:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathrm{F2P}$ $\displaystyle=100\,\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}F_{i}},$ $\displaystyle\mathrm{P2P}$ $\displaystyle=100\,\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}P_{i}},$ $\displaystyle\mathrm{Pass\ rate}$ $\displaystyle=\frac{100}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\!\left[f_{i}=F_{i}\ \land\ p_{i}=P_{i}\right].$

F2P measures how many new requirements are satisfied, while P2P measures whether existing behavior is preserved. Both metrics aggregate test nodes across tasks, giving greater weight to tasks with more test nodes. Pass rate measures the fraction of tasks for which every F2P and P2P test node passes.

All models are evaluated against the full release contract: 2,699 F2P test nodes, 562 P2P test nodes, and 101 tasks. Only passes confirmed by the verifier count toward the numerators. For trajectory analysis, we use the fraction of F2P test nodes passed within each task to relate outcomes to agent behavior.


#### 5.2 Experimental design

We evaluate eleven models at max effort in the CoWork setting: Claude-Opus-5.5 ( Anthropic, 2026 ) , GPT-6 Astra ( OpenAI, 2026 ) , Seed 2.1 Pro ( ByteDance Seed, 2026 ) , ByteDance’s Seed Evolving ( Volcano Engine, 2026 ) , Kimi K3 ( Moonshot AI, 2026 ) , DeepSeek V4 Pro ( DeepSeek AI, 2026c ) , DeepSeek V4.1 Flash ( DeepSeek AI, 2026b ) , GLM 5.3 ( Z.ai, 2026b ) , GLM 5.2 ( Z.ai, 2026a ) , and Qwen3.8-Max and Qwen3.8-Flash ( Alibaba Cloud Model Studio, 2026 ) .

GPT-6 Astra uses a Codex scaffold, and the other ten models use Claude Code scaffolds. In every CoWork run, the digital employees are backed by GPT-5.6 Terra at high reasoning effort, so all agents interact with the same colleague model. Each agent starts with a brief handoff and access to the organization. It identifies the requirements through workplace records and conversations with colleagues, implements the requested changes, and submits its work for F2P/P2P evaluation. Table 2 reports F2P, P2P, and Pass rate for all eleven models on the 101-task release contract.

We evaluate Seed Evolving and GPT-6 Astra under two conditions. In CoWork , the agent obtains requirements through workplace records and colleagues. In Oracle-Spec , the complete requirement set is provided at the start, and the agent has no colleagues to consult. Both conditions cover the same 101 tasks, with identical task definitions, repository states, and executable test contracts.

We compare F2P, P2P, and Pass rate across the paired runs to measure how access to complete requirements affects implementation outcomes.

We examine Claude-Opus-5.5’s CoWork trajectories by combining programmatic disclosure logs with a rubric-based LLM trajectory reviewer. For each run, we trace which requirements reached the agent, which remained unresolved, how it followed up with colleagues, and how it verified and reported its work.

We select Claude-Opus-5.5 because it achieves the highest CoWork F2P score among the evaluated models. The analysis covers its recorded runs and examines the relationship between requirement acquisition, agent behavior, and implementation outcomes.

Supplementary diagnostics appear in Appendix F ; Appendix H gives the measurement definitions, claim-classification rules, and evidence sources.


#### 5.3 Supplementary analysis metrics

We report the median per-run number of model calls, input tokens, output tokens, tool calls, and context compactions. Input usage accumulates tokens processed across calls within a run, including repeated context. We pair these usage medians with each model’s F2P on the full release contract. Figures 12 and 13 in Appendix G.1 report these comparisons.

For eight models, we report per-run medians of workplace queries before the first code edit, the tool-call index of that edit, and workplace queries over the full run (Figure 6 ). The same comparison includes median messages sent to colleagues.

Each task’s Oracle-Spec enumerates its requirements and identifies the responsible colleague or public record. Disclosure logs and saved tool replies establish which requirements were released by colleagues and which replies reached the agent.

We measure ask rate , the fraction of requirements held by colleagues that the agent raised with the responsible owner or obtained; targeting , the fraction of queried cards whose first question was directed to the accountable owner; gain per question , the number of disclosures per colleague message; follow-through , the fraction of raised requirements that were subsequently disclosed; redundancy , questions about cards whose requirements had already been fully disclosed; and unread disclosures , replies containing disclosed requirements that were absent from the agent’s recorded observations.

Supplying complete requirements improves partial delivery for Seed Evolving and GPT-6 Astra without increasing complete task success. #### 6.3 Claude-Opus-5.5: Following Each Requirement to Delivery

We analyze 101 Claude-Opus-5.5 CoWork trajectories, linking colleague interactions, requirement disclosures, executable test outcomes, and final handoff reports. This analysis examines where requirements remain unresolved as agents move from clarification to implementation, verification, and delivery.


##### 6.3.1 Clarification: reaching owners and raising requirements

Claude-Opus-5.5 directs its first question to the accountable owner for a mean of 93% of queried cards across runs (Table 7 ), yet obtains only 64% of the 2,176 colleague-held requirements (Figure 7 ). Of all colleague-held requirements, 16% are raised without obtaining disclosure, 17% remain unraised with owners already contacted about other cards, and 3% belong to owners never contacted. Thus, most missing requirements arise from incomplete clarification with already-contacted owners rather than failures to identify the right colleagues.

> Figure 7 : Requirement acquisition and loss in Claude-Opus-5.5. (a) Requirements held by colleagues, grouped by acquisition state. (b) F2P test-node pass rates against the full release contract, grouped by whether the corresponding requirement reached the agent. (c) Earliest observed loss stage (discovery, ask, or implement) for failed test nodes with linked requirements, evaluated against the full release contract. The implement category denotes unsatisfied behavior after the requirement reached the agent.

F2P test nodes linked to disclosed colleague-held requirements pass at 68.8%, compared with 49.8% for undisclosed requirements. Within tasks containing both types, the mean difference is 21.3 percentage points. However, among failed F2P test nodes with linked requirements, 56% are assigned to implement : the requirement reached the agent, but the tested behavior remains unsatisfied. Another 42% are associated with requirements not successfully obtained through owner clarification ( ask ), and the remaining 2% are attributed to discovery . These results reveal gaps both in obtaining requirements and in translating available information into verified implementations.


##### 6.3.2 Clarification over time: following up deferred questions

Among 450 owner–card threads in which a colleague initially defers an answer, Claude-Opus-5.5 follows up on 297, eventually obtaining disclosure in 205. The remaining 153 threads receive no follow-up (Figure 8 b). At the initial deferral, these abandoned threads have a median of 7.1 hours of execution budget remaining, and the agent continues working for a median of 3.4 hours afterward.

(b) Deferred threads grouped by owner and card, subsequent follow-up and eventual disclosure. (c) Timing of colleague messages within a run. Message activity across the run can coexist with individual threads receiving no follow-up.

Communication nevertheless continues late in execution: of 2,552 colleague message attempts, 20% occur during the final fifth of execution (Figure 8 c). These aggregate patterns show that sustained communication can coexist with individual questions left unresolved.

The PostCSS task (row 014) illustrates this difficulty. The agent raises card CSS-227 and follows up with additional evidence, but receives another deferral and eventually declares the streams complete without resolving the question. All 16 failed F2P test nodes in this run concern undisclosed colleague-held requirements. The case illustrates how unresolved clarifications can persist through delivery despite earlier attempts to obtain an answer.


##### 6.3.3 Implementation and verification of obtained requirements

The carapace-bin task (row 001) illustrates this gap. Claude-Opus-5.5 obtains all 25 colleague-held requirements, yet fails 22 of 35 F2P test nodes. The trajectory reviewer identifies incorrect usages, flags, and candidate descriptions, along with an unchecked build failure. Despite acquiring the requirements, the agent leaves substantial parts of the executable contract unsatisfied.

Figure 9 examines exploratory associations between the trajectory reviewer’s behavioral assessments and per-task F2P. Among the assessed dimensions, evidence-to-code translation shows the strongest association with F2P (Spearman $\rho=+0.36$ ), followed by causal debugging and verification design (both $\rho=+0.30$ ). The correlations for colleague-message count and tool-call count are $\rho=+0.21$ and $\rho=-0.21$ , respectively.

These exploratory results suggest that effective use of acquired information matters beyond the amount of interaction. Agents may obtain the necessary requirements yet fail to incorporate them correctly into code or verify the resulting behavior.

##### 6.3.4 Handoff: stopping decisions and reported status

The trajectory reviewer identifies 49 premature stops in which unresolved in-scope requirements still have actionable next steps (Figure 11 e in Appendix F.1 ). Among the 48 autonomous stops in this group, the median remaining execution budget is 2.8 hours. These observations suggest that the agent may terminate assignments despite having both outstanding work and opportunities to continue.

Among 826 requirements reported as completed at handoff, 255 (31%) fail their corresponding tests (Figure 8 a). Conversely, many unsatisfied requirements are omitted from the final report rather than explicitly identified as outstanding.

The HotswapAgent task (row 021) illustrates this discrepancy: the agent declares the assignment complete while its own notes still list 8 pending requirements. Together, these observations reveal difficulties in determining when an assignment is complete and communicating its remaining obligations accurately.


#### 6.4 Tracking Requirements Through an Assignment

Across the model comparisons, Oracle-Spec experiment, and trajectory analysis, requirements remain unsatisfied at multiple stages of an assignment. Supplying complete requirements improves partial delivery without ensuring whole-task success, while the trajectory analysis exposes gaps in implementation, verification, and handoff. Together, these findings suggest that reliable delivery requires maintaining the state of individual requirements throughout the engineering workflow.

One direction is to explicitly track each requirement’s accountable owner, unresolved questions, implementation status, verification evidence, and handoff claims. Such tracking could help agents revisit outstanding dependencies and distinguish implemented changes from verified outcomes. Whether this approach improves end-to-end delivery remains an open question.


### 7 Conclusion

We introduced SWE-CoWork , a benchmark that evaluates coding agents working within software organizations, where requirements must be recovered from colleagues and workplace records before being implemented and verified. Our evaluation reveals a substantial gap between partial implementation and complete task delivery. Agents frequently leave clarifications unresolved and, after obtaining requirements, may still fail to satisfy executable tests or report completion inconsistently with verified outcomes.

These findings highlight sustained requirement tracking as a key challenge for autonomous software engineering. Future agents will need to maintain continuity across collaboration, implementation, verification, and handoff to support reliable delegation of engineering work. SWE-CoWork provides a setting for studying these capabilities and can be extended to evaluate teams of autonomous programming agents.

### Appendix B Broader Benchmark Lineage

Table 4 compares repository and interactive benchmarks by their research focus, information sources and evaluation design. ### Appendix H Claude-Opus-5.5 Trajectories: Measurement Details

The analysis covers the 101-task release cohort. Behavioral measurements come from the available Claude-Opus-5.5 CoWork trajectories. Table 2 reports benchmark outcomes evaluated against the complete test contract. Each trajectory record includes its task version, package hash, run ID, requested models, scaffold version, runtime revision and budget. Runs resumed after a provider interruption are treated as single observations.

For each F2P test node represented in the trajectory archive, we map the tested requirements by comparing the per-node requirement files, test source code and upstream change titles with the Oracle-Spec for the same package.

We use card identifiers, document retrieval records and disclosure events to identify where requirements were missed or lost during information acquisition and use.

The workplace service logs each requirement disclosed to the agent and links the disclosure to the question that triggered it. A disclosure is considered visible when its reply body appears in a tool result saved in the trajectory. We associate questions with cards using the card identifiers mentioned in the questions. If the agent contacted a requirement’s owner but never mentioned its card, we classify the requirement as owner contacted, card not raised .

The trajectory reviewer, GPT-6 Astra at xhigh reasoning effort, assesses observable actions and outcomes along ten strategy dimensions and six collaboration dimensions. It evaluates ordered trajectories before private grading outcomes are attached. Each dimension uses four ordered categories: ineffective (an identifiable need remains unaddressed), partial (useful actions leave identifiable gaps), effective (appropriate actions resolve the local need) and adaptive (the agent connects evidence across steps to resolve uncertainty and adjust or verify its actions).

The categories are encoded as 0–3 for rank analysis. Each dimension is assessed using its own behavioral criteria. When the trajectory provides insufficient evidence or no opportunity to exhibit a behavior, the dimension is marked not assessable .

Category percentages are calculated separately for each dimension using assessable runs. Spearman correlations use runs with both an assessable category and an observed per-task F2P score. A trajectory may contribute to one dimension or diagnostic even when another dimension lacks a completed assessment.

For owner-question follow-through, a relevant question that lacks necessary follow-up may be classified as partial. Following the question through to an actionable answer is effective. Resolving consequential ambiguity through linked questions and corroborating evidence is adaptive.
