## Sensorimotor Alignment: A Platonic Proxy for Embodied Policy Success

Robot foundation models are typically evaluated through costly closed-loop rollouts, motivating low-cost offline proxies for model capability. The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH) [ 21 ] suggests that as representations improve, modalities such as vision and language converge toward a shared statistical model of reality. We ask whether motion trajectories form another such modality: as embodied models become more capable, should their sensory representations converge toward the structure of physical motion? To test this, we propose Sensorimotor Alignment ( $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ ), a training-free offline metric that measures how well a model’s sensory representation aligns with the geometry of physical motion. We find that $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ correlates positively with downstream robot task success across model families, making it a low-cost offline proxy for prioritizing models before closed-loop evaluation. $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ can also identify promising vision-language model (VLM) backbones before costly fine-tuning into vision-language-action models (VLAs). Beyond this correlation, $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ offers representation-level explanations for phenomena observed in VLAs and world action models (WAMs). With $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ , we extend PRH to robotics and make sensorimotor alignment measurable, offering a low-cost, representation-level view of robot foundation models that closed-loop success rates alone cannot reveal.

### 1 Introduction

Robot foundation models increasingly build on large pretrained vision-language models [ 4 , 3 ] , video models [ 50 ] , and world action models [ 15 , 28 , 5 ] . Yet selecting and evaluating these models remains expensive: the most reliable test is still closed-loop rollout in simulation [ 30 , 33 ] or the real world [ 1 , 9 ] , where evaluation is slow, noisy, and benchmark-dependent. This expense motivates low-cost offline proxies for policy capability.

A natural approach is to use existing perception-side proxies, such as visual question answering scores for VLMs [ 31 , 57 ] or pixel-level video quality for world models [ 18 , 52 , 23 ] . However, these metrics measure passive perception or generation quality, not whether a representation is organized for action [ 59 , 24 , 47 , 35 ] . Two scenes may look visually different but require similar robot motions; conversely, two visually similar scenes may demand different trajectories. For robot task success, a model must produce different motions across tasks even in similar scenes. This requires aligning the sensory representation with the motor representation.

This perspective connects robotics to the Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH) [ 21 ] , which argues that as models of different modalities scale and solve diverse tasks, they are converging to a shared statistical model of reality in each of their own representation spaces. For example, images and text are two projections of the same underlying world. As vision and language models scale, they increasingly agree on the relative distances between data points. We bring PRH to robotics and ask: can physical robot motion be viewed as another projection of that same world, such that more capable embodied models better align their vision and language representations with the structure of motion trajectories?

We test this prediction of PRH: the more capable an embodied model is, the more closely its sensory representations align with its motor representation. In other words, episodes nearby in sensory representation space should also be nearby in motor representation space.

We formalize this as Sensorimotor Alignment ( $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ ), a training-free metric, illustrated in Figure 1 . For each episode in a robot dataset, a model’s image and text features form its sensory representation, and the recorded end-effector trajectory forms its motor representation. We measure alignment by comparing each episode’s nearest neighbors in sensory and motor spaces. More overlap between the two neighbor sets means stronger sensorimotor alignment.

In Section 3 , we show that $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ correlates positively with downstream robot task success across model families, in simulated and real-world settings. The correlation is strong for Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) in closed-loop manipulation, indicating that models with higher sensorimotor alignment tend to perform better. $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ computed on raw Vision-Language Models (VLMs) also correlates with the task success of the resulting VLAs, more strongly than their visual question answering (VQA) scores do. For World Action Models (WAMs), $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ likewise correlates more strongly with task success than video-quality metrics such as FID [ 18 ] , SSIM [ 52 ] , and PSNR [ 19 ] , and for real-robot policies, $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ computed offline correlates with physical-robot task success on a shared set of tasks. Across these settings, sensorimotor alignment correlates with task success more closely than VQA or video-quality metrics.

Prior work has observed several phenomena in VLAs and WAMs only through closed-loop results, without explaining them at the representation level. We use $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ to supply that explanation, as shown in Section 4 . For example, fine-tuned VLAs often ignore the language instruction and act mainly on what they see; we find that their task success correlates with the $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ of their vision features, not their text features.

Our contributions are threefold:

- • Inspired by the Platonic Representation Hypothesis, we propose a new view of embodiment: more capable embodied models should align their sensory representations more closely with the structure of motion trajectories. To test this, we introduce $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ , a training-free offline metric that measures alignment between sensory and motor representations.

- • Across model families, in simulation and on real robots, $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ correlates positively with task success, making it a low-cost offline proxy for choosing models before closed-loop evaluation.

- • Furthermore, we use $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ to explain, at the representation level, several phenomena in VLAs and WAMs that were previously observed only through closed-loop results, making it a diagnostic tool for understanding how design choices shape embodied models.

We make sensorimotor alignment measurable, offering a low-cost, representation-level view of robot foundation models that closed-loop success rates alone cannot reveal.


### 2 Measuring Sensorimotor Alignment

Motivated by the Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH) [ 21 ] , we hypothesize that the more capable an embodied model is, the more closely its sensory representations align with its motor representation. Here, sensory representations are the model’s image and text features, and each episode’s end-effector trajectory is its motor representation.

To make this alignment measurable, we define Sensorimotor Alignment ( $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ ) by constructing both representations and comparing their neighborhoods, as follows:

> Figure 2 : Measuring Sensorimotor Alignment ( $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ ) by nearest-neighbor overlap. For the query episode (left), sensory neighbors (bottom right) have similar vision-text features, and motor neighbors (top right) have similar trajectories. Green checks mark shared neighbors; red crosses mark neighbors found only in the sensory set. A larger overlap means that episodes with similar sensory representations also follow similar motor trajectories.

Reference dataset. To measure cross-modal alignment, we use the DROID [ 26 ] and BridgeDataV2 [ 49 ] datasets, which pair sensory inputs with physical motions. From a dataset, we sample $N$ episodes and compute both representations from each episode. For episode $i$ , we randomly sample one image frame and pair it with the task instruction; this image–text pair forms the sensory input $\mathbf{x}_{i}$ . Sampling details are provided in Appendix A.1 .

Sensory representation. For each episode $i$ , we encode the image frame and text instruction with the vision and text encoders of a robot foundation model, obtaining a sensory embedding

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{z}_{i}=f(\mathbf{x}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d},$$

where $f$ applies the vision encoder to the image and the text encoder to the instruction, and we average-pool the output token features into a single $d$ -dimensional vector.

Motor representation. For the sampled frame in $\mathbf{x}_{i}$ , we extract end-effector positions, orientations, and gripper states over the next $h$ steps, forming episode $i$ ’s motor representation:

[Tableau] $$\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i},z_{i},\phi_{i},\theta_{i},\psi_{i},g_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times 7},$$

where row $u$ is the state $u$ steps after the sampled frame: $x,y,z$ are position coordinates, $\phi,\theta,\psi$ are roll, pitch, and yaw angles, and $g$ is the recorded gripper coordinate. We encode positions as vectors in $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ and orientation angles as rotations in $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ so that distances reflect the geometry of translations and rotations. Details are in Appendix A.2 .

Sensorimotor alignment. We measure alignment with a mutual nearest-neighbor metric comparing the $k$ -nearest-neighbor sets in sensory and motor spaces; greater overlap indicates stronger alignment between an episode’s sensory and motor representations.

We use cosine distance in sensory space and Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) [ 41 , 37 ] in motor space to compare trajectories at different speeds. For episodes $i$ and $j$ :

[Tableau] $$d_{\mathrm{sensory}}(i,j)=1-\cos(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j}),\qquad d_{\mathrm{motor}}(i,j)=\operatorname{DTW}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{j}).$$

Smaller distances mean closer neighbors. Appendices A.2 – A.3 detail DTW and normalization.

For each episode $i$ , we use $d_{\mathrm{sensory}}$ to form the sensory $k$ -nearest-neighbor set $\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{sensory}}(i)$ and $d_{\mathrm{motor}}$ to form the motor $k$ -nearest-neighbor set $\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{motor}}(i)$ . We then take the intersection of the two sets, divide its size by $k$ , and average over all $N$ episodes [ 21 ] :

[Tableau] $$m_{\mathrm{NN}}=\frac{1}{Nk}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{sensory}}(i)\cap\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{motor}}(i)\right|.$$ (1)

Greater overlap indicates that episodes with similar sensory representations also have similar motor trajectories, as shown in Figure 2 .

In practice, our reported $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ uses a centered, normalized variant of CKNNA with $k=10$ neighbors unless stated otherwise; details are given in Appendix A.3 .


### 3 Sensorimotor Alignment Correlates with Robot Task Success Rate

The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH) [ 21 ] argues that as models of different modalities become more capable, their representations converge toward a shared statistical model of reality. While the evidence for PRH comes from observational data such as images and text, robotics adds the motion the robot executes, which an embodied model must produce rather than observe. We test the prediction that models whose image–text representations align more closely with end-effector trajectories should achieve higher downstream task success.

We quantify this alignment using Sensorimotor Alignment ( $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ ), which compares episode neighborhoods in sensory and motor space, as defined in Section 2 . A positive correlation between $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ and task success would provide empirical support for this PRH-inspired view of embodiment. It would also support using $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ as a low-cost offline proxy for deciding which models to evaluate in closed loop first.

Section 3.1 establishes this positive correlation across 3 model families and real-robot policies. Section 3.2 shows that $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ correlates with task success more closely than VQA and video-quality metrics. Section 3.3 shows that the correlation holds across computation settings and drops in a negative control with a mismatched embodiment.


#### 3.1 $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ Correlates with Task Success Across Model Families

In Figure 3 , we test 3 model families and real-robot policies, comparing each model’s $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ on a reference dataset ( $x$ -axis) with its downstream manipulation success rate ( $y$ -axis).

> Figure 3 : Sensorimotor Alignment ( $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ ) correlates with task success across model families and in simulated and real-world evaluations. Each panel plots offline $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ ( $x$ -axis) against downstream task success rate ( $y$ -axis) for (a) fine-tuned vision-language-action models (VLAs), (b) raw vision-language models (VLMs), (c) world action models (WAMs), and (d) real-robot policies. For raw VLMs, task success is measured after VLA fine-tuning. These correlations (Pearson’s $r$ ; unadjusted one-tailed $p$ -values) support $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ as an offline proxy across model families.

Vision-language-action models (VLAs). We test 12 VLAs against their SimplerEnv [ 30 ] success rates. In Figure 3 a , the Pearson correlation $r\!=\!{+}0.717$ is strong, with a model-bootstrap $95\%$ confidence interval of $[{+}0.436,\,{+}0.912]$ . Thus, models with higher sensorimotor alignment tend to achieve higher task success rates. The correlation holds across reference datasets, model families, scales, and VLA training setups, as shown in Figure 7 and Appendix B.2 .

Vision-language models (VLMs). We ask whether $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ computed on a raw VLM, before any robot fine-tuning, correlates with the task success of the VLA that is later fine-tuned from that backbone. We compute $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ for 8 raw VLM backbones from VLM4VLA [ 59 ] and compare it with the SimplerEnv [ 30 ] success rates of the resulting VLAs. We find a strong correlation, $r=+0.681$ with one-tailed $p=0.031$ , as shown in Figure 3 b . $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ can therefore identify promising VLM backbones before costly VLA fine-tuning. Appendix B.3 lists the backbones and $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ settings.

World action models (WAMs). We also test WAMs, which build on video generators rather than VLMs. We compute $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ for 6 WAMs on the bimanual ALOHA dataset [ 61 ] and compare it with their success rates on RoboTwin 2.0 [ 33 ] . For WAMs, the sensory representation is the video-generator latent used for action decoding, and all 6 models share the same ALOHA trajectories and $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ settings. $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ again correlates strongly, $r=+0.718$ with one-tailed $p=0.054$ , as shown in Figure 3 c . Appendix B.4 lists the 6 WAMs and shared evaluation protocol.

Real-robot policies. We test whether the correlation observed in simulation extends to real robots. Because real-world success rates depend on many factors, such as the task set, robot, cameras, and protocol, we take them all from one shared evaluation: the physical-robot results that MolmoB0T [ 11 ] reports for 5 policies on 40 tasks. We then compute $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ for their public checkpoints on the same DROID reference dataset. We find a strong correlation with real-robot success, $r=+0.831$ with one-tailed $p=0.041$ , as shown in Figure 3 d . Appendix B.5 describes the shared evaluation conditions and checkpoint scoring.

We further validate that $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ correlates with downstream task success for frozen vision backbones under low-data behavior cloning and visual-reward learning, as shown in Appendix B.6 .


#### 3.2 $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ Correlates with Task Success More Closely Than Alternative Metrics

Prior work asks whether offline scores correlate with downstream task performance. VLM4VLA [ 59 ] examines the correlation between VQA benchmark scores and the performance of the resulting VLA policies, finding weak or even negative correlations. For video models, Tian et al. [47] find that the quality of predicted video agrees inconsistently with downstream performance across tasks. A common limitation is that these metrics measure language understanding or pixel-level video quality, neither of which depends on the motion the robot must execute. $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ instead compares the nearest-neighbor sets of sensory embeddings and motor trajectories. As we show below, $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ correlates more strongly with task success rate than any VQA or video-quality metric we evaluate.

$\mathrm{S}^{2}$ correlates with VLA success rate where VQA scores do not. We compare $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ with aggregate VQA scores as offline proxies, using the same 8 raw VLM backbones and evaluation setting as in Section 3.1 . We find that VQA scores show virtually no correlation with VLA success rate, at $r=-0.154$ (right panel of Figure 4 ). $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ , by contrast, correlates strongly, at $r=+0.681$ with one-tailed $p=0.031$ (left panel of Figure 4 ).

> Figure 4 : Sensorimotor Alignment ( $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ ) correlates with VLA success rate where VQA scores do not. Across 8 raw vision-language models (VLMs) [ 59 ] , $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ (left) correlates strongly with SimplerEnv task success after fine-tuning into vision-language-action models (VLAs), whereas visual question answering (VQA) scores (right) show virtually no correlation.

$\mathrm{S}^{2}$ correlates with WAM success rate more closely than video-quality metrics. We compare $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ with standard video-quality metrics (FID [ 18 ] , SSIM [ 52 ] , and PSNR [ 19 ] ) on 3 of the WAMs, Motus [ 5 ] , LingBot-VA [ 28 ] , and Vidar [ 15 ] , evaluated on RoboTwin. We find that these metrics correlate weakly with task success: SSIM reaches only $r=+0.558$ and PSNR only $r=+0.468$ , while FID correlates in the wrong direction, at $r=+0.680$ , associating worse video quality with higher success ( Figure 5 ). By contrast, $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ on ALOHA correlates more strongly, at $r=+0.999$ .

> Figure 5 : Sensorimotor Alignment ( $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ ) correlates with WAM success rate more closely than video-quality metrics. For 3 world action models (WAMs) evaluated on RoboTwin, video-quality metrics (SSIM, PSNR, FID) show no significant correlations with task success. In contrast, only $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ correctly recovers the success-rate ordering. Only 3 of the 6 WAMs are compared because the others do not generate future video during closed-loop task evaluation.


#### 3.3 $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ Is Robust to Evaluation Choices

We next check that the correlation does not depend on a particular choice of settings. We test this in two ways. First, we vary how $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ is computed and find that the positive correlation with task success holds. Second, as a negative control, we deliberately compute $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ on reference data from a mismatched embodiment, where a sound metric should lose this correlation. The correlation drops as expected, validating the design of $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ .

> Figure 6 : The correlation transfers across reference datasets. For 12 vision-language-action models, Sensorimotor Alignment ( $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ ) on DROID (Franka Panda, left) and BridgeDataV2 (WidowX, right) correlates strongly with the same SimplerEnv-WidowX task success rates.

> Figure 7 : Stable correlations across horizons. For 12 vision-language-action models, Sensorimotor Alignment ( $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ ) and SimplerEnv-WidowX success rate have Pearson $r>0.5$ at nearly all tested horizons $h$ (future steps) on DROID and BridgeDataV2.

$\mathrm{S}^{2}$ is stable across computation choices. In the first check, we vary eight settings: the reference dataset, the trajectory horizon, five implementation choices, and DTW warping. The correlation stays positive under all eight. We discuss the first two below; Appendix C.1 gives per-model $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ by horizon, and Appendices C.2 and C.3 cover the other six.

The correlation transfers across reference datasets. We compute $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ on both DROID (Franka Panda) and BridgeDataV2 [ 49 ] (WidowX), robots with different arm kinematics, and both correlate near $r\!=\!{+}0.7$ with task success for the 12 VLAs on SimplerEnv ( Figure 7 ).

The correlation is also stable across trajectory horizons. It stays above $r\!=\!0.5$ at all DROID horizons through $h\!=\!220$ and at BridgeDataV2 horizons from $h\!=\!3$ through $40$ ( Figure 7 ).

$\mathrm{S}^{2}$ is sensitive to embodiment mismatch. In the negative control, we compute $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ for 3 bimanual WAMs on a single-arm reference dataset (DROID) rather than the matched bimanual one (ALOHA). This embodiment mismatch drops the $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ –success correlation from $r\!=\!{+}0.999$ to $r\!=\!{+}0.246$ , indicating that $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ responds to the difference in arm configuration rather than reporting alignment indiscriminately. Appendix C.4 gives the full comparison.


### 4 Representation-Level Explanations with $\mathrm{S}^{2}$

Several phenomena reported for VLAs and WAMs rest only on closed-loop results and still lack a representation-level explanation. Because $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ correlates with task success, as shown in Section 3 , we use it to explain each of these phenomena, as summarized below:

- • Modality dominance : prior work observes that VLAs often struggle with instruction grounding: they act on what the scene visually suggests rather than on what the language instruction asks [ 14 , 13 , 60 ] . In Section 4.1 , we show that after VLA fine-tuning, task success correlates with vision-feature $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ but not text-feature $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ , indicating that fine-tuned VLAs draw on what they see, not on the instruction, to produce their motion.

- • Frozen encoders : prior work observes that freezing the vision encoder during VLA fine-tuning degrades downstream task performance [ 59 ] . In Section 4.2 , we show that freezing also prevents the $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ gain from end-to-end fine-tuning, indicating that VLAs gain performance by bringing their sensory representation closer to the motor representation.

- • Iterative denoising : prior work observes that WAMs can skip iterative video denoising at test time, decoding actions without generating future video, with little loss in task success [ 56 ] . In Section 4.3 , we show that the $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ of WAM features is already high at the earliest denoising step and changes little afterwards, indicating that the first denoising steps already yield features aligned closely enough with robot trajectories to act on.

- • Mid-depth blocks : prior work observes that mid-depth blocks of video diffusion transformers matter most for semantic reasoning [ 51 ] . In Section 4.4 , we show that these blocks also carry the strongest $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ , indicating that the layers that reason about video semantics are also the layers most aligned with robot motion.

#### 4.1 What Carries the Correlation: Vision, Text, or Both?

VLAs often struggle with instruction grounding [ 14 , 13 , 60 ] . We use $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ to explain this at the representation level, asking which modality the correlation with task success depends on: vision, text, or both. In Figure 8 , we compute $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ separately for each modality. We take the VLM output tokens read by the action head and form vision features from the image tokens, text features from the instruction tokens, and joint vision-text features from all tokens.

Fine-tuned VLAs rely mainly on visual rather than language representations. In the 12 fine-tuned VLAs, $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ from vision features correlates with SimplerEnv success rate at $r\!=\!{+}0.694$ , nearly matching the joint vision-text features at $r\!=\!{+}0.699$ , whereas text features show no significant correlation at $r\!=\!{+}0.237$ . The raw VLMs reverse this pattern: before fine-tuning, text features correlate at $r\!=\!{+}0.501$ , more strongly than vision features at $r\!=\!{+}0.425$ , and joint vision-text features correlate most strongly at $r\!=\!{+}0.681$ .

We infer that, after fine-tuning, VLAs produce their motion mainly from visual rather than language representations, which explains their weak instruction grounding. Appendix D.1 reports per-model $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ at all feature positions.


#### 4.2 Freezing Vision Encoders Limits $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ Gain

VLM4VLA [ 59 ] reports that VLAs perform substantially worse when their vision encoder stays frozen during fine-tuning. We use $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ to explain this drop at the representation level, testing whether freezing also prevents the sensorimotor-alignment gain from end-to-end fine-tuning.

Freezing the encoder reduces $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ by $41\%$ and SimplerEnv success rate by $40\%$ . These results suggest that end-to-end fine-tuning improves performance by aligning the sensory representation more closely with the motor representation. Appendix D.2 gives the full horizon sweep.

Most WAMs generate future video through iterative denoising and decode actions from the resulting latents. Fast-WAM [ 56 ] shows that WAMs can skip this video generation and decode actions directly, with little loss in task success. We examine the representation-level reason: whether features at the earliest denoising steps already align with robot trajectories.

We measure $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ for 3 WAMs [ 5 , 28 , 15 ] on RoboTwin [ 33 ] at 5 positions in each model’s native denoising schedule, from $0\%$ to $100\%$ , not at matched noise levels ( Figure 10(a) ). $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ is already high at the earliest step and remains nearly flat, varying by about $6\%$ at most from its initial value across the 3 models. We infer that early-step features are already sufficiently aligned with robot trajectories to generate actions. Because $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ correlates with WAM success rate ( Section 3.1 ), this result explains why skipping iterative video denoising barely lowers task success.


#### 4.4 Mid-Depth Blocks Carry the Strongest Sensorimotor Alignment

Chain-of-Steps [ 51 ] shows that mid-depth layers in Video Diffusion Transformers are especially important for semantic reasoning over objects and interactions. We ask whether the layers with the highest $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ also lie in this mid-depth range.

In Figure 10(b) , we compute $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ at different depths of 3 WAMs [ 5 , 28 , 15 ] on RoboTwin. All 3 share a 30-block video backbone; we extract features from Blocks 0, 7, 14, 21, and 29. Block 14 yields the highest $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ in every model. This peak falls in the mid-depth range that Chain-of-Steps [ 51 ] identifies as semantically important, suggesting that the layers handling video semantic reasoning align most closely with robot motion. ### 5 Related Work

Representation learning in robotics. Many robotics studies pretrain visual representations to improve task performance [ 34 , 40 , 32 ] . However, there remains limited understanding of which representational properties lead to downstream task success. A few studies ask what makes a pretrained vision encoder useful for robot learning. Hu et al. [20] evaluate 14 frozen vision encoders and find that the correlation between an encoder’s offline score and downstream task success rate is not stable across policy-learning methods. Burns et al. [8] find that emergent segmentation predicts manipulation success under visual distribution shifts, but only for Vision Transformers. Theia [ 43 ] hypothesizes that higher feature-norm entropy improves robot learning, but reports the supporting correlations only within separate groups of encoders. These studies examine only vision encoders rather than a broader range of embodied models, and none of them accounts for the trajectories the robot executes when measuring the representation. By contrast, $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ compares $k$ -nearest-neighbor sets in sensory and motor spaces. The same $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ measure correlates with task success across VLAs, raw VLMs, WAMs, and the encoders that Hu et al. [20] evaluate.

Platonic Representation Hypothesis. Recent work has applied the Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH) [ 21 ] to several new domains. In image generation, REPA [ 54 ] aligns diffusion-transformer features with self-supervised vision encoders, improving training speed and quality. CREPA [ 22 ] extends this recipe to video diffusion. In multimodal understanding, VIRAL [ 53 ] re-aligns multimodal large language models (LLMs) with their vision encoders, recovering visual detail lost in multimodal training. For cross-modal alignment, Schnaus et al. [42] match independently trained vision and language encoders through their shared geometry, without paired data. PRH has not yet been brought into robotics; we extend it to robotics by treating robot trajectories as another projection of the world, and find that more capable embodied models align their sensory representations more closely with it.


### 6 Conclusion

We introduce Sensorimotor Alignment ( $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ ), a training-free offline metric of alignment between sensory representations and end-effector trajectories. Across VLAs, raw VLMs, and WAMs in simulation, and across real-robot policies, $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ correlates positively with downstream task success, supporting a PRH-inspired view of embodiment. $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ correlates more strongly with task success than VQA scores and video-quality metrics, and explains, at the representation level, VLA and WAM phenomena previously observed only through closed-loop results: modality dominance, the performance drop from freezing the vision encoder, the small task-success cost of skipping iterative denoising, and the importance of mid-depth blocks for semantic reasoning. We use $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ only offline as a low-cost model-selection and diagnostic tool before closed-loop evaluation, but this correlation motivates optimizing embodied models directly for sensorimotor alignment.

Limitations. Our study has three main limitations. (1) The positive relationship between $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ and task success is correlational across the tested models; we have not yet demonstrated that sensorimotor alignment can be used directly during training to improve task success rates. (2) Models with similar $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ values can still achieve different success rates, so $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ is best used as a low-cost offline proxy for identifying promising models and VLM backbones before costly fine-tuning or closed-loop testing, which remains the final step of model selection. (3) Our evaluation covers limited model families, benchmarks, and training recipes, few models per comparison, and a single real-robot setting; broader generalization remains to be tested.

### Appendix A Sensorimotor Alignment Metric

This section gives the full computation of $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ summarized in Section 2 : the reference datasets and how we sample from them, the trajectory distance that defines motor neighbors, and the estimator that scores alignment between sensory and motor neighbor sets.


#### A.1 Reference Datasets and Sampling

We use three reference datasets: the single-arm DROID and BridgeDataV2 datasets for VLAs, raw VLMs, frozen vision encoders, and real-robot policies, and the bimanual ALOHA dataset for WAMs. DROID and BridgeDataV2 contain teleoperated robot demonstrations with RGB images, proprioceptive states, and language task annotations. Each state is a 7D vector $\mathbf{s}=(x,y,z,\phi,\theta,\psi,g)$ , where $(x,y,z)$ is the end-effector position in meters, $(\phi,\theta,\psi)$ are the roll, pitch, and yaw angles in radians, and $g$ is the recorded gripper position. This shared format supports the same DTW and $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ computation on both single-arm datasets.

BridgeDataV2. BridgeDataV2 [ 49 ] contains 53,192 WidowX episodes recorded at 5 Hz. We use the recorded 7D state and retain 38,307 episodes with non-empty task labels.

DROID. DROID [ 26 ] contains 95,658 episodes on a Franka Panda arm recorded at 15 Hz, spanning 86 task categories across 564 scenes in offices, kitchens, and homes. We form the 7D state by concatenating the 6D Cartesian end-effector pose with the gripper position, which is $0$ when open and $1$ when closed. We retain episodes with a language annotation. As the observation image, we use the exterior_image_1_left camera at $180\!\times\!320$ , the fixed exterior view closest to the third-person camera of BridgeDataV2.

ALOHA. For WAMs evaluated on bimanual RoboTwin, we sample $N=839$ trajectory windows from the bimanual ALOHA dataset [ 61 ] . Each trajectory row is the recorded 14-dimensional joint-position action vector, with six joints and a gripper coordinate per arm.

Anchor sampling across horizons. We compute $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ at several trajectory horizons $h$ . To keep these evaluations comparable, we draw one sample set at a maximum horizon $H_{\max}$ and reuse it at every shorter horizon. The sampling rule draws an integer anchor index $t_{i}$ for episode $i$ of length $L_{i}$ uniformly from $\{0,\ldots,L_{i}-1-H_{\max}\}$ . At horizon $1\leq h\leq H_{\max}$ , row $u$ of $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}$ is the recorded state at frame $t_{i}+u$ , for $u=1,\ldots,h$ . The image at frame $t_{i}$ and the task instruction form the fixed sensory input $\mathbf{x}_{i}$ , whose embedding $\mathbf{z}_{i}$ is computed once. The horizon sweep therefore changes only the trajectory length, not the episodes, anchors, or sensory inputs. On BridgeDataV2, we set $H_{\max}\!=\!40$ . From a pool of 7,762 episodes with $L_{i}>40$ , we retain non-empty task labels and at most three episodes per distinct task instruction, leaving $N=3{,}245$ . We sweep $h\in\{1,3,7,15,25,40\}$ . On DROID, we set $H_{\max}\!=\!220$ , which covers about the same fraction of the dataset-wide mean episode length. The sampling rule also requires $L_{i}\geq 2H_{\max}\!+\!1\!=\!441$ so that anchors are not concentrated at the start of short episodes. For the $N\!=\!6{,}000$ retained episodes, we sweep $h\!\in\!\{1,3,7,15,25,40,75,110,150,220\}$ .


#### A.2 Trajectory Distance

For BridgeDataV2 and DROID, we compare end-effector trajectories in each robot’s recorded base frame. We do not transform trajectories into a shared world or camera frame. Appendix C.2 compares this choice with positions expressed relative to the start of each trajectory.

Local cost. For the single-arm trajectories, let $\mathbf{p}_{i,u}=(x_{i,u},y_{i,u},z_{i,u})^{\top}$ and let $\mathbf{q}_{i,u}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}$ be the unit quaternion obtained from the recorded roll, pitch, and yaw using extrinsic rotations about the $x$ , $y$ , then $z$ axes. Quaternions $\mathbf{q}$ and $-\mathbf{q}$ represent the same rotation in $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ , the group of three-dimensional rotations. For steps $u$ and $v$ of episodes $i$ and $j$ , the local cost is

[Tableau] $\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{pos}}$ $\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{p}_{i,u}-\mathbf{p}_{j,v}\|_{2},\qquad d_{\mathrm{grip}}=|g_{i,u}-g_{j,v}|,$ $\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{rot}}$ $\displaystyle=2\arccos\!\left(\min\{1,|\mathbf{q}_{i,u}^{\top}\mathbf{q}_{j,v}|\}\right),$ $\displaystyle c_{uv}$ $\displaystyle=w_{\mathrm{pos}}d_{\mathrm{pos}}+w_{\mathrm{rot}}d_{\mathrm{rot}}+w_{\mathrm{grip}}d_{\mathrm{grip}}.$

The pair $(i,j)$ is suppressed in $c_{uv}$ and the component distances. All weights default to one. Components use meters, radians, and recorded gripper values without further scaling.

Dynamic time warping. We use the symmetric2 DTW recurrence with no warping window. For a fixed trajectory pair, let $D_{uv}$ be the accumulated cost through steps $u,v$ , with

[Tableau] $$D_{11}=c_{11},\qquad D_{u1}=\sum_{a=1}^{u}c_{a1},\qquad D_{1v}=\sum_{b=1}^{v}c_{1b}.$$

For $u,v\geq 2$ ,

[Tableau] $$D_{uv}=\min\{D_{u-1,v}+c_{uv},\ D_{u,v-1}+c_{uv},\ D_{u-1,v-1}+2c_{uv}\}.$$

For two $h$ -step trajectories, the distance is $D_{hh}$ without path-length normalization; $h=1$ gives the single-state cost $c_{11}$ . ALOHA trajectories (Appendix A.1 ) use the same DTW recurrence with Euclidean distance between 14-dimensional joint-position vectors as the local cost.


#### A.3 Alignment Estimator

We follow the progression from HSIC to CKA and centered kernel nearest-neighbor alignment (CKNNA) in Appendix A of Huh et al. [21] , specializing their construction to a sensory kernel and a motor neighbor matrix. CKA scores alignment across all sample pairs; CKNNA restricts the comparison to mutual nearest neighbors, which measures alignment locally.

Similarity matrices. For the $N$ paired samples in Section 2 , normalize sensory features as

[Tableau] $$\widehat{\mathbf{z}}_{i}=\frac{\mathbf{z}_{i}}{\max(\|\mathbf{z}_{i}\|_{2},\varepsilon_{z})},\qquad\varepsilon_{z}=10^{-12}.$$

Let $\mathbf{K},\mathbf{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}$ denote the sensory and motor matrices used in the score, with entries

[Tableau] $$K_{ij}=\widehat{\mathbf{z}}_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{z}}_{j},\qquad L_{ij}=\mathbb{1}\{j\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{motor}}(i)\}.$$

Here $K_{ij}=\cos(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j})$ , with zero features mapped to zero, so $\mathbf{K}$ is the inner-product (linear) kernel on $\ell_{2}$ -normalized features used by Huh et al. [21] . The motor set $\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{motor}}(i)$ contains the $k$ other samples with smallest DTW distance, as defined in Appendix A.2 . Thus DTW selects motor neighbors, and $\mathbf{L}$ assigns them unit weight; $\mathbf{L}$ is a directed similarity matrix, not necessarily a positive-semidefinite kernel. For $1\leq k<N$ , we write $\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{K}}(i)$ and $\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{L}}(i)$ for the sets of $k$ other sample indices with the largest entries in row $i$ of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{L}$ ; these are the sensory set $\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{sensory}}(i)$ and motor set $\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{motor}}(i)$ from Section 2 .

HSIC and centered kernel alignment. For two kernel matrices $\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}$ , let $\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{I}_{N}-\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}$ be the centering matrix, where $\mathbf{I}_{N}$ is the identity and $\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ is the all-ones vector. Define $\overline{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{H}$ and $\overline{\mathbf{B}}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{H}$ . The biased empirical Hilbert–Schmidt independence criterion (HSIC) and its normalized counterpart, centered kernel alignment (CKA) [ 27 ] , are

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathsf{HSIC}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})$ $\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(\overline{\mathbf{A}}\,\overline{\mathbf{B}})}{(N-1)^{2}},$ $\displaystyle\mathsf{CKA}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})$ $\displaystyle=\frac{\mathsf{HSIC}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})}{\sqrt{\mathsf{HSIC}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{A})\,\mathsf{HSIC}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{B},\mathbf{B})}}.$

Here $\operatorname{tr}$ denotes the trace and $\top$ denotes transpose; the subscript $\mathrm{b}$ identifies the biased empirical estimator. Expanding the trace writes HSIC as a sum over all ordered sample pairs,

[Tableau] $$\operatorname{tr}(\overline{\mathbf{A}}\,\overline{\mathbf{B}})=\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\overline{A}_{ij}\,\overline{B}_{ji},$$

so CKA measures alignment globally, across every pair of samples, and is defined when both self terms are positive [ 21 , 27 ] . Restricting this sum to mutual nearest neighbors, as below, biases the measure toward local structure.

For nearest-neighbor alignment, we use the diagonal-corrected estimator of Song et al. [44] , as in the released CKNNA implementation of Huh et al. [21] . 1 1 https://github.com/minyoungg/platonic-rep/blob/main/metrics.py Let $\widetilde{\mathbf{A}},\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}$ be copies of $\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}$ with zero diagonals. For $N>3$ , define

[Tableau] $$\widehat{\mathsf{HSIC}}_{\mathrm{u}}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})=\frac{1}{N(N-3)}\left[\operatorname{tr}(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}})+\frac{(\mathbf{1}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{1})(\mathbf{1}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}\mathbf{1})}{(N-1)(N-2)}-\frac{2\,\mathbf{1}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}\mathbf{1}}{N-2}\right].$$

The subscript $\mathrm{u}$ denotes this unbiased estimator, which corrects the finite-sample bias of HSIC under i.i.d. sampling. We apply the same algebra to the directed neighbor matrices: the trace term is $\sum_{i,j}\widetilde{A}_{ij}\widetilde{B}_{ji}$ , and we do not symmetrize the matrices.

Centered kernel nearest-neighbor alignment. For $\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\{\mathbf{K},\mathbf{L}\}$ , using the neighbor sets $\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{K}}$ and $\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{L}}$ defined above, we define the shared-neighbor mask for each matrix pair by

[Tableau] $$\alpha_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})=\mathbb{1}\{j\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{A}}(i)\cap\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{B}}(i)\},$$

where $\mathbb{1}\{\cdot\}$ is one when its condition holds and zero otherwise. Self-neighbors are excluded, and motor-neighbor indices are reused across models. The mask is directed: $\alpha_{ij}$ need not equal $\alpha_{ji}$ . As in Huh et al. [21] , “mutual” means membership in both neighbor sets.

Using $\odot$ for entrywise multiplication, define

[Tableau] $$\mathsf{Align}_{\mathrm{knn}}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})=\widehat{\mathsf{HSIC}}_{\mathrm{u}}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})\odot\mathbf{A},\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})\odot\mathbf{B}\right).$$

This follows the released CKNNA code’s masking-before-HSIC computation. Separate masks cover the cross term $(\mathbf{K},\mathbf{L})$ and the self terms $(\mathbf{K},\mathbf{K})$ and $(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{L})$ . Because $\mathbf{L}$ is binary, $\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\mathbf{K},\mathbf{L})\odot\mathbf{L}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\mathbf{K},\mathbf{L})$ : shared neighbors retain sensory similarities and receive unit motor weights.

The reported score follows the CKNNA normalization:

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{S}^{2}=\frac{\mathsf{Align}_{\mathrm{knn}}(\mathbf{K},\mathbf{L})}{\sqrt{\max\!\left\{\mathsf{Align}_{\mathrm{knn}}(\mathbf{K},\mathbf{K})\,\mathsf{Align}_{\mathrm{knn}}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{L}),\varepsilon_{A}\right\}}},\qquad\varepsilon_{A}=10^{-10}.$$ (2)

Following the relaxation of Huh et al. [21] , treating all shared neighbors as equidistant replaces the sensory weights by one, and the resulting count of shared neighbors recovers the overlap of Equation 1 : $\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{ij}(\mathbf{K},\mathbf{L})=Nk\,m_{\mathrm{NN}}$ , so $m_{\mathrm{NN}}=(Nk)^{-1}\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{ij}(\mathbf{K},\mathbf{L})\in[0,1]$ . The directed, centered score $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ keeps the sensory weights and is not guaranteed to lie in this interval.

#### B.6 Frozen Vision Encoders Under Downstream Policy Learning

[20] provide a benchmark: they evaluate 14 pretrained vision backbones (9 ResNet-50 [ 17 ] and 5 ViT-B/16 [ 12 ] ) on 21 tasks in Meta-World [ 55 ] , Robosuite [ 62 ] , and Franka-Kitchen [ 16 ] . Behavior cloning (BC) learns from expert demonstrations, and we use the smallest budget of 1 demonstration per task (5 on Robosuite). The visual reward function (VRF) method uses reinforcement learning, with rewards computed from distances between the backbone’s embeddings of agent and expert observations.

#### C.3 DTW Alignment

$\mathrm{S}^{2}$ aligns trajectory pairs with DTW before measuring their distance, so the same motion at different speeds counts as similar. To test whether the correlation depends on this warping, we compare DTW with the lockstep distance $d_{\mathrm{lock}}(i,j)=\sum_{u=1}^{h}c_{uu}$ , which matches frames by time index using the local cost in Appendix A.2 . The lockstep sum weights every matched state once, whereas symmetric2 DTW weights diagonal transitions twice. Table 6 reports the comparison. DTW raises $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ for all 12 VLAs from $h\!=\!7$ onward, and the gain grows with the horizon, from $1.2\%$ at $h\!=\!7$ to $34.1\%$ at $h\!=\!220$ , since longer trajectories contain more variation in speed. Because DTW raises the $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ of all models by a similar amount, $(34\pm 4)\%$ (mean $\pm$ standard deviation across models) at $h\!=\!220$ , the correlation with task success barely changes. Across the 10 horizons, $r$ ranges from ${+}0.617$ to ${+}0.713$ without DTW and from ${+}0.610$ to ${+}0.717$ with DTW, a relative difference between $-3.2\%$ and $+0.6\%$ . The $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ –success-rate correlation is therefore not an artifact of warping.

#### C.4 Sensitivity to Embodiment Mismatch

For the negative control in Section 3.3 , we score 3 bimanual WAMs evaluated on RoboTwin with two reference datasets that differ in arm configuration, as shown in Figure 12 . With the matched bimanual ALOHA reference [ 61 ] , $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ recovers the success-rate order of all 3 models, at $r\!=\!{+}0.999$ . With the single-arm DROID reference, the correlation drops to $r\!=\!{+}0.246$ with $p\!=\!0.421$ . $\mathrm{S}^{2}$ therefore responds to the mismatch in arm configuration rather than reporting alignment indiscriminately. This sensitivity complements the stability across single-arm references in Section 3.3 , where DROID and BridgeDataV2 differ in robot, scenes, and tasks yet give nearly the same correlation for the 12 VLAs. A reference dataset therefore need not come from the exact target robot, but it should match the target’s arm configuration.
