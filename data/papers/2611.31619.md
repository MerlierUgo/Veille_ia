## What a Forecasting Leaderboard Can Establish: Resolution Reporting for Zero-Shot Time-Series Models

A foundation model for time series is pretrained once and then asked to forecast series it has never seen, so the evidence for it is its score on held-out datasets. Such datasets are scarce, and a few public leaderboards carry most comparisons, yet none of them states how small a difference its datasets can detect. We call that quantity the resolution of a board and introduce Vernier , a tool that computes it from the files a board already publishes. Vernier also reports which entries a board cannot separate from its leader and whether its order changes when unrelated parties submit. The ten places of its top ten hold fewer than two resolvable steps. fev-bench , Chronos Benchmark II and TIME show the same pattern: their resolution is 3.6 to 19 times the gap between neighbours. We release Vernier, which runs this report with one command on any leaderboard that publishes per-dataset scores: https://github.com/therealram18/vernier .

### 1 Introduction

Foundation models for time series are pretrained on large heterogeneous corpora and then applied to series that were absent from training. The claim that distinguishes them is transfer to datasets nobody chose in advance, so the evidence has to span many domains, sampling frequencies and horizons. Independent public forecasting datasets are scarce, so comparisons run through a few shared boards. The one with the most entries is GIFT-Eval [ 1 ] . It evaluates every submission on the same 97 configurations, each one dataset at one sampling frequency and forecast horizon. It displays a strict order over 128 entries, and its per-configuration scores are public. Papers cite a position on it as evidence of state-of-the-art accuracy [ 2 ] .

Every measurement has a resolution, the smallest difference it can separate from noise. A board measures accuracy with a sample of datasets, and no forecasting board states the resolution of that sample. GIFT-Eval’s 97 configurations come from 28 datasets, four of which contribute eight configurations each, and the configurations of one dataset rise and fall together. Its evidence is 28 independent draws, and its top ten spans fewer than two of the steps they can resolve (Figure 1 a).


##### Vernier.

We treat a board as a measuring instrument and report what it can resolve. The report answers three questions from a board’s published scores alone (Section 3 ). How small a difference can it detect? Which displayed entries does it separate? Does its order depend on who else submitted?


##### Findings.

(i) GIFT-Eval resolves 6.8 rank places against a median gap of 0.91 between displayed neighbours. Closing that gap would take about 48 times as many datasets (Section 4 ). (ii) The test behind that number holds its level with 28 datasets. It declares a false difference 5.2% of the time, against 6 to 10% for a percentile bootstrap (Appendix A.2 ). (iii) The resolution exceeds the median gap on every board built from 15 to 39 public data sources (Appendix C ). It does not on BOOM, whose 2,554 series each count as a source. (iv) Rules that rank against the pool reversed 43 untouched top-ten pairs on four boards. (v) No board records how much history a model receives. Cutting it to 512 steps raised the error of three of four open models (Section 5 ).


### 2 Related work


##### Uncertainty on leaderboards.

Two forecasting boards already attach uncertainty to their entries. fev-bench reports paired bootstrap intervals over its tasks [ 3 ] , and TS-Arena collects forecasts before the outcome exists and reports intervals on its ratings [ 4 ] . Outside forecasting, rank intervals exist for language-model and tabular leaderboards [ 5 , 6 ] , and a quantization audit fixes the detectable difference before any run [ 7 ] . A mean-rank comparison between two entries is known to depend on the rest of the pool [ 8 , 9 ] , and that dependence has been measured on language-model arenas [ 10 ] . An interval describes one comparison. No forecasting board states the smallest difference that its datasets can detect, or tests whether its order depends on the pool.


##### Evaluation practice for pretrained forecasters.

Recent work revisits long-horizon evaluation [ 11 ] , audits pretraining corpora for contamination [ 12 ] and sweeps the history length given to a model [ 13 ] . We ask what a board must record for two evaluations of one model to agree.


### 3 What Vernier computes

Vernier reads one file, a score for every entry on every evaluation unit (a configuration on GIFT-Eval, a task on fev-bench), and answers three questions. Appendix A.4 states when each claim holds.


##### How small a difference can the board detect?

The units are a sample of the domains a pretrained model might meet, so the difference between two entries carries sampling error. Units from one source are not independent, so the source is the unit of sampling. We estimate the standard error (SE) of the mean difference with CR2, a cluster-robust estimator corrected for few clusters, with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom $\nu$ [ 14 , 15 ] . The resolution of a pair is the smallest true difference that a 5% test detects with 80% power, $(t_{0.975,\nu}+t_{0.80,\nu})\,\mathrm{SE}$ , the minimum detectable effect [ 16 ] . A board’s resolution is the median over the neighbouring pairs of its top ten.


##### Which entries cannot be separated from the leader?

A pair separates when the CR2 $t$ -test rejects a zero difference at the 5% level. We report counts uncorrected and after a Holm correction for multiple comparisons [ 17 ] . We repeat every test with a wild cluster bootstrap, which gives each source’s differences one random sign [ 18 ] . We also run its exact form, which lists every sign pattern. The exact form holds its level for any number of sources when the two entries are interchangeable within each source (Appendix A.3 ). Entries that do not separate from the leader form its tier .


##### Does the order depend on who else submitted?

A mean-rank rule scores an entry against the rest of the pool (it is pool-dependent ), so an unrelated submission can move it. We replay the board’s version history and skip any update in which the evaluation set changed. At each update we take the entries that sat in the displayed top ten beforehand and whose own result and metadata files are unchanged. We count the pairs among them that reverse. This is the bystander test .

##### Ten displayed places, fewer than two resolvable steps.

GIFT-Eval’s 28 datasets resolve 6.8 rank places. The median gap between displayed neighbours is 0.91, and the top ten spans 11.0 (Figure 1 a). Of the nine neighbouring pairs, the CR2 test separates one before a Holm correction (places 9 and 10, $p=0.036$ ). After the correction no neighbouring pair separates, under CR2, the wild cluster bootstrap or the exact tests. Each of the nine gaps is smaller than its own pair’s resolution. Closing the median gap would take about 48 times as many datasets (Appendix B.1 ).


##### The same report on five more boards.

We ran Vernier on five more forecasting boards that publish per-entry, per-unit scores: fev-bench and its 20-task subset [ 3 ] , Chronos Benchmark II [ 19 ] , TIME [ 20 ] and BOOM [ 21 ] (Table 1 ). On every board built from 15 to 39 public data sources the resolution is 3.6 to 19 times the median gap. At most one of nine neighbouring pairs separates under mean rank, win rate, skill score or geometric mean. With every family that shares a data source with GIFT-Eval removed, fev-bench still separates no neighbouring pair. On ten subsets of GIFT-Eval that its app shows as tabs, the ratio is 6.9 to 43 and no neighbouring pair separates. In November 2026 the newly released Timestral [ 22 ] sits 11th, 3rd and 4th on GIFT-Eval, TIME and fev-bench, and no board separates it from either neighbour. BOOM is the board that resolves. With each of its 2,554 series as a source, its resolution is 0.63 of its median gap, and all nine neighbours separate. Grouped into its 23 domains, five of the nine separate: independent sources set the resolution, not units.


##### Below the resolution, the pool sets the order.

A mean-rank rule ranks an entry against every other entry on each configuration, so a new arrival displaces entries that nobody reran. The displacement is small, and it reverses a pair whose gap is smaller. In the week to 2026-09-07 GIFT-Eval took three submissions from three unrelated organisations and changed nothing else. The order of Falcon-Agent, CastStar and limix_moe reversed completely (Appendix B.3 ). On fev-bench, second and third place (ChakraTS and TimesFM-3) reversed on 2026-10-14 when an unrelated entry arrived. On the four boards whose history we could replay, pool-dependent rules reversed 43 untouched top-ten pairs, in 31 of 226 updates (Tables 1 and 8 ). GIFT-Eval reversed 8 more in the ten weeks after the snapshot. The cause is the rule. The geometric mean of scaled error, an entry’s error divided by that of a seasonal-naive baseline, uses only the entry’s own scores (it is pool-independent ), so no arrival can reverse an untouched pair. Below the resolution, a displayed order also records who else submitted.


##### Recommendations based on tiers.

All ten entries at the top of GIFT-Eval are agentic pipelines, systems that select or combine several forecasters per series. Nine of the ten declare no reproduction code. The strict order, taken at face value, recommends a system a practitioner cannot rebuild. Under the displayed rule the leading tier has three entries. Under the pool-independent rule it has 17 entries under CR2, and 16 under a signed-rank test that holds its level on that rule. TimesFM-3, a pretrained model released with weights, is in both (Appendix B.2 ). The recommendation changes with the rule, so the evidence does not settle the choice between an agentic pipeline and a pretrained model.


### 5 Varying the history

Resampling published scores cannot expose sensitivity to how an evaluation was set up, because score files record no settings. The choice left most open is the length of history a model receives. We ran four open models at two history lengths, inference only: Chronos-Bolt-base [ 19 ] , Moirai-2.0-R-small [ 23 ] , TimesFM-2.5-200M [ 24 ] and Toto-Open-Base-1.0 [ 25 ] . The runs cover all 70 GIFT-Eval configurations whose median series holds at least 1,024 steps, from 18 datasets in 14 sources. The first length is the value in GIFT-Eval’s own reference notebook for that model, and the second is 512 steps. Twelve configurations, chosen by a rule fixed in advance, form a discovery set, and the other 58 a confirmation set. The estimate and the tests were fixed in advance (Appendix D ).

##### History length moves three of four models.

On the 70 configurations the shorter history raised the error of TimesFM-2.5 and of Toto by 15.3% and that of Moirai-2.0 by 6.2%. All three separate from zero after a Holm correction (Table 2 ), and they separate again on the confirmation set. For TimesFM-2.5 and Toto the error rises on 64 of the 70 configurations and in 13 of the 14 sources (one-sided sign test over sources, $p<0.001$ ). No effect is established for Chronos-Bolt. The estimates for TimesFM-2.5 and Toto lie above the 11.8% spread of the displayed top ten. Their intervals start near 4%, so a test against that spread does not reject. On the discovery set the newer TimesFM-3 and Timestral-209M rise by 31.8% and 28.9%. A documented length is also an upper limit: at the median evaluation window, 13 of the 48 discovery-set runs received less history than documented (Figure 3 ). Sampling is a second unrecorded setting. With one point forecast per series, Toto misses the board’s rows by 5.5%. With the reference notebook’s setting, the median of 256 samples, it reproduces them to 0.02%. We attribute the gap mainly to the sample count. A board should record the history received and the sampling setting.


### 6 Conclusion

Zero-shot forecasting boards display an order that is finer than their datasets can support. Below the resolution, a place reflects who else submitted and how the model was run as much as how well it forecasts. Shared boards remain the right way to compare these models, read at the precision they have. A board that reports its resolution, ranks with a pool-independent rule, shows tiers and records how each model was run loses nothing that it could measure. What no report can add is evidence. With each of its 2,554 series as an independent source, BOOM resolves its top ten, and the other boards cannot with a few dozen datasets. Better comparisons will come from more independent data, not from more submissions to the same datasets.

### Appendix A Method and validity


#### A.1 Details of the computation

Section 3 defines the report. For a displayed statistic and two entries, the difference on one evaluation unit is a difference of ranks under a mean-rank rule and of log scaled errors under a geometric mean. The displayed gap is the mean of these differences. The scaled error of an entry on a GIFT-Eval configuration is its MASE divided by the Seasonal Naive baseline’s MASE on the same configuration.


##### CR2 and its degrees of freedom.

Sources are the clusters. The working model treats the units of one source as equally correlated, with the pair’s own estimated correlation floored at zero. The degrees of freedom $\nu$ come from Satterthwaite’s approximation under that model [ 14 , 15 ] . Over the 45 top-ten pairs of GIFT-Eval $\nu$ runs from 11.41 to 17.07 under mean rank on MASE. The multiplier $t_{0.975,\nu}+t_{0.80,\nu}$ of the resolution is then about 3.0, against 2.80 in the large-sample limit. Holm corrections run over the nine neighbouring pairs when we count neighbours and over the 45 pairs of the top ten when we count all pairs. The leading tier of a rule holds the leader and every entry that the uncorrected test does not separate from it, among the first 40 entries in that rule’s own order.


##### Agreement with reference software.

On the 45 GIFT-Eval pairs under mean rank on MASE, the R package clubSandwich 0.7.0 [ 26 ] gives the same CR2 standard errors and, under independent errors, the same degrees of freedom. The largest relative difference is $2.5\times 10^{-15}$ . The R package dfadjust 1.1.0 computes the degrees of freedom of Imbens and Kolesár [15] . Given its correlation estimate our formula reproduces its values to $1.5\times 10^{-14}$ , and with its correlation floored at zero it gives exactly our counts. With its default, which does not floor the correlation, the same 1 of 9 neighbours and 12 of 45 pairs separate, and no neighbour after the Holm correction. The Python package wildboottest 0.3.2 gives the same $t$ statistic to $9\times 10^{-16}$ , and all 45 of its $p$ -values lie within 0.0048 of ours.


#### A.2 Does the test hold its level with 28 datasets?

A test over 28 clusters can reject too often. We measured how often four procedures declare a difference when there is none (Table 3 ). The first two are CR2 and the wild cluster bootstrap [ 18 , 27 ] . The third is a $t$ -test on the 28 dataset means, which weights every dataset equally. The fourth is a percentile bootstrap, under which a pair separates when its 95% intervals from resampling configurations and from resampling whole datasets both exclude zero. CR2 was named in advance as the primary procedure, to be replaced only if it fell outside 4 to 6% on the resampled real pairs under mean rank on MASE. Its rate there is 5.2%.

CR2 holds 4.4 to 5.8% in every setting but the most extreme one, where it reaches 6.8%. ##### The geometric mean.

On resampled real geometric-mean pairs these four procedures reject too often (Table 3 , last row). The rate is about 16% for CR2, the wild bootstrap and the percentile bootstrap, and 8.4% for the $t$ -test on dataset means. The cause is concentration. In 28 of the 45 pairs one dataset supplies more than half of the variance estimate, with a median share of 61% and a maximum of 93%. That dataset is most often solar , which contributes 8 configurations. Under mean rank the median share is 31% and 3 pairs exceed one half, because ranks are bounded. On TIME, one dataset supplies more than half of the variance in 18 of 45 pairs under the geometric mean and in 7 under mean rank. A pool-independent rule removes the dependence on other entries and concentrates each comparison on few sources. A board that adopts one should publish, beside each comparison, the largest share of the variance that one source supplies.


##### A test that holds its level on the geometric mean.

Four candidate tests, their order and a pass rule were fixed in advance. A test passes when, over 1,000 resamples of the 28 datasets per pair, its pooled false-alarm rate lies in 4 to 6%, at most 2 of the 45 pairs exceed 7.5% and none exceeds 10%. One candidate passes, the exact Wilcoxon signed-rank test with datasets as the units. It averages a pair’s differences in log scaled error within each dataset, ranks the 28 averages by absolute size, and compares the signed sum of ranks with all $2^{28}$ sign patterns. It is the test of Proposition 1 with a rank statistic. Its pooled rate is 5.6% and its worst pair 7.5%. The worst pair of CR2 in the same resamples is 37.0%. Sign-flip tests on the dataset means, with and without clipping, fail at 8.5 to 17.5%. The signed-rank test asks whether the typical dataset favours one entry, with each dataset counted once. It does not test the board’s configuration-weighted mean, and no test of that mean held its level at 28 datasets. On the displayed top ten it separates 1 of 9 neighbouring pairs and 12 of 45 pairs under the geometric mean. After a Holm correction it separates no neighbour and 1 of the 45. The conclusion under the pool-independent rule is therefore the same: no neighbouring pair separates after correction.


#### A.3 An exact test for interchangeable entries

Let $D_{g}$ hold the differences between two entries on the units of source $g$ , and let $T$ be any statistic of $D=(D_{1},\dots,D_{G})$ . Swapping the labels of the two entries inside source $g$ replaces $D_{g}$ by $-D_{g}$ . Under a rank rule with tied ranks averaged, the ranks of all other entries stay as they were.

Suppose the two entries are interchangeable within every source: the joint distribution of $(D_{1},\dots,D_{G})$ is unchanged when any subset of the $D_{g}$ change sign. Let $p(D)=2^{-G}\,\#\{s\in\{-1,+1\}^{G}:T(s_{1}D_{1},\dots,s_{G}D_{G})\geq T(D)\}$ . Then $\Pr\{p(D)\leq\alpha\}\leq\alpha$ for every $\alpha$ , every number of sources $G\geq 1$ and every set of source sizes.

The $2^{G}$ sign changes form a group, and the null distribution is invariant under it. The bound is the standard property of a randomization test [ 28 , Chapter 17] . ∎

With 28 datasets all $2^{28}$ sign patterns can be listed, so the test needs no random draws. We ran it with two statistics, the mean difference over its clustered standard error and the plain sum of the differences. The wild cluster bootstrap with the null imposed is the Monte Carlo form of the first. Each draw gives every dataset one sign with equal probability. The $p$ -value $(1+b)/(1+B)$ counts the observed statistic as one draw, so it obeys the same bound for any number of draws $B$ . We used $B=99{,}999$ . On every pair its $p$ -value is within 0.0035 of the exact one, and no neighbour or Holm decision differs (Table 5 ). The guarantee needs no large number of sources, no equal sizes and no thin tails. Table 3 agrees with it: the wild bootstrap rejects 4.6 to 5.4% in the five settings without lopsided dataset effects, and 5.9 and 7.0% in the two settings with them.


#### A.4 When each claim holds


##### What the tests answer.

They answer whether the displayed order would survive an equally reasonable draw of datasets from the population this board samples. On the fixed evaluation set the published means are exact.


##### The unit of sampling.

Datasets are the independent unit, and configurations of one dataset are correlated. Rolling windows and repeat runs vary inside a configuration’s score, so the standard error between datasets already contains their variation. A setting such as the history length is the same on every dataset, so no standard error contains it (Section 5 ).


##### How many more datasets.

The figure of 48 times as many datasets holds when new datasets vary as the current ones do. On the 25 fev-bench families that share no data source with GIFT-Eval the noise scale is 1.25 times as large, so the figure is a lower estimate (Appendix C ).


##### The history experiment.

The sign-flip test on source totals is exact when, with no true effect, the distribution of each source total is symmetric about zero. The changes are skewed toward a rise, which makes the test conservative for a rise. A false claim of a rise occurs in at most 1% of draws for the three models with an effect (Appendix D ). Each difference compares two runs of ours on one configuration, so it does not depend on any board row.

### Appendix D The history-length experiment


##### Configurations.

A configuration is eligible when the median series of its data holds at least 1,024 steps. A series shorter than 512 steps shows the model the same history at both lengths. Of GIFT-Eval’s 97 configurations 70 are eligible. They come from 18 base datasets, which we group into 14 sources: the three bizitobs datasets form one source, the two ETT datasets another and the two bitbrains datasets a third. Twelve of the 70, from 9 sources, form the discovery set. A rule fixed before any model ran chose them: 5 short, 3 medium and 4 long horizons in proportion to the eligible counts, all from distinct base datasets. The other 58 form the confirmation set.


##### Models.

The documented history length is the value in GIFT-Eval’s reference notebook for each model: 2,048 steps for Chronos-Bolt and 4,000 for Moirai-2.0. For TimesFM-2.5 the notebook passes the full history up to 15,360 steps, and Toto receives the smaller of 4,096 and the history available. The other setting gives every model the last 512 steps. Moirai-2.0’s checkpoint reports a maximum length of 512, and its notebook passes 4,000. On the discovery set the two settings gave different forecasts, with a median difference of 3.7%. Chronos-Bolt, Moirai-2.0 and TimesFM-2.5 are deterministic: their 72 values on the discovery set repeat on a second machine to a relative difference of $2.08\times 10^{-5}$ .


##### Effect and tests.

The effect of a model is the mean over configurations of the log of its MASE at 512 steps over its MASE at the documented length, turned back into a percentage. It equals the change in the geometric mean of scaled MASE. The first test asks whether the effect differs from zero. It is an exact sign-flip test on the 14 source totals, which lists all $2^{14}$ sign patterns. It is two-sided at 5% with a Holm correction over the four models, and the 95% interval inverts it. The second test asks whether the effect exceeds the spread of the displayed top ten on the same configurations. The spread is the largest of the ten entries’ geometric means of scaled MASE over the smallest, minus one. It is one-sided at 2.5%. The estimate, both tests and their order were fixed before any run.

On the 70 configurations the first test rejects after the Holm correction for Moirai-2.0, TimesFM-2.5 and Toto, and it rejects for the same three on the confirmation set (Table 9 ). ##### Error rate of the test.

The sign-flip test assumes changes that are symmetric when there is no effect, and these changes are skewed. We measured what the skew does. A check centres each model’s changes at zero, redraws them into the 14-source layout 20,000 times and counts the claims. For Moirai-2.0, TimesFM-2.5 and Toto, whose changes have their long tail on the side of a rise, the test errs toward a fall. It claims a rise in at most 1% of draws, against the 2.5% that one side of a 5% test allows, so it is conservative for the rise we report. Its false claims go to the other side, where the two-sided rate is 12 to 19%. TimesFM-2.5 and Toto also pass a test that uses directions only. For each of them the error rises on 64 of the 70 configurations and in 13 of the 14 sources. A sign test over the sources gives a one-sided $p$ of 0.0009. Chronos-Bolt’s changes are skewed the other way, and a false claim of a rise occurs for it in 4.75% of draws, above the 2.5% allowed.
