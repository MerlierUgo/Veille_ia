## Are Frontier VLM Agents Ready to Be Robot Generalists? An Empirical Study with the Embodied Agent Arena

A robot generalist must interpret a scene, act on it, and satisfy every condition of a task. How far do frontier vision-language models (VLMs) meet these demands? We introduce Embodied Agent Arena, evaluating ten VLM agents across Geometry, Spatial Reasoning, Affordance, Task Planning, and Manipulation. Its 1,000 cases combine 32 established sources with GeoProbe, our 168-case benchmark for camera, motion, depth, and scale estimation. A minimal harness leaves task decisions to the model through source-native interfaces. Closed-weight models lead all five domains, whereas open-weight VLMs show useful spatial reasoning but substantially lower planning and manipulation success. Beneath these averages, similar geometric errors conceal different scale and shape distortions, and valid region estimates need not identify usable action points. During execution, agents can achieve early subgoals yet lose object control, bind an action to the wrong receiving region, or stop before the required physical state holds. Additional computation does not uniformly resolve these failures: code repair can restore execution without completing the task, while self-review can reverse correct judgments. Failure costs also differ: the strongest planners concentrate failed-token expenditure in prolonged body-control tasks, whereas other models fail broadly across procedures. Matched traces show how successful recovery uses feedback to restore a missing prerequisite and advance the remaining task.

### 1 Introduction

A robot generalist must turn scene understanding into actions that satisfy a complete goal. In an object-placement task, locating the target and grasping it are intermediate steps; success also requires placing it at the specified destination. Vision-language models (VLMs) increasingly address spatial relations ( Ma et al., 2024 , Yang et al., 2024 ) and action-relevant grounding ( Wang et al., 2025a , Song et al., 2024 ) . Learned visuomotor policies connect observations to robot actions ( Kim et al., 2024 , Black et al., 2024 ) , while language-model controllers select skills or generate executable programs ( Ahn et al., 2022 , Liang et al., 2022 ) . Perception support and visual action rehearsal further extend these systems ( Fu et al., 2026 , Guo et al., 2026 , Li et al., 2026b , Zhang et al., 2026a ) . As these systems take on more diverse tasks, the central question becomes: Are frontier VLM agents ready to be robot generalists?

Answering this question requires identifying which task requirements remain unsatisfied. For example, a trajectory can closely follow a reference yet miss its endpoint, while approaching a seat and issuing a sit action need not establish a seated state. Average accuracy can obscure these failures, whereas final failure alone hides achieved subgoals. Evaluation must therefore distinguish prediction accuracy, achieved subgoals, and final task success.

To distinguish these requirements systematically, we introduce Embodied Agent Arena, evaluating ten VLM agents across Geometry, Spatial Reasoning, Affordance, Task Planning, and Manipulation (Figure 1 ). The arena combines established benchmarks with GeoProbe, whose controlled variations in camera motion, object motion, and scale references diagnose geometric estimation. A minimal harness retains source interfaces and leaves task decisions to the model. This framework separates metric precision, functional grounding, and native task success, while execution traces identify achieved subgoals and unmet requirements.

The comparison shows that closed-weight models lead all five domains, whereas open-weight VLMs complete far fewer Planning and Manipulation tasks. Yet the aggregate gaps conceal distinct errors. Comparable depth errors can arise from a common scale bias or from residual distortion; a region can overlap the correct functional support while its proposed action point misses that support. Execution makes these distinctions consequential: a valid placement can address the outer fixture instead of its basin, and a precise gripper motion can leave the object ungrasped. Longer sequences then require maintaining or restoring the physical state on which the next subgoal depends.

These findings also explain why additional computation has uneven value. Code repair may restore an admissible program without establishing the required object state, while self-review can fix coordinate bounds and introduce new relational errors. The costs are similarly structured: for the strongest planners, failed-token expenditure is concentrated in prolonged body-control attempts, whereas other models fail across a broader range of procedures. By connecting these distributions to matched action–feedback traces, the arena identifies whether recovery must change a target binding, repair a prerequisite state, or revise an unsupported claim of task completion.

Our contributions are:

- • An evaluation centered on complete task requirements. We evaluate prediction accuracy, functional grounding, subgoal achievement, and final task success across five domains.

- • A unified arena with a new geometric benchmark, GeoProbe. The 1,000-case arena combines 32 established sources with 168 GeoProbe cases, retaining native operations and success criteria in a shared model-directed evaluation framework.

- • A diagnosis of task failure after successful intermediate steps. Ten-model comparisons reveal complementary successes and shared failures; traces identify invalid endpoints, unmet goal conditions, and attempts that achieve no further subgoals.

Embodied evaluation has developed along two complementary directions. Control suites measure execution and task transfer ( James et al., 2019 , Mees et al., 2021 , Liu et al., 2023 ) . Varying environments and physical deployment broaden this evaluation to robustness and multiple dimensions of manipulation competence ( Chen et al., 2025a , Chen et al., 2026 ) . Visual diagnostics isolate spatial inference ( Ma et al., 2024 , Yang et al., 2024 ) and interaction grounding ( Song et al., 2024 , Qian & Fouhey, 2023 ) . Scene and task question answering ( Majumdar et al., 2024 , Sermanet et al., 2023 ) extends this diagnostic view to embodied decisions ( Li et al., 2024b , Qi et al., 2025 ) . Scene-linked assessment connects grounding, relational inference, and action to distinguish performance at individual stages from errors propagated between stages ( Ruan et al., 2026 ) . As agents undertake longer interactions, final success leaves important aspects of execution unresolved: adherence to constraints ( Park et al., 2026 ) , use of relevant history ( Luo et al., 2026 ) , and recognition of progress and recovery ( Liu et al., 2026 ) . These developments motivate measuring decisions within an episode alongside complete outcomes.

The transition from judgment to action depends on where control is represented. Learned policies map observations and instructions directly to actions ( Brohan et al., 2023 , Zitkovich et al., 2023 ) , with broader task coverage motivating generalist policies ( Ghosh et al., 2024 , Kim et al., 2024 , Black et al., 2024 ) . Language-mediated control instead composes supplied skills ( Ahn et al., 2022 , Huang et al., 2023b ) or executable programs ( Liang et al., 2022 , Singh et al., 2023 ) ; hybrid coordination selects between modular skills and learned policies from the current state ( Zhou et al., 2026a ) . Explicit programs make dependencies ( Fu et al., 2026 , Du et al., 2025 ) , geometric feedback ( Huang et al., 2023a ) , and reusable interfaces ( Li et al., 2026a ) available for model-directed composition. Evaluation consequently shifts from the availability of individual actions to the controller’s ability to coordinate a complete procedure.

Composition also requires evidence that remains useful as the scene changes. Visual grounding can fail under physical stress or misleading background cues ( Li et al., 2025 , Wu et al., 2026 , Huang et al., 2026a ) , motivating methods that repair context and reason over evolving spatial evidence ( Huang et al., 2026c , Zhao et al., 2026b , Zhao et al., 2026a ) . Demonstrations supply task context ( Jiang et al., 2023 , Huang et al., 2026b ) ; retained histories preserve state ( Zhang et al., 2026b ) ; verification checks whether execution meets its conditions ( Guo et al., 2024 , Huang et al., 2026d ) . This support changes what the overall system can accomplish ( Wang et al., 2026 ) , making the supplied interfaces and feedback part of the evaluation design.

Broader benchmarks bring perception and goal-directed interaction into one evaluation ( Yang et al., 2025a , Cheng et al., 2025 ) , extending visual probes toward navigation ( Ni et al., 2025 , Rasheed et al., 2026 ) and diverse manipulation tasks ( Zhang et al., 2024 ) . Table 1 compares their coverage, diagnostics, and scale. Embodied Agent Arena spans metric geometry, spatial inference, functional grounding, planning, and manipulation, with GeoProbe isolating camera, motion, and scale requirements. Its prediction metrics and execution records complement final task success by identifying estimation errors and unsatisfied goal conditions.

### 3 The Embodied Agent Arena


#### 3.1 Benchmark Construction and Capability Domains

Prediction, grounding, and interactive task success. The arena organizes 1,000 cases from 32 established sources and GeoProbe into five capability domains (Figures 1 – 2 ; Appendix A ). Each case specifies observations, a query or goal, available operations, and scoring. Geometry, Spatial Reasoning, and Affordance isolate what the agent must estimate or ground; Task Planning and Manipulation test whether decisions produce the required environment state. GeoProbe adds 168 cases that vary camera motion, object motion, and scale references in controlled Blender scenes, complemented by real images. Separating these factors makes it possible to distinguish a failure to infer the scene from a failure to complete an interaction.

Geometry: metric precision and feasibility. Agents estimate intrinsics, pose, depth, dimensions, or displacement from images. InFlux and Map-free test calibration and relative pose ( Liang et al., 2025 , Arnold et al., 2022 ) ; GeoProbe varies camera motion and scale references. TraceSpatial-Bench adds paths on 56 scenes ( Zhou et al., 2026b ) . Trajectory error quantifies path similarity; initiation, arrival, and collision checks test spatial feasibility.

Spatial Reasoning: relations and reference frames. Agents infer relations, viewpoints, configurations, and temporal order from images or video ( Ma et al., 2024 , Wang et al., 2025b , Yang et al., 2024 ) . Comparing object distances, imagining another observer’s view, and inferring camera motion require different reference-frame transformations. Questions specify the relevant frame and expect a category, quantity, or point set.

Affordance: contact and functional extent. Agents ground an intended action through a contact point or region. UMD tests tool functions such as cutting and scooping ( Myers et al., 2015 ) ; instruction- and action-conditioned 3DOI queries test interaction contacts ( Qian & Fouhey, 2023 ) . Point validity checks whether a predicted contact point lies inside the reference region. Region IoU measures intersection over union with the reference mask, using native boxes and, separately, direct polygons.

Task Planning: satisfying all goal conditions. Agents compose source-provided operations into household, navigation, or scientific procedures ( Shridhar et al., 2019 , Wang et al., 2022 ) , using visual, textual, or symbolic feedback. Compound goals require several conditions to hold, such as heating an object and placing it at the destination. For terminal state $s_{T}$ and required predicates $g_{1},\ldots,g_{K}$ , complete success is

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{Success}(s_{T},g)=\mathbf{1}\!\left[\bigwedge_{k=1}^{K}g_{k}(s_{T})\right].$$ (1)

Where available, condition-level records identify unsatisfied predicates in Equation ( 1 ); aggregate intermediate scores summarize partial task achievement.

Manipulation: contact conditions and sequential goals. Agents execute robot motions or skills for placement, insertion, articulation, and coordinated handling ( James et al., 2019 , Chen et al., 2025a ) . Language-conditioned variants change the target relation or object ( Zhang et al., 2024 ) ; sequential tasks require completing ordered subgoals from the states left by preceding actions. Native task success records completion, while condition-level scores expose achieved relations and control events.


#### 3.2 Unified Agent Harness

Task decisions remain with the model. The harness provides persistent interaction through each source’s observations and operations (Figure 2 d). Models interpret observations, select operations, and construct action sequences from feedback. We use native capability to mean these model-directed decisions through the supplied source interfaces, including their helpers. The baseline adds no perception or control modules beyond those interfaces. Geometry, Spatial Reasoning, Affordance, and Planning use a Python runtime; Manipulation follows its native agent loop. Persistent variables let the agent build on earlier computations, while images produced during a round enter the next model request. Appendix B.1 specifies the interfaces; the supplementary review study adds specialist measurements (Section 4.4 ) ( Zhang et al., 2025 , Huang et al., 2026d ) .

Termination is distinct from success. Fixed-input submissions commit predictions. Interactive episodes end on agent termination, an environment terminal condition, budget exhaustion, or an unrecovered API or execution error. The evaluator then checks the submitted output or environment state independently of the agent’s declaration. Declaring completion therefore cannot substitute for satisfying the goal. The execution budgets are specified in Appendix B.2 .

#### 3.3 Evaluation Protocol and Metrics

Task success and prediction accuracy. Spatial Reasoning and Planning report Pass@1; categorical spatial accuracy is also reported separately. Manipulation pairs native success with consecutive goals and task-specific condition or control-evidence scores. Geometry and numerical spatial queries use MAE, RMSE, AbsRel/MAPE, displacement error, or pose error as appropriate to the target. Tracing pairs trajectory RMSE with offline 3D constraint satisfaction.

Functional grounding. The main table reports box–mask IoU for native rectangles and polygon–mask IoU for direct polygons on the same cases. Both compare rasterized predictions with the reference mask. Contact validity, box–box IoU, and strict box success provide complementary diagnostics in the appendix.

Variation and resource use. Repeated metrics report the equally weighted mean and sample standard deviation (SD) of run-level scores. Continuous scores average valid predictions within each run; binary success rates retain all assigned cases, with error-terminated episodes scored as unsuccessful. Tokens, action calls, model rounds, and elapsed time describe different resources consumed before termination. Section 4.1 specifies repetitions, and Appendix B.3 defines aggregation and accounting.

#### 4.1 Experimental Setup

Models. We evaluate Astra, Sol, Fable, Opus 5.5, Qwen-Max , Gemini 3.8 Flash, Gemini 4 Argon, Qwen-397B , Qwen-27B , and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017 ( DeepSeek-1017 ). Qwen-397B , Qwen-27B , and DeepSeek-1017 have released VLM weights; the other evaluated visual models are closed-weight services. Appendix B.2 gives identifiers and release scope. DeepSeek-1017 uses the deepseek-flash endpoint updated on 17 October 2026.

Repetitions and execution conditions. Non-tracing Geometry, Spatial Reasoning, and point-and-box Affordance use five runs per case for nine models and three for Fable. We report the mean and sample SD across run-level scores. Tracing, direct polygons, Planning, and Manipulation each use one episode per assignment. Polygon IoU covers the same 70 Affordance cases for all ten agents (Appendix C.5 ). Planning and Manipulation retain all 157 and 182 binary assignments, respectively. Episodes terminated by API or execution errors count as unsuccessful, including two Qwen-Max HumanCLAW provider errors (Appendix B.4 ). All ten agents are evaluated on the same assigned tasks through source-native interfaces, with source-provided observations and native success criteria. #### 4.2 Main Results

Does perceptual strength predict execution success? Closed-weight models lead all five domains, with distinct profiles (Table 2 ). ① Broad strengths. Astra and Gemini 4 Argon occupy the top two positions in Spatial Reasoning and Planning, and the top three in Manipulation (Figure 3 ). ② Selective strengths. Qwen-Max nearly matches Astra in Spatial Pass@1, 68.9% versus 69.4%, but completes 36.9% versus 80.3% of Planning tasks. Conversely, Opus 5.5 leads Manipulation at 47.8% while trailing Astra and Fable in Planning. Fixed-input questions assess a supplied observation; interactive tasks additionally require choosing operations, tracking their effects, and meeting a terminal condition. These demands distinguish perceptual competence from procedural and control competence. ③ Open-weight execution gaps. Qwen-397B , Qwen-27B , and DeepSeek-1017 achieve 48.0–51.6% Spatial Pass@1, but only 19.1–31.2% Planning and 9.9–20.9% Manipulation success. Variation among closed-weight models likewise shows that release category does not specify a model’s execution strengths.

Where does model diversity help? Planning overlap separates broadly shared success from model-specific and shared failures (Figure 7 ). ① Broadly shared success is concentrated. Only eight of 157 tasks are solved by all ten models; seven belong to symbolic VirtualHome. Conversely, 39 tasks have only one or two successful models, including twenty ALFRED or ALFWorld tasks. The contrast places much of the disagreement in visually grounded household procedures, rather than distributing it evenly across sources. ② Complementarity has diminishing returns. Astra solves 126 tasks; adding Gemini 4 covers 137, and all ten cover 142. Thus one additional model supplies eleven of the sixteen extra successes, while the other eight add five. Yet the fifteen common failures are heterogeneous: eight sitting tasks, four ALFRED/ALFWorld procedures, two ScienceWorld experiments, and one television task. Their traces include both prolonged score plateaus and terminal failures after partial progress (Appendix J.6 ). These shared failures require different corrections.

Which rankings survive a different task mixture? ① A lead spanning sources. Removing ScienceWorld and VirtualHome lowers every Planning score, yet Astra still leads on the remaining 63 tasks at 66.7%, followed by Gemini 4 at 60.3% and Fable at 33.3%. On ALFRED and ALFWorld alone, Astra completes 29/40 and Gemini 4 30/40, versus 4–8/40 for each open-weight VLM. This advantage extends to visually grounded household procedures. ② A lead tied to source weight. Opus 5.5 leads Gemini 4 in Manipulation by 47.8% to 44.0% over assignments, but equal weighting of thirteen sources reverses their order to 44.5% versus 47.4% (Appendix J ). RoboTwin supplies 50/182 assignments and favors Opus, 23 versus fourteen successes, whereas Gemini 4 is stronger on CALVIN and RLBench. The same outcomes yield different leaders because the models specialize in different task collections. Aggregate superiority should therefore be read together with the distribution of task requirements.

Why do unsuccessful attempts dominate computation? Separating task frequency, source budgets, and recorded behavior changes the interpretation (Table A43 ). ① A costly residual in long interactions. HumanCLAW supplies only 13/157 tasks but 81.7% of Astra’s failed tokens; its eight sitting tasks alone supply 63.7%. These episodes allow 101 rounds and 2M tokens, versus eight rounds and 320k tokens for several other Planning sources. Excluding HumanCLAW lowers the failed-token share from 55.5% to 19.4% for Astra and from 64.0% to 33.5% for Gemini 4. Their failed-token expenditure is concentrated in a small, generously budgeted set of unresolved physical interactions. ② Failures spread across ordinary procedures. These shares remain 55.3–67.8% for the other closed-weight models and 73.6–85.8% for the three open-weight VLMs. The deficit persists in text-only ScienceWorld, beyond visual perception. On its matched animal-transfer task, DeepSeek spends three of eight decision rounds inspecting state and stops with 89 environment steps unused; Opus identifies the blue-jay egg as an animal and completes the focus-and-transfer procedure. Both execute without a code exception. Repeated inspection consumes scarce decision rounds without resolving which object satisfies the goal.


#### 4.3 Diagnosing Unmet Task Requirements

We distinguish errors in interpreting the observation, satisfying a precise condition, and completing dependent actions through diagnostic measurements and traces (Figures 4 – 8 ).

Geometry: which error remains after an apparently correct estimate? ① Scale bias and relative-shape error require different diagnoses. On GeoProbe depth, Gemini 4 and Qwen-397B have similar raw AbsRel, 0.299 and 0.312, but oracle scale alignment leaves 0.065 and 0.203. Gemini 4 has a median predicted/true depth ratio below one in all sixty case–run observations; much of its metric error is a common-scale component. The larger Qwen residual persists after per-case scalar rescaling. Astra combines 96.3% ordinal accuracy with 0.104 raw AbsRel, falling to 0.038 after alignment. Relative ordering, metric calibration, and residual shape thus separate models that a single error average would group together. ② Camera sensitivity differs from a motion-detection floor. With matched object interventions, Astra and Gemini 4 miss none of fifty moving observations under a fixed camera, but miss 26 and 37 under a large view change; neither falsely labels the stationary controls as moving. The additional errors therefore concern real object motion lost under camera change. In contrast, Qwen-27B misses all fifty moving observations in each camera condition. Its unchanged error is a failure floor, not robustness to the perturbation (Appendix D.1 ). ③ Projected endpoints do not settle physical constraints. Astra hits both image endpoint regions on 35/56 TraceSpatial paths, yet 21 of these fail a 3D boundary condition. Gemini 4 improves to 45 image-valid paths, with sixteen such failures, but has ten collision-only failures versus Astra’s one. The remaining failure profile differs across models: a path with valid endpoints must also respect intervening geometry. Joint checks distinguish projection, metric placement, and collision failures on the same trajectories (Appendix J.6 ).

Spatial Reasoning: is the failure in the relation or in returning an answer? ① Lateral errors persist across repetitions. All ten models reach 80–100% on BOP-ASK left/right and 90–100% on camera-depth ordering, but actor-relative sides separate them (Figure 5 ). Qwen-27B answers six of seven actor-left/right cases incorrectly in all five runs, despite 19/20 correct judgments on actor-front cases. Gemini 4 answers all 35 actor-left/right observations correctly. The repeated lateral errors span both left and right ground truths; they are neither a constant-side response nor a uniformly weak judgment of actor-centered relations. ② Video scores also include submission failures. Gemini 4 scores 30% on four hard video-direction cases, but its twenty runs contain six correct answers, four incorrect answers, and ten token-guard stops without an answer. On one question, three runs answer correctly and two never submit. Consequently, the low aggregate mixes relational mistakes with failure to deliver a decision under the execution budget. Separating these outcomes preserves the all-assignment score while identifying whether to improve spatial judgment or the delivery of a decision.

Affordance: which part of the action target is grounded? ① A valid point can leave the functional extent unresolved. Across 350 case–run predictions, Qwen-397B passes the point criterion alone 96 times, but both point and box criteria only eighteen times (Table A46 ). The matched pulling example shows the boundary error: all four displayed models select the handle but their contours cover too little of the annotated region, yielding 0.096–0.140 IoU (Figure A7 ). ② An accepted region need not supply a valid action point. Gemini 4 exhibits the reverse pattern sixteen times: its box passes, while its point lies outside the functional mask, although inside its own box. In a scissors-grasp case, rectangle IoU is 0.784 but the chosen point misses the grasp mask; this repeats in four of five runs. Region enclosure and selection of a usable point are distinct requirements, even for one correctly localized object. ③ Wrong-object selection precedes either geometric correction. In the matched RAGNet two-hand case, Opus 5.5 selects the referenced target and reaches 0.942 IoU; Astra, Fable, and DeepSeek outline other objects and score zero. A sharper boundary around the wrong object cannot repair the instruction–target binding. These cases separate selecting the entity, delineating its functional support, and choosing a point on that support.

Task Planning: does an executed action establish the required state? ① The right operation can target the wrong receiving region. On the matched ALFRED soap task, DeepSeek cleans the soap and places it in Bathtub , yet finishes with only 1/3 goal conditions. Gemini 3.8 initially reaches the same incomplete state, then picks up the soap and changes the destination to BathtubBasin , reaching 3/3; Qwen-Max also changes the receiving region after inspecting the parent receptacle and re-washing the soap (Table A45 ). The failure is more specific than forgetting a procedural step: a valid placement call refers to the outer fixture rather than its required basin. Feedback helps when it changes that binding, rather than merely repeating the intended operation. ② Attempted interaction can be mistaken for the terminal state. HumanCLAW sitting has no recorded success, but its process evidence separates target acquisition from interaction. Fable finds no target in eight episodes; Astra and Gemini 4 record both proximity within 20 cm and a sit action in five and seven, respectively (Table A27 ). On the same couch case, Gemini 4 and DeepSeek issue sit , then declare themselves seated and stop after 44 and 70 rounds; both fail the private native verifier. Here a visible approach and an issued command do not establish the requested body–object relation. The trace establishes a stopping misjudgment; the private verifier does not identify which pose or contact condition remained unmet. Qwen-397B and Qwen-27B likewise show proximity plus a sit action in one and two of eight episodes without success.

Manipulation: what must persist between successful actions? ① Starting a sequence is much easier than finishing it. Fable, Sol, Gemini 3.8, Astra, and Opus 5.5 reach CALVIN’s first goal in seven or eight of ten episodes, but reach the third in zero, one, four, seven, and four, respectively (Figure 8 a). Across all models, only nine of eighteen episodes reaching four goals finish the fifth. Aggregate first-goal success therefore hides both early and late attrition. ② Physical progress can be lost and rebuilt. The matched CALVIN sequence 0526 reveals one source of additional work. Opus and DeepSeek both finish under the same 40-round allowance, but use eighteen and 37 rounds. DeepSeek remains on block retrieval during rounds 17–27: measured block height returns to about 2 cm after a carrying attempt, and stacking begins only at round 28. Opus proceeds from retrieval at round 11 to stacking at round 13. The later subgoal inherits the actual object state left by preceding actions; an attempted carry can require renewed retrieval before the sequence advances. ③ Tool position and object state can disagree. On matched PlaceSphere, DeepSeek brings the gripper within 2.8 mm of its requested hover target, yet four later checks still return is_grasped=false ; the sphere shifts away from its initial location. Opus instead confirms a grasp, releases the sphere, and obtains the required on-bin and static conditions. Coordinate convergence alone therefore fails to certify transport. Contact-rich insertion imposes further constraints: Opus completes PickCube, PlaceSphere, and StackCube but fails PegInsertionSide and PlugCharger, whose charger criterion requires both 5 mm positional and 0.2 rad angular accuracy. These traces locate the missing evidence in grasp, object motion, and final contact conditions (Appendix J.6 ).


#### 4.4 When Feedback Changes the Outcome

Feedback can change an answer, make code executable, or help achieve a required state. We distinguish these outcomes before assessing the cost of further interaction.

Does reconsideration repair the error that determines acceptance? The paired review records distinguish changes in semantics, coordinate validity, and task acceptance (Table A34 ). ① Corrections compete with regressions. On eighteen Spatial cases, Qwen-27B ’s self-review fixes one wrong answer but changes two correct answers to wrong; crop review fixes two but breaks four. Both reviews change the correct “back” to “left” when judging which side of a girl faces a car. The net accuracy decrease therefore conceals genuine corrections alongside new reference-frame errors. Reinspection does not consistently preserve already-correct judgments. ② Valid coordinates are not sufficient grounding. Six of nine initial Affordance boxes extend beyond image bounds. Self-review repairs all six bounds violations, raising mean IoU from 0.034 to 0.152, yet no box reaches the 0.5 criterion. On a RAGNet scene, the revised point reaches the functional mask, while an entirely out-of-image rectangle becomes an in-image box with IoU 0.447. This is a concrete repair of representation validity, followed by a remaining error in functional extent. ③ Mean improvement and accepted corrections can diverge. Tool-assisted review produces a lower mean IoU of 0.145 but one accepted box; Astra also gains one accepted box. Thus a broadly improved set of still-insufficient predictions can yield fewer accepted boxes than a lower-mean set. The tool condition adds measurements and a larger response allowance (Appendix E.2 ); its aggregate gain describes that combined procedure. Paired self-/crop-review examples and their exact changes appear in Appendix J.6 .

Which feedback repairs the actual execution bottleneck? ① Interface compliance consumes interaction opportunities. Qwen-Max records execution errors in 103/200 CALVIN rounds, including 95 syntax errors; Fable has syntax errors in 34/36 erroneous rounds (Table A44 ). Syntax-invalid responses cannot carry out their intended control program. Treating the resulting failures as physical reasoning errors would overlook opportunities lost before environment interaction. ② Valid execution can still leave control unresolved. Among thirty CALVIN episodes with an observed native prefix and no execution error, 24 fail: eleven achieve no subgoal, and thirteen achieve only part of the sequence. Likewise, 62/65 error-containing episodes later execute code successfully, but only three finish (Figure 8 b). Code repair addresses an interface condition; it does not certify grasp, object placement, or subsequent subgoals. The PlaceSphere checks locate this distinction directly in the object’s measured state. ③ Productive recovery restores a prerequisite. On matched Archaeology Dating, Opus 5.5’s first two rounds fail before any environment action. It then acquires tools, excavates three sites, reads artifact ages, and places the flag at the oldest site, completing in round seven; Gemini 4 finishes in eight (Figure A16 ). DeepSeek reaches score 0.5, but its final three rounds raise code errors without new environment steps. The successful trace connects each correction to evidence needed by the next decision, while the stalled trace receives no further environmental feedback.

What does extra computation actually buy? ① Repeated inputs can dominate token cost. In seven HumanCLAW traces, 98.23–99.46% of reported tokens are prompt tokens, including the inputs processed for successive decisions. Gemini 4’s unresolved plant search records 1.95M tokens across 73 executed rounds, with 48 turns and 24 translations; its action descriptions repeatedly report no plant found. The token total principally reflects repeated input processing during unsuccessful search. In other traces the model stops shortly after a sitting attempt despite an unmet native condition. Search allocation and stopping assessment therefore require different diagnoses. ② The binding budget determines the next opportunity. DeepSeek’s ScienceWorld animal search exhausts decision rounds with 89 environment steps unused. Conversely, Gemini 4’s BEHAVIOR-1K toolbox attempt is blocked by the 640k-token ceiling after 37.5 minutes of a 180-minute allowance. More wall time would admit neither next decision under the recorded protocol. Even successful episodes separate costs: on matched chair navigation, Gemini 4 uses seventeen rounds and 158k tokens versus DeepSeek’s 65 and 938k, yet takes 437 versus 406 seconds. Progress per decision, token expenditure, and elapsed time must therefore be assessed separately against the resource that actually ends the attempt.


### 5 Conclusion

Closed-weight models lead this five-domain evaluation, while open-weight VLMs show useful spatial reasoning but substantially lower Planning and Manipulation success. The deeper distinction lies in the errors behind the scores: scale bias and residual geometric distortion, region enclosure and valid action-point selection, and executable operations and achieved object states require different corrections. During longer sequences, lost physical progress may have to be recovered before the next subgoal becomes feasible.

Feedback is useful when it resolves the particular requirement blocking progress. The matched traces show a receiving-region correction completing an otherwise valid procedure and a confirmed grasp enabling transport; they also show repeated valid code and unsupported stopping judgments leaving goals unmet. Token expenditure alone obscures this distinction. For Astra and Gemini 4, most failed tokens are concentrated in HumanCLAW, whereas unsuccessful attempts remain costly across other sources for the remaining models. Assessing frontier embodied agents therefore requires both complete-task outcomes and evidence of the state changes through which those outcomes are reached.

#### B.3 Scoring and Repeated Evaluation

Submission and termination. A final submission commits a fixed-input prediction. Interactive runs end when the agent stops, the environment reaches a terminal condition, a source-specific round, token, action, or time limit is reached, or an API or execution error terminates the episode. The evaluator scores the submitted output or recorded state independently of the agent’s declaration. A complete episode includes all its internal reasoning, actions, and recovery; these steps are not separate attempts.

Task scores and aggregation. Categorical questions use answer accuracy and interactive tasks use native goal success. Invalid or absent predictions and error-terminated episodes count as unsuccessful within the fixed assignment denominator. Termination reasons distinguish provider errors from code-execution errors and unmet task conditions. Invalid reference annotations are excluded before scoring. Continuous errors average valid predictions with compatible metrics and units within each run. Repeated metrics then weight the run-level scores equally, with valid coverage reported separately. Numerical Spatial Reasoning uses exact-match success alongside continuous errors, with the criterion

[Tableau] $$|\hat{y}-y|<10^{-6}.$$

This differs from VSI-Bench’s official mean relative accuracy. Point-set questions retain their benchmark-specific set criterion.

Pass@1 and repeated scores. For $N$ assigned cases and $R$ repetitions, let $s_{ir}\in\{0,1\}$ denote success on case $i$ in run $r$ . The average single-episode success rate is

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{Pass@1}=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{ir}\right).$$ (2)

This estimates success in one complete episode under the specified condition. Fresh agent state is used for formal repetitions. For any per-run score $M_{r}$ , we compute

[Tableau] $$\bar{M}=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}M_{r},\qquad v_{M}=\frac{1}{R-1}\sum_{r=1}^{R}(M_{r}-\bar{M})^{2},\qquad\mathrm{SD}_{M}=\sqrt{v_{M}}.$$ (3)

Tables using $\bar{M}\pm\mathrm{SD}_{M}$ report variation between run-level scores. Variance is supplied separately in squared metric units. Figure 4 averages valid-case errors within each run and then weights the recorded runs equally. Its coverage sensitivity instead fixes the cases valid in every shared repetition for each model pair; those common-cohort estimates are reported separately. Figure 5 a–b uses per-run categorical accuracies; panel c uses full-source native Pass@1. The original pairwise gaps average accuracy differences over shared run indices, with the comparator fixed per subtask. Run indices align reporting repetitions; seeds and tool configurations can differ. Run-to-run SD requires $R>1$ .

Scalar estimates and depth. For $J$ valid targets, the residual, mean absolute error, and root mean squared error are

[Tableau] $\displaystyle e_{j}$ $\displaystyle=\hat{y}_{j}-y_{j},$ $\displaystyle\mathrm{MAE}$ $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}|e_{j}|,\qquad\mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}e_{j}^{2}}.$

On nonzero targets, absolute relative error and its percentage form are

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{AbsRel}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{|e_{j}|}{|y_{j}|},\qquad\mathrm{MAPE}=100\,\mathrm{AbsRel}.$$

Size and focal-length estimates use absolute and relative errors. Depth additionally reports RMSE in meters. Depth errors are computed over positive finite predictions within each image, then averaged across images, with valid-point coverage reported. Both the fraction within 10% relative error and depth threshold accuracy use all queried points as their denominator; invalid predictions contribute zero. The latter is

[Tableau] $$\delta_{1}=\frac{1}{J_{\rm all}}\sum_{j=1}^{J_{\rm all}}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\max\!\left(\frac{\hat{y}_{j}}{y_{j}},\frac{y_{j}}{\hat{y}_{j}}\right)<1.25\right].$$

Ordinal accuracy is the fraction of eligible point pairs with the correct depth ordering, with invalid predictions counted as incorrect. Scale alignment removes the global depth factor.

Displacement and camera pose. For nonzero scalar or vector $y$ , relative motion error is

[Tableau] $$\frac{\|\hat{y}-y\|_{2}}{\|y\|_{2}}.$$

The MultiSPA-derived tolerance is 20%; a zero target requires zero absolute error. GeoProbe object-displacement diagnostics separately test movement status, direction, absolute distance error, and direction agreement with distance error at most 1 cm. Stationary cases are evaluated without division by zero. Camera translation and rotation errors are

[Tableau] $\displaystyle e_{t}$ $\displaystyle=\|\hat{\mathbf{t}}-\mathbf{t}\|_{2},$ $\displaystyle e_{R}$ $\displaystyle=\arccos\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{tr}(R^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{R})-1}{2}\right).$

Translation is reported in centimeters and rotation in degrees, clipping the arccosine argument to $[-1,1]$ . GeoProbe reports component errors and joint success within $2^{\circ}$ and 2 cm.

Camera calibration. Focal MAPE averages the absolute relative errors of $f_{x}$ and $f_{y}$ per case. Principal-point error, reported in pixels and also normalized by the image diagonal $D$ , is

[Tableau] $$e_{c}=\|\hat{\mathbf{c}}-\mathbf{c}\|_{2}.$$

Joint calibration requires both focal components to satisfy 10% relative error and $e_{c}\leq 0.02D$ . Main-table pose errors, complementary continuous metrics, and Figure 4 use equal-run means and run SD, as specified above.

Spatial tracing. For each case, predicted and reference paths are independently resampled to $L=100$ equally spaced arc-length positions. Trajectory error is

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{RMSE}_{\rm trace}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\|\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{\ell}-\mathbf{p}_{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}}.$$

We average this per-case value over valid scenes. The 2D metric uses normalized image coordinates; the 3D metric uses meters. The two metrics retain their respective units. The supplied variance is the population variance of the 56 per-scene RMSE values for a single run.

Offline 3D success requires valid initiation, target arrival, and collision checks to pass together. The predicted start must lie within 20 cm of the object point cloud or the reference start. At least one of the final three predicted points must lie within 20 cm of the target 3D box; for two-point paths, only the endpoint is checked. Translating the object point cloud to each checked predicted position must yield at most 20% overlap with the environment. Collision is checked at the sampled positions. Missing or invalid trajectories count as failures. 2D tracing reports continuous error. Qwen-27B trajectory outputs undergo format normalization that preserves all predicted coordinate values.

Native affordance grounding. For a directly predicted bounding box $B$ and ground-truth mask $G$ , the box interior is rasterized. Box–mask overlap and contact validity for the submitted point $p$ are

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{IoU}_{B,G}=\frac{|B\cap G|}{|B\cup G|},\qquad\mathrm{ContactValid}=\mathbf{1}[p\in G].$$

Contact validity uses a reference-box fallback only where a mask is unavailable. A separate diagnostic compares the prediction with the reference box $B_{G}$ :

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{IoU}_{\rm box}=\frac{|B\cap B_{G}|}{|B\cup B_{G}|}.$$

Strict box success requires $\mathrm{IoU}_{\rm box}\geq 0.5$ ; contact validity is scored independently. The source field named giou contains ordinary box–mask IoU, not generalized IoU. The separate polygon protocol rasterizes the directly submitted polygons into a region $P$ and scores

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{IoU}_{P,G}=\frac{|P\cap G|}{|P\cup G|}.$$

It adds no external segmenter or iterative segmentation refinement; Appendix C.5 gives output validation and coverage.

Resource accounting. Inference tokens, action calls, model rounds, and elapsed time measure different aspects of execution. The CALVIN recovery analysis counts episodes; detailed traces report actions and elapsed time. Token fractions use reported input and output usage, excluding missing values and unknown transport or retry usage. Episode duration includes worker setup, retries, and teardown for the Qwen HumanCLAW records; other durations use the reported episode total or last loop time. Request, trial, and native-call time limits are specified separately in Appendix E.1 .


#### B.4 Repetition Protocol and Result Accounting

Repetitions. Non-tracing Geometry, Spatial Reasoning, and point-and-box Affordance use five repetitions per case for nine models and three for Fable. Tracing, Planning, Manipulation, and direct-polygon prediction use one run per assignment for all ten models. Run-to-run SD is reported only for repeated measurements. Manipulation’s observation schedules appear in Appendices E.1 and C.4 .

Metric definitions and coverage. The 183-case Manipulation inventory contains 182 binary tasks and one continuous-return AnymalC-Spin task. AnymalC-Spin is evaluated separately through continuous return. For each model and configuration, native acceptance gives $s_{i}=1$ and task success is

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{SR}=\frac{100}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}.$$

Runs without native acceptance receive zero success credit, including timeouts, execution or infrastructure failures, and missing records. Each task contributes one run to the assigned denominator.

CALVIN scores a five-goal sequence as successful only when all five goals are completed in order. Let $L_{i}\in\{0,\ldots,5\}$ be the verified consecutive prefix. The average number of consecutive goals completed is

[Tableau] $$\bar{L}=\frac{1}{10}\sum_{i=1}^{10}L_{i}.$$

Unverified prefixes receive no goal credit; Appendix D.5.1 gives the case-level coverage.

For the other auxiliary metrics, case $i$ has $K_{i}$ scoring items and verified indicators $z_{ij}$ . The per-case score and reported mean are

[Tableau] $$u_{i}=\frac{1}{K_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{K_{i}}z_{ij},\qquad\bar{u}=\frac{100}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{i}.$$

Failed or unknown items contribute zero. This weights tasks equally even when $K_{i}$ differs. The diagnostics summarize recorded control events on fixed task subsets using the scoring items and evidence windows in Table A31 .
