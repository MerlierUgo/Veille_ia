1 Introduction
Advances in foundation models’ reasoning, planning, and tool-use capabilities are expanding the scope of
work that agents can undertake [1]. Their responsibilities increasingly extend beyond short, narrowly scoped
tasks to larger assignments that require them to maintain an overall objective, coordinate multiple stages,
and carry the work through to completion. Furthermore, recursive self-improvement [2] explores how agents
can improve their own models and runtime systems, bringing capability improvement itself into the range of
work agents undertake.
Humans are already delegating complex productivity tasks to agents across software engineering [3], office
work [4, 5], web and computer use [6–8], enterprise operations [9], and scientific research [10]. Frontier agents
such as Codex and Claude Code [11, 12] illustrate this shift by supporting multi-day, cross-session work with
suitable orchestration and taking on parts of result assessment and feedback-driven revision previously handled
by humans. Across these tasks, decisions often depend on evidence in related files, records, or applications
and on states established by earlier actions. A work-centric perspective traces these dependencies from the
initial request through execution to a verifiable outcome.
From this perspective, we introduce the concept of awork agent, defined as a system that uses a foundation
model as its decision-making core, relies on a runtime harness to organize environment observations and action
execution, and carries out delegated tasks within a stateful digital workspace. The foundation model provides
understanding, reasoning, and decision-making capabilities; the harness manages context construction, tool
invocation, memory, skills, execution control, and error recovery; and the workspace contains interconnected
objects such as files, applications, records, tasks, and other actors. A work agent aims to produce work outcomes
that satisfy task requirements, with execution results verified through observable evidence. This perspective
connects task objectives, model capabilities, system mechanisms, environment states, and verification, shifting
the unit of analysis from isolated model outputs or tool calls to the complete process of accomplishing digital
work.
Work agents operate under different conditions depending on the tasks they undertake. To characterize these
differences, we introduce two conceptually distinct yet complementary dimensions:❶work persistence
and ❷ work openness. Work persistence concerns the conditions under which delegated responsibility
can be considered complete, distinguishing work that concludes upon delivery from responsibilities that
remain active across multiple stages, feedback cycles, or external events. Work openness describes the extent
to which task-relevant objects, information, interfaces, and rules are specified before execution, or must
instead be discovered and interpreted during execution. These working conditions impose different demands
on an agent’s ability to understand its environment, manage task dependencies, maintain execution state,
and verify outcomes. Based on the functional requirements that arise throughout the work process, we
organize the core capabilities of work agents into five interrelated families: perception and understanding,
reasoning and decision making, planning and coordination, execution and state management, and verification,
recovery, and replanning. Together, these capabilities support the complete process of interpreting work
requirements, organizing and executing actions, and verifying results, forming an iterative execution cycle
driven by environmental feedback.
The performance of work agents depends not only on their core capabilities but also on how foundation models
and runtime harnesses work together. From an optimization perspective, some capabilities can be learned by
models through training data, interaction experience, and feedback, while others can be supported by external
mechanisms such as tool interfaces, memory management, and execution control. Improving work agents
therefore requires considering model optimization, harness optimization, and their joint optimization. From
an evaluation perspective, agent performance is reflected not only in the correctness of answers or individual
actions but also in the quality of work artifacts, changes to environment states, workflow completion, and
satisfaction of task requirements. Systematic evaluation across these dimensions provides a more comprehensive
understanding of the practical performance and reliability of work agents.
As agents move from executing bounded tasks toward sustained work delegation, their responsibilities
increasingly extend across multiple stages and events. In this setting, agents must maintain continuity despite
environmental changes, interruptions, and external feedback, while also leveraging experience accumulated
4

during execution to improve their capabilities without disrupting ongoing responsibilities. Work execution
and capability improvement thus form two interacting loops, raising new research challenges for the long-term
development of work agents. Personal work agents, human digital twins, and organizational work systems
provide research settings for sustaining goals between requests, representing a particular person’s judgment, and
coordinating responsibilities across roles. Future research must address reliability, environmental adaptation,
and outcome verification during sustained execution, while also considering the practical value of work
outcomes and the human and computational resources required to produce them. These challenges will shape
the transition from completing individual tasks to undertaking more persistent, complex, and valuable digital
work.
Based on this perspective, the main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
• A unified, work-centric framework for agent systems.We present a unified perspective centered
on digital work, characterizing the structure and execution of work agents through six elements: task,
model, harness, workspace, work outcome, and verification. We further describe the scope, organization,
and execution conditions of digital work through five work forms and two complementary dimensions,
work persistence and work openness.
• A systematic account of core capabilities and optimization for reliable work.We identify
five core capability families required by work agents and examine their roles under different working
conditions. We further examine training environments, data and reward design, model optimization,
harness optimization, and their joint optimization, highlighting how model learning and external system
support contribute to reliable work execution.
• An outcome-oriented perspective on agent evaluation.We systematically analyze existing
benchmarks, evaluation metrics, judging methods, and execution environments, examining how they
assess work artifact quality, environment-state changes, task completion, and constraint satisfaction.
This provides a structured perspective for understanding the practical performance and reliability of
work agents.
• Future research directions toward sustained work delegation.We explore emerging applications,
including personal work agents, human digital twins, and organizational work systems, and discuss key
challenges involving persistent responsibility, memory and adaptation, outcome verification, and the
value of work in open environments. We further examine the interaction between work execution and
capability improvement to outline research directions for next-generation work agents.
This paper focuses on digital knowledge work across office productivity, professional services, enterprise
operations, software engineering, and scientific research. Section 2 introduces the foundations of work agents,
including their system components, working conditions, and core capabilities. Section 3 examines five forms
of digital work undertaken by agents. Section 4 examines training environments, model optimization, harness
optimization, and their joint optimization. Section 5 covers evaluation benchmarks, methodologies, metrics,
and execution environments. Section 6 discusses future applications, technical frontiers, and the interaction
between work execution and capability improvement. Section 7 concludes the paper.
2 Foundation of Work Agents
Section 1 argued that the performance of a system that carries out digital work depends not only on its model
but also on its interfaces, the information available to it, the organization of its state, and the verification of
its results. Studying such systems therefore requires a common account of what the system consists of, the
conditions under which it works, what it must be able to do, and how it differs from related lines of agent
research. This section develops this account in four steps. Section 2.1 identifies the elements involved when
digital work is delegated to an agent and defines a work agent. Section 2.2 introduces two dimensions of
working conditions, work persistence and work openness, which describe when a responsibility may close and
how much the agent must find out before and while it acts. Section 2.3 identifies five core capabilities that
the work process requires and examines how the two dimensions raise their demands. Section 2.4 compares
work agents with long-horizon, computer-use, and office agents.

2.1 Definition of Work Agents
We begin with the system itself. By digital work we mean work carried out on digital objects such as files,
applications, and records, for example updating a report or handling a service request. When a person or an
organization hands such work to an agent, three things happen: the requester states what should be done, the
agent acts on these objects to do it, and the result is checked against what was asked. We call such work
delegated: the agent carries it out on the requester’s behalf and is accountable for the result. We first describe
the six elements involved in this process, as shown in Figure 1, then define a work agent in terms of them,
and finally describe how the elements interact during execution.
Task (T)specifies the objective, the relevant constraints, the available resources, the permissions, and the
conditions for valid completion. For an ongoing responsibility, it also specifies the conditions to maintain,
the agreed interval, and the criteria for closure or renewal.
Model (M)serves as the system’s decision-making core. Given context supplied by the harness, it interprets
the available information, weighs possible courses of action, and proposes responses or actions [13, 14].
Harness (H)organizes context construction, tool use, memory, skills, execution control, feedback handling,
and recovery, mediating between the model and the workspace [14].
Workspace (E)is the stateful environment in which work takes place. It contains files, applications, records,
messages, tasks, and other actors. These are linked to one another, and the agent accesses them through
interfaces within its permissions. Both the agent’s actions and external events change its state as work
proceeds.
Work Outcome (O)is the task-relevant result that the agent produces. Outcomes include answers, such as
analyses, plans, and executable specifications; artifacts such as documents, spreadsheets, presentations, and
code; state changes in applications or business records, such as updated records and sent messages; process
outcomes, such as a completed process or the stages, approvals, and handoffs finished so far; and maintained
6

conditions and event responses over an agreed interval. One assignment may combine several outcomes, such
as an updated workbook, a report, and the corresponding changes to business records, and require them to
remain consistent across related artifacts, records, and applications.
Verification (V)assesses work against task requirements. It inspects evidence from answers, workspace
states, artifacts, and workflow records (Section 5.2.1). Programs, humans, or model-based judges [15]
assess this evidence against task-specific criteria, which may be organized as rules, rubrics, or checklists
(Section 5.2.2). The checks cover task completion, correctness, completeness, quality, constraint satisfaction,
and intended effects. For example, SWE-bench [16] executes tests against a modified repository, whereas
PresentBench [17] applies task-specific checklist items through a model judge. Verification may be performed
by the agent during execution (Section 2.3.5) or after execution by the requester or a benchmark (Section 5);
its judgments support completion decisions, quality measures, and diagnostic feedback for subsequent work.
With these elements, we define awork agentas a system that uses a foundation model as its decision-making
core and a harness to organize observations and actions in a stateful workspace. It pursues a delegated work
objective under specified constraints, resources, and permissions, and its outcomes are assessed against task
requirements through observable evidence. The agent itself consists of the model and the harness, shown
as the dashed box in Figure 1; the task, workspace, outcome, and verification define the work for which
it is accountable. The definition covers a wide range of tasks. In the minimal case, a question-answering
system counts as a work agent when answering is delegated to it and its answers are checked against task
requirements. Its harness only assembles the question and the supplied material into context and returns the
model’s answer, and its workspace is that material, which the agent reads but does not change. A system
that edits workbooks or carries business processes to completion uses the same elements with a fuller harness
and a workspace that its actions change (Section 3).
The six elements interact in a loop during execution. The harness constructs context from the task, workspace
observations, and retained execution information and passes it to the model. The model proposes responses
or actions; the harness carries out the actions, receives observations, and uses them with verification feedback
to update the context for the next decision. At an assessment pointt, execution and verification have distinct

2.2 Working Conditions
Across tasks, these conditions differ in two respects: whether the work ends once the agent delivers its result,
and how much the agent must find out before and while it acts. We capture these two differences as❶ work
persistence and ❷ work openness. Section 2.2.1 defines work persistence and traces it through existing
research, Section 2.2.2 does the same for work openness, and Section 2.2.3 shows that the two dimensions are
conceptually distinct, illustrates their four combinations, and relates them to the forms of work examined in
Section 3.
2.2.1 Work Persistence
The first difference concerns when the agent may consider delegated work finished. In some tasks, the work is
finished once the agent delivers its result, such as an answer, a confirmed change, or a tested patch. In others,
the agent’s delivery alone does not finish the work: other participants must still assess, approve, or contribute,
7

or later events require earlier results to be revisited.Work persistencethus ranges from responsibility that
closes at delivery to responsibility that remains open across stages and events. As persistence increases, the
agent must carry state across waiting periods and events and must determine at each point which parts of its
responsibility are still open.
Existing research covers both sides of this range. Responsibility closes at delivery when closure depends only
on what the agent itself delivers. In early work on reading comprehension and question answering over text,
tables, documents, and charts [18–23], responsibility closes once an answer is returned. Tool-use research,
from Toolformer [24] and API-Bank [25] to Gorilla [26], extends the output from text to tool invocation.
When an action changes the workspace, the work also requires a confirmed effect: in AndroidWorld [27], a
messaging task counts as complete only when the intended message is recorded in the phone’s SMS database.
Responsibility can also close at delivery after a sequence of dependent actions. In SWE-bench [16], producing
a patch involves localization, editing, and testing; in MINT [28], tool outputs and language feedback across
turns guide subsequent actions.
On the other side, closure depends on more than the agent’s own delivery: on other participants or on later
events. When it depends on other participants, responsibility cannot close until they provide the confirmation
or contribution the task requires:τ-bench [29] withholds specified database updates until the user confirms
them, τ 2-bench [30] requires the agent to coordinate with a simulated user who carries out part of the work,
and TheAgentCompany [31] includes tasks that cannot proceed without information from simulated coworkers.
When it depends on later events, earlier results or completed stages may need to be revisited: in OSWorld
2.0 [32], revised budget and vendor constraints arrive through a team chat and require the agent to reconsider
affected entries in a purchase-order form. When the task defines an obligation over an agreed interval, the
agent remains responsible for responding to such events throughout that interval: E-Commerce Bench [33]
spans a simulated year of operational decisions, and VibeLifeBench [34] evaluates proactive and persistent
assistance as events change the environment over simulated weeks.
Dividing the examples by what closure depends on also separates persistence from task difficulty and execution
length. Answering a question can demand harder reasoning than submitting a form for approval, yet
responsibility for the answer closes at delivery, whereas the workflow remains open until approval is received.
The same holds for execution length, a central concern of long-horizon research (Section 2.4.1). A long run
may pass through many dependent steps, yet its responsibility still closes when the result is submitted; an
obligation to keep a service within its operating constraints may require only a short run each week, yet its
responsibility remains open for a quarter. The two examples sit on opposite sides of the boundary: the first
closes once its run delivers a result, whereas the second stays open across many runs until the agreed interval
ends.
2.2.2 Work Openness
The two examples at the end of Section 2.2.1 illustrate when responsibility may close, but do not specify how
much the agent must find out before and while it acts. The long run may operate on a repository whose
relevant files are named in the request, or on one whose relevant files it must first locate; the weekly run may
read the same fixed metrics each time, or have to trace which components a new incident affects. In some
tasks, the task world, that is, the objects, interfaces, rules, and actors relevant to the task, is specified with
the request. In others, the agent must establish during execution which objects are relevant and how they are
represented and related. It may also need to find out where the relevant state is held, how other actors have
changed it, and which actions it is authorized to take.Work opennessthus ranges from a predefined task
world to one discovered during execution. As openness increases, completing the work depends more on the
agent’s ability to build and update its understanding of the task world while acting in it.
As with persistence, existing research spans this range, and the examples below are ordered by how much
the agent must establish during execution. Early research largely studied predefined task worlds. Reading-
comprehension and question-answering tasks supply the passage, table, document, or chart to be analyzed [18–
23], and tool-use studies such as API-Bank [25], Gorilla [26], and BFCL [35] expose an explicit set of tools
and interfaces. The agent reasons or acts within a space that has already been defined, and the difficulty lies
in using the supplied material and tools correctly rather than in finding them.
8

Finding them becomes part of the task in web and computer-use environments. In WebArena [6], the
information needed for a task must be obtained by navigating and interacting with functional websites rather
than read from the prompt. OSWorld [7] extends the setting to real computer environments, where the agent
works across applications and heterogeneous digital objects. In Workspace-Bench [36], the agent must also
identify which files are relevant and how they depend on one another, so locating material across a workspace
becomes the core of the work.
Beyond objects, other environments make the state of the task world, the actions of other actors, and
authority part of what must be established. AppWorld [37] spreads the information a task needs across
multiple applications, so the agent must determine which applications and records hold it before acting. In
τ 2-bench [30], a simulated user acts on parts of the shared environment that the agent cannot operate, so
the agent must find out from the user what has changed. Authority must be established when the request
does not state who may do what. In EntCollabBench [38], instructions describe business goals rather than
workflows, and each agent may use only its own department’s tools, so the agent must infer which role is
responsible for each step and delegate to it. Applicable permissions may likewise have to be discovered during
execution: in a card-unlocking task inτ-Knowledge’s τ-Banking domain [39], the agent must retrieve the
relevant policy and inspect the card’s status to determine that a security hold prevents it from unlocking the
card and requires it to offer escalation to the security team.
2.2.3 Combining Persistence and Openness
Having defined both dimensions, we now consider how they combine. The two dimensions answer different
questions, as the two examples at the start of Section 2.2.2 already suggest: openness concerns what the agent
must find out, whereas persistence concerns when it may stop. The same workspace change can involve both
dimensions: discovering which objects it affects concerns openness, while responsibility that remains open as
further changes arrive concerns persistence. The two dimensions are thus conceptually distinct: a setting can
be high on one and low on the other.
As shown in Figure 2, all four combinations appear in existing research. Question answering (bottom left)
combines a predefined task world with responsibility that closes at delivery: the system answers from given
material, as in SQuAD [18] and DocVQA [22]. Information seeking (bottom right) asks the agent to discover
the relevant material during execution, yet responsibility still closes at delivery, as in GAIA [40], whose
questions require finding a source on the web, and Workspace-Bench [36]. Service workflows (top left) keep the
task world predefined, yet responsibility remains open while the requested updates await user confirmation, as
inτ-bench [29], where the tools, domain policy, and database are specified in advance. Ongoing work under
changing conditions (top right) combines a task world discovered during execution with responsibility that
remains open as later events arrive: revised constraints arriving mid-task in OSWorld 2.0 [32] require the
agent to find the affected entries, and in VibeLifeBench [34], some changes come without notification, so the
agent must re-inspect the environment to discover them.
These combinations describe the conditions of work rather than its form. Section 3 distinguishes five work
forms by the scope and organization of delegated work, from work at the Conversation Level, which answers
questions over supplied material, to work at the Workspace Level, which operates across related objects in a
workspace. Persistence and openness characterize the conditions under which each form is carried out: work at
the Workflow Level, for example, can run in the predefined task world ofτ-bench [29] or require the agent to
track a shared state that a simulated user also changes, as inτ 2-bench [30]. Because both dimensions change
what the agent must find out and keep track of, they also determine how demanding its core capabilities
become, which Section 2.3 examines.

and relationships: it identifies the objects involved in a task, interprets the content and structure of each

including where each target of an edit is located, for use by Reasoning and Decision Making. In the report
task, a value in the workbook becomes usable evidence only once its header, unit, and reporting period are
known, and an edit can be applied only once its target cells or slide element are located.

the functional types and content structures of reference slides. For location, SpreadsheetLLM [42] uses
structural anchors, cell-address mappings, and format aggregation to locate relevant spreadsheet regions,

2.3.2 Reasoning and Decision Making
Located evidence does not by itself determine what to do next. Reasoning and Decision Making weighs goals,
constraints, and evidence to decide how to proceed among the available alternatives. Goals determine the
desired result, and constraints limit acceptable actions; goals and some constraints are supplied with the
task, while further constraints may need to be established during execution. Evidence establishes the current
situation and accumulates as the work proceeds, and domain knowledge, learned by the model or retrieved,
determines how it should be interpreted. The result is a chosen approach, which Planning and Coordination
arranges into a plan. In the report task, such reasoning determines whether two figures are comparable, which
calculation is appropriate, and whether more information is needed before an edit is made.
Existing work studies both domain-guided decisions and the use of evidence gathered during work. For
domain-guided decisions, FinQA [21] requires domain knowledge to reason numerically over financial reports,
and τ-bench [29] makes domain policies and user requirements constrain tool-use decisions. For evidence
gathered during the work, ReAct [13] interleaves reasoning with actions and observations, SheetAgent [45]
queries relevant spreadsheet subviews for its planner and uses code-execution results to inform subsequent
operations, and CodeAct [46] expresses actions as executable code and feeds interpreter outputs and error
messages into subsequent decisions.
Reasoning and Decision Making thus provides decisions that can be checked later. To make this possible, the
harness records each decision in the shared work context, together with the goal it serves, the constraints that
applied, and references to the supporting evidence. Later steps can then tell whether it worked and whether
its basis still holds. In the report task, the record of a decision to recompute a growth rate shows which
figures were compared and why they were judged comparable. The revision reopens decisions like this one,
and the agent must decide whether it can act on the revision directly or must first gather more information.
2.3.3 Planning and Coordination
When delegated work involves several dependent actions or contributions from other participants, each decision
must be connected to the work that follows. Planning and Coordination arranges the dependencies and
handoffs among these actions and contributions. Planning specifies subgoals, required inputs, and the order
in which results become available [47–49]; coordination obtains and routes the information, authorization, or
artifacts supplied by other participants [50]. The result is a plan, which Execution and State Management

carries out. In the report task, the analysis must be completed before the dependent charts are revised, and
any information needed from collaborators must be obtained in time.
Representative work emphasizes different aspects of planning and coordination. Planning methods make
dependenciesexplicitandrevisethemasresultsarrive: DataInterpreter[ 51]representsworkflowsashierarchical
dependency graphs, records task results and status, and adjusts affected tasks as execution develops, and
Agent S2 [52] updates the remaining subgoals from the original task and new observations after each subgoal
concludes. Coordination frameworks make one participant’s output available as another’s input: AutoGen [53]
organizes role-based exchanges among agents, tools, and human input, and MetaGPT [54] passes structured
artifacts such as requirements and designs through a shared message pool and a subscription mechanism.
Planning and Coordination thus provides a plan that records the result each step produces, the inputs needed
to produce it, and the steps or participants that will use it, including dependencies that are still pending. In
the report task, the record leads from a revised data source directly to the analysis, charts, and slides that
must be redone. Because responsibility lasts until approval, the plan must span the assessment period and
take in the revision when it arrives, and the owner of the revised data must be identified during execution.
2.3.4 Execution and State Management
Plans take effect only when they are applied to the workspace. Execution and State Management takes
actions on workspace files, applications, and records through tools and interfaces, receives observations of
their state, and records progress. Execution performs operations through code, tool interfaces, or graphical
interfaces within the task’s authorized scope; state management tracks the objects and versions involved,
completed operations, returned results, and pending items. The two are connected because operations can
change workspace state or reveal information that affects the next decision. The results are the artifacts
produced and the records kept, which Verification, Recovery, and Replanning checks; in the report task, these
are the revised workbook and the list of calculations and dependent edits already completed.
To retain the effects and observations on which later decisions depend, existing systems maintain action
and state records during execution. SheetCopilot [4] executes spreadsheet operations through an observe–
propose–revise–act state machine and updates its action and state records after each successful execution.
InfiAgent [55] extends this practice beyond a single run: it stores plans, intermediate artifacts, and tool
outputs in a task directory and updates progress markers, so that an interrupted task can be resumed from
the record.
Execution and State Management thus provides a record that tells which effects can be relied on. The record
distinguishes anattempted action, anaccepted call, acommitted effect, and averified effect: an attempted
action has been issued; an accepted call has been acknowledged by the interface; a committed effect is reflected
in the target state; and a verified effect has additionally been checked against the relevant requirement. These
states need not coincide, so acceptance of a call alone does not establish task success. An operation whose
effect is known to be committed becomes the starting point for the next one, whereas an operation with an
uncertain effect remains pending until its state is inspected. In the report task, the saved workbook and its
current version are the basis for the next edit, whereas a chart update that may not have been applied stays
pending until the slide is checked. Because the work resumes when the revision arrives during assessment, the
record must be kept across sessions so that resumed work does not repeat edits already made; before reading
the upstream workbook, the agent must also establish that it is permitted to access it.
2.3.5 Verification, Recovery, and Replanning
Recorded changes show what has happened but not whether it satisfies the task. Verification, Recovery, and
Replanning confirms outcomes and guides correction. Its three functions proceed from assessment to corrective
action: verification checks results against task requirements and completion criteria, recovery repairs the
objects or operations responsible for a problem, and replanning revises the remaining steps when feedback
shows that a prerequisite or condition has changed. We examine representative work for each function in turn.
Verification relates each requirement to evidence that can establish whether it has been met. The appropriate
evidence depends on the property being checked, whether a benchmark defines the check or the agent runs
13

it itself. Behavior is checked by execution: SWE-bench [16] runs tests against the modified repository, and
SpreadsheetBench [56] applies a spreadsheet transformation to several workbook inputs. Claims are checked
against external information: CRITIC [57] uses external tools to obtain evidence for critiquing and correcting
its own outputs. Presentation is checked on the rendered artifact: DeepPresenter [58] inspects rendered slides
to identify issues such as overlap and overflow. The report task needs all three: executing its calculations,
checking its claims against their sources, and inspecting its rendered slides.
When verification detects a problem, recovery repairs it. Self-Refine [59] revises the current output from
generated feedback, whereas Reflexion [60] retains reflections on task feedback to improve the next attempt.
In a workspace, a repair must also target the object or operation responsible, such as a wrong formula or
an overflowing slide. When the effect of an earlier operation is uncertain, the agent must first inspect the
current state to decide whether to retain, repair, or retry it; this matters most for operations, such as sending
a message, that produce an additional effect when repeated.
Some problems cannot be repaired locally because a prerequisite or condition has changed. Replanning then
revises the remaining course of work: it uses the dependencies recorded in the plan (Section 2.3.3) to identify
the steps the change affects and draws on Planning and Coordination to rebuild them. LATS [61] searches
over alternative reasoning and action paths, using environmental feedback and reflection to choose among
them.
Verification, Recovery, and Replanning thus provides confirmed outcomes and corrections matched to the
scope of each problem: a local defect receives a local repair and recheck, whereas a changed prerequisite leads
to a revised plan and a check of its downstream results. After the revision, affected results already checked
must be re-verified, and the agent must first establish which source counts as evidence for the revised figures.
With it, the cycle in Figure 3 closes: a confirmed result is delivered or becomes the basis for further work, and
a remaining problem is directed to the capability it concerns, with missing evidence going back to Perception
and Understanding, a questionable decision to Reasoning and Decision Making, a changed prerequisite to
Planning and Coordination, and an execution error to Execution and State Management. Section 2.4 relates
this framework to existing agent paradigms.
2.4 Work Agents and Related Agent Paradigms
Relative to this framework, three established lines of agent research each focus on one facet of the work:
long-horizon agents on sustaining execution over many steps, computer-use agents on the action interface,
and office agents on the type of artifact produced. A work agent is instead defined by its delegated objective
and by outcomes assessed against task requirements, regardless of how long it runs, which interface it uses, or
what artifacts it produces. Sections 2.4.1 to 2.4.3 summarize the research focus of each paradigm and state
what the work-agent view adds, and Section 2.4.4 shows how the perspectives combine on one shared task.
2.4.1 Long-Horizon Agents
The first paradigm concerns sustaining execution over many steps. Long-horizon agents study how to maintain
task progress, context, and state over extended execution and how to handle dependencies whose consequences
emerge in later stages. A recent synthesis organizes this research into three levels [62]: the intra-context level
covers coupled reasoning steps, the cross-context level covers state continuity beyond a single context window,
and the cross-task level covers experience accumulation across tasks. Evaluations quantify this ability: the
METR time-horizon analysis [63] measures task length by the time human experts take to complete the
task and estimates the length at which an agent reaches a given success rate, while RE-Bench [64] compares
research-engineering performance under different time budgets.
To sustain such work, representative methods keep information available beyond a single context window.
InfiAgent [55] constructs its reasoning context from a snapshot of its stored task state and a fixed window of
recent actions. On the evaluation side, LongMemEval [65] tests memory through timestamped user–assistant
histories, including information extraction, multi-session reasoning, temporal reasoning, and updates to user
information, and LoCoMo [66] evaluates very long-term conversational memory.
14

These methods and measures concern continuity of execution and retained information. Work persistence asks
a different question: when responsibility may close (Section 2.2.1). Persistent delegation requires tracking
open obligations, responding to other participants and later events, and re-verifying earlier results against the
current workspace.
2.4.2 Computer-Use Agents
The second paradigm concerns the interface through which actions are taken. Computer-use agents study how
to complete tasks through software interfaces in browsers, desktop applications, and operating systems. A
central problem is to ground task intent in operations executable in the current interface: identifying relevant
controls, locating action targets, and deciding what to do next from the resulting interface state [67–71].
SeeClick [72] studies the visual-grounding part of this process by locating screen elements from screenshots
and instructions, and uses GUI-grounding pre-training to improve downstream task performance.
Grounding individual operations is not enough; the operations must also add up to the task outcome. Bench-
marks in this line, such as WebVoyager [73], therefore evaluate whole sequences of operations. WebArena [6]
checks, depending on the task, whether an answer, page location, or website state satisfies the intended goal,
and OSWorld [7] uses task-specific scripts to check the resulting files and application states.
The work-agent view places these interface operations within the delegated task as a whole. Computer use is
one interface through which the harness acts on the workspace [74]. Beyond the interface, this view also asks
when responsibility closes and which permissions and dependencies the agent must discover during execution,
including dependencies on objects that the interface does not show. Relating interface operations to these
questions shows what each operation contributes to the task.
2.4.3 Office Agents
The third paradigm concerns the type of artifact produced. Office agents, such as SheetCopilot, PPTAgent, and
Huawei Cloud OfficeAce [4, 5, 75], create and modify common work artifacts such as documents, spreadsheets,
and presentations. Task requirements concern both content and the structure and use of the objects that
carry it: spreadsheet operations must act on the correct data and cells, while presentation production must
balance content, layout, and overall coherence. The quality of the resulting artifact depends both on how
individual objects are manipulated and on how the task is organized.
Representative studies address these two aspects. For object manipulation, SheetCopilot [4] controls spread-
sheets through a set of atomic actions, and PPTAgent [5] generates slides from reference presentations through
code-based editing and evaluates content, design, and coherence. For task organization, OfficeBench [76]
provides single- and multi-application tasks involving documents, spreadsheets, calendars, and email; its
cross-application tasks require information obtained earlier to be used in later operations, and their results
are verified through matching rules or execution-based checks.
Office agents thus take office objects and the workflows around them as their main setting. The work-agent
view offers a framework for analyzing such work alongside software, service, and organizational work: it
describes office outcomes in the same terms as other work outcomes (Section 2.1), so that an updated workbook,
the corresponding changes to business records, and the approval required before publication can be analyzed
as parts of one delegated task.

3 Work Forms: What Work Do Agents Undertake?
Section 2 describes the system elements, working conditions, and core capabilities of work agents. Together,
these provide a foundation for explaining how an agent interprets a task, organizes its actions, and checks
their effects. The demands on these capabilities depend on the work delegated to the system. We examine
that work through three questions: what outcome must the agent produce, which objects and dependencies
fall within its responsibility, and what conditions determine completion? The five work forms below describe
how these responsibilities are organized.
Because a workflow can contain informational responses and local actions, Figure 4 assigns each representative
task setting to the broadest form it requires.
At the Conversation Level, the agent produces an informational result, whereas at the Atomic Action Level,
it executes a local operation on a work object. Moving to the Multi-step Level, the agent combines such
dependent actions to achieve a complete objective. This responsibility broadens at the Workflow Level, where
the process also becomes part of the delegated work and the agent advances it through stages governed
by procedural requirements and interactions with other participants. It extends further at the Workspace
Level, where the request no longer specifies every object involved and the agent must establish which files,
records, and applications bear on the work and how they are related. As the scope expands, the agent must
manage more of the dependencies surrounding an outcome, from intermediate results to stage transitions and
relationships among work objects. Table 3 summarizes the resulting work requirements.
3.1 Conversation Level
Work at the Conversation Level produces an informational result from a request and its associated evidence,
such as an answer, analysis, classification, or judgment. The agent interprets the relevant material and
applies the knowledge or computation needed to produce that result. Completion is assessed on the returned
information and does not require modifying the source artifacts, external work objects, or their states.
3.1.1 Producing Informational Outcomes
In these tasks, the delegated deliverable is information that answers the requester’s question or supports a
judgment. It may range from a short factual answer to an assessment, explanation, or detailed proposal.
SQuAD asks for an answer extracted from a supplied passage [18], FinQA requires a numerical answer
derived from financial text and tables [21], and MMTU includes classifications and judgments about whether
a statement is supported [77]. Professional requests extend these demands: AAAR-1.0 asks for equation
checks and critiques of research papers [78]; FinLFQA requests financial analyses grounded in sources and
calculations [79]; PRBench asks for justified legal and financial advice [80]; and MMLongBench includes
summarization of supplied PDF documents [81]. Responses can also take the form of plans or executable
specifications. In Idea2Plan’s direct-prompting setting, models develop a supplied research idea into a plan
that includes experimental design, while TravelPlanner’s sole-planning setting asks for a feasible itinerary
based on supplied travel information [82, 83]. WorfBench asks for a workflow graph of proposed subtasks and
dependencies [84]. Program outputs specify how an object can be constructed: BlenderLLM’s CADBench
asks for Blender scripts from modeling instructions, while the CADBench reconstruction benchmark asks for
editable CAD programs from supplied images or meshes [85, 86].
3.1.2 Interpreting Evidence and Deriving Responses
The complexity of a response task depends on both the source material to be understood and the reasoning
needed to process it. Prose presents facts in linguistic context, while tables relate values through rows and
columns [87, 88]. Document images carry information in page layout [89], while charts encode values and
relationships visually [23]. Extracting a value or comparing entries therefore depends on interpreting these
structures [21, 22, 90]. In GDP.pdf, a footnote changes which employers belong in a chronological ranking [91].
Repository code and structured data also appear as supplied evidence in LongBench v2’s multiple-choice
comprehension tasks [92].

The scope of the supplied material determines where relevant evidence must be found, while relationships
among its parts determine what must be connected. FinanceBench’s long-context setting supplies the relevant
filing in the prompt [93]; multi-hop and long-document tasks require linking supporting information across
passages, pages, or documents [19, 94–97]. A synthesis must further combine these contributions into an
account that preserves their meaning [79, 81]. ConvFinQA extends the dependency to conversation history,
with follow-up calculations using values or results established in earlier turns [98].
The requested inference determines how the evidence is used. A numerical answer may require computing
with selected values, sometimes through an intermediate program [21, 99, 100], while a legal conclusion may
require applying an explicit rule to the facts [101]. Scientific assessments draw on domain knowledge to
interpret the evidence [102, 103]. Plans require comparing available options against stated requirements and
commonsense feasibility constraints, including whether the proposed choices can be combined into a workable
arrangement [78, 83].
An informational response can explain why a report contains an incorrect value. Correcting that value extends
the responsibility to changing the relevant workbook entry and checking that the update has taken effect.
3.2 Atomic Action Level
This transition from generating information to modifying state defines the Atomic Action Level, which assigns
the agent a local objective with a clearly defined target and effect, such as correcting a spreadsheet cell,
repositioning a slide element, or updating a record field. Here, the level is determined by the local scope
of the work objective rather than the number of low-level execution steps: a single local action may still
require several mouse, keyboard, or API operations to complete. Regardless of the interface mechanics, the
agent must apply the change to the intended object while preserving any properties that the request leaves
unchanged [104].
3.2.1 Creating and Modifying Local Work Objects
A request may add a bounded element to an existing artifact, such as a formula [105, 106], a conditional
formatting rule [107], or a chart in WTM-Bench’s single-artifact tasks [108]. String transformations and
spreadsheet data manipulation can likewise be specified through examples or natural language [109, 110].
Existing content can also be edited: InstructExcel expresses spreadsheet operations as scripts, PPTC includes
individual edits to slide text, styles, and positions, and MiLDEdit specifies design-layer changes that preserve
surrounding layout and non-target content [111–113].
The same local scope applies to application records and communication state. WorkArena’s form tasks request
records with specified field values, while WorkBench includes scheduling a particular event and replying to or
deleting a selected email [9, 114]. Each request supplies a target and an intended change that execution must
realize.
3.2.2 Executing and Checking a Local Change
To carry out these requests, the agent must identify the target and specify the operation through an available
interface. ScreenSpot-Pro isolates target location in professional application screenshots [115]. BFCL’s single-
call tasks examine function and argument specifications; MedAgentBench adds patient-specific parameters
through a blood-pressure documentation request linked to the intended record [35, 116].
After execution, completion depends on the resulting state: an artifact must contain the requested content or
property, and an application record or message must reflect the intended change. In AndroidWorld’s messaging
task, the evaluator checks the destination number and message content in the phone’s SMS database [27].
Inspecting the resulting state during execution can likewise tell the agent whether the operation needs
correction.

3.3 Multi-step Level
Many work objectives cannot be met by a single local change: they depend on intermediate results that are
not available at the start. Preparing a forecast, for example, requires importing data before constructing
calculations and checking the resulting workbook. Intermediate operations establish information or state on
which later decisions depend. Work at the Multi-step Level coordinates these dependent operations toward a
complete objective, adapting subsequent actions to intermediate results and execution feedback [117]. Because
only the objective is delegated, completion is assessed on the final result, whichever sequence of actions reaches
it.
3.3.1 Completing Objectives through Intermediate Results
A complete objective may culminate in a usable artifact, an informational result assembled from acquired
evidence, or a change to an operational environment. Intermediate results supply the components and inputs
needed to complete that objective. In data-science tasks, data preparation and code execution lead to an
executable analysis, as illustrated by DataSciBench and ScienceAgentBench [10, 118]. Artifact production also
requires the parts to work together. Slide generation connects content selection and visual planning [119, 120];
each slide must fit the deck’s outline and surrounding narrative [5, 17]. In a financial workbook, a value
computed on one worksheet may supply calculations on another [121]. Completion therefore depends on both
the individual transformations and the relationships among their results.
Intermediate results can also supply the evidence for an answer or plan. TravelPlanner’s two-stage setting
requires collecting travel options through tools before assembling an itinerary that satisfies the user’s
constraints [83]. GAIA includes questions that require locating a study on the web before recovering the
requested fact [40].
For tasks that change an environment, intermediate changes establish the state needed to complete the
requested operation. Terminal-Bench 2.0 includes configuring systems and migrating databases [122]. In
AIOpsLab’s mitigation tasks, the objective is to restore a running service through interventions such as
configuration changes or version rollbacks [123].
Desktop and web tasks carry these intermediate results across applications. In OSWorld, payment records
determine the recipients of a reminder email; WebArena’s museum-itinerary example combines reference pages
and a map before recording the route in a project README [6, 7].
3.3.2 Managing Action Dependencies and Feedback
These dependencies shape the order and content of subsequent actions. A tool result can supply arguments
for a later call, as in MCP-Bench and MCP-Universe [124, 125]. An interpretation of business context can
determine which data to join or aggregate before a result is computed, as in DABstep [126]. A change of state
can enable a previously unavailable operation: ToolSandbox’s messaging example requires cellular service to be
enabled before the message can be sent [127]. TimeArena’s simulations add duration and resource constraints:
agents must schedule around unfinished actions and occupied objects while advancing independent work [128].
Feedback from execution can require revising the sequence. Execution feedback guides SheetAgent’s workbook
editing loop, while Spreadsheet-RL provides recalculated values during execution in Microsoft Excel [45, 129].
In DeepPresenter, rendered slides guide revisions; VIGA likewise executes candidate programs and inspects
the rendered scenes before continuing scene edits in BlenderBench [58, 130]. The agent must retain these
observations and changes so later actions use the current state.
In many organizational settings, completing a task is only one step within a broader operational process.
A finished financial forecast, for example, may need to pass through compliance verification, managerial
assessment, and budget allocation before taking effect, where advancing the work depends on procedural rules
and interactions with other participants.
20

3.4 Workflow Level
The Workflow Level widens what is delegated from a single objective to the operational process around it.
The process carries the objective through stages with distinct responsibilities and conditions for advancement,
and each stage establishes an output, decision, or confirmation on which another stage or participant relies.
The agent therefore coordinates the handoffs between stages and the procedural requirements that govern
them, including cases where intermediate findings change the remaining work. Because these stages belong
to the delegated work, completion covers the process outcome as well as the final output (Section 2.1): a
correct forecast still cannot take effect while a required approval is missing. CXBench scores final outcomes
and process compliance separately in regulated customer-service tasks, where a run that reaches the correct
final state but fails a process check, such as providing a required disclosure or obtaining consent, counts as a
false pass [131].
The same criterion separates workflow stages from the stages an agent system introduces into its own execution.
Anthropic’s Research system, for example, runs a research stage, in which a lead agent decides how many
search subagents to create and adds more when the evidence is insufficient, followed by a citation stage that
attaches sources to the final report [132]. The requester, however, asks for a sourced answer to a research
question, and even an explicit request for multi-agent execution specifies how the system should work without
defining any stage on which the result depends. Any other arrangement of stages that yielded an equally
well-sourced report would complete the request just as well, so the research and citation stages organize the
execution of a multi-step objective rather than forming part of the delegated work.
3.4.1 Carrying Work through Functional Stages
Stages group actions around an output or decision that subsequent work requires. A handoff can make an
artifact available under an agreed specification, confirm an input, or authorize further work. In WorkArena++,
employee-onboarding tasks connect user creation with ordering and assigning hardware [133]. A similar
handoff occurs in an OfficeBench training task: the agent reads an Excel participant list, creates calendar
events, and emails the training details to participants [76].
The same dependence on stage outputs appears in artifact production. MetaGPT follows the standard
operating procedures of a software team: a requirements document guides architecture and interface design,
then implementation and testing [54]. SlideRL uses research findings and an outline to guide slide generation
and revision [134]. EXP-Bench links experimental design and implementation to execution and conclusions,
while PaperBench requires implementing and executing a paper’s experiments to reproduce its empirical
results [135, 136]. DAComp makes the handoffs explicit in pipeline tasks that connect cleaning, entity
integration, and aggregation under specified data contracts [137].
3.4.2 Coordinating Workflow Progress
Once a stage has produced its output, assessment, confirmation, and policy conditions determine when work
can advance. In CHI-Bench’s healthcare administrative tasks, assessment can yield a decision or a request
for missing information [138]; τ-bench requires user confirmation before specified database updates [29].
In EnterpriseOps-Gym, warranty status determines whether the agent records a customer interaction and
schedules a warranty discussion [139]. AutomationBench’s meeting-conflict task applies a spreadsheet priority
policy, marks the lower-priority meeting for rescheduling, and reports the decision to Slack [140].
Progress also depends on other participants. Inτ 2-bench’s telecommunications tasks, the agent coordinates its
actions with changes made by a tool-using simulated user [30]. In EntCollabBench, roles with different tools
and data access pass intermediate outputs to one another, while TheAgentCompany includes tasks requiring
information from simulated coworkers [31, 38]. In Collaborative Gym, the agent’s literature search and the
human’s feedback feed revisions to a shared related-work draft [141]. Retaining pending changes and their
prerequisites lets the agent integrate each reply into the current workflow. These dependencies can extend
across repeated operations: E-Commerce Bench connects procurement, fulfillment, returns, and cash-flow
decisions over a simulated year [33].

3.5 Workspace Level
In the preceding forms, the request or task setup largely specifies which objects the work involves. As
agents become able to work across entire code repositories and an organization’s documents, emails, and
chats [11, 12, 142], a request can state only the goal. The agent must then establish which objects are relevant
and how their relationships affect completion. A report may depend on a particular workbook version, while
an approved decision may need to be reflected in several records. The Workspace Level makes discovering and
following these relationships part of the delegated responsibility. The agent locates relevant objects, reconciles
their evidence and versions, and, when the task requires updates, preserves consistency among the affected
objects.
3.5.1 Locating Relevant Objects in a Workspace
Relevant material may be distributed across documents and spreadsheets, message histories, code repositories,
or linked business records. Workspace-Bench makes identifying relevant objects and their dependencies part
of the task [36]. GraphForge constructs assignments requiring comparison, reconciliation, or derivation across
files, while WorkGenesis requires drawing on public source files and synthesized companion attachments
to produce occupational deliverables [143, 144]. OfficeQA Pro’s full-corpus tasks require finding the pages
needed for an answer, while DRBench’s application-based tasks require retrieving enterprise material for a
research report [145, 146]. Finding the right page can itself require reading charts and layouts, as in ViDoRe,
introduced with ColPali [147]. As a retrieved object points to further sources, the relevant context develops
through these connections.
3.5.2 Following Dependencies across Workspace Objects
The connections uncovered during search determine how retrieved objects fit into the task. They may be
explicit references, shared entities, or relationships of derivation and version. In CRMArena, identifying which
order a customer refers to requires relating product information to that customer’s purchase history [148].
LOCA-bench makes these cross-source relationships explicit in a task adapted from Toolathlon: assembling an
exam spreadsheet requires matching Canvas announcements and emails to the correct courses [149, 150]. In Loc-
Bench, repository tasks similarly require locating the files and functions relevant to an issue description [151].
Repository repair extends this process to modifying code and testing the affected components [3, 16, 152, 153].
Spider 2.0’s DBT tasks extend this dependence to data-transformation projects: the agent reads SQL, YAML,
and Markdown files to complete a missing transformation and check the resulting database state [154].
Using related objects together can also require reconciling what they say. Agora tasks require deriving a
numerical answer from documents whose terminology, units, and time periods must be reconciled, while
HERB queries connect people, products, and release events across heterogeneous enterprise records [155, 156].
APEX-Agents makes source selection concrete in a valuation task that combines the implied share price in a
discounted cash-flow model with earnings per share from a financial statement for a specified date [157]. Tracing
historical versions and derived-content relationships helps establish which sources support the conclusion [36].
Object relationships also determine what must be rechecked when work changes. In OSWorld 2.0, revised
budget and vendor constraints arriving through TeamChat require the agent to reconsider affected entries in
a purchase-order form [32]. Linking the updated constraints in the chat to the affected form entries identifies
which earlier work needs revision. Discovering these dependencies exercises work openness; maintaining the
result as relevant inputs change exercises persistence (Section 2.2).
3.6 Synthesis: Work Scope and Capability Demands
Across the five work forms, the foundational capabilities introduced in Section 2 support work over an
expanding set of dependencies. As an assignment grows, an interpretation of the evidence can affect decisions
and changes across several stages or objects. The agent must carry those dependencies through the work and
use feedback from the resulting state to decide what can proceed and what needs revision. Work complexity
thus scales through the web of dependencies that these capabilities must manage in concert.

4 Optimization: From Work Experience to System Capability
The capabilities required by work agents must be cultivated via systematic optimization. As discussed in
Section 2, this optimization can take place at both the model and harness levels. Model updates improve
interpretation, reasoning and action selection, while harness updates enhance the interfaces, tools, memory
and execution workflows that support the model. These improvements draw on diverse corpora and experience
gathered during work, including observations of the environment, action trajectories and feedback on task
outcomes. As illustrated in Figure 5, Section 4.1 examines the environments, data, and feedback that

Section 4.4 examines how models and harnesses can adapt together and support continued self-improvement.

4.1.1 Work Environment Construction
Training environments convert work requirements into tasks that agents can execute and evaluate. Each
task includes an initial workspace, available actions, a request, and checks for evaluating the results. These
components can be constructed from existing work records. For example, daVinci-Env reconstructs software
tasks from repositories, and Spreadsheet-RL uses paired workbooks and an Excel runtime [129, 158]. Real-
world tasks often depend on information spread across documents, applications, and people. Therefore,
training environments should preserve these dependencies so that agents can learn which actions to take and
how to verify their results.
Grounding tasks in work materials.Recent pipelines connect work materials to requirements in several
ways. NexForge organizes task generation around capabilities and task forms, while WorkGenesis builds
occupational requests with supporting resources [144, 159]. GraphForge represents evidence across retrieved
files as a graph, then uses it to generate instructions and source-linked rubrics [143]. WorkForge starts from
expert workflow plans, retrieves real files, and identifies facts that guide the instructions, solution plans, and
programmatic and semantic checks [160]. WorkBorne organizes tasks around artifact transitions: a specified
25

actor transforms input materials toward reference outputs [161]. Together, these approaches cover different
aspects of task construction, from generating realistic requirements to grounding them in evidence and defining
the expected results.
Constructing shared and changing state.Tasks spanning multiple applications also require consistent
states and operations. Agent World Model creates database-backed tool environments, EnvCraft builds
sequences of dependent tasks, and AgentMercury constructs business environments with shared entities and
constraints [162–164]. CoworkEnv jointly generates requests, service records, completion checks, and permitted
actions, then validates their consistency before collecting teacher trajectories [166]. Cowork-Gym introduces a
stateful user who can provide information, revise requirements, and modify the shared workspace [167]. Such
changes can affect later actions. For example, an updated customer record may change which operation is
valid, while a revised workbook may invalidate an earlier analysis. WorkRover repeatedly checks and repairs
consistency among the context, task, harness, and verifier [168]. These methods highlight the importance of
maintaining a valid and consistent environment as tasks progress.
CompoWorld extends environment construction to tasks involving multiple services [165]. It builds reusable
services with typed states and common interfaces, uses a world model for tools that are difficult to implement
reliably, and samples dependency graphs across services. Each service maintains its own state, while the
agent transfers information between services through observations and actions. This allows cross-service
dependencies to be controlled alongside the number of services. In a matched comparison using the same
backbone, training procedure, and 1K SFT trajectories, AutomationBench success increases from 12.83%
with single-service environments to 33.33% with composed environments.
Scaling coverage, depth, and validation.Training environments can scale by adding more workspaces,
more tasks within each workspace, or more rollouts per task. These choices expose agents to different materials,
task dependencies, and ways to complete or recover from work. WorkBorne compares broader artifact coverage
with more transitions per corpus under matched trajectory budgets [161]. Broader coverage performs better
at 1K, 3K, and 5K trajectories, while both strategies achieve similar results at 8K. WorkForge reports that
increasing training data from 1K to 16K trajectories improves ALE by 13.0 points and WildClaw by 8.4
points [160]. Its comparisons using equal numbers of trajectories also favor longer interactions, although these
trajectories differ in task content and decision depth. Together, these findings highlight the importance of
tracking both task diversity and interaction structure alongside training volume.
Reported dataset sizes also refer to different outputs of generation pipelines. WorkForge constructs 16,665
tasks, GraphForge retains 2,169 training trajectories from 2,153 selected tasks, and WorkBorne produces 7,882
task environments and 15,485 trajectories from 1,061 artifact corpora [143, 160, 161]. These counts are difficult
to compare without specifying what is measured and at which stage. Thus, we suggest reporting generation
attempts, accepted tasks, usable trajectories, and verification costs to assess how efficiently environment
construction produces useful training experience.
Validation is equally important as environments scale. An executable task may still contain incomplete
requirements, inconsistent sources, or a verifier that accepts incorrect results. Validation must establish both
that a task can be completed and that its verifier accepts correct solutions while detecting errors. It must
also cover any new materials introduced during scaling. In WorkForge’s matched 1K-trajectory ablation,
combining programmatic and semantic checks achieves an ALE score of 22.11, compared with 15.62 for
semantic-only and 14.89 for programmatic-only selection [160]. GraphForge’s corruption audit shows that
deleting a worksheet reduces the target rubric score by 0.377, while finer row-level, numerical, and citation
errors change scores by less than 0.03 on a 0–1 scale [143]. These results suggest combining source-based
judgments with exact checks of critical values and using deliberate errors to test verifier reliability. Failed
checks and their dependencies can also guide environment repairs, as discussed in Section 4.4.3.
Beyond scale and validation, training environments vary in difficulty, openness, persistence, and business
fidelity. Difficulty reflects the decisions and dependencies involved in completing a task, while openness
reflects what the agent must discover during execution. Persistence concerns how long the task state and
responsibilities remain relevant, and business fidelity describes how closely the materials and constraints reflect
real work. Human participants may also provide clarification, corrections, approvals, or final assessments
26

at different stages. A useful training set should combine controlled tasks that expose specific failures with
interactive workspaces that require multiple capabilities. Evaluation on new business tasks can then test
whether these capabilities transfer to unfamiliar materials and conditions.
4.1.2 Corpus and Trajectory Curation
Corpora provide the materials agents work with, while trajectories record how agents use those materials
to complete tasks. Documents, code, and completed artifacts offer domain knowledge and examples, while
interaction records capture observations, actions, and their results. Curation selects and organizes these
sources according to the capabilities being trained. For example, repository-level data can link issues to
relevant files and edits, while execution trajectories also capture tool outputs and failed attempts leading to
those changes [169]. Preserving this context helps models learn what actions to take and why. Task, workspace,
and trajectory identifiers support coverage analysis and data splits based on shared sources. Interactive tasks
also require records of changing requirements and actions taken by other participants [167]. CoworkEnv
collects teacher demonstrations from validated environments, where SFT improves the mean Claw-Eval score
from 46.6 to 64.1. The same environments also support student RL comparisons [166].
Generated trajectories should preserve the dependencies between actions. AgentTrek uses tutorials and guided
replay to construct web interaction trajectories [170]. EnterpriseLab derives tool dependencies from enterprise
schemas and reuses identifiers returned by earlier calls in later operations [171]. TDScaling organizes tools
into business scenarios and generates examples for underrepresented domains and reasoning patterns [176].
Exploration offers another way to collect experience. SEE builds a verified graph of mobile interface transitions
and generates tasks involving interruptions and recovery [177]. Scenario-based generation provides workflows
with clear goals, while exploration captures states that standard demonstrations may miss. Successful
trajectories show how to complete tasks, and recovery segments demonstrate how to proceed when execution
is disrupted.
Data selection should reflect the capabilities being trained. Artifact checks can filter for successful outcomes,
while failed attempts may still provide useful examples for recovery and state tracking. OpenThoughts-Agent
evaluates task sources, teachers, data mixtures, and filtering strategies through downstream training, including
comparisons under matched token budgets [172]. CurateEvo uses development-set failures to improve data
augmentation, filtering, and refinement [173]. As policies improve, repeated easy successes provide less useful
information, while errors involving changed inputs, missed requirements, or costly recovery can guide further
data collection. Construction and development data should remain separate from held-out evaluation tasks.
When tasks share files, splitting data by workspace or source family provides a stronger test of whether models
can generalize to unfamiliar materials and requirements.
4.1.3 Feedback Design
Feedback turns work requirements into learning signals. When success can be measured from the resulting
workspace, direct checks are possible. Spreadsheet-RL recalculates edited workbooks in Excel and compares
relevant regions against target workbooks [129]. Anchor derives business tasks and verifiers from constraint
programs to check feasibility, solution quality, and relationships among records [174]. Other tasks require
judgments about content and presentation. Preference-based reward learning uses human comparisons [178],
GraphForge links rubric items to source evidence, and SlideRL evaluates slide structure, rendering, and
content [134, 143]. CogEvol also checks rendered content and browser interactions [179]. The appropriate
feedback depends on the output, which may require checks of its values, organization, and behavior. Require-
ments specify what counts as success, while verifiers assess the supporting evidence. Keeping these roles
separate allows missing checks or parsing errors to be corrected without changing the task itself.
Feedback can also focus on requirements that agents frequently miss. The Completion-Focused Rubric Reward
of CompoWorld assigns greater weight to criteria with lower pass rates within a rollout group [165]. It rewards
partial progress but gives the maximum reward only when all criteria are satisfied. This encourages agents to
address the remaining steps needed to complete a workflow.
Final outcomes alone do not reveal which actions contributed to success. Intermediate feedback helps connect
27

results to earlier decisions [180, 181]. VERA restores workspace states and checks intermediate requirements
using executable tests and rubrics [175]. WorkRover’s Verifier-to-Turn Credit Assignment uses recorded
dependencies to assign bounded positive credit to earlier turns that contributed to verified progress [168]. For
example, reading a source may receive credit if its information supports a later correct change. Stage checks
identify when progress occurs, while dependency records help determine which earlier decisions contributed to
it. Both require sufficient records of workspace states and their relationships.
Reliable feedback must also account for changing requirements. Cowork-Gym links requirements to success
conditions, supporting evidence, and checkers while allowing users to introduce new constraints [167]. Main-
taining current requirements alongside relevant artifacts helps agents detect omissions when earlier instructions
leave the active context or source data changes. Verification also has a cost. Structural and state checks can
detect common errors efficiently, while semantic inspection and human assessment handle requirements that
need more context. As execution becomes faster and more parallel, verification may become a bottleneck.
Thus, we propose using dependencies among sources and artifacts to determine which checks need to be
repeated, which evidence remains valid, and when further assessment is necessary. Feedback design should
also account for the verification and rework required before an output can be accepted.
4.2 Model Optimization
Model optimization develops the knowledge and decision-making capabilities agents need to perform work. Pre-
training builds broad semantic and computational knowledge; mid-training adapts this foundation to domain
materials, interaction patterns, and long context; post-training strengthens behavior through demonstrations,
execution feedback, and teacher guidance. Figure 6 connects these roles. The model first learns about work
objects, then how to use their interaction histories, and how to improve decisions from task outcomes. The
stages differ in their data mixtures, prediction targets, and training schedules, while token prediction and
agent trajectories contribute across stages.
4.2.1 Pre-training
Pre-training establishes the world knowledge and general capabilities that work agents rely on to understand
unfamiliar tasks and environments. Through large-scale learning from text, code, mathematics, and other
materials, models acquire factual knowledge, domain concepts, common conventions, and computational
skills. These are essential for professional work, where requests often assume knowledge that users do not
explicitly provide. For example, an agent revising a budget report must understand financial categories and
units, interpret relationships between tables and written claims, and recognize how formulas or code produce
numerical results. This background knowledge allows agents to interpret workspace materials, understand
task requirements, and reason about possible actions before adapting to specific workflows. Work materials
can in turn enrich pre-training with structured and multimodal prediction signals. For autoregressive language
models, pre-training uses the standard next-token prediction (NTP) objective. Given a token sequencex, the

4.2.2 Mid-training
Targeted Knowledge Enrichment.Work agents often encounter tasks that require knowledge beyond
the information provided in a user request. Understanding an unfamiliar repository, interpreting business
records, or following domain-specific conventions may depend on knowledge that is underrepresented in general
29

pre-training data. Mid-training addresses this gap by increasing the density of specialized and long-tail
knowledge while preserving the relationships among relevant materials. GLM-5 organizes related files, issues,
pull requests, and commits into training sequences, then increases the proportion of long documents and
synthetic agent trajectories in later stages [187]. Step 3.5 Flash begins with software engineering and tool-use
data at 32K context, retaining 21% pre-training replay, before expanding to 128K with long documents,
synthetic reasoning, and agent data [188]. Kimi K2.5 similarly combines high-quality text and multimodal
materials while extending context from 32K to 256K [186]. These approaches increase exposure to specialized
work knowledge while using replay to preserve broader capabilities. For work agents, organizing knowledge
around related files, requirements, and revisions is particularly useful, as understanding a work object often
requires information distributed across multiple sources.
Agent Interaction Familiarization.Domain knowledge alone cannot prepare an agent for the interactions
required to complete work. An agent must also understand what a tool call changes, how to interpret its
response, and what to do when an operation fails or produces an unexpected result. Mid-training can introduce
these interaction patterns before post-training begins to optimize task behavior. DeepSeek-V4 incorporates
agentic coding data at this stage [182]. Nemotron combines repositories, pull requests, edit histories, and build
logs in a 500B-token mixture at 256K context, including 10% agent traces and 20% pre-training replay [189].
With subsequent SFT held fixed, this stage improves SWE-bench Verified Pass@1 from 60.7% to 64.5%.
daVinci-Dev further combines trajectories reconstructed from pull requests with trajectories collected through
actual tool execution [169]. Its mid-training uses both successful and failed executions, while later SFT focuses
on test-passing trajectories. This distinction is important for work agents, as unsuccessful interactions can
expose errors and intermediate states that success-only demonstrations may omit. Learning from these records
helps models become familiar with the observation, action, and state-change patterns involved in execution.
This provides a stronger starting point for post-training, where feedback can focus on improving decisions and
completing tasks reliably.
Long-Context Learning and Post-Training Readiness.Work agents frequently perform tasks in which
later decisions depend on information or changes from much earlier interactions. For example, modifying a
source workbook may affect several calculations and invalidate parts of a previously generated report. Simply
extending the context window does not ensure that a model can identify these dependencies or reconstruct the
current workspace state. TrajLong addresses this problem by transforming interaction histories into retrieval,
information-combination, and file-state reconstruction tasks using full-token next-token prediction [190].
Under a fixed 40B-token budget and identical later SFT, these tasks outperform raw-trajectory training
on all seven reported ablation benchmarks. Function-aware fill-in-the-middle training similarly uses code
dependencies to select functions or function groups for reconstruction, improving the mean across SWE-bench
Verified and Lite from 13.95 to 15.60 in a controlled 7B comparison [191]. The choice of prediction targets
also matters. In Nemotron’s 100B-token ablation, masking tool responses while retaining them as context
raises SWE-bench Verified Pass@1 from 27.5% to 37.8% after identical SFT [189]. This distinction is relevant
to agent training because tool responses provide evidence for subsequent decisions, even when the model
does not need to generate those responses itself. Efficient access to long histories presents another challenge.
Qwen3.8-Flash-Next introduces sparse attention during continued training at 256K context to reduce the
cost of accessing relevant information [192], connecting this stage to the memory mechanisms in Section 4.3.3.
Ultimately, the value of mid-training should be measured by its contribution to later optimization. Step 3.5
Flash reports stronger and more stable RL gains after incorporating task knowledge and tool-use patterns [188],
while Nemotron compares recipes through identical SFT runs because intermediate model metrics do not
reliably predict downstream agent performance [189].
4.2.3 Post-training
Post-training develops the policies that allow work agents to apply their knowledge to real tasks. Beyond
understanding a request, an agent must select appropriate actions, respond to changes in the workspace, and
determine whether its results satisfy the requirements. Different training methods provide supervision for these
decisions. Demonstrations show how tasks can be completed, execution rewards evaluate the consequences of
actions, teacher guidance improves decisions at states encountered by the agent, and retrospective learning
extracts useful information from previous attempts. Table 5 summarizes these methods according to their
30

learning signals and update mechanisms.
Learning from demonstrations.Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) teaches agents to follow instructions, use
tools, and produce completed work through examples [193–195]. Synthetic instructions provide additional
supervision before task-specific interaction [196]. For work agents, demonstrations can capture both the
steps taken during execution and the structure of an accepted deliverable. Kimi K2 generates tool-use
trajectories by constructing domains, tools, and tasks, simulating interactions, and filtering the results, while
also incorporating real execution environments into post-training [185]. Interactive demonstrations teach
agents how to respond to observations and tool feedback [197, 198]. Artifact-based supervision instead
connects task requirements to completed outputs. CogEvol trains on verified representations of slides and
interactive pages [179], while GraphForge uses source-linked rubrics to select generated solutions for rejection
fine-tuning [143]. CoworkEnv provides curated teacher executions that improve multi-turn performance
on Claw-Eval, with the same executable environments supporting subsequent RL [166]. Together, these
approaches teach agents how to carry out tasks and what acceptable results should look like.
Learning through execution rewards.Reinforcement learning improves an agent’s policy through the
outcomes of its own interactions [199–201]. Web navigation, device control, and repository repair provide
different environments in which actions can be executed and evaluated [202–204]. For artifact-producing
tasks, Spreadsheet-RL evaluates the resulting workbook, while SlideRL combines structural, visual, and
content feedback to assess generated slides [129, 134]. However, work agents often complete tasks through
many dependent steps, making it difficult to determine which earlier decisions contributed to the final result.
Intermediate rewards and credit assignment help address this problem. ADMIRE derives adaptive milestones
from successful GUI trajectories, while HiPER learns hierarchical policies with subgoals and associated value
estimates [205, 206]. GiGPO combines episode-level advantages with comparisons of actions taken from
repeated environment states [207]. WorkRover uses recorded dependencies to assign credit to earlier turns
that support verified progress [168]. These methods provide different ways to connect final outcomes with
intermediate decisions, allowing agents to learn from both completed work and progress made along the way.
Reliable execution feedback also requires accurate records of the interactions being trained on. Mercor and
SkyRL’s knowledge-work recipe uses 1,928 expert-created tasks and emphasizes environment repair, exact
token histories, and small-set overfitting tests before scaling RL [208]. Its loss ablations compare weighting at
the token, sequence, and prompt-group levels, with prompt-group weighting reducing the influence of unusually
long trajectories. The resulting 397B model improves held-out APEX-Agents Pass@1 from 16.11% to 27.29%.
These practices are particularly important for work agents, where tool failures, inconsistent environments, and
long execution histories can make rewards unreliable. Preserving the exact observations and actions behind
each reward helps distinguish policy errors from infrastructure failures and keeps learning aligned with the
interaction that actually occurred.
Learning from teacher guidance.Demonstrations alone may not cover the states an agent encounters
during its own execution, especially after incorrect actions or unexpected tool responses. On-policy distillation
addresses this gap by providing teacher guidance at states reached by the student [209]. TCOD gradually
increases student-controlled interaction, while Guided-OPD reduces teacher-controlled turns and combines
teacher imitation with distillation on student-generated states [210, 211]. This allows supervision to follow
the developing policy more closely [212, 213]. Step-wise on-policy distillation adjusts guidance based on
student–teacher divergence [214]. Other methods improve supervision at specific decisions. LiteGUI selects a
human-verified valid action that matches the student’s attempted action, while Gated Hindsight Distillation
uses a later screenshot to guide an earlier decision [215, 216]. Foundation-model training also uses on-policy
distillation to combine specialized capabilities or preserve them across training stages [182, 187]. For work
agents, these methods are useful because guidance can address errors and partial progress encountered during
actual execution, rather than relying only on previously collected demonstrations.
Learning from accumulated feedback.Work agents can also learn from information accumulated across
attempts, including corrections, execution feedback, and completed results [217–219]. SDPO provides textual
feedback to a self-teacher and distills its improved predictions into the acting policy, while Skill-SD additionally
conditions the teacher on short summaries of earlier attempts [220–222]. RePro uses completed attempts to
teach models which intermediate decisions indicate useful progress [223]. These approaches reuse information
31

revealed during execution to improve future behavior [224]. For example, when generating a report from
multiple sources, demonstrations can teach the initial workflow, execution rewards can assess the completed
report, and teacher guidance can help correct errors encountered during revision. Retrospective feedback can
then turn these corrections into useful experience for subsequent tasks. Section 4.4.3 extends this process
beyond model updates to systems that also improve their environments, external procedures, and methods of
learning.

4.3.1 Observation and Action Interfaces
Observation interfaces determine what information an agent can inspect before making a decision. For work
agents, this often involves locating relevant evidence within large repositories, documents, or tool outputs.
SWE-agent introduces agent-oriented repository operations that make software files easier to search, inspect,
and edit [3]. TACO further improves observations by discovering terminal-output filters, refining them
during execution, and reusing effective rules across tasks [225]. These filters reduce unnecessary output while
preserving relevant state and error information. The main consideration is whether an interface provides the
evidence needed for the next decision without consuming excessive context or requiring repeated inspection.
Action interfaces determine how an agent translates its decisions into operations on files, applications, and
services. Unclear tool descriptions or inconsistent argument conventions can cause repeated errors, even when
the intended operation is appropriate. Trace-Free+ trains a tool-description generator, gradually moving
from supervision based on execution traces to generation using tool descriptions alone [226]. JTPRO jointly
optimizes global instructions and tool descriptions using execution feedback [227]. The former learns tool-use
conventions that can transfer across tool sets, while the latter coordinates instructions within a particular
environment. Both aim to improve tool selection and argument generation, helping agents carry out the
operations required by a task.
Executable support can also correct errors during tool execution. PRISM analyzes tool traces, state changes,
and errors to improve prompts, middleware, or both [228]. Middleware can correct arguments, check
prerequisites, and provide error information that supports recovery. These mechanisms are particularly
important when work agents modify shared records or related objects. For example, checking a file version
beforewritinghelpsensurethataneditappliestothesameobjecttheagentpreviouslyinspected. Toolresponses
should also indicate what changed so the agent can decide whether to continue or recover. Evaluating interfaces
with the same model and tasks helps isolate improvements from external support. In the Mercor/SkyRL
study, correcting tool behavior and observation handling improves the base agent before weight training, while
the training gains also transfer to a held-out code-based harness [208]. Such comparisons help distinguish
improvements in the execution environment from improvements learned by the model.
4.3.2 Workflows and Execution Control
Workflow optimization improves how an agent organizes and executes work across multiple steps [241, 242].
The editable components range from prompts [243–245] and connections between model calls [246, 247]
to executable harness code [248, 249]. Prompt optimization can use feedback-guided revision [250, 251],
reinforcement learning [252], or evolutionary search [253, 254]. More recent approaches extend these ideas to
complete execution procedures. GEPA uses execution traces and outcomes to refine prompts [229], AFlow
searches over workflow graphs [230], and Meta-Harness modifies harness code [231]. These methods provide
different levels of control over how an agent performs work [255]. For example, an improved workflow may
inspect additional evidence before editing, verify intermediate results before dependent operations, or introduce
a recovery step after failure. Its effectiveness should be measured by whether these changes improve execution
on subsequent tasks.
Artifact production illustrates how feedback from individual tasks can improve reusable workflows. AutoDesign
uses rendering, validation, and critic feedback to revise a poster, then analyzes recurring failures to propose
changes to its design harness [232]. This separates improving the current deliverable from improving the
procedure used for future tasks. JIT-Agent takes a related approach by training a separate model to construct
task-specific harnesses using execution reward, latency, and cost from a frozen executor [233]. The designer
learns how to organize execution and produces an explicit procedure for the acting agent. These approaches
show how experience from completed work can guide the design of more effective workflows without requiring
changes to the underlying executor.
Execution control must also preserve progress when work is interrupted, requirements change, or responsibilities
are transferred. For ongoing tasks, a workflow needs clear conditions for continuing, revising previous results,
and determining when delivery is complete. These conditions relate to the requirements and responsibilities
discussed in Section 2. For example, a report awaiting source confirmation should retain that dependency, while
34

a completed deliverable should be checked against the latest requirements before the task closes. Repeated
inspections, retries, and human corrections provide evidence of where workflow changes could reduce delays
and rework. Methods based on retrospective self-preference, adaptive curricula, and task-dependent harness
routing further use feedback to select and deploy suitable procedures [256–258]. Evaluating both the quality
of accepted results and the effort required to produce them helps ensure that workflow optimization improves
real task execution.
4.3.3 Long Context, Memory and Skills
Context management determines which parts of a growing work history remain available for each decision.
Work agents may accumulate lengthy tool outputs, repeated observations, and information that becomes
outdated as the workspace changes. A larger context window alone does not resolve these issues. MemGPT
manages information between active context and external storage, while TACO adjusts the amount and
form of tool output retained during execution [225, 234]. For ongoing work, the active context must also
preserve current requirements, relevant object versions, and unresolved dependencies. These records help
agents determine whether earlier observations and decisions remain valid. Effective context management
therefore supports efficient access to information and reliable continuation after interruptions or revisions.
Memory optimization focuses on preserving useful experience across tasks. ReasoningBank extracts strategies
from successful and failed attempts for later retrieval [235], while ACE maintains an evolving playbook through
generation, reflection, and curation [236]. Skills extend this idea by representing reusable procedures that
guide future execution. SkillOpt improves procedural documents through validated edits [237], SkillAlpha
learns editing policies and evaluates them through downstream execution [238], and CodeSkill extracts, revises,
and maintains a library of coding skills [239]. These approaches allow agents to reuse lessons from previous
work rather than rediscovering procedures for every task. Execution feedback also helps determine which
instructions remain useful and which should be revised as new situations arise.
As reusable experience accumulates, agents must decide which procedures to retain, how to combine them,
and when to apply them. SkillSmith addresses interactions and dependencies among skills under a limited
context budget and considers which operations should be represented as skills or executable tools [240]. Stable
operations can be implemented as tools, while skills provide guidance on when and how to use them. This
distinction is important for work agents that repeatedly perform related tasks with different materials and
constraints. Task-specific state and reusable experience also require different update schedules. A change to a
source workbook should immediately update the current task record, while a revised reporting procedure
should be validated across multiple tasks before broader reuse. Recording the sources and dependencies of
both enables targeted updates. For example, a reporting skill may describe how to reconcile information
from several workbooks, while the task record identifies which workbook version has been checked and which
approval remains pending. Maintaining this distinction allows agents to reuse established procedures while
keeping their execution aligned with the current workspace.
4.4 Model--Harness Coupling and Joint Optimization
The effectiveness of a work agent depends on how well its model and harness work together. The model
must learn to use the information and tools provided by the harness, while the harness must offer interfaces
and procedures that the model can use reliably. This raises an important question of where improvements
should be implemented. A capability may be learned through model training, provided through retrieved
context, encoded as a reusable skill, or implemented directly in control code. We examine how models are
trained within a harness, how the two components can be optimized together, and how repeated execution

4.4.1 Harness-Aware Policy Training
A training harness determines what a policy can observe and do. Tool schemas define available operations
and their arguments, context management controls access to previous interactions, and execution rules
determine how actions affect the workspace. Harness-aware training allows models to learn under these
conditions. Spreadsheet-RL trains agents with a spreadsheet runtime and toolset, while UI-Copilot provides
structured observations, progress summaries, and a copilot for retrieval and calculation [129, 259]. Through
these interactions, the policy learns when to rely on internal reasoning and when to use external tools. The
deployment harness must therefore provide the interfaces and support that the policy has learned to use.
Training systems can also optimize a policy while preserving the agent’s existing execution process. Agent
Lightning connects policy optimization with agent execution, allowing the harness to manage interactions
while the training system learns from the resulting decision records [260]. The available tools, memory access,
and execution rules all influence the experience collected during training. For example, a model trained with
compressed observations needs to learn how to retrieve information that is no longer available in its active
context. The training harness therefore shapes the situations and decisions the policy learns to handle, even
when only model weights are updated.
Models can also learn to construct tools for future tasks. SMITH alternates between tool construction and
tool use, adds generated tools to a reusable pool, and rewards their validity and downstream usefulness [271].
As the available tools evolve, the policy learns how to use the capabilities it creates. For work agents, this
provides a way to turn repeated operations into reusable executable tools while learning when to construct
and invoke them [272]. Feedback from subsequent execution helps determine which generated tools are useful,
allowing the model to improve based on their contributions to later tasks.
4.4.2 Joint Model--Harness Adaptation
Joint adaptation coordinates model learning with changes to external support, which can be revised at a
different pace [273]. BetterTogether alternates between prompt and weight optimization, while Co-Harness
uses improved harnesses to generate model-training trajectories and then adapts the harness to the updated
model [261, 263]. Fast-Slow Training similarly alternates prompt search with blocks of reinforcement-learning
updates [262]. In these approaches, each component is optimized using experience collected with the current
version of the other. This interaction matters because a procedure that compensates for an earlier model’s
limitations may become unnecessary after training, while a stronger model may be able to use workflows that
were previously unreliable.
