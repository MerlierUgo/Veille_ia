## PCBRouteAgent: How Close Are LLM Agents to Becoming PCB Routing Engineers?

Automating printed circuit board (PCB) routing requires coordinating board-level connectivity decisions with geometric execution under strict design rules. Although recent agents can interact with native routing tools, reliable routing on complex boards remains challenging. We present PCBRouteAgent , a multimodal PCB routing agent inspired by how human engineers coordinate global strategy with local execution. A Global Routing Planner combines board images and structured electrical and geometric information to specify connection topology, routing order, corridors, and layer transitions. A Detailed Routing Executor realizes these guides through KiCad’s native routing engine and performs bounded local recovery. Unresolved failures are returned as structured feedback, enabling targeted guide revisions while preserving accepted routes. We evaluate PCBRouteAgent on 247 synthetic and real-world boards from four PCBWorld-Bench test splits. It achieves clean-pass rates of 100%, 94.9%, 100%, and 40% on D2, D3-A, D3-B, and D3-C, respectively. On the three real-world subsets, PCBRouteAgent outperforms the best evaluated baseline in clean-pass rate by 13.9, 36, and 10 percentage points, respectively. Case studies reveal limitations in routing-order planning and signal-specific constraints, highlighting a substantial remaining gap between connection completion and electrically validated PCB designs. The project page and code are available at https://chip-ai-agent.github.io/research/pcbrouteagent and https://github.com/chip-ai-agent/PCBRouteAgent , respectively.

### 1 Introduction

Printed circuit boards (PCBs) provide mechanical support and electrical interconnections for electronic components. Before a circuit design can be fabricated as a PCB, its schematic connectivity must be translated into a physical board layout. This process includes component placement, which determines the positions and orientations of component footprints, followed by routing, which realizes the required electrical connections. As illustrated in Figure 1 , routing connects the pads belonging to each electrical net 1 1 A net is an electrical connectivity group in a PCB design, specifying the component terminals that must be electrically connected according to the circuit schematic. through copper traces and, when necessary, vias that enable transitions between copper layers. A net may contain two or more pads, all of which must form a connected network while remaining electrically isolated from other nets. The resulting routing must satisfy design rules governing clearance, trace width, vias, and board boundaries, as well as signal-specific constraints such as differential-pair requirements [ 1 , 2 ] . Beyond establishing valid connections, routing also seeks to limit wirelength and unnecessary layer transitions while preserving space for other nets. These requirements make PCB routing a coupled physical design problem in which each routing decision affects the connections that remain to be completed.

> Figure 1 : PCB routing connects required pads with copper traces and vias while satisfying design rules.

Routing complex PCBs remains labor-intensive, motivating automatic routers that reduce the need for manual trace construction. Classical rule-based methods use heuristic search [ 3 ] , net ordering, and congestion-aware costs [ 1 ] to construct routes under geometric constraints. Although these methods automate much of the process, complex boards can still require careful tuning and manual intervention [ 2 ] . To reduce this reliance on handcrafted decisions, learning-based approaches have been explored for routing tasks, including global routing [ 4 ] , net ordering [ 5 ] , and PCB fanout [ 6 ] . Their effectiveness, however, depends on how routing decisions are represented and connected to physical execution. As discussed in PCBWorld [ 2 ] , grid-based formulations face rapidly growing decision spaces as routing resolution increases, while approaches that guide an existing autorouter leave much of the physical routing process to that router. These limitations motivate studying agents that interact directly with a design engine and adapt their decisions using execution feedback. PCBWorld [ 2 ] provides such an environment by exposing KiCad’s native routing operations and design-rule feedback to reinforcement-learning policies and tool-using LLM agents. This interaction allows agents to observe the consequences of routing decisions and revise subsequent actions as the board evolves.

Despite this progress, reliable agent-based PCB routing remains challenging. The LLM agents evaluated in PCBWorld [ 2 ] still lag behind rule-based routers in completing connections while satisfying design rules, particularly on complex real-world boards. Providing access to native routing operations and execution feedback does not, by itself, ensure effective board-level coordination. An agent must decide which connections to prioritize, allocate limited routing space across competing nets, and determine when a layer transition is necessary. These decisions are coupled: a locally feasible route may block subsequent connections, while a connection that repeatedly fails may require a change in its topology or layer assignment rather than another local attempt. Moreover, as routing proceeds, the agent must distinguish difficulties that can be resolved locally from those requiring a broader strategy revision. These challenges highlight the need for an explicit mechanism that coordinates board-level planning with geometric execution and uses routing failures to revise the plan.

To address these limitations, we propose PCBRouteAgent , a multimodal PCB routing agent inspired by how human routing engineers coordinate global strategy with local execution. Engineers consider connection priorities, routing regions, and layer usage while constructing individual traces, and revisit these decisions when local obstacles prevent further progress. Following this principle, PCBRouteAgent integrates a Global Routing Planner and a Detailed Routing Executor through a feedback-driven planning–execution loop. The planner combines board images with structured electrical and geometric information to organize connectivity demands into routing guides specifying connection topology, routing order, corridors, and layer transitions. The executor translates these guides into physical traces and vias through KiCad’s native routing engine, using bounded local recovery to handle geometric difficulties. When recovery is insufficient, it returns structured execution evidence to the planner, which generates targeted guide revisions while preserving accepted routes. This design enables board-level guidance to adapt to the physical constraints revealed during execution.

We evaluate PCBRouteAgent on PCBWorld-Bench [ 2 ] , comprising 128 synthetic boards and 119 real-world boards across four test splits. PCBRouteAgent achieves a 100% clean-pass rate on D2. On the real-world D3-A, D3-B, and D3-C subsets, PCBRouteAgent achieves clean-pass rates of 94.9%, 100%, and 40%, respectively, exceeding the best evaluated baseline on each split by 13.9, 36, and 10 percentage points. These baselines achieve 81%, 64%, and 30% clean pass: PCBWorld PPO w/o finish [ 2 ] on D3-A, and Freerouting [ 7 ] on D3-B and D3-C. It also outperforms the published PCBWorld GPT-5.4 agent [ 2 , 8 ] , whose clean-pass rates are 42% on D3-A and 0% on D3-B. On D3-C, PCBRouteAgent reaches 94.9% mean routability and reduces the average design-rule violation count from Freerouting’s 32.0 to 6.3 per board. Despite these gains, our case studies reveal important limitations (Appendix D ). Earlier routing decisions can block subsequent connections, while fully connected outputs can overlook differential-pair relationships. A substantial gap therefore remains between strong benchmark performance and the broader requirements of PCB engineering.


### 2 Related Work


#### 2.1 PCB Routing

PCB routing connects the pads specified by a netlist while respecting the geometry of components, existing copper, board boundaries, and electrical design rules. Classical approaches search for feasible paths and then refine them according to costs such as wirelength, congestion, and the number of vias. In IC routing, TritonRoute [ 9 ] illustrates the global–detailed decomposition: global routing determines coarse topology and layer guidance, while detailed routing realizes traces and vias under geometric design rules. Lee’s maze-routing algorithm [ 3 ] established a systematic way to find connections in a discretized search space; subsequent PCB routers have incorporated constraints that simple shortest-path search cannot capture. For example, Lin et al. [ 1 ] address complicated board constraints and differential pairs within a unified routing algorithm. These methods demonstrate the value of explicit geometric search and rule-aware optimization, but they also make clear that routing quality depends on decisions made across multiple scales: the order in which nets are attempted, the space reserved for future connections, and the local shape of each trace. Learning-based work has targeted different parts of this decision process. Liao et al. [ 4 ] investigate deep reinforcement learning for global routing, while FanoutNet [ 6 ] learns decisions for the specialized PCB fanout problem. GPCB [ 10 ] uses a generative pretrained Transformer to predict routing-related network-flow patterns and then constructs physical routes from those predictions. More recently, world-model reinforcement learning [ 5 ] has been integrated with Freerouting so that learned guidance can be combined with an existing autorouter. These systems differ in what they predict and in how much of the routing procedure is handled by a separate search or routing engine. PCBWorld [ 2 ] provides another setting: it exposes KiCad’s native routing operations to reinforcement-learning policies and tool-using LLM agents, and evaluates the resulting board with engine-based design-rule checks. This interactive setting makes it possible to observe how a routing decision changes the board before choosing the next action. Our work uses that setting to study the agent’s ability to coordinate routing decisions over an evolving board, rather than treating each connection as an isolated path-finding problem.


#### 2.2 LLM Agents for EDA

LLM agents have begun to use EDA tools as part of longer design workflows, where a model plans tool calls and uses their feedback to guide later steps. ReAct [ 11 ] interleaves reasoning with environment actions, while Reflexion [ 12 ] uses feedback and reflective memory to improve subsequent attempts without updating model weights. Such workflows require the agent to track design state, choose suitable tools, and recover when a tool reports an error. ChatEDA [ 13 ] connects language-model planning to EDA tool invocation in an RTL-to-GDSII flow, and EDAid [ 14 ] investigates collaboration among multiple agents for electronic design automation. VerilogCoder [ 15 ] focuses on HDL development, using planning and feedback from code-oriented tools, including waveform tracing, to revise generated Verilog. Although these systems address different stages of hardware design, they share a useful pattern: the agent proposes an action, receives evidence from a domain tool, and uses that evidence to decide what to do next. PCB layout presents a different physical interface to this pattern. PCBAgent [ 16 ] combines an RL-based placement agent with an LLM agent for interactive component-placement optimization; placement choices affect routability, but placement alone does not test whether an agent can complete the required copper connections. Recent PCB benchmarks have made such distinctions more visible. PCB-Bench [ 17 ] evaluates LLMs on PCB placement and routing tasks, including the interpretation of board information. OmniRouting [ 18 ] assesses routing reasoning under geometric, design-rule, and electrical constraints, and includes a tool-augmented task. These evaluations help characterize what a model understands or proposes, while an interactive routing environment additionally reveals whether a sequence of actions yields a valid board as the geometry changes. PCBWorld [ 2 ] supplies this engine-grounded interface and evaluates completed boards with native design-rule feedback. PCBRouteAgent therefore studies an LLM agent in the closed loop between planning, tool execution, board-state observation, and validation. The emphasis is on the agent’s ability to maintain global routing objectives while adapting its next decision to the board produced by earlier actions.


### 3 Methods

From the perspective of a PCB routing engineer, routing requires decisions at two spatial scales: coordinating connection priorities, routing regions, and layer usage across the board, and realizing individual traces around pads and obstacles. A locally feasible connection may consume space needed by another net, while a promising board-level strategy may prove geometrically infeasible during execution. This motivates separating global planning from detailed routing and revising the plan when local difficulties arise. A similar separation is used in IC routing flows such as OpenROAD [ 19 ] , where global routing produces guides for detailed routing.

We adopt this principle for PCB routing. As illustrated in Figure 2 , PCBRouteAgent is a multimodal agentic framework for PCB routing that integrates two tightly coupled components: a Global Routing Planner and a Detailed Routing Executor. The Global Routing Planner takes on the board-level planning responsibilities of a human PCB routing engineer, developing and maintaining a high-level routing strategy that covers key decisions such as connectivity planning, routing priorities, and spatial and layer planning. Guided by this strategy, the Detailed Routing Executor establishes physical connections through KiCad’s native push-and-shove (PNS) router [ 20 , 2 ] while handling local obstacles and geometric constraints, generating structured execution feedback when routing attempts fail. The Global Routing Planner then leverages this feedback and multimodal board context to revise its strategy and guide subsequent execution, enabling the agent to iteratively adapt its routing decisions. The overall procedure is summarized in Algorithm 1 in Appendix A .

#### 3.1 Task Formulation

Given an initial PCB $B_{0}$ , a target net set $\mathcal{N}$ , and design rules $\mathcal{R}$ , the routing task is to produce a routed board $B_{r}=\mathcal{F}(B_{0},\mathcal{N},\mathcal{R})$ , where $\mathcal{F}$ denotes the routing agent that maps the input board, target nets, and design rules to the resulting board. Here, $B_{0}$ specifies fixed component placements, pad geometries, copper layers, obstacles, and existing routing; $\mathcal{N}$ specifies the required electrical connections; and $\mathcal{R}$ defines track widths, clearances, and via constraints. The agent accesses these inputs through board images and structured board information. The output $B_{r}$ contains physical traces and vias saved in a KiCad PCB file. The objective is to connect all pads belonging to each target net while satisfying the design rules and keeping wirelength and via count low.


#### 3.2 Global Routing Planner

Routing multiple nets on a shared PCB requires coordinating limited routing space while maintaining electrical connectivity. To address this challenge, the Global Routing Planner first organizes disconnected components within each net into a connection topology and then assigns spatial routing guides to support their physical realization. The resulting routing guide provides the Detailed Routing Executor with a structured plan for establishing these connections.


##### 3.2.1 PCB BoardRepresentation

Effective global routing requires understanding both the outstanding connectivity demands and the physical constraints under which these connections must be established. Given the initial PCB $B_{0}$ , target net set $\mathcal{N}$ , and design rules $\mathcal{R}$ , we construct a multimodal board representation comprising board images $\mathcal{I}$ and structured text $S$ . Through PCBWorld’s KiCad-based engine [ 2 , 20 ] , we extract pad geometries, copper layers, obstacles, and existing routing from $B_{0}$ , target-net connectivity from $\mathcal{N}$ , and routing constraints from $\mathcal{R}$ . This information is represented in two complementary forms. The visual modality $\mathcal{I}=\{I_{\mathrm{layout}},I_{\mathrm{front}},I_{\mathrm{back}},I_{\mathrm{demand}}\}$ depicts the overall board layout, front- and back-copper states, and outstanding connectivity demands. The textual modality $S$ serializes pad coordinates and geometries, layer information, net assignments, connectivity clusters, and design rules. For each target net $n\in\mathcal{N}$ , electrically connected pads form clusters $\mathcal{C}_{n}=\{C_{n,1},\ldots,C_{n,K_{n}}\}$ , whose remaining connections define the routing demands. Together, the images provide spatial context, while the structured text supplies precise identifiers and numerical constraints for global planning.


##### 3.2.2 Connection Topology and Routing Order

Although the board representation identifies the remaining connectivity demands, it does not specify how disconnected clusters should be connected. For a net containing multiple clusters, numerous connection topologies can satisfy the same electrical requirements, while independently connecting every pair would introduce unnecessary routing. To avoid exhaustively searching this combinatorial space, we formulate connectivity planning as a minimum spanning tree (MST) [ 21 ] problem over the disconnected clusters, yielding a compact initial topology. For each target net $n$ , we construct a weighted graph $G_{n}=(\mathcal{C}_{n},\mathcal{E}_{n},w_{n})$ , where vertices represent connectivity clusters and edges represent candidate inter-cluster connections. Each candidate edge $e\in\mathcal{E}_{n}$ is assigned a weight $w_{n}(e)$ based on the minimum Euclidean distance between pads in the corresponding clusters, with an additional penalty for incompatible pad layers. The resulting MST $T_{n}=(\mathcal{C}_{n},\mathcal{E}_{n}^{*})$ specifies $K_{n}-1$ connection tasks spanning the $K_{n}$ clusters. It minimizes the estimated total cost under this heuristic, while leaving the physical realization of these connections to subsequent routing stages.

Beyond determining the connection topology within each net, the planner must also coordinate routing across multiple nets that share limited board space. We therefore initialize the routing order using the total estimated connection length of each net’s spanning tree, prioritizing shorter nets first. This provides a lightweight starting strategy for organizing routing tasks, without requiring an exhaustive search over possible execution orders. Both the connection topology and routing priorities remain adjustable as subsequent execution reveals routing constraints not captured by the initial estimates.


##### 3.2.3 Spatial and Layer Planning

While the connection topology specifies which clusters should be connected, it does not determine how these connections should traverse the physical board. Directly prescribing exact trace geometries at the global planning stage would require resolving fine-grained obstacles and design-rule constraints before routing execution. Instead, we separate high-level spatial guidance from low-level geometric realization, allowing the Global Routing Planner to guide routing decisions while preserving flexibility for local obstacle avoidance. Specifically, for each connection edge $e$ , we use the endpoint pads selected during topology construction to initialize ordered corridor guidance $\mathcal{Q}_{e}=(\mathcal{Q}_{e,1},\ldots,\mathcal{Q}_{e,m_{e}})$ , where $m_{e}$ is the number of corridor stages for connection $e$ . When the endpoints share a copper layer, we construct a single corridor $\mathcal{Q}_{e,1}$ from their axis-aligned bounding box, expanded by a margin $\delta$ in each direction. The resulting rectangle provides spatial guidance while leaving the exact trace geometry to the Detailed Routing Executor.

Beyond spatial guidance, routing may also require transitions between copper layers when the endpoints cannot be connected on a common layer. We split the connection at its midpoint and construct two layer-specific corridors, $\mathcal{Q}_{e,1}$ from the source pad to the midpoint and $\mathcal{Q}_{e,2}$ from the midpoint to the target pad, using the same bounding-box expansion. A via-transition region $\mathcal{V}_{e}$ is initialized by expanding the midpoint by $\delta$ in each direction. Rather than fixing an exact via location, the planner specifies a candidate region in which the Detailed Routing Executor can search for a feasible transition using KiCad’s native routing engine. The resulting connection topology, routing order, and spatial and layer guidance are consolidated into an initial routing guide, which is validated and translated into routing tasks for the Detailed Routing Executor.


##### 3.2.4 Feedback-Driven Replanning

Although heuristic initialization provides an efficient starting point, its simplified cost estimates and spatial guidance cannot fully anticipate the geometric constraints encountered during physical routing. Connections that appear feasible at the planning stage may fail due to obstacles, restricted routing space, or layer constraints. To address this gap, the Global Routing Planner revises its routing strategy using evidence obtained from detailed execution.

When a connection remains unresolved after the Detailed Routing Executor’s local recovery attempts (Section 3.3.2 ), the executor produces structured feedback $\mathcal{F}_{e}$ for each unresolved connection $e$ , describing the failed attempts, last reached positions, potential obstacles, and deviations from the original guide. These feedback packets are aggregated and provided to the multimodal LLM used by the Global Routing Planner, together with the initial board images $\mathcal{I}$ , structured board information $S$ , the current routing guide, and updated connectivity summaries. The initial images remain fixed across replanning rounds; execution feedback and connectivity summaries provide observations of the evolving board. By jointly considering spatial context and execution evidence, the planner can revise high-level decisions that local routing alone cannot resolve.

At replanning round $t$ , let $\mathcal{G}_{t}$ denote the current routing guide, $\mathcal{D}_{t}$ the batch of feedback packets $\mathcal{F}_{e}$ for unresolved connections, and $\mathcal{K}_{t}$ the updated connectivity summaries. The multimodal LLM $\mathcal{M}_{\theta}$ generates a guide patch, a structured set of local updates to the current routing guide, as

where $\theta$ denotes the model parameters, $\Delta\mathcal{G}_{t}$ is the structured output patch, and $P_{\mathrm{sys}}$ and $P_{\mathrm{replan}}$ are the system instructions and replanning prompt template, respectively. Their templates are listed in Appendices B.1.1 and B.1.2 .

The planner outputs a structured guide patch specifying targeted changes to connection endpoints, routing corridors, via-transition regions, or routing order. The patch updates the affected portions of the current guide while preserving previously accepted routes outside that scope. Before execution resumes, the patch is checked for consistency with the current snapshot and guide revision, as well as valid references to clusters, corridors, and copper layers. If validation fails, the same planner may be asked to correct its response using the previous output and validation errors; the correction prompt template is listed in Appendix B.2 . The validated patch is merged into the guide, and affected connection tasks are recompiled for the Detailed Routing Executor. This planning–execution loop continues until the target connections are completed, no further tasks can be scheduled, or the execution or replanning budget is exhausted.


#### 3.3 Detailed Routing Executor

While the Global Routing Planner determines which connections to establish and where they should be routed, realizing these decisions requires resolving precise geometric constraints that cannot be fully captured by high-level guidance. The Detailed Routing Executor translates the routing guide into physical traces and vias through KiCad’s native PNS router, attempts bounded local recovery when routing fails, and returns unresolved failures to the Global Routing Planner for guide revision.


##### 3.3.1 Guided Routing Execution

The Detailed Routing Executor takes the routing guide produced by the Global Routing Planner and compiles its planned connection edges into executable tasks. For each connection edge $e$ , a task specifies the source and target connectivity clusters, candidate pad anchors, ordered layer-specific corridors $\mathcal{Q}_{e}$ , and optional via-transition regions $\mathcal{V}_{e}$ . The executor uses this guidance together with the design rules $\mathcal{R}$ to select routing targets and invoke native routing operations. These tasks are scheduled according to the routing priorities established by the Global Routing Planner, with connections within each net following their predefined task order. For each task, the executor selects a source anchor from the designated cluster that lies within the initial routing corridor and supports its assigned copper layer, prioritizing candidates closer to the next routing target. A compatible target anchor is identified from the destination cluster based on the final routing stage.

Instead of prescribing a fixed trace path, the executor progressively advances through the planned routing stages by selecting intermediate waypoints. For consecutive corridors $\mathcal{Q}_{e,j}$ and $\mathcal{Q}_{e,j+1}$ on the same layer, it identifies their overlapping region and generates candidate waypoints at the region center and surrounding positions, accounting for trace width and clearance requirements. These candidates are attempted sequentially through PNS, which constructs the physical trace between the current routing position and the selected target while handling local geometric constraints. Upon a successful attempt, the executor updates its routing position and proceeds to the next stage.

When a connection requires a layer transition, the executor instead generates candidate via locations within the designated transition region $\mathcal{V}_{e}$ , accounting for via dimensions and clearance requirements. It then attempts to establish the transition before continuing on the next copper layer. After traversing the planned routing stages, the executor attempts to reach the target cluster and verifies whether the source and target clusters have become electrically connected. This connectivity-based verification ensures that task completion reflects an established physical connection rather than merely a successful intermediate routing operation.


##### 3.3.2 Local Recovery and Execution Feedback

Although PNS can handle many local geometric constraints, routing attempts may still fail when obstacles block the selected path, restrict access to intermediate waypoints, or prevent a layer transition. Immediately triggering global replanning for every failure would introduce unnecessary overhead, particularly when alternative local paths remain available. We therefore employ a bounded recovery strategy that progressively explores alternative routing options before escalating unresolved failures to the Global Routing Planner.

When a routing attempt fails, the executor first explores local geometric alternatives through PNS, using walkaround to navigate obstacles and shove to adjust obstructing traces when necessary. If the current target remains unreachable, it attempts alternative waypoints within the designated routing region. For more challenging same-layer obstacles, a grid-based A* search [ 22 ] identifies potential detours, which are subsequently executed through PNS. The executor can further backtrack to earlier routing stages or switch to alternative source anchors within the same connectivity cluster. These operations expand the local search space while preserving the connection topology and layer-transition regions established by the Global Routing Planner. To avoid excessive exploration, recovery attempts are bounded by retry and execution budgets.

When local recovery is exhausted or a planned layer transition cannot be completed, the executor generates a structured feedback packet $\mathcal{F}_{e}$ for the current connection $e$ , describing the attempted routing operations, last reached position, potential blocking obstacles, affected routing regions, and deviations from the original guidance. Failed tasks are rolled back to a consistent checkpoint, while previously accepted connections outside the affected scope are preserved. Rather than reporting failure as a binary outcome, the executor provides actionable evidence that helps the Global Routing Planner identify which higher-level decisions may require revision. This separation allows local geometric difficulties to be addressed through efficient recovery operations, while reserving global replanning for failures that cannot be resolved within the existing routing strategy. The resulting feedback is passed to the Global Routing Planner for the feedback-driven replanning described in Section 3.2.4 .

The execution controller terminates the routing loop when all target connections are established, no further tasks or permitted recovery attempts remain, or the execution budget is exhausted. Connections that exhaust their replanning allowance are recorded as unresolved while other tasks may continue. A checkpoint or rollback failure also triggers early termination. On termination, the system saves the last consistent board state and reports completed and unresolved connections. Routing success is determined by the official evaluator applied to the saved board.


### 4 Experimental Setup

We follow the agent-comparison setting of PCBWorld-Bench [ 2 ] . Each instance is a board in KiCad’s [ 20 ] native .kicad_pcb format, with reference routes removed; component placement, netlist, and design rules remain fixed. The task is to complete the connections on that board, and the final board is evaluated by KiCad rather than by the agent’s own estimate of success. We use the same three test sets as the PCBWorld main comparison: 128 synthetic, two-layer gridless boards from D2-test; 99 small open-source boards from D3-A; and 10 medium open-source boards from D3-B. We additionally include the 10 large open-source boards in PCBWorld’s [ 2 ] fixed D3-C evaluation subset. PCBWorld excludes one of the 100 D3-A boards because its engine interface cannot load it. The D3-B and D3-C test subsets contain two-layer boards selected across each split’s pad-count range; D3-C contains boards with more than 100 pads. D2 tests routing on boards drawn from the synthetic distribution, while all three D3 splits test transfer to real designs with their original design-rule settings. The Reference rows in Tables 2 – 4 evaluate the original routed designs retained in PCBWorld’s open-source board corpus [ 2 ] . Each routing input is obtained by removing the target routing while preserving component placement, the netlist, and design rules; the Reference is the retained design before this removal, scored using the same official engine-based evaluator. D3-A and D3-B Reference values are taken from PCBWorld’s published Table 22, while the D3-C Reference values are evaluated locally. These rows provide an existing-design reference for comparison with automatically produced routes. We use PCBWorld’s published board identifiers and split definitions to keep the comparison board-for-board.

We use PCBWorld’s KiCad-based board evaluator [ 2 ] for the reported routing quality. Published D2, D3-A, and D3-B baseline values in Tables 1 – 4 use PCBWorld’s single-rollout (@1) means from Table 22. The main tables report routing quality. We initialize routing corridors with an expansion margin of $\delta=1.5$ mm. The controller uses a per-board replanning budget of 12, with at most four replans per connection and five batch-replanning rounds. Local recovery allows two stage backtracks, one same-layer A* detour, and two alternative-anchor attempts per connection, with at most one rip-up per obstructing net. For Claude Opus 5.5, D2 and D3-A use a maximum output length of 32,000 tokens with medium reasoning effort; D3-B uses a 32,000-token output limit, and D3-C uses 64,000 tokens with low effort. Reported Opus outputs undergo up to five guarded angle-repair passes, accepting changes only when connectivity, design-rule checks, and wirelength do not worsen. We score all saved boards with PCBWorld’s official evaluation settings. The potential penalizes incomplete connections, error-level design-rule violations, wirelength, and vias; warning-level issues do not affect the score. Prompt templates are provided in Appendix B .

The local Haiku comparison also shows that PCBRouteAgent requires fewer computational resources (Appendix C ). Across all 247 boards, model calls decrease from 22,529 to 172, combined input and output tokens from 105.7M to 12.0M, and API cost from $20.72 to $4.77. These correspond to approximately $131\times$ fewer model calls, $8.8\times$ lower token usage, $4.3\times$ lower API cost, and $14\times$ lower cumulative runtime. Together with the higher clean-pass rates on all four splits, these results show that the planning–execution framework improves both routing completion and computational efficiency.

Improved completion need not produce shorter routing. On D3-B, the Opus variant uses 10.9% more wirelength than Freerouting while improving clean-pass rate by 36 percentage points. On D3-C, its wirelength is 8.2% higher, with a comparable via count of 21.3 versus 21.4. These results indicate a remaining opportunity to improve routing compactness while maintaining connectivity. Wirelength and via count must also be interpreted alongside completion: methods that leave many connections unrouted can obtain low physical costs simply by constructing less copper.

Our case studies reveal two limitations beyond aggregate routing scores (Appendix D ). First, early routing decisions can restrict access to pads and prevent later connections from being completed despite local recovery. Second, complete connectivity does not ensure that signal-specific requirements, such as differential-pair routing [ 31 ] , are addressed. In the USB example (Figure 3 ), PCBRouteAgent completes all 55 connections but routes D+ and D- independently, without explicitly representing their differential-pair relationship. This result calls for electrical validation beyond connectivity and basic design-rule checks. These findings indicate that approaching the capabilities of a PCB routing engineer requires stronger coordination across routing decisions and explicit modeling of signal-specific constraints.

### 6 Conclusion

We presented PCBRouteAgent, a multimodal PCB routing agent that coordinates global planning and native geometric execution through structured feedback. Inspired by the interaction between board-level strategy and local routing decisions in engineering workflows, the framework combines routing guides, bounded local recovery, and targeted guide revisions. Evaluation on 247 synthetic and real-world boards shows that PCBRouteAgent achieves complete, rule-clean routing on D2 and D3-B and improves clean-pass rate over the best evaluated baseline on all three real-world subsets. Large-board completion and routing compactness remain important challenges. Future work will investigate more effective spatial and layer planning, adaptive recovery budgets, and routing refinement that reduces physical cost while preserving completed connections.

#### D.2 Failure analysis of incomplete routing

Earlier routing decisions can consume the local space needed by later connections. On the D3-C board in Figure 5 , the Opus variant completes 70 of 83 connections, whereas the reference completes all 83. Two signal nets must connect the controller and the sensor: SCL carries the clock signal and SDA carries data.

> Figure 5 : Controller and sensor regions in the reference (left) and PCBRouteAgent output (right). Dashed lines indicate retry guides, not laid copper.

The saved sequence routes sensor power before these signals, without reserving pad access or space for layer-changing vias. Initial attempts stall near crowded controller pads. Changing layers on retry gets the signals out, but their approaches to the sensor encounter the existing power route. Repair removes unfinished attempts while retaining that route, so the local restriction remains. The reference completes both signals with different paths and layer choices. This case suggests broader look-ahead in ordering and repair: reserve pad access, prioritize connections with few alternatives, and revisit earlier routes when needed.


#### D.3 Electrical constraints beyond routing completion

Full connectivity does not by itself establish electrical usability. On the D3-B board in Figure 3 , the Opus variant completes all 55 connections but routes USB D+ and D- independently.

The two USB traces carry data between the connector and controller as a differential pair [ 31 ] , for which joint control of spacing and length balance is recommended. The reference traces run side by side and each measures 30.25 mm. PCBRouteAgent produces separate paths of 34.03 mm and 36.49 mm. The planning input specifies individual net widths and clearances but does not encode the pair relationship. This exposes a limitation in signal-specific planning: the task specification and checker need to capture constraints that depend on signal roles, in addition to connectivity and basic design rules. Electrical validation is still required to determine whether this full-speed USB link operates reliably.
