## Ultimate3D: Teaching Multimodal LLMs 3D Camera-Object Relations by Large-Scale Visual Instruction Data

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) struggle with accurately capturing camera-object relations, especially for object orientation, camera viewpoint, and camera shots. This stems from the fact that existing MLLMs are trained on images with limited diverse camera-object relations and corresponding textual descriptions. To address this, we propose a synthetic generation pipeline to create large-scale 3D visual instruction datasets. Our framework takes 3D assets as input and uses rendering and diffusion-based image generation models to create photorealistic images preserving precise camera-object relations. Additionally, large language models (LLMs) are used to generate text prompts for guiding visual instruction tuning and controlling image generation. We create Ultimate3D, a large-scale 3D-grounded 2D visual instruction dataset. Ultimate3D consists of 240K VQAs with precise camera-object annotations, and corresponding benchmark. MLLMs fine-tuned on our proposed dataset outperform commercial models by a large margin, achieving an average accuracy improvement of 29.7% on camera-object relation recognition tasks. Our code, dataset, and benchmark will contribute to broad MLLM applications.

The advent of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) Dai et al. (2023) ; Li et al. (2023a) ; Liu et al. (2024b) ; Liu et al. (2024a) ; Li et al. (2024a) ; Chen et al. (2024c) ; Lin et al. (2024) ; Tong et al. (2024a) ; Meta (2024) ; Google DeepMind (2026b) ; Bai et al. (2025) ; Yang et al. (2025) have demonstrated remarkable success on tasks including image captioning, embodied robotic manipulation, and visual question answering (VQA). MLLMs bridge visual understanding from vision encoders Dosovitskiy (2020) ; Oquab et al. (2023) with powerful verbal capability from large language models (LLMs) Chiang et al. (2023) ; Touvron et al. (2023) . Collectively this provides end-to-end inferencing across image and text modalities. In fact, the recently released Gemini-3.5 Google DeepMind (2026a) , GPT-5.5 Singh et al. (2026) and Claude-4.7-Opus Anthropic (2026) have extended their performances to be comparable to human intelligence in some of the experimented tasks Yang et al. (2023) .

However, research works have found needs and weaknesses of MLLMs Tong et al. (2024b) ; Fu et al. (2024) ; Yuksekgonul et al. (2022) . Tong et al. Tong et al. (2024b) point out the deficiency in visual representations learnt by ViT Dosovitskiy (2020) . In particular, two weakness categories (i.e., orientation/direction, viewpoint/perspective) focus on camera-object relations such as distinguishing simple concepts about object orientation (”right”, ”front”, ”back”, etc.), camera viewpoint location (”top”, ”bottom”, etc.), and camera-shot styles (”close-up”, ”long-shot”, etc.). As two examples in Fig. 1 show, current SOTA model (i.e., Gemini-3.5) struggle with understanding such camera-object relations. The challenges to improving 3D-aware MLLM abilities on recognizing camera-object relations include: (1) capturing the complexity of the task, and (2) having a dataset containing millions of image-text pairs as is a common requirement for training MLLM on spatial perception tasks Cheng et al. (2024) ; Chen et al. (2024a) ; Guo et al. (2024) .

Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, none of the existing MLLMs or training datasets focus on the aforementioned camera-object relations. Prior methods for MLLM training data generation can be divided into the following two groups: (1) Relabeling of existing real images . These works focus on generating textual captions by deep learning models given existing images Chen et al. (2024b) ; Li et al. (2024b) ; Zhang et al. (2024b) ; Sharifzadeh et al. (2024) ; Zhang et al. (2023b) ; Du et al. (2023a) . Specifically, SpatialRGPT Cheng et al. (2024) and SpatialVLM Chen et al. (2024a) address spatial perception and reasoning weakness by providing a comprehensive data annotation pipeline leveraging multiple image segmentation, depth estimation, and camera calibration models to produce dense predictions for real images. But, in practice the accuracy of the predicted labels is not high and the dataset size and distribution is constrained by existing image datasets which predominantly only contain ”front” view objects. (2) Generating synthetic images . Typically text-to-image DMs are utilized for synthetic image generation based on provided text prompts Liu et al. (2024c) ; Du et al. (2023b) ; Li et al. (2023b) . But, their score-based data curation is not robust to camera-object relations, or generation failures (See Appendix 10 ). Alternatively, simulation-based generation pipelines for visual reasoning Johnson et al. (2017) ; Liu et al. (2019) ; Greff et al. (2022) may provide ground truth camera-object relations, but the generated image quality, complexity, and diversity are still far from realistic. In summary, existing synthetic dataset generation pipelines fail to provide image-text pairs with ground truth camera-object relation, realistic image quality, and diverse image subject and context.

In this work, we propose a synthetic generation pipeline to create unlimited image-text pairs focusing on camera-object relations. With 3D assets (e.g., 3D models with texture, materials, and animation) as input, our framework utilizes a renderer (e.g., Blender) with arbitrary camera-object relation parameters to render a series of 3D ground truth priors (i.e., RGB, depth, segmentation). Those 3D priors are used as comprehensive conditions for stacked diffusion-based generation models (e.g., ControlNet) to generate photorealistic images preserving precise camera-object relations. Simultaneously, LLMs are used to generate versatile text prompts for the controlling of image context and object appearance, and to generate diverse VQA prompts regarding camera-object relations. With our framework, we have created the Ultimate3D dataset as a future benchmark for camera-object relations. The proposed framework serves as a data-generation pipeline that produces photorealistic image-text pairs with precisely controlled camera-object relations from 3D assets. We then use standard fine-tuning protocols to evaluate the effectiveness of Ultimate3D across multiple open-source MLLMs, including LLaVA-, Llama-, Gemma-, and Qwen-based models. Models fine-tuned on Ultimate3D outperform commercial MLLMs by a large margin (i.e., an average 29.7% accuracy improvement) on recognizing camera-object relations.

Our contributions include:

- • A synthetic generation pipeline for unlimited image-text pair creation of diverse camera-object relations.

- • A dataset and benchmark for further improving and evaluating camera-object relations in MLLMs.

- • Extensive evaluation across multiple open-source MLLMs, showing that Ultimate3D fine-tuning consistently improves camera-object relation recognition and outperforms commercial SOTA models.

#### 2 Related Works


##### 2.1 Datasets and Benchmarks for MLLMs

MLLMs use various dataset recipes for visual instruction tuning Dai et al. (2023) ; Li et al. (2023a) ; Liu et al. (2024b) ; Liu et al. (2024a) ; Li et al. (2024a) ; Chen et al. (2024c) ; Lin et al. (2024) ; Tong et al. (2024a) . Typically, dozens of datasets, focusing on different areas, are combined to enable the comprehensive abilities of MLLMs. Datasets include image captioning Lin et al. (2014) ; Young et al. (2014) ; Yu et al. (2016) , visual reasoning Hudson and Manning (2019) ; Liu et al. (2023) , general VQA data Gurari et al. (2018) ; Wang et al. (2023a) ; Liu et al. (2024b) on knowledge grounding Marino et al. (2019) ; Lu et al. (2022) , image understanding Goyal et al. (2017) , and OCR Mishra et al. (2019) ; Singh et al. (2019) . Further, most visual instruction datasets are relabeled or created from other existing image-based datasets (e.g., COCO Lin et al. (2014) , LAION Schuhmann et al. (2022) , LVIS Gupta et al. (2019) , Visual Genome Krishna et al. (2017) , ADE20K Zhou et al. (2019) ). As far as we know, none of the datasets focus on camera-object relations. Thus, the missing ground truth camera-object relation data exacerbates the task for deep learning models, such as SpatialRGPT Cheng et al. (2024) and SpatialVLM Chen et al. (2024a) . In fact, multiple papers have indicated MLLMs’ poor distinguishing ability on camera-object relations Tong et al. (2024b) ; Fu et al. (2024) . Our paper fills this gap by providing a generation pipeline, dataset, and benchmark.

In particular for the visual instruction data generation, LLMs, like GPT-4o OpenAI (2023) , have been successfully utilized in LLaVA Liu et al. (2024b) ; Liu et al. (2023) for dialogue text instruction generation, and used in MMVP Tong et al. (2024b) for answer grading. However, generating high-quality images corresponding to proposed text prompts has not been well developed. In this project, we leverage the diverse text generation capability of GPT-4o for the text prompt generation and we provide an image generation pipeline regarding 3D-awareness.


##### 2.2 Simulation-based Image Generation

CLEVR Johnson et al. (2017) and Kubric Greff et al. (2022) use 3D engines to generate synthetic images by rendering 3D primitives at multiple arbitrary camera-object relations. Generally, simulation-based image rendering easily preserve ground truth camera-object relations in arbitrary viewpoint settings. However, the generated images are neither photorealistic nor diverse. Realism is limited by the assets’ level of detail and textures, and diversity is restricted by the small number of assets. These limitations constrain the usage of synthetic images in MLLM training to only object counting or simple spatial reasoning tasks Tong et al. (2024a) . In this project, we leverage the 3D geometry prior rendered by a 3D renderer to precisely guide the image generation pipeline for preserving ground truth camera-object relation.


##### 2.3 Generative Images

Generative models provide advanced image generation by iteratively restoring the original data by denoising from Gaussian noise Ho et al. (2020) ; Song et al. (2020) ; Song et al. (2025) ; Song et al. (2024) ; He et al. (2023a) ; He et al. (2024) ; Song et al. (2023) ; Liao et al. (2025) ; Hua et al. (2025) ; Sun et al. (2025) ; Tang et al. (2025a) ; He and Aliaga (2024) ; Tang et al. (2025b) ; He and Aliaga (2023) ; He et al. (2023b) ; Yuan et al. (2025b) ; Yuan et al. (2025a) . Open-sourced Stable Diffusion Rombach et al. (2022) , and SDXL Podell et al. (2023) enable high-resolution and photo-realistic image generation and editing. While generative models provide versatile context and subject, representative works (e.g., AURORA Krojer et al. (2024) , EditBench Wang et al. (2023b) , Prompt-to-Prompt Hertz et al. (2022) , and MagicBrush Zhang et al. (2024a) ) fail to control camera-object relations in the generated content, and quality guarantees rely on human checking. ControlNet Zhang et al. (2023a) , built on versatile backbone DMs (e.g., SDXL), provides spatial control in the generated images by using various image priors (e.g., Canny edges, semantic segmentation, relative depth).

In particular, Ma et al. Ma et al. (2024a) explore using synthetic image generation for 3D pose estimation by conditioning diffusion models on pose angles. However, their approach does not address advancing MLLMs 3D spatial understanding, nor does it consider VQA instruction generation. Moreover, their reliance on 2D Canny priors (i.e., silhouette) brings severe artifacts for images with backward viewpoint and complex depth variations. In contrast, our method incorporates depth priors to enhance geometric accuracy and leverages SDXL’s superior fidelity to improve image realism. Further discussions and user studies are in Sec. 4.5 and Sec. 4.6 .


#### 3 Method

Our framework is described in Fig. 2 and Appendix 19 . Given input 3D asset $\mathcal{A}$ , its category $c$ , and arbitrary camera-object relation $\beta$ , our framework generates photorealistic image $I_{syn}$ preserving $\beta$ , and corresponding text QA instruction $\mathcal{T}_{qa}$ . Our pipeline consists of 3D visual prior rendering (Sec. 3.1 ), diverse image description generation (Sec. 3.2 ), controlled image generation (Sec. 3.3 ), and 3D-aware text instruction generation (Sec. 3.4 ).

> Figure 3: 3D Visual Prior Rendering. With a general camera model, our method utilizes a 3D Renderer (e.g., Blender) to render multiple 3D visual priors given arbitrary camera-object relations. $O$ : camera center; $A$ : object center; $D$ : camera-object distance.


##### 3.1 3D Visual Prior Rendering

Our system renders several 3D visual priors including 2D depth images, Canny-edge images, and segmentation masks from arbitrary viewpoints. These 3D visual priors contain the scene’s geometry and will be used together with LLM-generated image descriptions to collectively control the DM-based image generation in Sec. 3.3 .

Camera-Object Relation Definition. As Fig. 3 shows, we define camera-object relations using an explicit 3D asset representation and camera-object relative pose.

Each input 3D asset is represented as a textured polygonal mesh $\mathcal{A}=(V,F,\mathcal{P}),$ where $V=\{\mathbf{v}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},i=1...N\}$ denotes the mesh vertices, $F$ denotes the mesh faces, and $\mathcal{P}$ contains appearance information such as materials and textures. We associate each asset with an object coordinate frame $\mathcal{F}_{O}$ , whose origin is located at the object center $A\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$ (the red point in Fig. 3 ), whose vertical axis specifies the canonical upright direction, and whose unit vector $\mathbf{f}_{O}$ specifies the canonical object-facing direction.

We further define a world coordinate frame $\mathcal{F}_{W}$ and a camera coordinate frame $\mathcal{F}_{C}$ . Let $W_{T_{O}}\in SE(3)$ and $W_{T_{C}}\in SE(3)$ denote the object-to-world and camera-to-world rigid transformations, respectively. The relative transformation from the object frame to the camera frame is therefore $C_{T_{O}}=(W_{T_{C}})^{-1}W_{T_{O}}$ , which corresponds to the standard 6-DoF camera-object relative pose.

In our setting, we constrain the asset to remain upright, fix camera roll, and orient the camera optical axis toward the object center $A$ . Under these constraints, the camera-object relative pose can be compactly parameterized by $\beta=(\varphi,\theta,D)$ , consisting of three components:

- • The object orientation angle $\varphi\in[0,2\pi)$ , which represents the relative azimuth between the asset’s canonical facing direction and the camera-facing direction. Let $\mathbf{f}_{W}$ denote the canonical facing direction transformed into the world coordinate frame, and let $\mathbf{u}_{W}$ denote the world-up direction. For any vector $\mathbf{x}$ , define its projection onto the horizontal plane as $\Pi_{h}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}-(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{W})\mathbf{u}_{W}$ , Let $\mathbf{f}_{h}=\frac{\Pi_{h}(\mathbf{f}_{W})}{\|\Pi_{h}(\mathbf{f}_{W})\|_{2}},\qquad\mathbf{q}_{h}=\frac{\Pi_{h}(A-O)}{\|\Pi_{h}(A-O)\|_{2}},$ where $O$ is the camera aperture center. We define $$\varphi=\operatorname{atan2}\left(\mathbf{u}_{W}^{\top}(\mathbf{q}_{h}\times\mathbf{f}_{h}),\mathbf{q}_{h}^{\top}\mathbf{f}_{h}\right)\bmod 2\pi.$$ Thus, $\varphi$ is the counterclockwise azimuth of the asset-facing direction with respect to the camera-facing direction $\overrightarrow{OA}$ .

- • The viewpoint angle $\theta\in[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}]$ , which represents the elevation of the camera with respect to the horizontal plane of the asset. Let $\mathbf{r}=O-A$ denote the vector from the asset center to the camera. We define $$\theta=\operatorname{atan2}\left(\mathbf{r}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{W},\|\Pi_{h}(\mathbf{r})\|_{2}\right).$$

- • The camera-object distance $D>0$ , defined as the Euclidean distance between the camera center and the object center, measured in units of the object’s size, $$D=\frac{\|O-A\|_{2}}{s(\mathcal{A})},$$ where $s(\mathcal{A})$ is the largest extent of the asset’s axis-aligned bounding box. In practice each asset is rescaled to unit extent before rendering, so $D$ equals the camera distance in Blender units. Under our fixed 35mm focal length, $D\approx 1$ corresponds to the object spanning the full image frame, and larger $D$ yields a smaller apparent object size.

Thus, $\beta$ should be interpreted as a three-parameter representation of the constrained camera-object relative pose, rather than as a general representation of arbitrary 3D geometry. Compared with Ma et al. (2024a) , this explicit 3D camera-object parameterization allows us to systematically control object orientation, camera elevation, and camera-object distance during dataset generation.

Visual Prior Rendering. Given an arbitrary camera-object relation $\beta$ , the system renders a corresponding set of geometrically aligned 2D visual priors $I_{\beta}$ (Fig. 3 ) using a 3D renderer $R$ . We use a perspective camera with a default focal length of 35mm, while its extrinsic parameters are determined by $\beta$ . The asset remains upright and the camera is oriented toward its center. The rendered RGB images are further processed to provide Canny edges as additional visual priors.

Because all rendered modalities are produced from the same underlying 3D mesh and camera parameters, they are pixel-aligned and geometrically consistent with the specified camera-object relation $\beta$ . Images rendered under different values of $\beta$ therefore provide controlled variations in object orientation, camera viewpoint, and camera-shot distance while preserving exact 3D geometric supervision.


##### 3.2 Diverse Image Description Generation

Our approach uses GPT-4o as a LLM to generate diverse text prompts for image descriptions ( $\mathcal{T}_{img}$ ) for DM-based image generation; an example is in Fig. 2 . Our framework uses system prompting and few-shot prompting tricks which are broadly utilized for image captioning and QA composing Liu et al. (2023) ; Liu et al. (2024b) .

For the generation of $\mathcal{T}_{img}$ , the LLM is guided to generate diverse descriptions of background context and object details given the object category $c$ . The diversity and rationality of the description directly influences the image generation quality (see Appendix 12 ). More details are in Appendix 7 .


##### 3.3 Controlled Image Generation

We utilize DMs to generate photorealistic images $I_{syn}$ given comprehensive visual prior $I_{\beta}$ . A successful generation of $I_{syn}$ shall preserve ground truth camera-object relation information, and be of high fidelity. We first describe a traditional text-to-image DM pipeline, extend it to stacked multiple ControlNets using various images of $I_{\beta}$ , and obtain precisely-controlled image generation.

Text-to-Image Generation. We use text-to-image DM (i.e., SDXL-1.0 Podell et al. (2023) ) as our backbone. The denoising U-Net, $\mathcal{G}_{\theta}$ parameterized by $\theta$ , conducts iterative denoising diffusion steps of Gaussian noise $\epsilon$ for a total of $T$ time steps. A text-encoder transforms the text prompt $\mathcal{T}_{img}$ into text embedding for guidance injection. The iterative denoising is defined as:

[Tableau] $$z_{t-1}=\mathcal{G}_{\theta}(\mathcal{T}_{img},z_{t},t),t=T,...,1$$ (1)

where $z_{T}=\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ , and $z_{0}$ is the latent variable of the generated image. It can be decoded to an RGB image by pretrained image decoder $\mathcal{D}$ as $I_{0}=\mathcal{D}(z_{0})$ .

Precise Controlling via Multiple ControlNets. ControlNet Zhang et al. (2023a) guides image generation by injecting additional controlling features derived from various 2D image visual priors. There are dozens of off-the-shelf ControlNets, each pretrained by a single type of visual prior. In our work, we leverage multiple ControlNets to capture comprehensive visual priors from $I_{\beta}$ (depth, Canny edge, etc.) where each one is weighted by coefficients $w$ . The denoising process using multiple ControlNets ( $\mathcal{C}$ ) is defined as:

[Tableau] $$z_{t-1}=\mathcal{G}_{\theta}(\mathcal{T}_{img},z_{t},t,\sum_{k=1}^{|\beta|}w_{k}\cdot\mathcal{C}^{k}(I^{k}_{\beta})),t=T,...,1.~$$ (2)

Similarly, the output generated image $I_{syn}=\mathcal{D}(z_{0})$ and $I_{syn}$ is expected to have ground truth camera-object relations.


##### 3.4 Text Instruction Generation

We utilize GPT-4o as a LLM to generate QA pairs on camera-object relation ( $\mathcal{T}_{qa}$ ) for visual instruction tuning (more details in Appendix 8 ).

Specifically, the LLM is prompted to generate QA pairs given the camera-object relation $\beta$ . We subdivide each component of $\beta$ into categorical types for effective referring through textual response by MLLMs:

- • Object orientation angle $\varphi$ is classified into eight object orientation types, each covers a bin of $\frac{\pi}{4}$ azimuth (i.e. ”right”, ”front right”, ”front”, ”front left”, ”left”, ”back left”, ”back”, ”back right”).

- • The viewpoint angle $\theta$ is classified into three viewpoint types, each covering a range of $\frac{\pi}{3}$ elevation angle (i.e. ”horizontal”, ”top”, ”bottom”).

- • The camera-object distance $D$ (Sec. 3.1 ) is measured in multiples of the object’s size, with $D\approx 1$ corresponding to the object filling the image frame. The three camera-shot types (i.e. ”close-up”, ”medium-shot”, ”long-shot”) are decided by two cutoff values, 1.25 and 3.0.

Examples of generated multiple-choice style QA pairs as MMVP benchmark Tong et al. (2024b) are showed in Fig. 2 . For question generation, we prompt LLMs to generate template questions for each prediction task of object orientation, viewpoint type, and camera-shot type. We list all possible options, including the ground truth $\beta$ , in random order. MLLMs are expected to choose the option according to $\beta$ .

#### 4 Experiments


##### 4.1 Visual Instruction Generation

Data Source. Our framework uses open-sourced 3D assets datasets from ObjaverseXL Deitke et al. (2023) ; Deitke et al. (2024) and ShapeNet Chang et al. (2015) . Partial 3D assets are labeled following the synset list from ImageNet-1K Russakovsky et al. (2015) . We leverage this subset and define the asset category by its synset label. In particular, we manually select 100 synsets with explicit ”facing” orientation from the original list of ImageNet. In total, there are 1196 assets used for synthetic visual instruction generation.

ControlNets. We utilize SDXL-1.0 Podell et al. (2023) as the backbone of ControlNets. For selection of multiple visual priors, we found using both Canny edges and relative depth provides the best mix of camera-object relation preservation and image quality. The diffusion step $T=30$ , and relative weights for depth and Canny edge ControlNets are 0.5 and 0.8, respectively. Several seconds are needed to generate an image by a single H100 GPU. Ablations and user studies are shown in Sec. 4.5 and Sec. 4.6 .


##### 4.2 Ultimate3D Dataset and Benchmark.

Our framework creates the Ultimate3D , a large-scale 3D-grounded 2D visual instruction dataset whose samples are generated from explicit 3D assets and controlled camera geometry, providing precise ground-truth camera-object relation annotations. Ultimate3D consists of 85K synthetic images and 18K cropped images of a single human per photograph and corresponding human orientation labels from MEBOW Wu et al. (2020) . For the synthetic images, we use 1180 3D assets covering 100 categories. For each 3D asset, there are 72 possible camera-object relations (8 orientations, 3 viewpoints, and 3 camera shot types) and we render a corresponding synthetic image for each relation. Then, each synthetic image is described by up to 3 textual QAs corresponding to object orientation, camera viewpoint, and camera-shot type, yielding a total of 240K corresponding QAs. Examples of synthetic images are in Appendix 15 . The images from MEBOW are useful because otherwise there is a strong lack of 3D assets of human which we find crucial to fine-tuning. The MEBOW images only have one QA corresponding to human orientation.

The Ultimate3D benchmark (Fig. 4 ) has two sets: (1) Synthetic Set. It contains 1200 synthetic images and 3600 QAs generated by our framework. Each image and QA has been manually reviewed to ensure correctness. This set covers all 100 categories with explicit facing orientation. The 3D assets utilized for benchmark generation do not overlap with those used for dataset generation. (2) Real Set. It contains two portions. The first portion is 2443 real images collected from Pascal3D+ Xiang et al. (2014) covering 10 categories (e.g. aeroplane, bicycle, etc.). Each image corresponds to a QA based on provided labels of object orientation or camera viewpoint. The second portion is 800 real images of humans collected from the MEBOW dataset Wu et al. (2020) . Each image corresponds to a QA on human orientation based on provided labels. In total, there are 3243 VQAs in the Real set. We resample the real images to keep a uniform distribution for each orientation and viewpoint type. Since no camera-shot information is provided in the real image set, the QAs in the real set exclude camera-shot type questions.

Both the Ultimate3D dataset and the benchmark have no camera-object relation bias, which is very uncommon in the real image datasets (e.g. most images capture objects facing straight to the camera Xiang et al. (2014) ). In contrast, in our dataset the number of images in each orientation, viewpoint, and camera-shot type are evenly distributed.


##### 4.3 Advancing 3D-Aware MLLMs

We explore how much our synthetic Ultimate3D dataset can improve the MLLMs recognition capability on camera-object relation. The evaluation results on both synthetic and real image VQA benchmarks indicate the fine-tuned baseline models are significantly improved from random guess and outperform commercial SOTAs.

Training Details. We fine-tune and evaluate several recent open-source MLLM families, including LLaVA-1.5/1.6 Liu et al. (2024a) , Llama-3.2-Vision-11B Meta (2024) , Gemma-4 models Google DeepMind (2026b) , and Qwen-based models Bai et al. (2025) ; Yang et al. (2025) . All models are fine-tuned for one epoch on the Ultimate3D training set. To evaluate the effect of training on Ultimate3D, we compare each base model with its corresponding fine-tuned version. We adopt two fine-tuning protocols depending on the model family. For LLaVA and Llama-3.2-Vision, we follow their official full fine-tuning recipes Liu et al. (2024a) ; Meta (2024) , updating the MLP vision-language connector and the LLM decoder while freezing the vision encoder. For LLaVA, we use BF16 precision, a learning rate of $2\times 10^{-5}$ , cosine scheduling with a warm-up ratio of 0.03, zero weight decay, and gradient checkpointing. For Llama-3.2-Vision-11B, we use BF16 precision, fused AdamW, a learning rate of $2\times 10^{-5}$ , a per-device batch size of 2 with gradient accumulation of 4, and gradient clipping at 1.0. For Gemma-4 and Qwen-based models, we use parameter-efficient fine-tuning primarily based on LoRA. For the Qwen3.5 and Qwen3-VL series, we use Unsloth-based parameter-efficient fine-tuning with a hybrid trainable-parameter policy: the vision encoder is kept frozen, the language-model attention and MLP projections are adapted with LoRA (rank 16, $\alpha$ =16, dropout 0, no bias terms, no RSLoRA), and the vision–language connector (merger) is optimized as full weights. For Gemma-4 models, we use plain LoRA with rank 16, $\alpha=32$ , and dropout 0.05, applied to compatible linear layers in the language model and vision embedding projection. Gemma-4 models are trained in BF16 using fused AdamW, an effective batch size of 4, and a constant learning rate of $1\times 10^{-4}$ without warmup or weight decay. For Gemma-4-31B, we resumed from the best intermediate checkpoint with a learning rate of $3\times 10^{-5}$ , cosine decay, and weight decay of 0.01 due to late-stage instability. Training takes approximately 12 hours on 4 H100 GPUs for LLaVA and Llama-3.2-Vision, up to 2.5 days on a single H100 GPU for Gemma-4, and up to 8 days on a single RTX 6000 GPU for Qwen-based models.

Quantitative Results. The quantitative results of response accuracy ( $\%$ ) are shown in Tab. 1 . We assess the effect of training on Ultimate3D through base-to-fine-tuned comparisons within each model family. Overall, we observe a consistent trend: off-the-shelf MLLMs struggle with camera-object relation recognition, whereas task-specific fine-tuning on Ultimate3D substantially improves performance across multiple model families. This difficulty appears even for strong general-purpose vision-language models, including LLaVA, Llama-3.2-Vision, Gemma-4, Qwen-based models, and closed commercial MLLMs. Several baseline models tend to collapse to a small set of frequent answers, such as predicting “front” orientation or “top” viewpoint regardless of the actual image content. As a result, their accuracies are often close to random-guess levels, e.g., $33.3\%$ for three-way viewpoint recognition and $12.5\%$ for eight-way orientation recognition. This weakness may be related to biases in commonly used visual instruction datasets, where most images are captured from canonical front-facing directions and from common side or above viewpoints. The poor off-the-shelf performance across both older and more recent MLLM families suggests that camera-object relation understanding remains underrepresented in existing MLLM training data.

Fine-tuning on our synthetic Ultimate3D dataset substantially improves camera-object relation recognition across all evaluated open-source model families. On the Ultimate3D benchmark, fine-tuned LLaVA models significantly outperform their corresponding base models across object orientation, camera viewpoint, and camera-shot recognition. For example, fine-tuned LLaVA-1.6-13B improves orientation accuracy from $16.1\%$ to $72.4\%$ , viewpoint accuracy from $30.7\%$ to $72.3\%$ , and camera-shot accuracy from $42.3\%$ to $94.8\%$ . The smaller LLaVA-1.6-7B model achieves similarly competitive performance, reaching $71.5\%$ , $71.3\%$ , and $94.1\%$ on these three tasks, respectively. This suggests that the ability to distinguish camera-object relations does not necessarily require a very large backbone, but instead benefits strongly from task-specific supervision.

The same conclusion holds for Llama-3.2-Vision, Gemma-4, and Qwen-based models. Llama-3.2-Vision-11B obtains the largest average improvement among the models in Tab. 1 , with an average gain of $60.46$ percentage points across the three camera-object relation tasks. The original released model often refuses to answer some recognition questions, e.g., responding that it cannot provide information about the individual in the image, while the fine-tuned model produces valid relation predictions and achieves the best orientation accuracy in Tab. 1 . These results indicate that Ultimate3D supervision can correct both answer-format failures and visual relation recognition failures.

Recent Gemma-4 and Qwen-based models further confirm that the benefit of Ultimate3D supervision is not specific to the LLaVA architecture. Before task-specific fine-tuning, Gemma-4 models achieve only $36.0$ – $51.1\%$ overall accuracy when averaging all reported relation-recognition columns. After LoRA fine-tuning, they reach $69.4$ – $71.3\%$ overall accuracy, corresponding to an average improvement of $26.3$ percentage points. The improvements are consistent across all three relation types, with average gains of $20.0$ – $37.5$ percentage points on object-direction recognition, $9.2$ – $26.7$ percentage points on viewpoint recognition, and $38.0$ – $49.1$ percentage points on camera-shot recognition.

Qwen-based models show a similar pattern. Their base versions achieve $37.3$ – $48.9\%$ overall accuracy, while the fine-tuned versions reach $69.5$ – $75.7\%$ overall accuracy, improving by $30.5$ percentage points on average. In particular, fine-tuned Qwen3-VL-32B achieves the strongest overall performance among the evaluated open models in Tab. 1 , reaching $75.7\%$ average accuracy across all reported columns. It also obtains strong performance on both synthetic and real subsets, suggesting that the learned camera-object relation knowledge is not restricted to rendered synthetic images.

Among closed commercial MLLMs, newer models generally perform better than earlier GPT-4o- and Claude-3-era models on some orientation and viewpoint columns, but the improvement is not uniform across tasks. For example, Gemini-3.5-Flash with the high thinking setting achieves stronger orientation accuracy than its minimal-thinking counterpart, improving from $56.8\%$ to $65.3\%$ on the Ultimate3D orientation column. However, increasing the thinking budget does not consistently improve all relation types: its camera-shot accuracy decreases from $37.1\%$ to $33.8\%$ . This suggests that additional reasoning budget can help in some spatial-relation cases, but it does not resolve the lack of grounded camera-object relation understanding.

Compared with closed commercial MLLMs, our fine-tuned open models achieve especially strong performance on the Ultimate3D relation-recognition tasks. Although recent commercial models improve over earlier commercial baselines on some orientation and viewpoint columns, they remain substantially weaker on several Ultimate3D tasks, especially camera-shot recognition, where fine-tuned open models consistently reach around $90\%$ accuracy or higher. Overall, these results demonstrate that Ultimate3D provides effective supervision for camera-object relation understanding, improves both synthetic and real-image evaluation performance, and generalizes across multiple MLLM families, including LLaVA, Llama, Gemma, and Qwen.

Qualitative Results.

We show qualitative comparisons in Fig. 4 . Commercial SOTA models (e.g. Gemini-3.5-Flash) and open-source models (e.g. Gemma-4-31B-it) struggle with precise recognition of all three types of camera-object relations. For the two commercial models, their object-orientation predictions are often unreliable despite producing plausible natural-language explanations. We also observe systematic biases in viewpoint and camera-shot recognition: both commercial models tend to underestimate viewpoint angle, e.g., mistaking top views as horizontal, and overestimate camera-shot distance, e.g., mistaking medium-shot images as long-shot. The original Gemma-4-31B-it model also shows strong response biases across all types of questions. Its orientation predictions are biased mostly toward “Left” and “Right”, its viewpoint predictions are dominated by “Horizontal”, and its camera-shot predictions are biased toward “Long-shot”. In comparison, the Gemma-4-31B-it model fine-tuned on our Ultimate3D dataset provides more plausible predictions across all three tasks on both the synthetic and real subsets of our benchmark. Additional comparisons are provided in Supplemental Sec. 14 .

We hypothesize that these behavioral biases arise from dataset bias and the lack of camera-object visual instruction data in current MLLM training pipelines. Without modifying the model architecture or training scheme, our fine-tuned Gemma-4-31B-it model substantially improves camera-object relation recognition and outperforms strong commercial models on several Ultimate3D tasks, despite relying only on task-specific supervision. This indicates that the lack of targeted camera-object relation data is a major factor behind current MLLMs’ weakness on camera-object relation recognition.

Limitations. The numerical prediction of camera-object relation $\beta$ is more challenging for MLLMs compared to the categorical prediction we report. Preliminary tests show MLLMs can handle numerical prediction of camera-object relation for a single category, but struggle with a hundred categories as in our dataset. Since our main contribution is the discovery of the dataset bottleneck and the generation pipeline, we leave addressing this limitation as future work.

#### 5 Conclusion

In this work, we discover the dataset bottleneck of MLLMs for distinguishing camera-object relations. Given only 3D assets, our framework is able to generate unlimited image-text pairs of diverse camera-object relations with no distribution bias. We also provided Ultimate3D dataset and benchmark MLLM community. Moreover, fine-tuned open-sourced MLLMs using our dataset outperform SOTA commercial models by a significant margin. We plan to extend our framework for multi-object image generation with additional viewpoints, and improve numerical prediction capability on camera-object relation.

#### 12 Factors influencing Image Generation

Several factors may lead to unreasonable artifacts in our image generation pipeline. In Fig. 8 , the upper-left example shows that bottom-view camera-object relations can introduce physical anomalies, such as the tricycle appearing with its front lifted unnaturally. The upper-right example highlights that low-quality 3D assets can produce fragmented depth maps or Canny edge visual priors, resulting in unrealistic generated images. The lower-left example shows that small subjects viewed from a long-shot perspective may lead to duplicate subjects in the generated image. The lower-right example illustrates how complex 3D asset structures can cause texture blending issues, particularly where intricate geometry is present.

There are several ways to reduce the influences from those factors. First, choosing 3D assets with reasonable number of meshes, and with plausible textures may filter the low-quality and texture blending failures. Second, using simple and compact image description prompts to generate long-shot camera-shot images will significantly reduce the duplicate subject failures. Reduce the image generation with high elevation angle from bottom view will significantly reduce physical anomaly.

#### 18 Additional Discussions

We provide some insights of using synthetic generated visual instruction dataset for MLLM finetuning. For the task of camera-object relation recognition for 100 categories (as we collected in Ultimate3D dataset), a dataset comprising 100K to 1M VQAs will significantly improve baseline MLLMs by one-epoch finetuning. Further scaling up the dataset volume, finetuneing epochs, or model parameters may yield saturation.

Moreover, the quality of synthetic dataset is crucial to the improvement margin. Using dataset with higher success rate (ours is 93.07% as main paper Sec. 4.6 ) provides better performances than using dataset generated other alternative backbones (i.e. SD-V1.5).

The coverage of arbitrary camera-object relation is also important for generalization. Our preliminary test using real images which are highly biased to front-facing direction from Pascal3D+ Xiang et al. (2014) , results in poor generalization on uncovered orientations and viewpoints. This illustrates the importance of using synthetic dataset to eliminate the dataset biases.

For numerical prediction of camera-object relation, our preliminary test shows plausible success using MEBOW Wu et al. (2020) dataset to improve MLLMs on predicting human orientation angles. The average degree prediction accuracy is competitive to the original paper Wu et al. (2020) . However, the extended numerical prediction of 100 categories and 3 types of camera-object relations may require even larger dataset volume than Ultimate3D . Our framework is able to scale up the dataset volume. But due to the limitation of computing power, we leave it as interesting future work.
