## A Statistical Framework for Training Data Auditing of Large Language Models

Training-data auditing asks whether data of interest were used to train a large language model (LLM), a question central to privacy auditing, copyright protection, and benchmark-contamination detection. These concerns are particularly relevant to the use of copyrighted books and scientific papers in pre-training, as well as synthetic text and medical question–answer records in fine-tuning. In practice, training membership signals may be concentrated in only a few passages of a long document, making it important to aggregate evidence within each document without diluting these localized signals. Beyond individual documents, auditing a target collection requires an overall assessment based on the document-level evidence. In this paper, we propose a unified statistical framework for document-level membership inference, target-collection global auditing, and suspicious-subset identification. We integrate complementary chunk-level membership evidence with Cauchy combination to construct document scores for document-level membership inference. The document scores and their calibrated $p$ -values further enable an overall assessment of training-data collection use and the identification of suspicious documents through multiple testing. Under the stated exchangeability conditions, we establish finite-sample validity of document-level $p$ -values, type-I error control for global auditing, and false discovery rate control for subset identification. Case studies on BookTection, Pile–arXiv, Cosmopedia, and HealthCareMagic demonstrate the effectiveness and reliability of the proposed framework in auditing pre-training and fine-tuning data.

Keywords: Membership inference; multiple testing; large language model; training data auditing.


### 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are trained on vast text corpora whose composition is often undisclosed ( Brown et al. 2020 ) . Their ability to memorize and reproduce training content raises privacy and copyright concerns ( Tang et al. 2024 , Duarte et al. 2024 ) , while the inclusion of benchmark evaluation data can compromise model assessment ( Oren et al. 2023 ) . These concerns motivate training-data auditing, which seeks to determine whether particular texts were used during training ( Shi et al. 2024 ) . Membership inference (MIA) ( Shokri et al. 2017 ) formulates this question as a binary decision between member and non-member status. This task is fundamentally a statistical decision problem: the auditor must extract membership signals from limited and heterogeneous model outputs, aggregate diverse and dependent evidence, and translate the aggregate into membership decisions with controlled error rates. Meeting these requirements calls for principled statistical methodology for training-data auditing at both the document and collection levels. Accordingly, we develop a unified statistical framework for training-data auditing that addresses three complementary objectives: document-level membership inference, global auditing of a target collection, and identification of suspicious subsets.

The first objective is document-level auditing : determining whether a specific document was included in the target model’s training set. Documents such as scientific papers and Wikipedia pages can span thousands of tokens, while membership signals may be concentrated in localized paragraphs ( Chen et al. 2026 ) . Auditing such documents therefore involves extracting evidence from smaller units, referred to as chunks , and aggregating it at the document level ( Puerto et al. 2025 ) . For each chunk, auditing methods derive membership evidence statistics from model outputs, such as perplexity and log-probability-based scores. These statistics exploit differences in model fit: the overfitting nature of large language models can lead to lower prediction loss on training text than on non-member text, corresponding to lower perplexity and higher average token log-probabilities ( Maini et al. 2024 ) . These statistics may be dependent across chunks within the same document, making evidence combination and aggregation statistically challenging.

The second objective is target-collection global auditing : examining evidence of training-data use across a collection of documents. Dataset inference ( Maini et al. 2024 ) addresses this objective by testing whether any members of the target dataset were used for training. This problem is particularly relevant to data owners, businesses, and rights holders seeking to verify large-scale use of their data. For example, a scholarly publisher may suspect that its journal corpus was used in LLM pre-training, while an AI company may seek to determine whether its proprietary reasoning dataset was used to fine-tune a competitor’s model. However, the statistical tests and uncertainty estimates used by these approaches rely on independence assumptions and lack corresponding validity guarantees for collections of dependent documents.

The third objective is suspicious-subset identification . It determines which specific documents in a target collection are likely to be training members. Existing dataset-level methods mostly provide aggregate conclusions about collection-level usage and have paid comparatively little attention to itemized evidence. In copyright and compliance auditing, rights holders and auditors often need an itemized list of suspicious documents to support data-use claims and guide further verification. Because such a list may support legal or regulatory action, the proportion of false inclusions must be controlled. We formalize this requirement through false discovery rate (FDR) control under dependent document signals ( Benjamini and Hochberg 1995 ) . The result is an itemized list in which the expected proportion of false accusations is bounded by a prespecified level $q$ , yielding evidence reliable enough to support legal, regulatory, or organizational review.

In this paper, we propose a multi-level statistical framework for training-data auditing, as illustrated in Figure 1 . The framework unifies the three auditing objectives by translating chunk-level membership evidence into document-level conclusions and collection-level decisions. These transitions introduce distinct statistical challenges. Within a document, heterogeneous and dependent chunk-level statistics complicate aggregation, which may dilute localized membership signals or amplify noisy evidence ( Chen et al. 2026 ) . Across documents, the signals are also dependent because all documents are scored by the same fitted rule and calibrated against the same non-member collection. This dependence among document scores complicates both the calibration of global tests and FDR control for suspicious-subset identification. To address these challenges, we integrate complementary statistical tools into a unified auditing framework: Cauchy combination ( Liu and Xie 2020 ) aggregates local evidence into document scores sensitive to localized signals, and empirical calibration against held-out non-member documents then provides a basis for individual membership testing. At the collection level, an Energy permutation test ( Székely and Rizzo 2013 ) is applied to assess distributional departures from non-member behavior, while the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) procedure ( Benjamini and Hochberg 1995 ) is considered to select suspicious documents using the calibrated document-level $p$ -values. Under the stated exchangeability conditions, which permit dependence among document scores, we establish type-I error control for individual and global testing and FDR control for subset identification.

Our framework applies to both pre-training and fine-tuning data auditing. We study four case studies to evaluate the proposed framework. For pre-training, we examine copyrighted books from BookTection and scientific papers from Pile–arXiv, where auditing involves aggregating local membership evidence across long documents. For fine-tuning, we examine synthetic text from Cosmopedia and medical question–answer records from HealthCareMagic to assess whether these data were used to fine-tune the pretrained models. Across these case studies, we assess the framework’s detection performance and statistical reliability at all the document, collection, and subset levels.

Our contributions are threefold. First, we formulate LLM training-data auditing through three complementary statistical objectives: document-level membership inference, target-collection global auditing, and suspicious-subset identification with explicit error control. Second, we develop a unified framework that combines heterogeneous chunk-level evidence and uses Cauchy combination to construct document scores sensitive to localized membership signals. These scores support empirically calibrated individual testing, Energy-based global auditing, and BH subset identification. Third, under the stated conditional exchangeability conditions, we establish finite-sample validity of document-level calibration, type-I error control for individual and global testing, and FDR control for subset identification. We further quantify the additional error caused by departures from exchangeability.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the preliminaries, the datasets, and formulates the auditing problems. Section 3 presents the proposed statistical auditing framework. Section 4 establishes its theoretical guarantees under conditional exchangeability and quantifies the effects of departures from these conditions. Section 5 evaluates the framework through four case studies spanning pre-training and fine-tuning data auditing. Section 6 concludes the paper.


### 2 Preliminaries, Datasets, and Problems


#### 2.1 Preliminaries


##### Autoregressive Language Models

Autoregressive LLMs model by predicting each token conditional on its preceding context ( Brown et al. 2020 ) . Let $\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{L})$ denote a token sequence of length $L$ , and let $\mathbf{w}_{<t}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{t-1})$ denote the prefix preceding token $w_{t}$ . For a model with parameters $\theta$ , the sequence likelihood factorizes as

[Tableau] $$p_{\theta}(\mathbf{w})=\prod_{t=1}^{L}p_{\theta}(w_{t}\mid\mathbf{w}_{<t}),$$ (1)

where $p_{\theta}(w_{t}\mid\mathbf{w}_{<t})$ is the conditional probability assigned to token $w_{t}$ . These models are typically trained by minimizing the negative log-likelihood (NLL) of the training sequences.

These token-level conditional probabilities are also the quantities on which auditing is based. Evaluating them for a given sequence requires a single forward pass rather than text generation, and their negative logarithms are the per-token losses from which perplexity and the other membership evidence statistics below are obtained.


##### Auditor Access

We consider a black-box setting in which the auditor can query the target model with a token sequence and obtain the conditional probability of each token given its preceding context. The auditor has no access to the model’s internal parameters or the membership labels of the target documents. Such token probabilities can be obtained through token-scoring APIs or self-hosted model interfaces. The Min- $K\%++$ features used in our experiments additionally require access to the full next-token predictive distribution or the corresponding moments defined in Supplementary Section S1.

For fine-tuning data auditing, we additionally assume the auditor has black-box query access to the model used to initialize fine-tuning, which serves as a reference model. This assumption reflects a common scenario in which an organization fine-tunes a publicly available model on private data, while the initial model remains available for querying ( Touvron et al. 2023 ) . Comparing the outputs of the target and reference models provides evidence of changes in model behavior associated with fine-tuning.


##### Membership Signals and Evidence

Training-data auditing uses observable model outputs, such as token-level conditional probabilities, as membership signals . Auditing methods transform these signals, optionally together with auxiliary information, into scalar statistics that quantify membership evidence for a queried sequence. For example, perplexity (PPL) summarizes how well the model predicts the sequence, whereas Min- $K\%$ focuses on its least predictable tokens. Smaller PPL values and larger Min- $K\%$ values indicate stronger membership evidence. Other methods incorporate reference-model outputs or textual characteristics to adjust these statistics. These methods provide different aspects of the model’s behavior, motivating us to combine their statistics into chunk-level membership evidence for subsequent document-level auditing. The individual evidence statistics are detailed in Supplementary Section S1.


#### 2.2 Datasets

LLM training commonly involves large-scale pre-training followed by fine-tuning on selected data, and our framework audits data use in either stage.

We study BookTection ( Duarte et al. 2024 ) and Pile–arXiv ( Gao et al. 2020 ) for pre-training data auditing, and Cosmopedia ( Ben Allal et al. 2024 ) and HealthCareMagic ( Li et al. 2023 , HCM) for fine-tuning data auditing. Membership is defined with respect to the training stage under audit.

BookTection comprises excerpts from $165$ books, with roughly $34$ original passages per book. We use the benchmark’s Medium setting, which provides passages with a nominal length of $128$ tokens. Each book serves as a document-level inferential unit, with its passages treated as chunks. Following the benchmark’s publication-date labeling rule, we treat $105$ classic books published in or before $2021$ as candidate members and $60$ books published in $2023$ as non-members. Books published in $2022$ are excluded because their membership status is ambiguous under this rule. This benchmark allows us to evaluate how aggregating passage-level membership evidence supports individual book auditing.

Pile–arXiv consists of full-text scientific papers from the arXiv component of The Pile ( Gao et al. 2020 ) . The public release used here contains $79{,}046$ papers from the first training shard and $4{,}841$ papers from the official held-out validation and test splits. Each record contains a paper’s main text, mathematical expressions, references, and source metadata. We treat each paper as a document-level inferential unit. For Pythia target models trained on the corresponding Pile corpus, papers from the training partition are members, while those from the held-out partitions are non-members.

Cosmopedia provides synthetic English text, including educational explanations, textbook-style passages, and other expository content. We use its web_samples_v2 subset, which contains $10{,}345{,}867$ records generated from web-page excerpts. Each record includes generated text, a generation prompt, and metadata describing the source, format, intended audience, and token length. We use the generated text as the document-level input and fine-tune Pythia-2.8B on selected documents using the next-token prediction objective. Documents used in this fine-tuning run are members, while withheld documents are non-members.

HealthCareMagic (HCM) contains $112{,}165$ health-care question–answer records, each comprising an instruction, a patient question, and an answer. We treat each complete record as a document-level inferential unit. We adapt Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct to the medical domain through supervised fine-tuning, using the instruction and patient question as conditioning context and the answer as the supervised response. Records used for gradient updates are members, while withheld records are non-members.


#### 2.3 Problem Formulation

Let $\mathcal{F}_{\theta_{\mathrm{target}}}$ denote the target LLM with parameters $\theta_{\mathrm{target}}$ and trained on an undisclosed corpus $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}$ . For a target collection $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{target}}=\{d_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}$ of $m$ documents, let $y_{i}:=\mathbb{I}\{d_{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}\}$ denote the unknown membership label of document $d_{i}$ . We formulate training-data auditing through the following three objectives.


##### Problem 1: Document-level individual auditing

For each target document $d_{i}$ , the auditor seeks to determine whether it was used to train $\mathcal{F}_{\theta_{\mathrm{target}}}$ . Taking the document as the basic inferential unit, we formulate this objective for document $i\in\mathcal{D}_{target}$ as

[Tableau] $$H_{0,i}:\;d_{i}\notin\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}\qquad\text{versus}\qquad H_{1,i}:\;d_{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}.$$ (2)

Non-membership serves as the null hypothesis, so that a document is flagged as a suspected member only when there is sufficient evidence to reject $H_{0,i}$ .


##### Problem 2: Collection-level global auditing

At the collection level, the auditor examines whether the distribution of membership evidence differs between the target collection and known non-members. Let $\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{cal}}=\{d_{\ell}^{\mathrm{cal}}\}_{\ell=1}^{n_{\mathrm{cal}}}$ denote a calibration collection of $n_{\mathrm{cal}}$ documents drawn from the same underlying distribution as the target documents but confirmed absent from $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}$ . Let $T(\cdot)$ denote the document-level membership statistic, and let $\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{target}}$ and $\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{cal}}$ denote the distributions of this statistic under the target and calibration populations, respectively. We formulate global auditing as the two-sample hypothesis test

[Tableau] $$H_{0}^{\mathrm{glob}}:\;\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{target}}=\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{cal}}\qquad\text{versus}\qquad H_{1}^{\mathrm{glob}}:\;\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{target}}\neq\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{cal}}.$$ (3)

Rejection indicates a collection-level departure from the evidence distribution of known non-members.

An alternative approach combines the individual tests in Equation ( 2 ), for example through the Bonferroni rule $\min_{i}p_{i}\leq\alpha/m$ , where $p_{i}$ is the document-level $p$ -value and $\alpha$ is the significance level ( Bland and Altman 1995 ) . This rule flags a collection when at least one document provides sufficiently strong evidence after multiplicity adjustment. As the evidence is usually weak and distributed across several documents, however, no individual $p$ -value may reach this threshold even though the collection exhibits a distributional shift. The two-sample formulation aims to capture such collective evidence.


##### Problem 3: Suspicious-subset identification

A global decision summarizes evidence across a collection but does not identify which documents warrant further investigation. Suspicious-subset identification addresses this need by returning a data-dependent discovery set $\widehat{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}$ where $m=|\mathcal{D}_{target}|$ , containing the indices of documents flagged as suspected members. For the prespecified family of all $m$ document-level hypotheses, we seek to control the expected proportion of non-members among the selected documents at a nominal false discovery rate (FDR) level $q\in(0,1)$ :

[Tableau] $$\operatorname{FDR}(\widehat{\mathcal{S}}):=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|\widehat{\mathcal{S}}\cap\mathcal{I}_{0}|}{|\widehat{\mathcal{S}}|\vee 1}\right]\leq q,$$ (4)

where $\mathcal{I}_{0}:=\{i:d_{i}\notin\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}\}$ and $\mathcal{I}_{1}:=\{i:d_{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}\}$ denote the index sets of non-member and member target documents, respectively. The expectation is taken over the complete reporting procedure under repeated sampling.


### 3 The Statistical Multi-Level Auditing Framework

> Figure 1 : Multi-level statistical framework for LLM training-data auditing. Target documents and held-out non-member calibration documents follow the same scoring pipeline: each document is split into chunks, membership evidence is extracted from the target model for each chunk, and the evidence is aggregated into a document score. These scores support three auditing tasks. An Energy permutation test compares the target and calibration score distributions for global auditing. Empirical calibration yields document-level $p$ -values for individual auditing, and applying the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to these $p$ -values identifies a suspicious subset.

In the proposed framework, documents serve as inferential units, while chunks serve as operational units for extracting membership evidence ( Chen et al. 2026 ) . The overall pipeline is summarized in Figure 1 . We first combine membership evidence for each chunk and aggregate it into a document-level score. Empirical calibration of this score yields a $p$ -value for individual membership testing. We then compare target and non-member document-score distributions through an Energy permutation test for global auditing, and apply BH to the document-level $p$ -values for suspicious-subset identification.


#### 3.1 Constructing Chunk-Level Membership Evidence

For each target document $d_{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{target}}$ , we apply a fixed chunking rule to obtain $\operatorname{Chunk}(d_{i})=\bigl\{c_{j}(d_{i})\bigr\}_{j=1}^{J(d_{i})}$ , where $J(d_{i})$ is the number of chunks and $c_{j}(d_{i})$ is the $j$ -th chunk. For each chunk $c$ , we compute $d$ membership-evidence statistics, $(e_{1}(c),\ldots,e_{d}(c))$ , from the available model outputs and auxiliary information. To define these statistics, write a chunk as a token sequence $c=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{L})$ . Examples include the following:

- • Perplexity score, defined as the exponential of the average per-token NLL, $$e_{\mathrm{PPL}}(c):=\exp\Bigl\{-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{t=1}^{L}\log p_{\theta_{\mathrm{target}}}\bigl(w_{t}\mid\mathbf{w}_{<t}\bigr)\Bigr\};$$ with smaller values indicating stronger membership evidence.

- • Min- $K\%$ score ( Shi et al. 2024 ) , defined as the average log-probability over the least predictable tokens, $$e_{\mathrm{Min}\text{-}K,\rho}(c):=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{\rho}(c)|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{\rho}(c)}\log p_{\theta_{\mathrm{target}}}\bigl(w_{t}\mid\mathbf{w}_{<t}\bigr),$$ where $\mathcal{T}_{\rho}(c)$ contains the indices of the $\rho\%$ tokens with the smallest log-probabilities. Larger values indicate stronger membership evidence.

Additional evidence statistics are defined in Supplementary Section S1. These statistics differ in scale and evidence direction, and their relative usefulness may vary across auditing settings. We therefore learn a combination that converts them into a scalar chunk-level membership evidence $e_{\mathrm{com}}(c)$ .

We fit this combination using an auxiliary fitting collection $\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{fit}}=\{(d_{k}^{\mathrm{fit}},y_{k}^{\mathrm{fit}})\}_{k}$ , comprising a small set of documents with membership labels known to the auditor. Such labels may be available through interaction with the model developer to establish which documents were used or withheld during training. We apply the same chunking rule to each fitting document and assign its label $y_{k}^{\mathrm{fit}}$ to every resulting chunk $c_{j}(d_{k}^{\mathrm{fit}})$ .

Let $y(c)$ denote the label assigned to chunk $c$ , where $y(c)=1$ indicates membership and $y(c)=0$ non-membership. We model its membership probability using logistic regression,

[Tableau] $$\Pr\{y(c)=1\mid e_{1}(c),\ldots,e_{d}(c)\}=\sigma\!\left(\beta_{0}+\sum_{r=1}^{d}\beta_{r}e_{r}(c)\right),$$ (5)

where $\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})$ is the sigmoid function, $\beta_{0}$ is the intercept, and $\beta_{r}$ is the coefficient associated with the $r$ -th evidence statistic. The coefficients are estimated using the pooled chunk–label pairs $(c_{j}(d_{k}^{\mathrm{fit}}),y_{k}^{\mathrm{fit}})$ from the fitting collection. Their signs and magnitudes determine how the evidence statistics contribute to the combined score.

After fitting, we freeze the evidence map and use its logit as the combined chunk-level membership evidence:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle e_{\mathrm{com}}(c)=\log\frac{\widehat{\Pr}\{y(c)=1\mid e_{1}(c),\ldots,e_{d}(c)\}}{\widehat{\Pr}\{y(c)=0\mid e_{1}(c),\ldots,e_{d}(c)\}}=\widehat{\beta}_{0}+\sum_{r=1}^{d}\widehat{\beta}_{r}e_{r}(c).$ (6)

Larger values indicate stronger membership evidence. The logit of the conditional class probabilities approximates the log-likelihood ratio between the member-labeled and non-member-labeled evidence distributions, up to an additive constant determined by their class proportions. This interpretation connects the learned evidence to likelihood-ratio-based membership inference ( Carlini et al. 2022 ) .


#### 3.2 Cauchy Combination for Document-Level Individual Auditing

Document-level auditing requires aggregating the chunk-level evidence into a single document score. Membership evidence may be concentrated in a few passages, while the remaining chunks contribute weak or uninformative evidence ( Chen et al. 2026 ) . Simply averaging chunk-level evidence ( Duarte et al. 2024 ) can dilute these localized signals. Moreover, chunks within a document may be dependent because they share topics and context. We use Cauchy combination ( Liu and Xie 2020 ) to construct a document score sensitive to strong local evidence, followed by empirical document-level calibration for individual testing.

We first convert each chunk’s combined evidence $e_{\mathrm{com}}(c)$ into an empirical $p$ -value, which requires the distribution of $e_{\mathrm{com}}$ under non-membership. We introduce a base collection $\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{base}}$ of known non-member documents and apply the same chunking rule to obtain $n_{\mathrm{base}}$ chunks, $\{c_{h}^{\mathrm{base}}\}_{h=1}^{n_{\mathrm{base}}}$ . Their evidence values, computed using the frozen evidence map, provide an empirical reference for non-member behavior. For each target chunk $c_{j}(d_{i})$ , we compute the empirical right-tail $p$ -value,

[Tableau] $$p_{ij}^{\mathrm{ck}}=\frac{1+\sum_{h=1}^{n_{\mathrm{base}}}\mathbb{I}\!\left\{e_{\mathrm{com}}(c_{h}^{\mathrm{base}})\geq e_{\mathrm{com}}(c_{j}(d_{i}))\right\}}{n_{\mathrm{base}}+1},\quad i=1,\ldots,m,\quad j=1,\ldots,J(d_{i}).$$ (7)

A smaller $p_{ij}^{\mathrm{ck}}$ indicates that the target chunk exhibits stronger membership evidence relative to the non-member base chunks.

We aggregate the empirical chunk-level $p$ -values $\{p_{ij}^{\mathrm{ck}}\}_{j=1}^{J(d_{i})}$ computed for the $J(d_{i})$ chunks $\{c_{j}(d_{i})\}_{j=1}^{J(d_{i})}$ of document $d_{i}$ into the document score

[Tableau] $$T_{\mathrm{Cauchy}}(d_{i})=\frac{1}{J(d_{i})}\sum_{j=1}^{J(d_{i})}\cot\bigl(\pi[p_{ij}^{\mathrm{ck}}]_{\varepsilon}\bigr),$$ (8)

where $\cot(\pi p)=\tan\bigl((0.5-p)\pi\bigr)$ is the Cauchy transformation and $[p]_{\varepsilon}=\min\{1-\varepsilon,\max\{\varepsilon,p\}\}$ clips $p$ to $[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]$ . The clipping parameter $\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)$ is fixed in advance and shared across all target and calibration documents, ensuring numerical stability. The Cauchy transformation maps small $p$ -values to large positive contributions, values near $0.5$ to contributions near zero, and values above $0.5$ to negative contributions. Consequently, sufficiently small $p$ -values from a few informative chunks can contribute substantially to the document score, allowing localized membership signals to drive document-level decisions. Larger values of $T_{\mathrm{Cauchy}}(d_{i})$ indicate stronger membership evidence.

To obtain an individual auditing decision, we calibrate the document score using the held-out non-member collection $\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{cal}}=\{d_{\ell}^{\mathrm{cal}}\}_{\ell=1}^{n_{\mathrm{cal}}}$ . This collection is held out from fitting and selecting the complete scoring rule. Let $T_{\mathrm{agg}}$ denote the fixed document scoring rule, with $T_{\mathrm{agg}}=T_{\mathrm{Cauchy}}$ for the construction above. Applying the same rule to target and calibration documents gives the empirical right-tail $p$ -value

[Tableau] $$p_{i}=\frac{1+\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{\mathrm{cal}}}\mathbb{I}\!\left\{T_{\mathrm{agg}}(d_{\ell}^{\mathrm{cal}})\geq T_{\mathrm{agg}}(d_{i})\right\}}{n_{\mathrm{cal}}+1},\qquad i=1,\ldots,m.$$ (9)

At a prespecified significance level $\alpha\in(0,1)$ , we reject $H_{0,i}$ when $p_{i}\leq\alpha$ . Alternative aggregation rules are discussed and compared in Section S2 of the Supplementary Materials.

For the Cauchy score, an analytic counterpart of Equation ( 9 ) is available without any calibration data. If the chunk-level $p$ -values were independent and uniformly distributed under the null, and no clipping were applied, $T_{\mathrm{Cauchy}}(d_{i})$ would follow a standard Cauchy distribution under $H_{0,i}$ , and the upper-tail probability

[Tableau] $$u(d_{i}):=1-F_{\mathrm{Cauchy}}\{T_{\mathrm{Cauchy}}(d_{i})\}=\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{\pi}\arctan\{T_{\mathrm{Cauchy}}(d_{i})\}$$ (10)

would be an exact document-level $p$ -value ( Liu and Xie 2020 ) . Our chunk-level $p$ -values are empirical and dependent, and the Cauchy transformation is clipped. We therefore use the empirical calibration in Equation ( 9 ) for individual testing and subset identification, whose validity under conditional exchangeability is established in Theorem 4.3 . The analytic form Equation ( 10 ) is used below as a bounded score transformation for the Energy two-sample comparison.


#### 3.3 Energy-Based Two-Sample Testing for Global Auditing

Building on the document-level scores above, we now address global auditing through the two-sample testing problem formulated in Equation ( 3 ). Specifically, we compare the document-score distributions of $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{target}}$ and the held-out non-member calibration collection $\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{cal}}$ . For the Cauchy document statistic in Equation ( 8 ), we apply the bounded transformation in Equation ( 10 ), writing $u(d):=\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{\pi}\arctan\{T_{\mathrm{Cauchy}}(d_{i})\}$ . Its bounded range $(0,1)$ improves the numerical stability of the subsequent distance computations.

Let $F_{\mathrm{target}}$ and $F_{\mathrm{cal}}$ denote the population distributions of $u(d)$ for target and calibration documents, respectively. Following Equation ( 3 ), we test $H_{0}^{\mathrm{glob}}:F_{\mathrm{target}}=F_{\mathrm{cal}}$ against $H_{1}^{\mathrm{glob}}:F_{\mathrm{target}}\neq F_{\mathrm{cal}}$ . We measure the distributional difference using the energy discrepancy ( Székely and Rizzo 2013 ) ,

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{E}(F_{\mathrm{target}},F_{\mathrm{cal}})=2\mathbb{E}|U-V|-\mathbb{E}|U-U^{\prime}|-\mathbb{E}|V-V^{\prime}|,$$ (11)

where $U,U^{\prime}$ are independent draws from $F_{\mathrm{target}}$ , $V,V^{\prime}$ are independent draws from $F_{\mathrm{cal}}$ , and the two pairs are independent. The discrepancy is zero if and only if the two distributions coincide. We compute the sample energy statistic $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{obs}}$ from $\{u(d_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}$ and $\{u(d_{\ell}^{\mathrm{cal}})\}_{\ell=1}^{n_{\mathrm{cal}}}$ , with its implementation specified in Supplementary Section S4.7.

We obtain the collection-level $p$ -value by a permutation procedure ( Phipson and Smyth 2010 , Hemerik and Goeman 2018 ) : drawing $B$ uniform permutations of the document-level group assignments, each producing a permuted statistic $\mathcal{E}_{b}$ with $b=1,\dots,B$ , we compute

[Tableau] $$p_{\mathrm{global}}=\frac{1+\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbb{I}\{\mathcal{E}_{b}\geq\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{obs}}\}}{B+1}.$$ (12)

At a prespecified significance level $\alpha$ , we reject $H_{0}^{\mathrm{glob}}$ when $p_{\mathrm{global}}\leq\alpha$ . Rejection indicates that the target collection differs from the non-member calibration population in the distribution of document scores.

Existing mean-based two-sample comparisons, such as the $t$ -test used for dataset inference ( Maini et al. 2024 ) , focus on differences in location and need not detect changes in dispersion or shape. The energy discrepancy therefore provides a broader basis for global auditing, since it responds to any of these departures rather than to location shifts alone.


#### 3.4 BH Procedure for Suspicious-Subset Identification

Suspicious-subset identification seeks to determine which target documents warrant further investigation while controlling false discoveries. Following Problem 3, we formulate this task as multiple testing of the complete prespecified family of $m$ document-level hypotheses. We apply the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) procedure ( Benjamini and Hochberg 1995 ) , with the false discovery rate measuring the expected proportion of non-members among the selected documents.

Let $p_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq p_{(m)}$ denote the ordered document-level $p$ -values, with corresponding document indices $i_{(1)},\ldots,i_{(m)}$ . At the prespecified FDR level $q$ , BH finds the largest rank satisfying

[Tableau] $$R_{\mathrm{BH}}=\max\left\{r\in\{1,\ldots,m\}:p_{(r)}\leq\frac{r}{m}q\right\}.$$ (13)

If no rank satisfies the inequality, we set $R_{\mathrm{BH}}=0$ . The resulting discovery set is

[Tableau] $$\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{BH}}=\{i_{(1)},\ldots,i_{(R_{\mathrm{BH}})}\},$$

with $\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{BH}}=\varnothing$ when $R_{\mathrm{BH}}=0$ .

The empirical $p$ -values are dependent because they share the same calibration collection. Classical BH theory provides FDR control under positive regression dependence on the subset of true null hypotheses ( Benjamini and Yekutieli 2001 ) . For the empirical calibration in Equation ( 9 ), Theorem 4.5 establishes finite-sample FDR control under Assumption 4.2 (i): conditional on the fixed scoring procedure and the scores of member target documents, the calibration and non-member target scores are jointly exchangeable. Under this condition, applying BH to all $m$ hypotheses controls the expected proportion of non-members in $\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{BH}}$ at level $q$ .


### 4 Theoretical Guarantees

We study the statistical properties of the proposed auditing framework. We first characterize how localized chunk-level evidence contributes to the document score. Under conditional exchangeability, we then establish the validity of empirical document-level $p$ -values, type-I error control for Energy-based global auditing, and FDR control for BH subset identification. Finally, we quantify how departures from exchangeability affect these guarantees. Proofs are provided in Supplementary Section S3.

Recall that the target collection $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{target}}=\{d_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}$ contains $m$ documents, each document $d_{i}$ comprising $J(d_{i})$ chunks. The base collection $\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{base}}$ supplies $n_{\mathrm{base}}$ non-member chunks for chunk-level calibration. The calibration collection $\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{cal}}=\{d_{\ell}^{\mathrm{cal}}\}_{\ell=1}^{n_{\mathrm{cal}}}$ contains $n_{\mathrm{cal}}$ non-member documents used for document-level calibration and the comparison sample in global auditing.


#### 4.1 Localized Chunk-Level Evidence

Membership evidence may be concentrated in a small subset of a document’s chunks. The Cauchy combination allows these chunks to influence the document score even when other chunks contribute weak evidence ( Liu and Xie 2020 ) . The following proposition quantifies this contribution.

Let $S_{i}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,J(d_{i})\}$ be a nonempty subset of chunk indices. Suppose that $p^{\mathrm{ck}}_{ij}\leq u$ for all $j\in S_{i}$ . If the remaining chunks satisfy $\sum_{j\notin S_{i}}\cot\!\bigl(\pi[p^{\mathrm{ck}}_{ij}]_{\varepsilon}\bigr)\geq-\bigl(J(d_{i})-|S_{i}|\bigr)b$ , where $\varepsilon\leq u<1/2$ and $b\geq 0$ , then

[Tableau] $$T_{\mathrm{Cauchy}}(d_{i})\geq\frac{|S_{i}|}{J(d_{i})}\cot(\pi u)-\frac{J(d_{i})-|S_{i}|}{J(d_{i})}b.$$ (14)

The first term bounds the contribution from the informative chunks in $S_{i}$ , while the second bounds the negative offset from the remaining chunks.

Proposition 4.1 characterizes the contribution of localized chunk-level evidence to the Cauchy document score. For small $u\geq\varepsilon$ , the positive term is approximately $|S_{i}|/\{\pi J(d_{i})u\}$ . By comparison, averaging $1-p^{\mathrm{ck}}_{ij}$ across chunks limits the contribution from $S_{i}$ to at most $|S_{i}|/J(d_{i})$ . Cauchy aggregation therefore amplifies sufficiently small chunk-level $p$ -values. This amplification allows a small subset of chunks to yield a positive document score when its contribution outweighs the negative offset from the remaining chunks. The normalization by $J(d_{i})$ remains: the contribution depends on both the strength of the localized evidence and the proportion of chunks carrying it.


#### 4.2 Error Control under Conditional Exchangeability

Let $T_{\mathrm{agg}}$ be a measurable real-valued document scoring rule, such as the Cauchy-combined score in Equation ( 8 ). Let $\mathcal{G}$ denote the sigma-field generated by the target model, the fitting collection, the fitted evidence map, the base collection, the chunking rule, and the aggregation rule. Conditional on $\mathcal{G}$ , the complete scoring procedure is fixed and applied identically to all documents. The remaining randomness comes from the target and calibration collections.

The error guarantees rely on permutation symmetry among the relevant document scores. We state the conditions separately for document-level calibration and subset identification, and for global auditing.

- (i) Conditional on $\mathcal{G}$ and the scores of all member target documents, the scores of all calibration documents and all non-member target documents are jointly exchangeable.

- (ii) Under the global null $H_{0}^{\mathrm{glob}}$ , the scores of all target and calibration documents are jointly exchangeable conditional on $\mathcal{G}$ .

Conditional on $\mathcal{G}$ , exchanging the positions of calibration and non-member target scores leaves their joint distribution unchanged. Condition (i) requires this symmetry to hold even after conditioning on the member target scores. This additional requirement supports BH error control because its rejection threshold depends on both member and non-member scores. The symmetry allows calibration scores to provide a reference for non-member target scores, even when these scores are dependent. Condition (ii) imposes the corresponding symmetry on all target and calibration scores under the global null, providing the basis for the reassignment of document-level group labels in Energy permutation testing.

The document-level $p$ -values share the same calibration collection and are therefore generally dependent ( Bates et al. 2023 ) . Our error guarantees accommodate this dependence under the conditional exchangeability assumptions on the document scores.

The following three theorems establish document-level $p$ -value validity, global type-I error control, and FDR control for suspicious-subset identification, corresponding to Problems 1, 2, and 3, respectively.

Under Assumption 4.2 (i), for every true null $H_{0,i}$ and every $\xi\in[0,1]$ , the empirical document-level $p$ -value in Equation ( 9 ) satisfies

[Tableau] $$\Pr_{H_{0,i}}(p_{i}\leq\xi\mid\mathcal{G})\leq\frac{\lfloor(n_{\mathrm{cal}}+1)\xi\rfloor}{n_{\mathrm{cal}}+1}\leq\xi.$$ (15)

If the target document score and all calibration document scores are distinct almost surely, equality holds in the first inequality. Consequently, rejecting $H_{0,i}$ when $p_{i}\leq\alpha$ controls its conditional type-I error at any level $\alpha\in(0,1)$ .

Theorem 4.3 establishes validity after any fixed measurable document scoring rule satisfying the exchangeability condition. Under this symmetry, a non-member target score is compared with calibration scores on the same basis; when scores are distinct, its rank is uniformly distributed over the $n_{\mathrm{cal}}+1$ possible positions. The floor term in Equation ( 15 ) reflects the discrete resolution of empirical calibration. For example, when $n_{\mathrm{cal}}=99$ , the possible $p$ -values are $0.01,0.02,\ldots,1$ . Testing at $\alpha=0.025$ is therefore equivalent to rejecting when $p_{i}\leq 0.02$ , giving a conditional type-I error of at most $0.02$ . This bound is attained when scores are almost surely distinct. Counting calibration scores equal to the target score in the right tail preserves validity and can make the test more conservative.

For global auditing, the same fixed transformation is applied to target and calibration scores before computing the Energy statistic. This transformation preserves the conditional exchangeability in Assumption 4.2 (ii), yielding the following guarantee.

Under Assumption 4.2 (ii), let $p_{\mathrm{global}}$ be the Energy permutation $p$ -value computed using $B$ independent uniform permutations, drawn independently of the data and preserving the target and calibration group sizes. For every $\alpha\in(0,1)$ , define $\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}=\{p_{\mathrm{global}}\leq\alpha\}$ . Then

[Tableau] $$\Pr_{H_{0}^{\mathrm{glob}}}(\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}\mid\mathcal{G})\leq\frac{\lfloor(B+1)\alpha\rfloor}{B+1}\leq\alpha.$$ (16)

Theorem 4.4 provides type-I error control for testing equality of the target and calibration score distributions.

Suspicious-subset identification requires controlling false discoveries across the complete target family. The next result establishes this guarantee for BH applied to the empirically calibrated document-level $p$ -values.

Under Assumption 4.2 (i), applying BH to all $m$ empirical document-level $p$ -values at level $q\in(0,1)$ gives

[Tableau] $$\operatorname{FDR}(\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{BH}}\mid\mathcal{G})=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{|\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{BH}}\cap\mathcal{I}_{0}|}{|\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{BH}}|\vee 1}\;\middle|\;\mathcal{G}\right]\leq\frac{|\mathcal{I}_{0}|}{m}q\leq q,$$ (17)

where $\mathcal{I}_{0}$ is the set of non-member target indices. The bound holds when calibration scores equal to a target score are included in its empirical right-tail count.

Theorem 4.5 extends individual error control to simultaneous selection across the target collection. Under Assumption 4.2 (i), it accounts for the shared calibration collection and the data-dependent BH threshold, bounding the expected proportion of non-members among the selected documents.


#### 4.3 Robustness to Approximate Exchangeability

The preceding error guarantees rely on conditional exchangeability. We now quantify how departures from this condition affect these guarantees. We measure these departures by the total variation distance between the actual joint score distribution and a reference distribution satisfying the corresponding exchangeability condition.

Conditional on $\mathcal{G}$ , let $\mathsf{J}$ denote the joint distribution of all target and calibration document scores, and let $\mathsf{J}_{\star}$ be an ideal counterpart. Membership labels are held fixed under both distributions. For document-level testing and BH subset identification, $\mathsf{J}_{\star}$ satisfies Assumption 4.2 (i); for global testing under $H_{0}^{\mathrm{glob}}$ , it satisfies Assumption 4.2 (ii). For some $\delta\in[0,1]$ , suppose that, almost surely,

[Tableau] $$d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathsf{J},\mathsf{J}_{\star})=\sup_{A}\left|\mathsf{J}(A)-\mathsf{J}_{\star}(A)\right|\leq\delta,$$

where the supremum is over measurable events. Thus, the actual and ideal conditional joint distributions assign probabilities differing by at most $\delta$ to any event.

Under the joint total variation bound and the corresponding exchangeability conditions for $\mathsf{J}_{\star}$ , the following guarantees hold. For every $i\in\mathcal{I}_{0}$ and every $\xi\in[0,1]$ ,

[Tableau] $$\Pr(p_{i}\leq\xi\mid\mathcal{G})\leq\min\left\{1,\frac{\lfloor(n_{\mathrm{cal}}+1)\xi\rfloor}{n_{\mathrm{cal}}+1}+\delta\right\}.$$ (18)

For the BH discovery set at level $q\in(0,1)$ ,

[Tableau] $$\operatorname{FDR}(\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{BH}}\mid\mathcal{G})\leq\min\left\{1,\frac{|\mathcal{I}_{0}|}{m}q+\delta\right\}.$$ (19)

Under $H_{0}^{\mathrm{glob}}$ , the Energy permutation test in Theorem 4.4 satisfies

[Tableau] $$\Pr_{H_{0}^{\mathrm{glob}}}(\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}\mid\mathcal{G})\leq\min\left\{1,\frac{\lfloor(B+1)\alpha\rfloor}{B+1}+\delta\right\}\leq\min\{1,\alpha+\delta\}.$$ (20)

Theorem 4.6 identifies $\delta$ as an additional error cost of departure from exchangeability. A discrepancy of at most $\delta$ between the conditional joint score distributions increases each of the document-level type-I error, global type-I error, and BH FDR bounds by at most $\delta$ . The guarantees therefore remain close to their exact-exchangeability bounds when this joint discrepancy is small. This result motivates the use of calibration populations representative of non-member targets and sampling schemes compatible with the required permutation symmetry. Supplementary Section S7 examines empirical calibration robustness on HCM by varying the number and answer-length composition of non-member calibration documents.


##### Summary.

The analysis characterizes how localized chunk-level evidence contributes to document scoring and establishes finite-sample error guarantees for the three auditing objectives. Under the stated conditional exchangeability conditions, empirical calibration supports valid individual testing, Energy permutation testing controls global type-I error, and BH controls FDR for suspicious-subset identification. The robustness bounds quantify the additional error cost when the joint score distribution departs from these conditions.


### 5 Experiment

We evaluate the training-data-auditing framework through four case studies covering two training stages:

- • Pretraining. We study copyrighted books from BookTection (Supplementary Section S5) and scientific papers from Pile–arXiv (Section 5.1 ) to examine how combining and aggregating passage-level evidence supports long-document auditing.

- • Fine-tuning. We study synthetic text from Cosmopedia (Section 5.2 ) and medical question–answer records from HealthCareMagic (HCM; Section 5.3 ) to evaluate membership evidence associated with model adaptation and its use for individual and collection-level decisions.

The three case studies in the main text each address all three auditing objectives: document-level individual auditing (Problem 1), collection-level global auditing (Problem 2), and suspicious-subset identification (Problem 3). The BookTection study in the Supplementary Material focuses on Problem 1.

For individual auditing, we report the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and the true positive rate (TPR) at specified false positive rates (FPRs), assessing overall discrimination and identification under restricted false-positive budgets. For global auditing, we report the rejection rate at $\alpha=0.05$ , defined as the proportion of sampled target collections for which the global null hypothesis is rejected. For suspicious-subset identification, we report empirical power and empirical false discovery rate (FDR) at $q=0.10$ , measuring the average proportion of true members identified and the average proportion of non-members among selected documents, respectively.

Collection-level experiments use $1{,}000$ sampled target collections per configuration, with collection sizes $m\in\{30,50,100\}$ and member ratios from $10\%$ to $100\%$ . We additionally evaluate entirely non-member collections to assess false positives in global auditing. Detailed experimental settings and evaluation protocols are provided in Supplementary Section S4.

#### 5.4 Summary and Implications

We summarize the experimental findings and their implications for practical training-data auditing.

Identifying specific training documents with controlled errors requires more refined statistical inference than detecting a collection-level departure. The high rejection rates for collections containing members and the member subsets recovered by BH at the prespecified FDR levels illustrate the framework’s ability to address both objectives. Even when recovery is incomplete, identifying a subset of documents with strong membership evidence can provide statistical support and concrete leads for investigating training-data use in copyright disputes.

In the cases examined, post-training data exhibit stronger document-level membership signals than pretraining data. Document-level discrimination is higher on Cosmopedia and HCM than on Pile–arXiv. One explanation is the extent of training exposure: documents used in relatively few parameter updates may leave weak membership signals, whereas repeated use during post-training may strengthen the model’s fit to those documents and increase their separation from non-members. The Cosmopedia and HCM experiments use checkpoints after ten and two fine-tuning epochs, respectively, consistent with this interpretation. These findings suggest that auditing performance should be interpreted in relation to training exposure, since limited identification may reflect weak observable signals left by the target documents.

Interpreting a distributional departure as evidence of training use requires a suitable non-member comparison population. Calibration documents should represent target non-members, since differences in topic, time, or source can affect scores without training exposure. The error-control guarantees rely on the respective conditional exchangeability assumptions and a scoring rule fixed before document calibration. Documenting calibration-data provenance and examining sensitivity to plausible non-member populations help assess whether these conditions support the audit conclusions.

The reported performance describes auditing with specified model access and auxiliary data. Deployment requires the model outputs and any reference models used by the chosen evidence statistics, auxiliary membership labels for fitting, and held-out non-members for calibration. Within each experimental configuration, we vary target samples while fixing the scoring rule, calibration collection, and evaluation pools. Higher-quality reference data and more reliable membership labels may further improve auditing performance.


### 6 Conclusion

Data owners need reliable evidence of whether their documents were used to train LLMs. A central statistical difficulty is that membership signals are dependent: chunk-level evidence within a document shares context, and document-level scores across a collection share the same fitted evidence map and calibration data, which is a source of dependence that prior auditing methods largely ignore. The dependence nature of these signals is not well recognized, and existing methods often fail to give reliable guarantees in data-auditing problems.

We develop a framework that remains valid and useful under such dependence: Cauchy combination aggregates dependent chunk-level evidence into document scores, an Energy two-sample test assesses collection-level usage from dependent document scores, and the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure selects suspicious documents with false discovery rate control, all with finite-sample guarantees under conditional exchangeability. Case studies on books, scientific papers, synthetic text, and medical question–answer records illustrate its use in auditing both pretraining and fine-tuning data.

Our framework uses auxiliary data to construct empirical evidence and held-out non-members to calibrate decisions. Common MIA approaches use shadow models trained on different datasets ( Shokri et al. 2017 , Carlini et al. 2022 ) or held-out data for evidence construction ( Xie et al. 2024 , Maini et al. 2024 ) . For a fixed model, texts newly created and published after release supply non-members; earlier Wikipedia articles and public corpora widely used in LLM training supply candidate members. Such data are generally easier to obtain than multiple LLM-scale shadow models, whose training is costly and time-consuming.

Several questions merit further study. How can reference documents be selected or reweighted to account for temporal and domain shifts while retaining error control? With a limited reference-data budget, how should documents be allocated to improve detection power? Addressing these questions would help extend reliable auditing to settings with less knowledge of the target model’s training data.
