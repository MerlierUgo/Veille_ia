## Hy-Nomas: Towards Free-form Interactive World Generation

Interactive world generation requires models to follow successive commands while preserving relevant history and action consequences. We present Hy-Nomas , a video world model with an 8B diffusion backbone that supports free-form text instructions and continuous camera control across multiple interaction turns. It generates variable-length segments conditioned on current inputs and cached history. Our central design aligns structured video captions with action-aware causal attention: descriptive context remains accessible across segments, while action instructions are restricted to their intended temporal windows. A vision-language Prompt Enhancer (PE) translates user requests and multimodal interaction histories into structured conditions for the next segment. A multi-stage curriculum progressively introduces causal generation, text interaction, camera control, and training on model-generated histories, followed by diffusion distillation. In human evaluation, Hy-Nomas shows superior performance over existing world models. We release model weights, code and benchmarks, together with a lightweight adaptation workflow, to support reproducible research on interactive world generation.

### 1 Introduction

We define a video world model as an interactive virtual environment that renders visual observations of future states in response to user commands. Users observe the current world, decide how to act, and the model generates new visual observations that reflect both their commands and the preceding interaction history. This closed interaction loop extends the objective of conventional video generation, where a fixed prompt typically specifies a short video clip in advance. An interactive world must accommodate input commands that arrive during generation, model action-dependent dynamics, and preserve the consequences of earlier actions. Recent systems, including Genie 3 ( Google DeepMind, 2025 ) , HappyOyster ( Alibaba Cloud, 2026 ) and LingBot-World v2 ( Gao et al., 2026b ) , have made substantial progress in navigation, semantic intervention, and long-horizon generation ( Bolton et al., 2025 ; Bar et al., 2025 ; Yang et al., 2023 ) . Building on these advances, we introduce Hy-Nomas, an 8B-parameter world model focused on execution of free-form text instructions and their integration with camera navigation across multi-turn interactions. Hy-Nomas enables users to direct both what happens in a world and how they explore it through one generative backbone.

Building such a model requires more than simply replacing full attention with a causal mask. A pretrained bidirectional diffusion transformer (DiT) ( Peebles and Xie, 2023 ) must be adapted to generate successive segments from available history while following the current user command, preventing information leakage that breaks causality, and supporting efficient reuse of cached context ( Yin and others, 2025 ; Zhu and others, 2026 ; Zhao and others, 2026 ) . Because each generated segment becomes evidence for the next, small errors can compound into appearance drift, implausible motion, and scene breakdown; training must therefore prepare the model for its own imperfect predictions ( Huang et al., 2026 ; Yang and others, 2025 ; Decart et al., 2024 ) . Multi-turn interaction adds a second difficulty: persistent scene information must remain accessible, while an action instruction should take effect only during its intended time interval ( Chen and others, 2026 ; Mao et al., 2025a ; Zhang and others, 2026 ; NVIDIA et al., 2025 ) . The model must also coordinate free-form semantic commands with geometric camera controls, and retain identities, layouts, and state changes when previously observed content returns into view ( He et al., 2024 ; Xiao et al., 2025 ; Yu et al., 2025a ) . Finally, temporally causal information flow alone does not establish physical causality. Learning plausible action-to-state transitions requires training data that connects actions with their observable consequences and evaluation that tests these behaviors across successive interactions ( Ying et al., 2026 ; Xu et al., 2026a ; Bai et al., 2026 ) .

Hy-Nomas can generate visual worlds with different styles and viewpoints, supporting minute-scale rollouts with successive instructions for subject actions, object interactions, scene changes and camera motion, as shown in Figure 1 . Free-form text prompts and camera control commands can be interleaved within the same session, with historical visual and semantic context carried forward through the autoregressive key-value (KV) cache. A vision-language model (VLM), the Prompt Enhancer (PE) ( Qwen Team, 2026b ) converts the user’s request and current observations into a structured prompt with an appropriate duration for the next segment. Our evaluation demonstrates that Hy-Nomas is superior to existing world models across a range of interactive tasks.

We build Hy-Nomas by co-designing our data strategy, video captioning, model architecture, and training strategy towards free-form interactive world generation. Our data pipeline sources videos with physical interaction, human and object motion, camera movement, and long-range dependencies; video captioning records persistent subject identities, scene changes, and timestamped actions, complemented by estimated camera trajectories ( Huang et al., 2025a ) . The model architecture design couples these annotations with action-aware causal attention. Descriptive text remains available across subsequent segments, while action-specific text is restricted to its originating segment and annotated time window. This separation is designed to retain descriptive context and the visual consequences of an action without repeatedly reissuing the action itself. Mixed text-only and camera-conditioned training supports transitions between two interaction modes. Starting from HunyuanVideo 1.5 ( Wu et al., 2025 ) , we progressively introduce segment causality, localized action conditioning, and camera control, followed by high-quality annealing and segment forcing into the model training process. To make this training practical, we combine a high-compression video autoencoder ( Chen et al., 2025 ) , FLOPs-balanced packing of variable-length videos ( Krell et al., 2022 ) , attention kernels that execute heterogeneous masks ( Tao and Huang, 2025 ) , distributed sequence and model-state sharding ( Jacobs et al., 2023 ; PyTorch Contributors, ) , and stage-dependent memory management, supporting packed sequences of up to $360K$ tokens. Bounded-cache inference and a LoRA workflow that transfers adapters from the base model to its distilled counterpart further support deployment and customization for downstream applications.

Our main contributions are:

- • An interactive 8B world model. We develop Hy-Nomas for multi-turn free-form interaction and continuous camera navigation within one autoregressive DiT.

- • Aligned supervision and action conditioning. We combine structured chronological captions, persistent subject descriptions, timestamped actions, and camera trajectories with action-aware causal attention that distinguishes enduring context from temporally localized instructions.

- • A scalable training recipe and open-weight model. We integrate a progressive strategy to gradually extend the model’s capability, together with bounded-cache inference and lightweight downstream adaptation. We release model weights and code to support further research.

- • Evaluation of world-model capabilities. We assess multi-turn text interaction, camera navigation, interleaved control, and spatial memory, demonstrating the advantages of Hy-Nomas while identifying the remaining challenges in world modeling. We release all the benchmarks to facilitate reproducible research on world-model evaluation.


### 2 Data

We construct a large-scale, heterogeneous video corpus spanning real and virtual environments. Our end-to-end pipeline converts raw videos into training samples through standardized preprocessing, filtering, semantic deduplication, camera-trajectory annotation, and structured captioning. We adjust the sampling distribution across training stages to address weaknesses identified in each stage. Figure 2 summarizes the pipeline.

#### 2.1 Data Filtering

The raw videos vary in encoding types, duration, frame rate, resolution, and encoding quality. We standardize preprocessing by first dividing each video into 5-minute segments and then splitting these segments at detected shot boundaries. Our shot boundary detector is based on OmniShotCut ( Wang et al., 2026a ) and fine-tuned on high-quality annotations to improve accuracy in challenging cases. We transcode the resulting clips into a unified format and filter them by basic video properties (e.g., duration, aspect ratio, and resolution) and visual quality metrics (e.g., motion, clarity, and aesthetic scores). Additional filters screen for derivative content and abrupt transitions.


#### 2.2 Data Distribution

We extract a visual embedding for each video using Qwen3-VL-Embedding ( Li et al., 2026 ) , group videos by visual similarity through hierarchical clustering, and apply SemDeDup ( Abbas et al., 2023 ) within each cluster to remove semantically redundant clips.

We construct a hierarchical taxonomy covering semantic content and visual attributes. Content categories include different types of interaction, object manipulation, human and object movement, and camera motion; visual attributes describe camera perspective, subject type, subject and camera motion patterns, and recording methods. We fine tune a VLM to label each video according to this taxonomy, enabling targeted data selection and sampling along complementary semantic and visual dimensions.

We adapt the sampling distribution across training stages to address weaknesses identified in earlier stages. To select relevant data, we associate each video with two sets of labels: predicted video-level taxonomy labels and labels derived from hierarchical clustering. Since visual clustering is unsupervised, we manually annotate clusters at an intermediate level of the hierarchy to make them usable for data selection.

Depending on the target content category or visual attribute, we select videos using taxonomy labels, cluster-level annotations, or a combination of both. We then adjust the sampling weights of the selected subsets in subsequent training stages to prioritize areas that need further improvement.


#### 2.3 Camera Trajectories

We use ViPE ( Huang et al., 2025a ) to estimate camera trajectories. To manage its substantial computational cost, we apply it to a subset of videos suitable for trajectory estimation.

To scale annotation, we upgrade the camera calibration and segmentation modules with more capable pretrained models while retaining the existing depth estimator. We modularize the pipeline and apply adaptive spatiotemporal downsampling to reduce memory usage and improve throughput. We also tune the backend SLAM sliding window and optimization schedules to balance optimization scope against accumulated trajectory drift.

We additionally train a video-based inverse dynamics model (IDM), initialized from a pretrained VPT ( Baker et al., 2022 ) backbone, to predict discrete camera motion (such as WASD keyboard inputs) directly from video. Training combines synthetic camera-motion videos, and ViPE-derived motion labels refined using dense optical flow. Manually annotated shake and zoom data provide additional supervision, and unavailable labels are masked during training. The IDM predictions support camera motion categorization and data filtering, helping identify clips with inconsistent motion labels or unreliable trajectory annotations.

We filter in-the-wild videos both before ViPE estimation to reduce computational cost and afterward to exclude clips with static cameras, shot cuts, lens distortion, or local estimation instabilities. This filtering proceeds in four sequential steps, with only clips that pass each step advancing to the next:

- 1. We restrict the optical-flow motion-score range to reject static scenes and abrupt spikes.

- 2. For the remaining clips, we run CoTracker3 ( Karaev et al., 2025 ) to track a uniform grid of points and filter out videos in which the inter-frame displacement of visible points exceeds $20\%$ of $\max(H,W)$ or the median total displacement of tracked points is below $30\%$ of $\max(H,W)$ .

- 3. We then use AnyCalib ( Tirado-Garín and Civera, 2025 ) to jointly estimate per-frame focal length and radial distortion, penalizing clips with excessive focal-length variation, abrupt focal-length changes, overly large focal lengths, or strong distortion.

- 4. Finally, retained clips undergo ViPE estimation, followed by a trajectory audit to detect optimization failures and degenerate motion scales. We reject trajectories exhibiting repeated large orientation jumps within local windows, excessive inter-frame viewing-angle offsets, translation spikes relative to the local mean inter-frame displacement, or insufficient overall camera displacement.


#### 2.4 Video Captioning

We design structured video captions to preserve subject identity, scene context, and action chronology, with camera motion explicitly tagged for flexible supervision. Figure 3 and 4 illustrates the caption design and multi-turn annotation procedure.

> Figure 3: Structured video captioning. The VLM annotates segments sequentially, maintains subject identities, interleaves scene and camera descriptions chronologically, and records subject actions separately with timestamps. A final text-only turn reviews, translates, and validates the annotations to produce aligned English and Chinese captions. During training, camera-motion sentences and their tags are removed when camera trajectories are available and retained otherwise.

Our captions use structured JSON with video-level and segment-level information. Video-level fields specify visual style, camera perspective, and a shared subject list. Each subject has an explicit identifier and a static profile anchored to its earliest clearly identifiable appearance; subsequent actions and state changes reference this identifier to maintain continuity across segments. Within each segment, scene descriptions and camera-motion sentences are interleaved chronologically in the description field. Subject actions and their visible outcomes are recorded separately, with start and end timestamps for each action.

The first segment establishes the scene; subsequent segments describe changes relative to the preceding segment without repeating unchanged context. Action and camera-motion descriptions are limited to each segment’s temporal boundaries, and action entries form a chronological, non-overlapping sequence. This representation records scene changes and action timing to support segment-wise autoregressive rollout training.

We enclose camera-motion sentences in explicit <CameraN> and </CameraN> tags so they can be selected independently of scene and action descriptions. For training segments with ViPE-estimated trajectories, we remove the camera-motion descriptions and their enclosing tags, using the estimated trajectories for explicit camera-motion supervision. For segments without trajectory annotations, we retain the tagged sentences as textual supervision. This complementary strategy supports camera control through either keyboard inputs mapped to trajectories or natural-language motion instructions.

We generate captions in two stages. First, we extract visual features using V-JEPA 2 ( Assran et al., 2025 ) and divide each clip into shorter segments based on temporal changes in these features. In the second stage, a VLM annotates the segments sequentially in a multi-turn conversation.

The first turn establishes the initial scene and subject profiles; subsequent turns describe scene changes and current actions while refining subject identities and video-level labels using accumulated evidence. Previous segments and annotations remain in the conversation context to support temporal and semantic consistency.

For subsequent segments, the VLM uses visual evidence only from the current and preceding segments to ground its descriptions and reduce hallucination. After all segments have been processed, a final text-only turn reviews the accumulated annotations, corrects earlier drafts, and resolves inconsistencies while preserving the supplied segment boundaries. It produces the final English caption and a structurally aligned Chinese translation, both following the format in Figure 4 . In this same turn, the VLM checks the JSON structure, required fields, subject references, tags, segment and action timestamps, and consistency between different language versions, then corrects detected inconsistencies before finalizing the output.

> Figure 4: Structured caption format . Video-level fields define perspective, style, and shared subject profiles. Segment descriptions interleave scene information and camera-tagged motion chronologically, excluding main-subject references. The first segment provides a full description; later segments describe changes. Subject actions and their visible outcomes are recorded separately in action-tagged entries using subject tokens. Action intervals are non-overlapping and remain within segment boundaries.

We use different captioning models across training stages to balance annotation cost and caption quality. For data-intensive stages, we employ Gemma 4 ( Gemma Team, 2026 ) and Qwen3.5-122B-A10B ( Qwen Team, 2026a ) for efficient large-scale captioning. For the annealing stage, where annotation quality is prioritized over throughput, we use Kimi K3 ( Moonshot AI, 2026 ) to generate detailed segment-wise annotations.

Hy-Nomas builds upon HunyuanVideo 1.5 ( Wu et al., 2025 ) . We introduce segment-wise causal attention for autoregressive world generation (Section 3.1 ) and camera cross-attention for explicit camera control (Section 3.2 ). #### 3.1 Segment-wise Causal Attention

Unlike conventional video generation, video-based world models simulate evolving world states in temporal order, requiring a causal modeling framework. However, the fully bidirectional attention in our base text-to-video (T2V) model does not enforce this causal structure. We therefore replace it with segment-wise causal attention aligned with the temporal structure of the input captions. Specifically, each caption includes explicit temporal boundaries that partition the video and text tokens into temporally aligned semantic segments. Attention is bidirectional within each segment and causal across segments, allowing tokens to attend only to the current and preceding segments. We illustrate this design in Figure 6 (a). Query tokens in segment $j$ can attend to all key and value tokens from the current and preceding segments $0,\ldots,j$ , but not to future segments, enforcing causal information flow when modeling evolving world states.

We adapt the bidirectional attention to segment-wise causal attention during the early training stages. However, we observe two limitations of this masking design:

- 1. Within-segment action misalignment. We observe that actions within a segment can occur in the wrong temporal order. This behavior stems from unrestricted attention to text tokens within each segment: query tokens can attend to the entire segment caption, including actions happening from a different time. This weakens the temporal alignment between video tokens and their corresponding action descriptions in the same segment.

- 2. Cross-segment action repetition. We also observe that previously generated actions can recur across segments as generation progresses. This behavior is rooted in the continued accessibility of past action descriptions: video tokens in later segments can still attend to action descriptions from earlier segments, prompting the model to replay past actions.

To address these issues, we introduce action-aware causal attention , which extends segment-wise causal attention with a finer-grained masking scheme to distinguish actions within and across segments, as illustrated in Figure 6 (b). Each segment’s text tokens are divided into a descriptive head for the segment’s environment and action tails $a_{0},\ldots,a_{n}$ , each specifying an individual action and its corresponding temporal window. This masking scheme addresses the two aforementioned issues as follows:

- 1. Within-segment action alignment. Query tokens within an action window are restricted to attending only to the associated text tokens and the descriptive head tokens from the same segment. This preserves environmental context while reducing action-order errors and premature action execution.

- 2. Cross-segment action isolation. Descriptive heads remain accessible to the current and subsequent segments, whereas action tails are restricted to their originating segment. This preserves environmental context across segments while blocking access to past action descriptions, reducing action repetition in later segments.

Applying action-aware causal attention during the later training stages substantially reduces action-order errors and cross-segment action repetition.


#### 3.2 Camera-Trajectory Conditioning

Hy-Nomas supports continuous camera control during segment-wise autoregressive generation. For each segment, given camera-to-world poses $P_{t}$ , we encode frame-to-frame relative motion as $\Delta P_{t}=P_{t-1}^{-1}P_{t}$ , with the first increment set to the identity. Relative transformations are computed independently within each segment, avoiding dependence on the global coordinate frame and camera poses from preceding segments.

Frame-to-frame translations are normalized by a single video-level scale, defined as the 99th percentile of translation magnitudes across all frames. This limits the influence of extreme values on the normalization scale while preserving relative motion speeds. We pad the beginning of the camera sequence by repeating its first transformation until the sequence length is divisible by four. Each group of four consecutive $4\times 4$ transformation matrices is then flattened into a 64-dimensional vector and processed by a linear projection followed by a residual MLP, yielding one camera token per VAE latent frame. In each MMDiT block, visual tokens attend to temporally aligned camera tokens within the same segment through cross-attention:

[Tableau] $$\widetilde{V}=V+\operatorname{CrossAttn}(V,C).$$ (3)

Here $V$ denotes the visual tokens, and $C$ denotes the temporally aligned camera tokens within the same segment. In cross-attention, visual tokens serve as queries, while camera tokens provide keys and values. Recent interactive world models instead rely on coarser action interfaces: some quantize both camera translation and rotation into fixed directional commands ( Mao et al., 2025b ; Happy Oyster Team, 2026 ) , while others keep rotation continuous but restrict translation to discrete keyboard actions ( Yu et al., 2025b ; He et al., 2026 ; Robbyant Team et al., 2026 ) . Our relative-pose trajectory keeps both translation and rotation continuous, enabling finer-grained control over direction, speed, and scale.

To support interleaved camera and text control during inference, we randomly disable camera conditioning on a per-segment basis during training, exposing the model to alternation between camera- and text-controlled segments. For segments with camera conditioning enabled, we remove camera descriptions from video caption to prevent the model from using them as shortcuts during training. For segments without camera conditioning, we retain these descriptions to enable text-driven camera control.

At inference, keyboard inputs are converted into camera trajectories. W / S control forward and backward motion, A / D control lateral motion, and mouse or arrow-key inputs control yaw and pitch rotations. These translation and rotation increments are accumulated frame by frame to construct camera-to-world poses. Motion speed, scale and duration can be adjusted without retraining.


### 4 Training Infrastructure

To efficiently process long, variable-length videos when adapting a pretrained video generator into an interactive world model through multi-stage training, we optimize distributed parallelism, data loading, and memory usage. This section describes the detailed infrastructure optimizations.


#### 4.1 Model and Data Parallelism

Training long, token-packed video sequences requires distributing both model state and activation computation across multiple GPUs. We combine Ulysses-style sequence parallelism (SP) ( Jacobs et al., 2023 ) , data parallelism (DP), and FSDP2 hybrid sharding ( PyTorch Contributors, ) . Sequence parallelism partitions a single packed sample across a small group of GPUs, data parallelism assigns different packed samples to different groups, and FSDP2 shards model parameters, gradients, and optimizer states within each group. This organization enables sequences of up to 360K tokens while keeping parameter-sharding and sequence-parallel communication localized to small groups. On top of this parallel layout, we use a mask-flexible attention kernel for the heterogeneous segment- and action-aware masks, FLOPs-balanced sequence packing, a distributed Muon optimizer, and asynchronous checkpointing, which together determine the end-to-end throughput and robustness of long-running jobs.

We adopt Ulysses sequence parallelism ( Jacobs et al., 2023 ) with $N_{\mathrm{SP}}=8$ to reduce activation memory for packed video and text sequences of up to 360K tokens. Both video and text tokens are partitioned along the sequence dimension across eight GPUs, with each rank holding approximately 45K tokens at maximum sequence length. During attention, an all-to-all collective transforms sequence shards into head shards, allowing each rank to compute a subset of attention heads over the full sequence, followed by a reverse all-to-all to restore sequence sharding for local normalization, residual, and MLP operations.

Each eight-GPU SP group forms one logical data-parallel replica and processes a different packed batch. For $N_{\mathrm{GPU}}$ total GPUs and sequence-parallel degree $N_{\mathrm{SP}}$ , the number of replicas is $N_{\mathrm{DP}}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{GPU}}}{N_{\mathrm{SP}}}.$ The precomputed packing plan assigns a different pack to each replica, while gradients are synchronized across replicas to maintain a consistent global model update. Because the gradient synchronization waits for the slowest replica, the packs are balanced by their estimated FLOPs rather than by sample count or token count (see Section 4.3 ), reducing workload variation caused by differences in video resolution, duration, and mask density.

We employ FSDP2 ( PyTorch Contributors, ) with a logical distributed mesh organized as

[Tableau] $$[\text{DP replication},\ \text{DP sharding},\ \text{sequence parallelism}].$$ (4)

The explicit DP-shard size is set to one. In our implementation, the DP-shard and SP dimensions are combined when constructing the FSDP shard group. Consequently, parameters, gradients, and optimizer states are effectively sharded across the same eight GPUs that form one SP group, and the resulting model shard is replicated across the data-parallel groups.

This topology avoids performing parameter all-gathers and gradient reduce-scatters over the entire job. These collectives remain within the eight-GPU shard group, while gradient synchronization across replicated groups provides global data-parallel scaling. Parameter all-gathers and gradient reduce-scatters are overlapped with forward and backward computation whenever their dependencies permit. The design therefore trades local communication for substantial reductions in per-GPU model-state memory.


#### 4.2 GPU Memory Optimization

All main transformer blocks use activation checkpointing ( Chen et al., 2016 ) . Standard checkpointing stores the block input during the forward pass and reconstructs intermediate activations during backpropagation, requiring both the attention and MLP computations to be executed again. Following the principle of selective activation checkpointing ( PyTorch Team, 2025 ) , we additionally support selective recomputation through skip_attn_recompute . When enabled, the attention output and required softmax statistics are retained from the original forward pass. Backward recomputation reuses these saved attention results and recomputes only the remaining block operations, including the MLP. This option preserves attention gradients while avoiding a second execution of the attention forward kernel, trading additional activation memory for lower recomputation cost.

The optimal memory policy depends on the training algorithm. During one-shot training, particularly after the introduction of per-layer camera attention, we enable selective attention checkpointing, asynchronous activation offload, and camera-module checkpointing. Saved attention outputs and checkpoint inputs can be transferred to pinned CPU memory, reducing GPU memory pressure while preserving the compute savings from skipping attention recomputation.

During segment forcing, we instead disable attention-output retention and activation offload. Segment-wise streaming backward already bounds peak memory by releasing intermediate activations as the serial rollout progresses. In this regime, retaining attention outputs increased memory by approximately 1.6 GB per rank without providing a meaningful speed improvement. We therefore use ordinary attention recomputation together with streaming backward.


#### 4.3 Training Throughput Optimization

To improve training throughput on long videos with varying spatial resolutions and durations, we employ sequence packing ( Krell et al., 2022 ) to combine 480p and 720p videos spanning 5–75 seconds into token-budgeted batches. Packing preserves each token’s sample identity and original spatial, temporal, and segment metadata, with block-diagonal attention preventing information leaking across videos. Conditioning signals are packed with video tokens to preserve text–segment and camera–frame alignment, while the training loss is computed only over valid video tokens.

Balancing token counts alone does not ensure balanced computation, as segment-wise causal attention incurs substantially different costs depending on the number of unmasked query–key pairs. We therefore estimate the computational cost of each sample directly. Let $n_{i}$ denote the number of valid video and text tokens of sample $i$ , and let

[Tableau] $$A_{i}=\sum_{p\in i}\bigl|\{q:M^{(i)}_{pq}\neq-\infty\}\bigr|$$ (5)

denote the number of unmasked query–key pairs under its segment- and action-aware mask $M^{(i)}$ . For segment lengths $m_{1},\ldots,m_{S}$ , $A_{i}$ is dominated by $\sum_{j=1}^{S}m_{j}\sum_{k\leq j}m_{k}$ , which can be computed from segment metadata without materializing the mask. We model the estimated per-sample training FLOPs as

[Tableau] $$F_{i}=\alpha n_{i}+\beta A_{i},$$ (6)

where $\alpha$ captures the per-token cost of linear operations across the 54 blocks, including camera cross-attention, and $\beta$ captures the per-pair cost of $QK^{\top}$ and $PV$ operations. Both coefficients account for backward propagation and activation recomputation, and are derived analytically from the architecture in Table 1 and calibrated against measured step times.

We construct the packing plan offline in two stages. First, samples are grouped into packs satisfying the token budget $\sum_{i\in\mathrm{pack}}n_{i}\leq N_{\max}$ , while targeting similar estimated FLOPs per pack. Second, packs are assigned to the $N_{\mathrm{DP}}$ replicas using a longest-processing-time-first greedy algorithm, sorting packs by estimated FLOPs in descending order and assigning each to the least-loaded replica. This balances computational workloads across replicas and reduces synchronization-induced idle time. The resulting plan is stored alongside the data index and loaded by the dataloader without additional runtime scheduling overhead, with consistent packing assignments across ranks within each sequence-parallel group.

Our training combines segment-causal and action-aware attention with block-diagonal boundaries across packed videos, resulting in complex attention masks over sequences of up to 360K tokens. Dense masks are prohibitively expensive at this scale, while standard FlashAttention kernels ( Shah et al., 2024 ) cannot efficiently handle such irregular attention patterns. We therefore adopt the Flex-Flash-Attention (FFA) kernel from MagiAttention ( Tao and Huang, 2025 ) , which represents structured masks as compact attention slices and skips masked attention blocks. We construct these slices directly from sample, segment, and action metadata without materializing dense masks. We further integrate FFA with eight-way Ulysses sequence parallelism, where each rank processes two of the 16 attention heads over the packed sequence using the same slice specification. This avoids the repeated key–value communication of context-parallel approaches while retaining only two all-to-all collectives per attention layer.

We train the DiT using Muon ( Jordan et al., 2024 ; Liu et al., 2025b ) for the weight matrices in attention and feed-forward layers, and AdamW ( Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019 ) for embeddings, biases, and other remaining parameters. To support Muon’s full-matrix orthogonalization under FSDP2 parameter sharding, we adopt the distributed Muon implementation from Canzona ( Wang et al., 2026b ) .

We build upon ByteCheckpoint ( Wan et al., 2024 ) to asynchronously save FSDP2-sharded model and optimizer states, overlapping serialization and storage I/O with training through pinned CPU memory. To ensure deterministic resumption, we additionally checkpoint all dataloader states, including packing-plan positions and sampler states, together with all CPU and GPU RNG states. Restoring these states preserves data ordering and stochastic operations across training interruptions. Furthermore, our global tensor metadata enables checkpoint resharding across different GPU counts and parallel configurations without offline conversion.


### 5 Model Training

We progressively adapt a pretrained bidirectional DiT into an interactive world model through coordinated curricula in causality, interaction, and context length. Section 5.1 explains the design of this progression, and Sections 5.2 – 5.5 describe its implementation, from continual pre-training to few-step autoregressive generation. Section 5.6 presents a lightweight recipe for downstream adaptation.


#### 5.1 Training Curriculum

Our training strategy introduces each capability on top of an already learned foundation. Table 2 summarizes the five stages, with continual pre-training (CPT) divided into three successive regimes. Together, these stages advance three complementary curricula.

We progress from segment-wise causal attention to action-aware causal attention , followed by segment forcing and diffusion distillation . Segment-wise masking first establishes the information flow required for autoregressive generation: each segment uses its current instruction and preceding history, while attention remains bidirectional within the segment. Action-aware masking then refines this structure by setting bounded temporal scope for each action instruction, addressing action-order errors and the repetition of earlier actions. Segment forcing subsequently trains the model with cached context from its own predictions, exposing the model to imperfect histories before inference. Finally, causal distillation reduces the sampling steps while further mitigating error accumulation due to training and inference mismatch. This curriculum progressively convert a bidirectional DiT into an efficient autoregressive model.

We extend a bidirectional, noninteractive text-to-video DiT into a model supporting multi-turn free-form text interaction , and then free-form text interaction with continuous camera control . Segment-wise captions and causal attention teach the model to respond to successive text instructions while carrying forward relevant visual context. Camera-conditioned CPT adds geometric control capabilities after the foundation for text-prompt interaction has been established. Mixing text-only and camera-conditioned segments, together with segment-wise camera dropout, exposes the model to transitions between different interaction modes within a sequence. The resulting model can continue an evolving world under either semantic instructions or explicit camera motion.

We combine mixed-resolution and mixed-length training with a progressive increase in the maximum packed sequence length. The context length is set to $180K$ tokens before action-aware CPT and increases to $360K$ tokens from action-aware CPT onward. Mixing resolutions and durations balances spatial detail and temporal coverage; expanding the context length helps the model to understand longer histories and generate richer instruction video sequences. Sequence packing and memory optimizations in Section 4 make this progression practical. A context length of $360K$ tokens is sufficient to generate minute-scale visual worlds at $720p$ resolution.

#### 5.2 Continual Pre-Training

We initialize Hy-Nomas from HunyuanVideo 1.5 ( Wu et al., 2025 ) and adapt its VAE to DCAE-V ( Chen et al., 2025 ) , increasing spatial compression from $16\times 16$ to $32\times 32$ while keeping temporal compression as a factor of four. This significantly reduces the latent token count at a given video resolution and duration, making training on longer sequences more feasible. We first freeze the intermediate DiT blocks and update only the input projection and output layers for 20K steps, then train the full DiT for another 60K steps.

CPT first establishes segment-wise causal attention on diverse, variable-length videos with dense structured captions, then continues with higher-quality data under the same attention structure. We subsequently introduce action-aware masking with temporally localized annotations. The key transition is to supervise when an instruction applies: descriptive context remains available across segments, whereas action instructions are restricted to their originating segment and annotated temporal windows. Increasing the context length at this stage allows training to include longer action sequences and histories. The attention design is specified in Section 3.1 .

At the end of CPT, we introduce the camera-control interaction capability. Normalized camera extrinsics are encoded as one token per latent frame and injected through frame-wise cross-attention after every MMDiT block. Both the base DiT and the camera modules remain trainable, allowing camera control to be learned jointly with visual quality and prompt adherence. During this training stage, both the camera trajectory and camera-related text descriptions are dropped with $50\%$ probability for each segment. This allows camera-control interaction and text-prompt interaction to coexist within the same video while preventing text-based camera descriptions in captions from leaking the camera motion.


#### 5.3 High-Quality Annealing

After broad capability acquisition, we anneal on high-quality, predominantly single-subject videos from various targeted sources. Kimi K3 ( Moonshot AI, 2026 ) is used to generate more accurate dense captions, and we increase the maximum text length from 768 to 1,280 tokens to accommodate more detailed segment descriptions and longer action sequences. The model architecture and flow-matching ( Lipman et al., 2023 ) objective remain unchanged.

We use different supervision for annealing data according to its camera trajectory quality. Videos whose ViPE trajectories pass validity and motion-variance filters enter the camera-conditioned path; those with unreliable trajectories remain useful as text-conditioned training samples. Camera trajectories are normalized with a shared video-level scale preserving relative motion magnitudes across segments. This concentrates learning on precise text and camera motion supervision while retaining useful videos that lack reliable camera trajectories.


#### 5.4 Segment Forcing

Segment Forcing adapts the model to the evolving history it consumes during autoregressive inference. Inspired by training with model-generated context in Self Forcing ( Huang et al., 2026 ) , we combine sequential segment-wise denoising, one-step context estimation, and streaming backward. This exposes later segments to prediction errors while retaining direct flow-matching supervision for each current segment.

Given a video divided into multiple segments, the autoregressive DiT processes the segments sequentially. At segment $s$ , the model takes the noisy latent $\mathbf{x}_{s,t}$ as input and predicts its flow-matching velocity $\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{s}$ , conditioned on the KV cache constructed from preceding segments and prompts. We apply the flow-matching MSE objective ( Lipman et al., 2023 ) only to the current segment prediction. The resulting loss is immediately backpropagated, after which the computation graph and intermediate activations of the current segment are released to save GPU memory.

To construct the context for the next segment, we perform one-step denoising from the same noisy latent $\mathbf{x}_{s,t}$ using the predicted velocity:

[Tableau] $$\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{s,t_{\mathrm{ctx}}}=\mathbf{x}_{s,t}+(t_{\mathrm{ctx}}-t)\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{s},\qquad t_{\mathrm{ctx}}\sim\mathcal{U}(0.1,0.3).$$ (7)

The sampled range approximates the noise levels of context segments encountered during autoregressive inference. The resulting low-noise latent is appended to the KV cache and used as context for subsequent segments. We omit this cache update for the final segment because no later segment consumes it.

By repeatedly generating and caching its own context, the model is trained under a history distribution that closely matches autoregressive inference. Meanwhile, streaming backward keeps only the latest segment’s computation graph in memory. Gradients are accumulated across segments, followed by a single optimizer step after the full sequence has been processed. The complete pipeline is illustrated in Figure 7 .

> Figure 7: Segment Forcing with streaming backward. Each current segment receives a flow-matching loss; a one-step low-noise estimation supplies cached context for later segments. Streaming backward retains only one segment’s computation graph at a time, accumulating gradients for a single optimizer step after the full sequence. The cache update of the final segment is skipped.


#### 5.5 Diffusion Distillation

Starting from the AR model trained with segment self-forcing, we use two-stage distillation to reduce the sampling budget from 100 to 4 network function evaluations (NFEs) per segment. The two stages establish few-step generation and then refine visual fidelity and motion stability while preserving semantic alignment and camera controllability.

Stage I: Few-Step Initialization. Drawing on consistency-based distillation ( Song et al., 2023 ; Wang et al., 2024 ) , we initialize the student with a causal adaptation of Phased Consistency Models (PCM). The teacher’s denoising trajectory is partitioned into phases, and the student learns to map noisy states within each phase to its lower boundary. Training uses teacher forcing: each segment is conditioned on ground-truth history, text, and camera inputs. The resulting few-step checkpoint initializes Stage II.

Stage II: Quality Refinement. Starting from the PCM checkpoint, we refine the student under hybrid forcing ( Hong et al., 2025 ) to improve visual fidelity and motion stability while preserving semantic alignment. For longer sequences, a noise-perturbed ground-truth prefix initializes the student’s causal KV cache. The student then generates the suffix autoregressively with 4 NFEs per segment, using each generated segment as context for the next. This combines ground-truth context with training on the student’s own generated history. Shorter sequences are generated entirely by the student.

We jointly optimize the student with a causal adaptation of Decoupled Distribution Matching Distillation (DDMD) and an adversarial hinge objective, drawing on distribution-matching distillation ( Liu et al., 2025a ; Yin et al., 2024b ; Yin et al., 2024a ) and adversarial distillation ( Sauer et al., 2023 ; Lin et al., 2025a ; Lin et al., 2025b ; Cheng et al., 2026a ; Cheng et al., 2026b ) , respectively. DDMD samples noise levels independently for CFG and DM, restricting CFG to the remaining denoising interval while sampling DM over the full interval ( Liu et al., 2025a ) . DDMD supervision is applied only to the final generated segment, with gradients stopped through earlier segments and their cached representations. The teacher remains frozen, while the fake-score model is trained with flow matching ( Lipman et al., 2023 ) on detached student samples. The adversarial objective complements DDMD with supervision from high-quality real videos to improve visual fidelity and motion stability.

At inference, the distilled student generates segments autoregressively with 4 NFEs per segment, conditioned on text, camera inputs, and previously generated history. After each segment is generated, we perturb it with flow noise at $\sigma=0.206897$ before updating the causal KV cache for subsequent segments.


#### 5.6 Downstream Adaptation via LoRA

> Figure 8: Downstream LoRA adaptation workflow. Train LoRA adapters on the base autoregressive model (before distillation), then merge the learned weights into the distilled model for efficient deployment, without full-model fine-tuning or repeating distillation.

To help users customize our model for their use cases, we provide a recommended workflow based on Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) ( Hu et al., 2021 ) . We present this workflow as our best-practice recipe for downstream adaptation and encourage users to follow it when developing their own specialized models.

We recommend training LoRA adapters on the base autoregressive model before distillation, then merging the learned LoRA adapter weights into the distilled model for deployment. The workflow is illustrated in Figure 8 . Users can follow this recipe with on the order of 100 task-specific samples selected to reflect their intended visual style, usage scenario, or application domain. This workflow enables lightweight customization without full-model fine-tuning or repeating the distillation pipeline, while retaining the inference efficiency of the distilled model. The following examples illustrate these best practices and provide practical references for users adapting the model to their own applications.

As an example of visual-style adaptation, we train a cinematic-style LoRA adapter on the base autoregressive model using cinematic videos paired with high-quality captions from K3 as our SFT data. We set the LoRA rank to 256 and alpha to 512, and train for 500 iterations with a learning rate of $10^{-4}$ . For effective domain adaptation, we recommend using a slightly higher learning rate than that used for base-model training. Due to the small size of the SFT dataset, we employ sequence packing in the data loader such that each training iteration covers the entire dataset, making 500 iterations equivalent to 500 epochs. We then merge the resulting LoRA adapter weights into the distilled model for inference.

Beyond visual-style adaptation, robotics is another potential application of this recipe. We perform task-specific robotics LoRA fine-tuning on a set of robotics dual-arm egocentric task videos ( RealSource, 2025 ) , which is not overlapped with our training set. We conduct the LoRA tuning for approximately $1,500$ epochs on the selected 128-sample tuning set with a LoRA rank of 512.

In Section 7.4 , we provide a preliminary evaluation of the LoRA-adapted model against the distilled model before merging the LoRA weights.


### 6 Model Inference

Interactive deployment requires translating user intent into suitable conditioning inputs and generating responses efficiently. To address these complementary requirements, we develop a VLM-based Prompt Enhancer (PE) and optimize the model’s inference pipeline.

The PE translates user intent into structured instructions for the video generator. Given previously generated video segments, the current user instruction, and optional camera-control signals, the PE produces a prompt for the next segment. This design builds on recent approaches to streaming text control and agent-mediated world interaction ( Yang and others, 2025 ; Gao et al., 2026b ) , while using a structured caption format aligned with our training annotations.

> Figure 9: Prompt Enhancer (PE) workflow . PE generates an initial dense caption in the first turn. ##### 6.1.1 Prompt Enhancer Workflow

Prompt Enhancer (PE) converts user instructions into structured dense captions for segment-by-segment video generation. At each turn, PE produces one caption for the next segment, which the DiT then uses to generate the corresponding video. The interaction proceeds causally: each new turn conditions on the current user input and the complete history accumulated so far.

Formally, the caption at turn $t$ is generated as

[Tableau] $$y_{t}\sim p_{\theta}\!\left(\cdot\mid S,u_{t},H_{t-1},V_{<t},m_{t}\right),$$ (8)

where $S$ is the system prompt, $u_{t}$ is the current user input, and $m_{t}$ specifies the rewriting mode. The dialogue history $H_{t-1}$ contains all preceding user requests and PE responses, while $V_{<t}=(V_{1},\ldots,V_{t-1})$ contains all previously generated video segments. Both histories are empty at the first turn. After each round, the dialogue and generated video are retained as context for subsequent turns.

The output format depends on the turn and the selected control mode:

- • First-turn initialization. The user provides subject and environment descriptions, together with any specified visual style and viewing perspective. PE returns video-level metadata and the first segment caption, including visual_description_long to establish the scene and contained_actions to specify subject actions, according to the caption format defined in Figure 4 .

- • Subsequent turns in text prompt mode. PE combines the current user description with the full dialogue and video history to produce an incremental dense caption. The output contains description_change for scene and camera updates relative to the established context, and contained_actions for the next segment’s subject behavior.

- • Subsequent turns in camera control mode. PE outputs only description_change , leaving contained_actions empty. The user-defined camera trajectory is supplied separately to DiT alongside this incremental caption.


##### 6.1.2 Prompt Enhancer Training

To support local deployment, we train a lightweight PE model using a dataset of multi-turn multimodal conversations. Each turn pairs the current user instruction and previous dialogue and video history with a structured dense caption response. We construct the data in two ways. First, we synthesize user prompts from existing dense captions, checking that the prompts preserve the specified subject attributes, scene content, and action ordering. Second, we collect real user prompts as seed data and augment them into more synthetic user prompts. We then run these user prompts through the complete PE and video generation pipeline, recording the caption response and generated video at each turn. Each PE turn uses the current instruction and the full dialogue and video history from previous turns. The dataset includes scene initialization and follow-up interactions across different subjects, environments, viewpoints, and control modes. We also add examples to expand the coverage of action types, different action combinations, and temporal orderings, and check for inconsistencies between instructions, captions, and successive segments. We use Qwen3.8-27B as our base model and train our PE through supervised fine-tuning.


#### 6.2 Inference Optimization

To make our model accessible to users with limited GPU resources, we optimize both autoregres- sive generation and video decoding for deployment on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU. We com- bine INT8 storage, sliding-window attention (SWA), and CPU offloading to reduce GPU memory usage, and use sparse attention to improve generation efficiency. For video decoding, we train a PixelDiT decoder based on PixelUMM ( Wei et al., 2026 ) and optimize it for single-GPU inference.

We reduce inference memory primarily through INT8 quantization, sliding-window attention (SWA), and CPU offloading. We quantize model weights and cache historical attention keys and values in INT8 with floating-point scales, dequantizing them to BF16 during computation while keeping the current segment’s attention inputs in BF16. SWA maintains a bounded history on the GPU, preventing the KV cache from growing with rollout duration. We further offload inactive model components to CPU memory between pipeline stages, such as offloading the VAE during DiT denoising and loading the text encoder only when needed. This reduces peak memory usage at the cost of CPU–GPU transfer latency. Peak GPU memory also depends on the current segment length and temporary computation buffers. For example, generating a 100-second video across seven segments at 1280 $\times$ 704 resolution and 24 FPS requires 20.53 GiB of peak GPU memory. To further accelerate inference, we use dense attention for the first two denoising steps, followed by sparse attention that retains 25% of candidate video blocks in subsequent steps. These optimizations may introduce subtle differences that become more noticeable over successive segments during autoregressive generation.

We replace the DCAE-V decoder with a pixel-space diffusion decoder initialized from the vision part of PixelUMM ( Wei et al., 2026 ) . The decoder takes the DCAE-V latents as a condition and recovers the corresponding video from pixel-space Gaussian noise. When reconstructing videos with complex natural textures, the original DCAE-V decoder, which operates on highly compressed latents, tends to output a repetitive grid-like pattern in place of the true texture. In contrast, our decoder uses its pretrained generative prior to restore realistic high-frequency details. The decoder is trained with PixelUMM’s diffusion loss, first on images and 40-frame videos at $256\times 256$ , and then on images and longer videos at 384p–720p. For long videos, we adopt the temporal tiling strategy of the H3 decoder ( MiniMax, 2026 ) rather than that of the DCAE-V decoder, decoding overlapping chunks of 40 frames with a 20-frame overlap blended by cosine weights. Spatially, instead of overlapping tiling, which incurs substantial redundant computation, we decode each frame in a single pass and use sliding-tile attention ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) , in which each patch attends only to the patches in a local spatial neighborhood across all frames in the chunk. Finally, we distill the decoder with DMD2 ( Yin et al., 2024a ) for one-step inference, and accelerate its inference on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU with FP8 quantization.

#### 7.1 Hy-Nomas-Bench and Metrics

The overview of Hy-Nomas-Bench is shown in Figure 10 . We construct four benchmarks totaling 280 test cases that assess model capabilities through free-form text prompts, keyboard-based camera commands, and interleaved text and camera inputs, as well as spatial memory across successive interactions. The benchmarks cover first-person, second-person, third-person following, and third-person observer viewpoints in photorealistic, game-like, and animated environments. We release original user prompts for evaluating other models through their native interfaces, alongside PE-enhanced prompts following our structured caption format to reproduce our results 2 2 https://huggingface.co/datasets/tencent/Hy-Nomas-Bench . Camera controls provide frame-aligned keyboard states and reference poses with explicit timing and motion conventions. All four benchmarks cover complex multi-turn interactions with cases that require the model to generate videos longer than $60$ seconds.

We consider four dimensions to evaluate world models. Prompt following measures adherence to free-form text instructions; physical plausibility assesses the realism and consistency of generated physical interactions; camera control measures how accurately the model responds to navigation commands; and memory consistency assesses spatial coherence when revisiting previously observed environments. These criteria are designed to cover different capabilities of world models, i.e . , instruction execution, plausible dynamics, navigation, and spatial recall. Conventional evaluation metrics used in video generation are not considered in Hy-Nomas-Bench (such as visual fidelity, aesthetics, audio quality, etc . ), as we focus on capabilities that are crucial to world models.

A closely related work is WBench ( Ying et al., 2026 ) , which evaluates text-prompt and camera-control capability separately, since most existing models can only handle one type of interaction. In contrast, we introduce interleaved text and camera control within a single continuous test case. We also assess spatial memory through specially designed camera trajectories that leave and return to an earlier viewpoint, testing whether previously observed anchor objects and environments remain present and consistent. We describe each benchmark below.

This benchmark examines whether instruction execution remains consistent with an evolving scene across successive turns. We construct 60 scenarios with 2–6 turns, using prompts curated by human annotators. The sequences include interactions that depend on entities and states established in preceding turns, requiring models to execute new instructions while retaining relevant scene context. This introduces dependencies across turns beyond isolated action completion: previously introduced subjects must remain identifiable, object interactions must remain compatible with the current scene, and action transitions must remain physically plausible. The resulting sequences probe both local instruction adherence and the persistence of scene content throughout the whole video.

This benchmark targets the combination of different keyboard actions and reversibility of camera controls. The 100 cases cover all eight basic keyboard actions and all 24 two-key combinations that exclude opposing inputs along the same control axis. These combinations encompass simultaneous translations, simultaneous rotations, and coupled translation–rotation motions, testing whether individually meaningful controls remain correctly composed when applied together. Each segment holds a fixed action for 2–4 seconds and is followed by an equal-duration inverse action. Under the specified motion-integration rules, the reference trajectories return to the same position and orientation at each pair boundary. This construction provides controlled motion intervals and known return poses for examining directional adherence, coupled-motion errors, and drift over successive outbound–return pairs.

This benchmark tests whether control responsiveness and scene continuity persist when switching between text prompts and camera control within a rollout. The 60 cases contain 2–7 segments, each specifying either a free-form semantic interaction or an explicit camera-control sequence. Bench-3 covers both text-to-camera and camera-to-text transitions. Text segments introduce actions and scene changes, while camera segments navigate the scene under prescribed camera trajectories. It requires models to preserve the consequences of earlier interactions during navigation and interpret subsequent text instructions correctly to update the scene. World models need to respond correctly when switching between different control signals.

This benchmark evaluates whether previously observed content remains consistent after leaving the field of view. We construct 60 first-person scenarios, evenly distributed across real-world and game-style indoor and outdoor environments. Each scenario contains a distinctive static anchor object and eight camera-control segments forming four outbound–return pairs, covering translations, rotations, and more complex camera motions. A static anchor object is used as a reference to identify any memory inconsistency. The camera trajectories are designed to take the anchor out of view before returning to a previously observed viewpoint. The task of Bench-4 is to compare initial and revisited frames at matched camera positions and orientations, and assess the differences between the frames. We focus on the anchor’s identity and appearance, as well as the surrounding scene layout when measuring memory consistency. Figure 11 illustrates the anchor-based camera trajectory designs.

#### 7.3 Comparison with Other World Models

Overall, Hy-Nomas demonstrates superior interaction capability and supports different control signals for interaction. Notably, Genie 3 does not provide a free-form text interaction capability in its official API. Happy Oyster adopts separate models for each control signal, thus can not support text and camera interleaved interaction. Although LingBot-World v2 can cover all the settings by adapting its open-source model, it exhibits substantially weaker performance in following free-form text instructions, as its text-based control is implemented by selecting VLM-generated proposals to guide the generation of the subsequent video segment, rather than taking free-form text instructions from the user. From Bench-1 to Bench-3, Hy-Nomas consistently achieves the best performance, highlighting both its versatility and effectiveness in handling heterogeneous control signals. Despite these advantages, spatial memory remains an area for improvement. ### 8 Related Work

The definition of world model now encompasses a broad range of objectives and approaches. Representation-based approaches, such as V-JEPA ( Bardes et al., 2024 ) , learn predictive visual features as a foundation for video understanding. Geometry-based approaches, including systems from World Labs ( World Labs, 2025 ) , emphasize persistent 3D representations that support rendering and navigation. Reconstruction models such as VGGT ( Wang et al., 2025 ) provide complementary building blocks for recovering scene geometry from visual observations. Video-based approaches model world evolution through generated observations, drawing on large collections of videos to learn visual dynamics and action-conditioned transitions ( Bruce et al., 2024 ; Yang et al., 2023 ) . A central question is whether learning from visual appearances and motion alone is sufficient to capture the underlying physical dynamics, or whether additional structural priors and supervision are required. Controlled studies have revealed limitations in the physical generalization of video generation models, even as model capacity and training data scale ( Kang et al., 2025 ) . Meanwhile, recent work has demonstrated that post-training techniques, such as DPO ( Wallace et al., 2024 ; Rafailov et al., 2023 ) , can improve physical consistency ( Cai et al., 2026 ) . Nevertheless, whether further scaling of data and model capacity, combined with reinforcement learning (RL), can fundamentally overcome these limitations and enable video-generation-based world models to learn robust physical dynamics remains an open research question.

In this sense, world modeling brings together several long-standing goals of computer vision: 3D reconstruction, video understanding, and visual generation ( Ren et al., 2025b ; Ren et al., 2025c ; Bahmani et al., 2024 ) . It also raises a broader question: can intelligence emerge from perceiving the visual world? A complementary direction uses a language- or vision-language-model agent to write and refine Blender code for constructing or editing 3D scenes, as explored in BlenderAlchemy ( Huang et al., 2024 ) . Such a pipeline could additionally incorporate generative video editing to enhance visual realism. This design places planning and program synthesis in an agent, while delegating scene execution and rendering to graphics tools. It illustrates a possible division of responsibilities, without establishing where intelligence must reside. Whether multimodal learning contributes distinct forms of intelligence beyond providing interfaces for perception and generation remains an open question.

Across these approaches, world models offer promising applications in autonomous driving ( Hu et al., 2023 ; Wang et al., 2023 ; Ren et al., 2025a ; Du et al., 2023 ; NVIDIA et al., 2025 ) , robotics and action-conditioned simulation ( Yang et al., 2023 ; Zhou et al., 2024 ; Gao et al., 2026a ; NVIDIA et al., 2026 ; Jang et al., 2025 ) , and controllable game generation ( Bruce et al., 2024 ; Feng et al., 2026 ; Li et al., 2025 ; Valevski et al., 2025 ; Che et al., 2024 ; Tang et al., 2025 ) . Hy-Nomas pursues the video-based direction, motivated by the scalability and diversity of available video data. Our focus is on turning this visual experience into interactive generation, where user actions shape subsequent observations and the evolving state of the world.

Diffusion models have become a major approach to video generation ( Ho et al., 2022 ; Blattmann et al., 2023b ; Kodaira et al., 2026 ; HaCohen et al., 2025 ; Team Wan et al., 2025 ; Chen et al., 2024b ; Blattmann et al., 2023a ) that extend image diffusion models ( Rombach et al., 2022 ; Saharia et al., 2022 ; Ramesh et al., 2022 ; Dai et al., 2023 ; Betker et al., 2023 ; Podell et al., 2023 ; OpenAI, 2025 ; Black Forest Labs et al., 2025 ; Esser et al., 2024 ; Cao et al., 2025 ) to temporally coherent video synthesis through spatiotemporal modeling and joint image–video training. Diffusion Transformers ( Peebles and Xie, 2023 ) establish a scalable backbone for latent diffusion, while Sora ( Brooks et al., 2024 ) and MovieGen ( Polyak et al., 2024 ) illustrate the potential of large-scale video generation using spacetime latent patches. Seedance ( Team Seedance and others, 2026 ) further advances visual quality and the commercial potential of video generation. In comparison, HunyuanVideo 1.5 ( Wu et al., 2025 ) , the backbone of our model, provides an efficient 8B-parameter foundation that balances visual quality with computational accessibility. Beyond fixed-length generation, Diffusion Forcing ( Chen et al., 2024a ) combines causal sequence prediction with independently sampled noise levels, supporting flexible generation horizons. Self Forcing ( Huang et al., 2026 ) addresses the training–inference mismatch in autoregressive video diffusion by training on model-generated histories with KV caching. Building on these directions, Hy-Nomas adapts a pretrained video generator to interactive, segment-wise generation. Its temporally aligned captions, and segment-wise and action-aware attention connect user instructions to specific portions of the rollout, while causal KV caching retains context across successive interactions.

Video-based world models extend video generation toward action-conditioned, interactive environments ( Hong et al., 2025 ; Sun et al., 2025 ) . Research in this area emphasizes several complementary dimensions, including physical plausibility for physical AI, responsive controllability, and persistent world state. Beyond generating visually convincing sequences, an interactive world model must respond to actions introduced during a rollout and preserve their consequences in subsequent observations.

Genie ( Bruce et al., 2024 ) learns controllable environments from videos without ground-truth action annotations through a latent action model and autoregressive dynamics. Genie 2 ( Google DeepMind, 2024 ) and Genie 3 ( Google DeepMind, 2025 ) further develop action-controllable environments, with Genie 3 emphasizing real-time interaction and improved visual consistency. LingBot-World v2 ( Gao et al., 2026b ) explores long-horizon causal generation, more diverse action-control events, and agentic control through pilot and director components. HappyOyster ( Happy Oyster Team, 2026 ) provides interactive world creation through Directing and Wandering modes, which we evaluate for text interaction and camera navigation, respectively.

These approaches share challenges in responsive control, physical plausibility, spatial memory, and efficient long-horizon generation. Within this direction, Hy-Nomas focuses on native alternation between free-form textual instructions and continuous camera control in one 8B model. It combines temporally structured supervision, persistent causal context, and a VLM-based director that translates user intent into segment-level prompts and generation durations. Our evaluation examines text interaction, camera control, interleaved control, and spatial memory separately to characterize these complementary capabilities.


### 9 Discussion and Future Directions

Gaming and robotics place different demands on world models. For gaming, a practical challenge is how quickly the model responds to a player’s input. Turning the camera or issuing an action should produce a visual response with minimal delay, while repeated interactions should preserve scene consistency. Distillation can reduce the number of denoising steps ( Yin et al., 2024a ; Zhao et al., 2026 ; Liu et al., 2026 ) , and quantization can reduce memory use and computational cost ( Chen et al., 2026 ; Feng et al., 2025 ) . These gains must be evaluated alongside visual quality and consistency over long rollouts ( Xu et al., 2026b ; Xu et al., 2026a ) . For robotics, the challenge extends beyond generating a plausible response: the predicted outcome must match the effect of an executable robot action ( Zhu et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2026c ) . For example, a grasp prediction should reflect whether the gripper makes contact with an object and successfully lifts it. World-action models such as DreamZero ( Ye et al., 2026 ) jointly predict actions and future visual states, providing a potential route toward this capability. Adapting Hy-Nomas to robotic control would require training on videos paired with robot actions and evaluating whether its predictions support successful task execution. Datasets such as Open X-Embodiment ( Khazatsky et al., 2024 ; Open X-Embodiment Collaboration, 2024 ) provide a starting point, although coverage across tasks, environments, and robot embodiments remains limited. As an initial step in this direction, we demonstrate LoRA-based fine tuning on robotics adaptation.

Long-horizon world generation requires remembering previously observed scenes to maintain consistency when users revisit them. Retaining the full KV cache on the GPU becomes increasingly costly as rollouts grow. One possible approach is to offload older context to CPU memory or persistent storage and selectively retrieve information relevant to the next segment ( Yu et al., 2025a ; Yu et al., 2026 ) . When a user returns to a room, for example, the system could retrieve earlier views of that room rather than attend to the entire interaction history. WorldMem ( Xiao et al., 2025 ) demonstrates the value of retrieving historical observations for consistency when revisiting scenes.

Different types of information may benefit from different memory representations: geometry can encode spatial relationships, reference images can preserve visual appearance, and text can summarize events and state changes, such as a door being opened or an object being moved Huang et al. (2025b) . In our spatial-memory experiments, explicitly describing the expected scene content when revisiting a location can improve consistency. An agent could use this mechanism to specify which objects should reappear, where they should be, and what has changed since the previous visit, while visual references could help preserve their precise appearance. Cached keys and values offer another form of memory by retaining features that the generator can directly reuse, although managing their GPU memory footprint requires offloading, compression, or eviction. A practical agentic system would coordinate these memory representations, retrieve relevant information, and update stored records as the world evolves, ensuring that outdated observations do not override the current scene state.

Retrieval alone, however, cannot account for changes that occur outside the field of view. Consider a wall clock: after the user leaves a room and returns ten minutes later, the clock should remain in the same place with the same appearance, but display a time ten minutes later. Reusing an earlier image would recover its appearance without updating its time. An agent could instead record the clock’s identity, location, and observed time, compute its updated state from elapsed simulation time, and pass that state together with a reference image to the generator. Inspired by memory management in language-model agents ( Packer et al., 2023 ) , this approach would expose memory retrieval, state updates, prompt rewriting, and video generation as tools. LingBot-World v2 ( Gao et al., 2026b ) provides an encouraging example of agent coordination for interactive world generation. An important next step is to test whether such coordination can maintain explicit world states and render them faithfully over long interactions.

Despite having only 8B parameters, Hy-Nomas achieves stronger text-driven action control than the larger models evaluated in our benchmark. We hypothesize that accurate, detailed captions contribute to this result by explicitly describing changes over time, which reduces the burden on the diffusion transformer to "imagine" unspecified content and allows it to devote more capacity to just rendering the described scenes and actions correctly. This hypothesis is consistent with findings that improved captions strengthen prompt following in image generation ( Betker et al., 2023 ) . These observations suggest a potential direction for future systems: using highly detailed prompts to specify scene content and temporal evolution, with diffusion models serving primarily as renderers of these descriptions. Such an approach places greater demands on the quality and specificity of both training captions and inference-time prompts, making caption and Prompt Enhancer central challenges. Data curation should therefore consider not only visual quality but also whether the caption accurately describes the video. For example, a clip containing motion blur during a rapid camera turn may still provide useful supervision if the caption describes both the camera motion and the resulting blur. Similarly, explicitly captioning a cut or an editing effect may reduce ambiguity about the observed transition.

Higher resolution and longer temporal context increase the number of video tokens, raising training costs, inference latency, and KV-cache memory requirements. Efficient attention, sequence parallelism, and cache management are therefore critical to extending rollouts and enabling interactive generation. These infrastructure advances also make it more practical to explore larger world models. Recent progress in video generation, exemplified by Seedance 2.0 ( Team Seedance and others, 2026 ) , motivates further investigation into the relationship between model capacity and generation quality. MAGI-2 Preview ( Sand.ai, 2026 ) explores this direction through a mixture-of-experts architecture (MoE), scaling total capacity while limiting the number of parameters activated per token. A central question is whether such investments translate into better world-model capabilities, including actions following, predicting physically consistent outcomes, and preserving scene details when revisiting locations after long rollouts. These capabilities require direct evaluation and cannot be inferred from improvements in visual quality alone.
