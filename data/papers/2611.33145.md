## Lost on Campus : Evaluating Embodied Spatial Reasoning of Vision-Language Models in the Wild

Embodied Spatial Reasoning (ESR) is central to deploying Vision-Language Models (VLMs) in real-world embodied tasks, yet remains inadequately evaluated. Existing benchmarks primarily assess spatial reasoning in static images or synthetic indoor environments, where semantic shortcuts often suffice, making it difficult to faithfully evaluate spatial reasoning capabilities in the wild. In light of this, we present Lost on Campus , a benchmark for evaluating ESR in large-scale real-world outdoor 3D environments reconstructed by 3D Gaussian Splatting. Our benchmark introduces a unified reasoning-action evaluation framework that seamlessly integrates diagnostic QA for isolated reasoning with closed-loop interactive navigation for active reasoning under multimodal instructions. To enable fine-grained diagnosis, we evaluate six capability aspects of ESR: action grounding, spatial foresight, metric awareness, goal-directed planning, ego-allocentric localization, and spatio-temporal consistency. Extensive experiments reveal that: (i) Compared to indoor settings, real-world outdoor environments impose substantially higher demands on spatial reasoning, where existing models exhibit poor performance; (ii) VLMs still struggle with fine-grained visual alignment, with failures in spatial foresight and ego-allocentric localization emerging as primary bottlenecks; (iii) Improving multimodal interaction capabilities and long-term spatial reasoning is crucial for advancing embodied intelligence. These findings highlight that faithful evaluation of ESR demands benchmarks tightly coupling perception, reasoning and action within realistic and continuous environments.

### 1 Introduction

Spatial reasoning is a foundational capability for Vision-Language Models (VLMs) [ 1 , 2 , 5 , 8 , 16 , 18 , 24 , 27 , 33 , 43 , 46 , 47 , 58 ] that aspire to perceive, navigate, and interact with the physical world. While recent VLMs have demonstrated remarkable proficiency across a spectrum of visual understanding tasks, a critical question remains: how well can they truly reason about space, particularly in embodied settings where reasoning must translate into action?

Prior works have examined complementary slices of spatial reasoning, yet primarily confine their scope to static images or passive scenes [ 51 , 53 ] . Recent benchmarks have evolved to incorporate multi-view images to capture viewpoint changes [ 12 , 50 ] , or video inputs that evaluate spatio-temporal understanding capabilities [ 25 ] . While these efforts advance the evaluation of spatial relationships, they predominantly treat the model as a passive observer. In other words, they assess whether a model can comprehend a spatial problem from isolated visual observations, leaving its ability to act spatially within a continuous environment largely untested.

Embodied benchmarks and their associated simulators provide interactive environments to address this problem, but they concentrate overwhelmingly on indoor scenes ( e.g. , Habitat [ 36 ] , AI2-THOR [ 22 ] ). However, indoor settings inherently exhibit high semantic density within spatially constrained layouts. In such environments, models can often avoid authentic spatial reasoning by exploiting structural priors or semantic shortcuts, relying on conceptual associations and semantic pattern matching ( e.g. , locating a refrigerator implies proximity to a kitchen) rather than actual geometric understanding. While semantically sparse outdoor scenes help decouple the evaluation of spatial reasoning, existing benchmarks still lack realism or continuity. For instance, synthetic platforms like EmbodiedCity [ 11 ] fail to capture real-world fidelity, while street-view datasets such as Touchdown [ 7 ] are restricted to discrete topological graphs that preclude continuous actions and fine-grained geometric reasoning. Consequently, a unified evaluation that tightly couples egocentric spatial perception with embodied interaction in realistic and continuous environments remains absent.

To fill this gap, we present Lost on Campus , a benchmark for evaluating ESR in large-scale real-world outdoor 3D environments. As shown in Figure 1 , we reconstruct diverse regions of a university campus using Octree-based Gaussian Splatting [ 20 , 35 ] , yielding a photorealistic, view-consistent and continuously navigable simulation platform. It supports long-horizon closed-loop interaction with high-fidelity egocentric observations for embodied evaluation in real-world open spaces.

Built on this environment, we introduce a unified reasoning-action evaluation framework for VLMs, consisting of two complementary paradigms. First, Closed-Loop Interactive Navigation evaluates reasoning in action, aiming to assess whether VLMs can effectively translate spatial observations and reasoning into coherent embodied behavior. We further evaluate navigation under three distinct forms of spatial instruction to probe VLMs’ spatial reasoning ability in action. Second, diagnostic QA evaluates spatial reasoning in isolation through six diagnostic tasks targeting different aspects of ESR. Unlike traditional spatial QA on static and disconnected images, our diagnostic protocol is automatically generated from the navigation rollouts across models. Grounded in embodied context, it maps end-to-end task execution back to underlying ESR capabilities, enabling fine-grained evaluation.

We summarize our contributions as follows.

- • Outdoor real-world evaluation environment. We construct a photorealistic and continuous outdoor 3DGS campus environment for embodied VLM evaluation, supporting high-fidelity rendering and long-horizon interactive navigation.

- • Unified reasoning-action evaluation framework. We evaluate VLMs through closed-loop interactive navigation and diagnostic QA, combining reasoning in isolation with reasoning in action for fine-grained analysis of ESR capabilities.

- • Empirical findings on outdoor ESR. We comprehensively benchmark representative open- and closed-source VLMs, show that outdoor ESR remains under-solved, identify spatial foresight and ego-allocentric localization as primary bottlenecks for embodied navigation.

### 2 Related Work


##### Spatial reasoning evaluation for VLMs.

Early evaluations of VLM spatial reasoning focused on reasoning within static single-image scenes, bounding the assessment to qualitative relational understanding [ 9 , 19 , 26 , 34 , 37 , 40 , 42 , 48 , 55 ] . Recent benchmarks have extended spatial evaluation with multi-view images, videos, and 3D annotations [ 12 , 54 , 56 , 13 , 53 , 59 , 60 ] . These efforts introduce richer spatial cues, including viewpoint changes, temporal continuity, depth and 3D geometry across indoor scenes, autonomous-driving scenarios, object-centric 3D datasets and outdoor stereo videos. Such designs substantially broaden the scope of VLM spatial evaluation beyond single images [ 12 , 25 ] . However, they still largely evaluate models as passive observers over pre-collected visual inputs or annotated geometric cues [ 53 , 12 ] . The model is asked to answer spatial questions, but its judgments are not coupled with embodied decisions or evaluated through their consequences in navigation. In contrast, our trajectory-grounded QA acts as a diagnostic probe generated from navigation trajectories in a large-scale outdoor environment. It situates spatial understanding within closed-loop embodied behavior.


##### Embodied navigation benchmarks.

Planning and navigating through unknown environments remain fundamental challenges for embodied agents. Recent benchmarks evaluate VLMs as high-level decision makers tasked with interpreting egocentric observations and following instructions [ 10 , 28 ] . As summarized in Table 1 , most existing efforts [ 21 , 39 , 28 , 31 , 52 , 11 ] primarily focus on increasing instruction complexity. Although these tasks have driven progress in instruction-following, they concentrate on benchmarking the multimodal understanding of language and visual semantics, without systematically assessing spatial perception and reasoning [ 10 , 28 ] . Several recent efforts have advanced toward spatial evaluation. For instance, NavBench [ 31 ] assesses action consequences in spatial understanding; however, it operates within discrete environments, precluding the evaluation of fine-grained egocentric control. Other efforts, such as NavSpace [ 52 ] , advance toward quantitative spatial evaluation in indoor scenes. However, such scenes are limited in spatial scale, semantic diversity and visual variability, hindering evaluation from accurately reflecting real-world conditions.


##### 3D reconstruction and image-based rendering for embodied simulation.

Simulation environments are central to embodied AI, supporting evaluation and scalable training. Indoor environment datasets and simulators [ 6 , 36 ] have provided important foundations for embodied reasoning benchmarks. However, many existing environments are primarily built upon mesh-based scene representations and may lack the visual fidelity needed for fine-grained perception and motion cues. Meanwhile, existing outdoor environments either rely on discrete panorama graphs [ 7 ] or synthetic rendering engines [ 11 ] , leaving realistic and continuous outdoor embodied evaluation less explored. Recent progress in large-scale 3D reconstruction and novel view synthesis, particularly 3DGS [ 20 ] , introduces promising solutions to create photorealistic replicas from video captures. However, large-scale reconstructed environments that combine photorealistic appearance, metric consistency, and continuous outdoor interaction remain limited. Such environments can complement existing indoor and synthetic or graph-based outdoor platforms, and support broader evaluation of VLMs under realistic egocentric perception and embodied interaction.

### 3 The Lost on Campus Benchmark


#### 3.1 The 3D Outdoor Environment

Data Collection. The Lost on Campus environment is constructed from real-world, large-scale campus captures using Insta360 X4 cameras. The collected panorama frames are split into four perspective images, each with a $120 ​ °$ field of view (FoV). These four frames together cover the horizontal $360 ​ °$ FoV around the camera. Overall, this dataset contains over 50k calibrated video frames on fifteen academic halls and road intersections, covering an area of approximately $2\times 10^{5}~\mathrm{m}^{2}$ .

While frames within a single video are easier to calibrate, frames between videos introduce challenges, e.g. , through inconsistent lighting, visual ambiguities, etc. To achieve accurate metric-scale camera calibration across 50k video frames, we use a mobile RTK device to capture centimeter-accurate GPS coordinates for each image. This allows us to (1) perform dense 3D reconstruction on regions where multiple videos overlap, and (2) introduce consistent metric-scale reconstruction, which is strictly required for evaluating VLMs on distance-aware spatial understanding.

Photorealistic 3D Reconstruction. For photorealistic 3D reconstruction and rendering, we employ a modified Octree-GS [ 35 ] with (1) appearance and transient embeddings to account for lighting and transient variations and (2) a triangle primitive [ 17 ] for better geometry recovery and collision detection. To avoid sky floaters, the sky is independently modeled with a neural field through sky-masking. Extended reconstruction details are provided in Appendix A.1 . We further analyze reconstruction fidelity and the potential effect of rendering artifacts on downstream benchmark evaluation in Appendix B .

Environment Characteristics. As illustrated in Figure 2 , the constructed environment comprises 15 distinct outdoor scenes, categorized into two tiers of topological complexity. Intersection-level environments (4 scenes) are reconstructed from three-way or four-way junctions, focusing on evaluating a VLM’s spatial decision-making. Building-level environments (11 scenes) are reconstructed from images surrounding a building. Quantitatively, each scene features an average 3D reconstructed area of approximately 12.7k m 2 , with a navigable area of around 6.5k m 2 .


#### 3.2 Benchmark Construction


##### Overview.

As shown in Figure 1 , Lost on Campus evaluates embodied spatial reasoning along two complementary axes: closed-loop interactive navigation and diagnostic spatial QA. For navigation, we formulate all evaluations as image-goal navigation, where an agent must navigate through the 3DGS environment from a start pose to a goal depicted by an image. For diagnostic QA, we construct a navigation-grounded QA protocol where questions are generated from the agent’s completed navigation trajectories within our 3DGS environment, grounding spatial reasoning evaluation in embodied experience. This automated annotation-free design enables scalable evaluation across models and allows direct investigation of the relationship between navigation performance and underlying spatial understanding.


##### Navigation case generation.

We construct navigation cases through an automated pipeline that samples start–goal camera pose pairs from the raw reconstruction trajectories. In the general setting , we generate 100 cases per scene (1,500 cases in total across 15 scenes), with start–goal distances uniformly distributed in the range of 5–25 m. This setting provides a standardized evaluation of image-goal navigation. To further investigate the spatial reasoning capabilities of VLMs in complex scenarios and under richer spatial cues, we construct an additional instruction setting comprising 255 challenging cases, including complex scenarios such as long-distance navigation and intersections, to evaluate how VLMs integrate various forms of spatial information into embodied actions.


##### Diagnostic spatial QA.

Beyond end-to-end navigation, we design a fine-grained diagnostic QA protocol comprising six tasks that probe different aspects of ESR. The QA data is automatically constructed from the navigation outputs of different VLM models. Each task contains 1,000 questions, yielding a unified evaluation set of 6,000 QA pairs in total. The six diagnostic tasks, illustrated in Figure 2 , are defined as follows.

- 1. Action Grounding. Given a sequence of $N$ consecutive egocentric observation images, the VLM is asked to output the $N{-}1$ primitive actions that produced the observed transitions. This task evaluates whether the model can infer ego-motion from visual changes.

- 2. Spatial Foresight. Given the current observation image and a sequence of primitive actions, the VLM must select the correct post-action observation from a set of candidate images. This task probes whether the model can mentally simulate the visual consequence of a given action sequence, demonstrating implicit world modeling capabilities.

- 3. Metric Awareness. Given a goal image and a sequence of egocentric observations captured at varying distances to the goal, the VLM is asked to identify the closest or farthest frame, or to judge the overall movement trend (approaching vs. receding). This task evaluates the model’s sensitivity to metric distance changes from visual cues.

- 4. Goal-directed Planning. Given the current observation and a goal image, the VLM must select the correct intermediate subgoal image from candidates, corresponding to a viewpoint that lies along the path to the goal. This task assesses the model’s ability to decompose a navigation objective into spatial sub-plans.

- 5. Ego-allocentric Localization. Given the current egocentric observation and a bird’s-eye-view (BEV) map, the VLM must identify its own position on the BEV map. Alternatively, given a goal image and its own marked position on the BEV, the model needs to localize the goal on the map. This task evaluates perspective-to-allocentric spatial transformation and self-localization.

- 6. Spatio-temporal Consistency. Given a sequence of egocentric observation images with a specific object highlighted in the first frame, the VLM is asked to determine whether the object remains visible in the final frame, and the object’s movement trend or spatial position relative to the agent after the full motion sequence, even when the object is no longer visible and the model must reason about its position from memory. This task probes the model’s ability to maintain a coherent spatial representation of tracked objects under ego-motion.


### 4 Experiments and Analysis

We evaluate a set of proprietary and open-source foundation models: (1) Proprietary models include Gemini-3-Flash [ 14 ] and GPT-5-Mini [ 38 ] . (2) Open-source models include Qwen3.5-9B/27B [ 32 ] , Qwen3-VL-8B/32B-Instruct [ 4 ] , Gemma-4-31B [ 44 ] and InternVL3.5-8B [ 47 ] .

Implementation Details. The reconstructed 3DGS environments support continuous camera motion, allowing observations to be rendered from arbitrary camera poses. The agent state is represented by a continuous position and orientation, with each primitive action updating the camera pose by the corresponding metric translation or rotation before rendering the next observation. For evaluation, we control the agent through a discrete set of metric action primitives, where each action applies a fixed translation or rotation to the current camera pose. At each navigation step, the agent receives RGB observations from three egocentric directions: front, left ( $+90^{\circ}$ ), and right ( $-90^{\circ}$ ). The resolution of each visual observation is set to $512\times 512$ . The action space consists of a set of primitive actions, including moving forward or backward by 1 m, turning left or right by $45^{\circ}$ , turning left or right by $15^{\circ}$ , and stopping. For efficiency, the agent is allowed to predict an action sequence containing multiple primitive actions at each decision step. An episode terminates when the agent outputs the stop action, and the maximum episode length is set to 100 steps. Additional implementation details are provided in Appendix C .

Metrics. For multiple-choice spatial QA, we use Accuracy as the primary metric. For navigation tasks, we report Success Rate ( SR ), Oracle Success Rate ( OSR ), and Success weighted by Path Length ( SPL ) under both 2.5 m and 5.0 m success thresholds. SR measures whether the agent stops within the goal region, OSR measures whether the agent ever reaches the goal region during the trajectory, and SPL accounts for path efficiency among successful episodes. We further report auxiliary navigation metrics averaged over trajectories: Distance to Goal ( DTG ), final-view similarity ( Sim. ), progress score ( Prog. ), trajectory length ( Traj. ), inference requests ( Calls ), and primitive actions ( Actions ). DTG measures the final distance from the agent to the goal after termination. Sim. measures the image similarity using DINOv2 [ 29 ] between the agent’s final front view and the goal image. Prog. measures the average effective distance gained per step toward the goal. Traj. records the total traveled distance. Calls counts the number of inference requests. Actions counts the total number of executed primitive actions, where each inference request may return multiple primitive actions.

Outdoor ESR remains severely under-solved. Even the strongest model, Gemini-3-Flash, attains only 49.60% SR@5 and 25.73% SR@2.5, while no open-source model exceeds 11% SR@2.5. This is far below typical indoor image-goal navigation results, indicating that real-world outdoor embodied spatial reasoning is substantially harder than static image-level spatial understanding, in part because outdoor scenes lack the strong indoor semantic shortcuts on which VLMs often rely.

Models often approach the goal but fail to localize and stop correctly. Across nearly all models, we observe a large gap between SR@2.5 and SR@5 and also a wide gap between OSR and SR: the agent frequently passes near the target but fails to terminate at the correct location. This is most extreme for InternVL3.5-8B, which attains a relatively high OSR@5 of 48.60% yet collapses to only 5.33% SR@2.5. Applying post-hoc 3DGS pose optimization at the agent’s final stop (Table 3 ) recovers $+1.94$ to $+10.00$ SR@2.5 across models, confirming that endpoint localization, goal verification, and self-aware stopping are dominant failure modes.

Progress and efficiency are not sufficient for success. Strong intermediate behavior does not guarantee successful termination. Qwen3.5-27B records the highest SPL@5 (31.91%, exceeding Gemini-3-Flash’s 31.16%), the lowest DTG (9.48 m), and the highest progress score (Prog $=$ 0.51), yet its SR@2.5 (9.93%) trails Gemini-3-Flash by more than 15 points. This shows that efficient movement must be coupled with reliable goal recognition and spatial state estimation; otherwise, agents that traverse the environment efficiently still fail to commit to the correct stop.

#### 4.2 Diagnostic Capability Breakdown

We evaluate the same VLMs on the diagnostic spatial QA protocol introduced in Section 3.2 , and use them as complementary diagnostic probes of spatial capabilities involved in the embodied perception–action loop.

Spatial capabilities exhibit highly non-uniform performance. Averaged across the eight evaluated models, the six tasks form a clear difficulty hierarchy: Action Grounding (64.9%) and Goal-directed Planning (63.9%) are relatively stronger, while Spatial Foresight (49.8%) and Ego-allocentric Localization (39.6%, only $\sim$ 15 points above 4-way chance) remain consistently weak. Even the strongest model, Gemini-3-Flash, scores only 41.7% on Ego-allocentric Localization, and Spatial Foresight degrades sharply for smaller models (down to 30.5% for Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct).

Controlled intervention on localization. To examine whether localization-related errors materially affect navigation performance, we conduct controlled oracle interventions on Qwen3.5-27B using a fixed scene-balanced subset of 150 episodes. We progressively introduce spatial information, including the BEV map, oracle goal position, and oracle agent pose (position and heading), as summarized in Table 5 . Compared with the original baseline, the (b) BEV-only, (c) Position-only, and (d) Goal-only settings show limited SR improvements. In contrast, (e) Full Oracle, which directly provides oracle goal position, agent position, and heading on the BEV, without requiring the model to infer them, yields +11.34 SR@2.5, +10.00 SR@5, and +20.67 OSR@5 over (a) Original baseline. These gains estimate the potential navigation improvement if the model were able to infer these quantities accurately. Together with the consistently low Ego-allocentric Localization QA scores across models, these results indicate that localization-related errors are an important contributor to navigation failure.

Controlled intervention on spatial foresight. We further examine whether access to action-conditioned future observations improves navigation performance, using the same Qwen3.5-27B model and the same scene-balanced 150-episode subset. At each decision step, the model proposes multiple candidate action sequences, and the environment renders the true future observation resulting from each candidate. We then select the executed candidate using three strategies: (b) DINOv2 similarity between the rendered future observations and the goal image, (c) the VLM directly inspecting and reasoning over the rendered future observations, and (d) an oracle selecting the candidate with the shortest distance to the goal. The results are summarized in Table 6 . The results show that navigation performance improves substantially when action-conditioned future observations are provided. Together with the consistently weak Spatial Foresight QA performance, this suggests that foresight-related limitations contribute to closed-loop navigation failures. We additionally analyze potential shortcuts in both diagnostic QA and navigation, including linguistic and label-distribution biases as well as simple exploration and visual-matching baselines; see Appendix D.1 .

Diagnostic QA and navigation therefore provide complementary views of ESR. Diagnostic QA isolates capability-specific weaknesses under controlled conditions, whereas closed-loop navigation evaluates whether these capabilities can be integrated consistently over a sequence of perception–action decisions. The agreement between diagnostic patterns and navigation behavior, together with the controlled interventions above, supports the use of the two evaluation modes as complementary components of the Lost on Campus benchmark.

We ablate three components of our navigation prompt template on Qwen3.5-27B: spatial reasoning Principles , structured chain-of-thought Navigation CoT , and explicit Thought output field (Table 7 ).

All three components contribute, with distinct roles in success and efficiency. Removing the Principles causes the largest SR drop ( $-6.74$ to 27.33%) and the shortest trajectory (11.09 m), indicating that without spatial reasoning guidance the agent terminates prematurely. Removing Navigation CoT yields moderate degradation in both SR ( $-2.80$ ) and SPL ( $-3.69$ ), showing that step-level reasoning supports more reliable action selection. Removing the Thought field causes only a small SR drop ( $-2.87$ ) but a catastrophic collapse in efficiency: SPL falls by $10.45$ points, DTG rises to 14.25 m, and trajectory length explodes to 48.82 m ( $>3\times$ baseline). Without explicit reasoning before action, the agent wanders extensively before occasionally stumbling onto the goal. Together, these results justify all three components: Principles guide goal-directed termination, Navigation CoT supports reliable per-step decisions and the Thought field is essential for path efficiency.

Embodied spatial reasoning requires more than recognizing spatial relations; it requires using spatial information to guide continuous action under evolving egocentric observations. This suggests that larger VLMs benefit more from explicit visual spatial structure than from language-only route descriptions in embodied spatial reasoning. ### 5 Conclusion

We introduced Lost on Campus , a large-scale real-world outdoor benchmark for evaluating Embodied Spatial Reasoning in VLMs. By combining a photorealistic and continuously navigable 3DGS campus environment with closed-loop interactive navigation and diagnostic spatial QA, our benchmark evaluates both spatial reasoning in action and fine-grained spatial understanding. Extensive experiments show that current VLMs remain limited in outdoor ESR, with spatial foresight and ego-allocentric localization emerging as key bottlenecks. Results under spatially guided navigation further reveal a persistent gap between spatial instruction and action execution.

Limitations . While Lost on Campus advances the evaluation of embodied spatial reasoning in realistic outdoor settings, several limitations remain. All scenes are reconstructed from a single university campus using static captures, which may not fully represent the geographic, architectural, and dynamic diversity of real-world outdoor environments ( e.g. , urban streets, changing weather, moving pedestrians). The current action space consists of predefined primitive actions that enable reproducible evaluation but do not capture the continuous motor control required by real robotic platforms. Since the environments are reconstructed using 3DGS, they may still exhibit inherent differences from the physical world, including view-synthesis artifacts and missing dynamic elements. We empirically analyze reconstruction fidelity and the effect of rendering artifacts in Appendix B . Finally, the diagnostic QA protocol is automatically generated from navigation rollouts, which enables scalability but may introduce biases tied to the specific trajectories used for construction.

Broader Impact . We view Lost on Campus as a positive contribution to AI safety: by providing a rigorous, fine-grained benchmark for outdoor embodied spatial reasoning, it enables the community to precisely identify where current VLMs fall short, such as in spatial foresight and self-localization, before these models are deployed in safety-critical settings like autonomous navigation or assistive robotics. Rather than serving solely as a cautionary tool, the benchmark offers a constructive pathway toward more reliable embodied agents through targeted, capability-level evaluation. We note that all visual data undergo strict anonymization to remove personally identifiable information, and that the large-scale 3DGS reconstruction and VLM evaluation entail substantial computational costs. The benchmark release is restricted to academic research under responsible-use guidelines to mitigate potential misuse.

### Appendix A The Lost on Campus Benchmark Construction Details


#### A.1 Environment Reconstruction


##### Data Sanitization and Privacy Compliance

To ensure the visual fidelity of the reconstructed simulation and prevent the introduction of training artifacts, we perform a systematic filtering of the projected perspective images prior to reconstruction. Specifically, the bottom-facing projections, which inevitably capture the static human operator during the data collection process, are entirely discarded. We follow a strict data anonymization protocol across the entire dataset. All collected visual frames are reviewed to identify and obfuscate Personally Identifiable Information (PII). All captures were collected in publicly accessible outdoor areas with approval, and faces and license plates are detected and obfuscated before reconstruction and release.


##### Environment Reconstruction

Regarding the topological scope of the reconstructed scenes, our pipeline is designed to ensure geometric consistency across different evaluation scales. As introduced in Section 3.1 , Building-level environments are generated by utilizing the comprehensive set of images captured along the entire perimeter of large architectural structures. These extensive reconstructions provide the facility to evaluate VLM agents on complex, long-horizon spatial instructions. To facilitate localized evaluations and create smaller-scale environments ( e.g. , specific intersections or localized pathways), rather than independently training separate 3DGS models for these smaller zones, we directly extract and isolate the relevant sub-regions from those larger-scale reconstructions.


#### A.2 Navigation Case Construction

We automatically construct the benchmark from the training camera trajectories of each 3D Gaussian Splatting scene. The construction procedure is as follows.

Candidate pair generation. We enumerate candidate start–goal view pairs from the training trajectory and retain only those whose 3D Euclidean distance lies between 5 m and 25 m. To avoid large elevation changes, we further require the vertical displacement between the two views, measured along the local up direction of the start view, to be no greater than 1 m.

Distance-stratified sampling. From the valid candidate pool, we sample the final evaluation pairs using distance-stratified sampling. Specifically, we partition the 5–25 m start–goal distance range into 20 equal-width intervals of 1 m each, and select 100 start–goal pairs so that the number of sampled pairs is approximately balanced across intervals. This reduces bias toward any particular travel distance and ensures a more even coverage of the full 5–25 m distance range. By default, each view is used at most once across all selected pairs, preventing repeated reuse of the same camera observation in different benchmark instances. After sampling, the direction of each selected pair is randomized with probability 0.5, so either endpoint may serve as the starting view.

Pose perturbation and geometric constraints. For each selected endpoint, we generate a start viewpoint by perturbing the original camera pose and re-rendering the scene from the perturbed pose. In our setting, the camera orientation is randomly rotated around its local up axis, with the rotation angle sampled uniformly from $[-180^{\circ},180^{\circ}]$ . We do not apply additional horizontal translation perturbation. To further reduce height variation, we project each goal position onto the horizontal plane of the corresponding start position so that the start and goal share the same elevation. In addition, the goal camera is roughly oriented in the direction from the start to the goal (within $\pm 90^{\circ}$ ), which excludes degenerate cases in which the goal view is opposite to the intended navigation direction.


#### A.3 Spatial QA Construction

> Table 9 : Organization of the six diagnostic probes. The probes are organized along three aspects of the embodied perception–action loop.

[Tableau] ESR Aspect Diagnostic Probe Detail Coupling actions to observations Action Grounding Relating visual transitions to the agent’s own actions Spatial Foresight Predicting future visual changes induced by actions Maintaining spatial state during movement Metric Awareness Estimating relative distance and spatial progress Ego-allocentric Localization Anchoring egocentric observations in a global spatial frame Spatio-temporal Consistency Maintaining a consistent representation of spatial relations under ego-motion Relating observations and spatial states to goals Goal-directed Planning Inferring implicit route structure from observations and reasoning about spatial relations among current, intermediate, and goal viewpoints

As shown in Table 9 , we organize the six diagnostic tasks along the embodied perception–action loop. Closed-loop embodied spatial reasoning requires the agent to (i) couple actions to observations, (ii) maintain spatial state during movement, and (iii) relate observations and spatial states to goals. Accordingly, the six tasks are designed as complementary diagnostic probes spanning these aspects and different levels of analysis, rather than as an exhaustive or orthogonal decomposition of ESR.

We automatically construct the diagnostic spatial QA protocol from navigation rollouts produced by different VLM agents. Following Section 3.2 , the protocol decomposes embodied spatial reasoning into six diagnostic tasks, each targeting a distinct spatial capability. For each task, we first collect candidate samples from rollout trajectories under task-specific temporal and geometric constraints, then generate the correct answer together with plausible distractors, and finally subsample the resulting pool to balance scenes, source agents, and rollout cases.


##### Action Grounding.

We parse each rollout into contiguous segments of valid primitive actions and aligned camera poses, and extract short windows of consecutive observations. A sample with $N$ observation frames is paired with the corresponding $N-1$ ground-truth primitive actions between adjacent frames. We only retain windows in which every transition between adjacent observations is associated with a valid primitive action and a temporally continuous, renderable pose sequence.


##### Spatial Foresight.

We sample a start observation and a short action sequence from a rollout trajectory, and use the true post-action observation reached after executing the sequence as the correct answer. Each sample is converted into a four-way multiple-choice question by adding three distractors. The distractors are generated from three sources: an action sequence obtained by modifying a single action in the ground-truth sequence, a real pose from another step of the same rollout, and a random action sequence of the same length. All distractors are filtered to remain sufficiently separated from the ground-truth future pose and from one another.


##### Metric Awareness.

We construct a scene-level pose pool and sample a goal pose as the distance reference. Each candidate observation is associated with its camera-center distance to the goal. We then generate two forms of questions. In trend questions, the sampled distances must satisfy one of four strict patterns: approaching, receding, approaching-then-receding, or receding-then-approaching. In extremum-selection questions, the model is asked to identify the closest or farthest frame in the sequence. Candidate sequences are further filtered by distance-gap, spatial-continuity, and view-consistency constraints.


##### Goal-directed Planning.

We operate on the reconstructed camera trajectory of each scene and enumerate initial-goal pairs under distance and temporal-gap constraints. For each valid pair, the correct subgoal is sampled from the middle portion of the trajectory interval between the initial and goal viewpoints. Distractors are sampled from outside this interval while remaining spatially close to the endpoints. Additional directional constraints are imposed so that distractors remain visually plausible without lying on the true intermediate route.


##### Ego-allocentric Localization.

We combine rollout poses with the original scene trajectory to generate BEV-based localization questions. We first identify valid initial-goal pairs from rollout trajectories under distance and directional consistency constraints, and then instantiate two task variants. In initial localization , the correct option is the true initial position, while the distractors are drawn from one rollout pose and two scene-trajectory poses. In goal localization , the correct option is the true goal position, while the distractors are drawn from one rollout pose, one scene-trajectory pose, and one local perturbation around the goal. All candidate markers are required to remain spatially separated on the map.


##### Spatio-temporal Consistency.

We extract short consecutive observation windows from rollout trajectories. For each window, we render the first frame and detect a salient object, represented by a semantic tag and a 2D bounding box. Using the first-frame depth map, we estimate the object’s 3D world position and project it through the full motion sequence. This process yields three labels: whether the object remains visible in the final frame, its relative direction with respect to the final camera, and its apparent horizontal motion pattern across the sequence. The final relative direction is discretized into eight camera-centric sectors. Depending on whether the horizontal motion signal is sufficiently clear, each sample is instantiated as either a visibility-and-motion question or a visibility-and-position question.

#### C.2 Prompt Template for Navigation Task

At each navigation step, we use structured prompting to obtain the model’s action decision. The system prompt specifies the required output format, which includes a thought field and an action field. VLMs struggle to convert linguistic spatial relations into interactive action execution. Adding textual spatial instruction, which we hope to probe the agent’s ability to convert egocentric linguistic spatial relations into embodied interaction, leads to limited meaningful gains. Among the models that benefit from this setting, the SR improvement ranges from $+0.78$ to $+2.35$ , as shown in Table 8 . This suggests that VLMs may possess partial static understanding of language-based spatial relations, but such understanding does not reliably transfer to embodied action execution.

Larger VLMs show emerging self-localization and active planning ability. The Goal-Annotated BEV variant measures whether models can use global goal information to support self-localization and active planning during interaction. Compared with Image-Goal Nav. , the Goal-Annotated BEV setting yields the greatest SR improvements for the two larger models. Qwen3.5-27B improves from 3.92% to 8.63%, and Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct improves from 5.88% to 9.02%. It also produces the largest OSR gain for Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct, increasing by $+7.06$ . These gains suggest that larger VLMs have the potential to exploit global spatial structure and self-tracking information, sometimes moving closer to the target even when final success remains limited. Overall, larger VLMs exhibit an emerging ability for self-localization and active planning. Yet absolute success rates remain below 10%, even in this best-case setting, confirming that converting allocentric spatial information into long-horizon embodied action remains the central bottleneck.

Global visual routes remain difficult to execute in embodied interaction. The Visual Route Map variant provides the route as visual annotations, reducing the need to infer a route from scratch. For Qwen3.5-27B, Visual Route Map improves SR from 3.92% to 8.24%, suggesting that route annotations can support embodied navigation for larger models. However, this improvement is not consistent across models: for Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct, SR increases by only $+1.96$ . These results suggest that the bottleneck is not route planning alone, but also the concrete execution of a global route during embodied interaction.
