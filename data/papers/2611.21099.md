## ForceHarness: Turning Force into Action and Physics for Frozen Frontier Models

Frontier multimodal models can now control robots zero-shot from images, yet many manipulation tasks depend on contact forces that images cannot show. Bringing force to an API-only frozen model raises two challenges. First, contact evolves much faster than an API model can decide, so the system must close the loop on force beneath the model. Second, a frozen model cannot interpret raw force streams, so force must be returned in a form the model can reason about. Therefore, we present ForceHarness, a harness that lets frozen models use force without demonstrations or finetuning. After calibrating hand force from a wrist sensor or joint torques alone, ForceHarness addresses the first challenge with self-terminating force primitives that regulate force on their own and return at a contact event. To address the second challenge, a physics translation layer turns each force trace into physical quantities with uncertainty as the tool result. To evaluate ForceHarness against expert force controllers and learned policies under identical conditions, we also build HiddenPhys, a seven-task MuJoCo benchmark with hidden physical variables. On a real arm without a wrist force sensor, ForceHarness raises the zero-shot success rate of GPT-6 Astra from 3.3% to 90.0%. In simulation, it adds 17.5 percentage points on the public ForceBench suite and 34.3 percentage points on HiddenPhys, surpassing force-aware policies trained on demonstrations on both benchmarks and matching hand-written expert controllers on HiddenPhys. Ablations further show that smaller models make significantly better decisions when given physical quantities rather than raw force values.

### 1 Introduction

Frontier multimodal models can now control robots directly without task-specific training. For example, GPT-6 Astra runs behind an API with frozen weights and still controls a robot zero-shot from images through a pose interface [ 43 , 27 ] . Much of this progress comes from the harness that wraps the frozen model and provides semantic action units [ 5 ] , virtual camera views [ 32 ] , agent-written tools and code [ 13 , 17 ] , or low-level VLA skills [ 44 ] . However, existing harnesses mainly help the model see and move.

Beyond seeing and moving, many manipulation tasks in the physical world depend on contact forces that images cannot show. For example, driving a screw requires pressing down while turning until the thread engages, and sorting identical-looking bottles requires comparing their weight. Existing work brings force into such tasks mainly through learning. Specifically, force-aware policies [ 39 , 18 , 15 ] and benchmarks [ 14 ] add wrist force as a network input to visuomotor policies [ 6 ] and vision-language-action (VLA) models [ 24 ] . However, this approach requires demonstrations for every task and updates model weights, so it cannot be applied to an API-only frozen model. Meanwhile, existing harnesses for frozen models that expose contact typically pass raw wrench and tactile values [ 7 , 29 ] or ask the model to generate force-control code [ 4 , 28 ] . Yet these designs still leave the model to parse raw sensor numbers or write low-level control without calibrated physical feedback. We therefore ask: can a frozen frontier model understand force and use it to complete manipulation tasks that cannot be done without it?

Answering this question raises two challenges. First, contact evolves much faster than an API model can decide, so the system must close the loop on force beneath the model through general skills that regulate force on their own under the model’s direction. Second, a frozen model cannot interpret raw force streams or choose actions from raw numbers alone, so the system must return force in a form the model can reason about .

Therefore, we present ForceHarness, a harness that lets a frozen frontier model use wrist force without demonstrations or training. As shown in Figure 1 , ForceHarness places three layers between the model and the robot. At the bottom, a force sensing and calibration layer estimates hand force from a wrist sensor or joint torques alone and removes sensor offsets. In the middle, self-terminating force primitives address the first challenge by turning common contact skills such as guarded moves, sliding, and insertion into callable tools. Once called, each primitive regulates force on its own and returns at a contact event. At the top, a physics translation layer addresses the second challenge by converting each primitive’s force trace into physical quantities with uncertainty estimates and status flags, such as payload mass, contact height, and friction. At each turn, the model calls a primitive, reads the returned physical quantities, and decides what to do next. Together, these three layers form a general set of building blocks that bridges a frozen frontier model and low-level force control.

We evaluate ForceHarness on a real robot arm without a wrist force sensor and across two simulation benchmarks, as summarized in Figure 1 . On the real robot, we design six contact-rich tasks and find that ForceHarness raises the zero-shot success rate of GPT-6 Astra from 3.3% without force to 90.0%. In simulation, the same model with ForceHarness operates from RGB images on the public ForceBench suite [ 14 ] and improves from 70.0% to 87.5%, surpassing the force-aware policies trained on that benchmark. To further compare frozen models with classical controllers and learned force policies under identical conditions, we build HiddenPhys, a seven-task MuJoCo benchmark with hidden physical variables. On HiddenPhys, ForceHarness raises the success rate of GPT-6 Astra from 62.1% to 96.4%, matching hand-written expert force controllers and far exceeding Diffusion Policy [ 6 ] , $\pi_{0.5}$ [ 24 ] , and ForceVLA [ 39 ] trained on about 200 demonstrations per task.

Our contributions are:

- • We propose ForceHarness , a three-layer harness of force calibration, self-terminating force primitives, and physics translation that bridges an API-only frozen model and low-level force control without demonstrations or training.

- • We build HiddenPhys , a seven-task MuJoCo benchmark with hidden physical variables that includes expert force controllers and learned force policies evaluated on identical seeds.

- • Experiments on a real robot and two simulated benchmarks show that ForceHarness lets frozen frontier models make full use of force for contact-rich manipulation, outperforming both training-based methods and the same harness without force.


### 2 Related work


##### Harness for embodied AI.

GPT-6 Astra showed that a frozen frontier model, given images and a pose interface, can act as a zero-shot robot policy [ 43 ] , and systematic evaluations have mapped what such models can and cannot do as embodied policies [ 11 , 27 ] . Following Code as Policies [ 20 ] , in which a language model writes robot programs over control primitives, the harness around the frozen model has become a design variable of its own. Some harnesses change how the model acts, through semantic action units [ 5 ] , a task-agnostic agent harness [ 31 ] , agent-written tools [ 13 ] , or code that runs with no model in the loop [ 17 ] . Others change what it perceives, through calibrated perception tools [ 19 ] or rendered virtual views [ 32 ] . Others add memory of demonstrations and past interaction [ 21 ] , orchestrate learned policies as skills [ 44 , 10 ] , or let a second agent revise the harness itself [ 30 , 36 ] . However, few of these harnesses consider force: most have no force channel, and the rest mainly pass raw readings or a force cap to the agent [ 9 , 8 , 37 , 7 ] . ForceHarness is instead built around force: it regulates force beneath the model and reports it to the model as physical quantities.


##### Force for embodied AI.

Force has been central to manipulation since hybrid position/force control [ 26 ] and impedance control [ 16 ] , and force-torque measurements can identify a payload [ 1 ] or locate a contact [ 2 ] , but such methods are engineered task by task. Learned policies instead take force as a network input [ 15 , 23 , 39 , 18 , 33 , 12 ] , and ForceBench [ 14 ] compares integration designs on $\pi_{0.5}$ ; all of them require demonstrations or simulated training, and weight updates. Language models have also used force at inference time by reading sensed properties as sentences [ 45 ] , generating code with force feedback [ 4 , 22 ] , tuning the impedance of a base policy [ 40 , 41 ] , or choosing recovery actions above a trained skill [ 25 ] . Recent studies give GPT-6 Astra tactile or force readings directly [ 29 , 38 , 35 , 34 , 46 ] , and FORCA [ 28 ] has a coding agent write force-feedback programs and revise them between rollouts. ForceHarness differs in bringing control and perception of force together with no training: primitives regulate force beneath the model, and the model reads calibrated physical quantities after each one.

#### 3.1 Overview

ForceHarness sits between a frozen model and a robot and has three layers, shown in Figure 2 . The bottom layer, force sensing and calibration , turns the robot’s raw signals into a calibrated estimate of the force that the environment applies to the hand. The middle layer is a menu of self-terminating force primitives , contact skills that regulate force on their own and stop at a contact event. The top layer, the physics translation layer , converts the force trace of each primitive into physical quantities with uncertainties.

Because the model is reached only through an API and needs seconds for each decision, force enters the loop twice. Inside a primitive it controls contact at the rate of the controller, and in the tool result it informs the model’s next decision. None of the layers assumes a particular arm, and Sec. A of the supplementary material lists the constants of each robot and simulator.


#### 3.2 Force sensing and calibration

The bottom layer provides one signal to the layers above, the force $F$ that the environment applies to the hand. A wrist force/torque sensor measures it directly. Many arms have no such sensor, so the layer can also recover the force from the external joint torques $\tau_{\mathrm{ext}}$ that a torque-controlled arm estimates. It solves for the wrench $w$ at the flange by least squares, then removes a zero point $b$ and flips the sign,

[Tableau] $$w=(J^{\top})^{+}\,\tau_{\mathrm{ext}},\qquad F=-(w_{F}-b),$$ (1)

where $J$ is the flange Jacobian and $w_{F}$ is the force part of $w$ .

Such an estimate has little noise, but its zero point drifts as the arm moves and changes pose, and no filter removes a bias that changes this slowly. The layer therefore controls when it reads rather than how it filters. It tares at rest , re-zeroing $b$ before each empty-hand primitive so that the zero is taken close to the pose of the coming contact. After each primitive it lets the arm settle, then reads , relaxing the controller onto the measured pose and averaging the force over a short still window. Finally, it reports changes , giving each still reading relative to a reference from the same episode, such as the empty hand or the moment of the grasp, so that the model compares readings instead of trusting absolute values. In simulation, the wrist sensor is instead zeroed at reset and after reorienting the tool.

#### 3.3 Self-terminating force primitives

A primitive packages one contact skill as a tool that the model calls with a few parameters, such as the direction to move and the force to hold. Table 1 lists the twelve force primitives in five groups, and ten further tools move the hand, grip, perceive, or end the episode. Three design choices let a model that decides only every few seconds use these tools.


##### Force through the impedance controller.

Most arms accept position targets rather than forces, and their impedance controller acts as a spring between the hand and the target. A primitive therefore applies force $-F$ by letting a target pose $p^{*}$ lead the measured pose $p$ ,

[Tableau] $$-F\approx K\,(p^{*}-p),\qquad\lVert p^{*}-p\rVert\leq L,$$ (2)

where $K$ is the stiffness of the controller and the clamp $L$ on this lead bounds the force that any primitive can build. Every primitive thus runs on the stock controller and leaves the low-level control of the robot unchanged.


##### One shared loop.

All primitives run in the same loop and differ only in a small control law. At every tick the law reads the tared force and the pose and returns an increment to $p^{*}$ , and the loop clamps the lead, sends the new target, and checks for contact events to decide whether to stop. To hold a force $F_{d}$ along a direction $u$ , the law turns the force error into a target velocity, which is admittance control,

[Tableau] $$v=\mathrm{clip}\big(k_{f}\,(F_{d}-F_{u}),\,\pm v_{\max}\big),\quad\Delta p^{*}=u\,v\,\Delta t,$$ (3)

where $F_{u}=-F\cdot u$ is the reaction against $u$ . The target keeps moving until the error vanishes, so the outer law integrates the force error while the inner spring supplies the force.


##### Self-termination.

A primitive ends on an event rather than on a clock. It stops as soon as one of the contact events listed in Figure 2 occurs or its own goal is met, and a force cap and a time limit stop it if nothing else does. It then unloads the spring and holds the hand still while the model thinks. This division of work is what makes a slow decision rate workable. The model decides what to do and how hard to press, the primitive decides when to stop, and a fixed guard beneath both enforces limits that the model cannot change.


#### 3.4 A representative primitive: search

We illustrate the design with search , the primitive in Figure 2 , which finds a hole whose position the model knows only roughly. The model chooses how hard to press, how wide to search, and how deep the part must sink. The primitive moves toward the surface until it touches it and then keeps pressing with the target force $F_{d}$ through Eq. 3 , while the target travels outward on an Archimedean spiral in the plane of the surface, as the inset of Figure 2 shows. When the part passes over the hole, the reaction falls, the spiral pauses, and the same law drives the part down. The primitive reports that the part dropped in once it has sunk past the set depth, or that no hole was found if the spiral leaves the search range first, and in both cases it also reports how far the part advanced and the lateral force at the end. Most other primitives follow the same pattern of one force law along the contact normal plus their own motion and stop conditions, and Sec. A.4 of the supplementary material specifies them one by one.


#### 3.5 Physics translation layer

A frozen model has not learned to read raw force, and the hundreds of samples that a primitive logs give it little basis for its next decision. The top layer therefore translates each force trace into a few physical quantities, the numbers a person would read off the trace, and returns them as the tool result. Its estimators are deliberately simple and fixed, built only from averages and least-squares fits with propagated errors. Every quantity follows the recipe shown as a strip in Figure 2 . The layer removes the zero and any held load, keeps only the window in which the quantity has a single meaning, and reports the value with its uncertainty $\sigma$ and with flags that warn, for instance, of lost contact. It contains no task thresholds, so it states how heavy a load is or how far a plug advanced but never whether that is enough, which is left to the model.

For example, payload mass comes from a still hold and friction from the part of a slide that stays in contact,

[Tableau] $\displaystyle m$ $\displaystyle=\lvert\bar{F}_{z}\rvert/g,$ $\displaystyle\sigma_{m}$ $\displaystyle=\sqrt{s_{z}^{2}/n+\sigma_{b}^{2}}\,/\,g,$ (4) $\displaystyle\mu$ $\displaystyle=\overline{\lvert F_{t}\rvert}/\bar{F}_{n},$ $\displaystyle(\sigma_{\mu}/\mu)^{2}$ $\displaystyle=\big(\sigma_{t}/\overline{\lvert F_{t}\rvert}\big)^{2}+(\sigma_{n}/\bar{F}_{n})^{2},$ (5)

where $s_{z}$ is the sample standard deviation of the vertical force $F_{z}$ over the $n$ samples, $\sigma_{b}$ is the residual zero bias, and $\sigma_{t}$ and $\sigma_{n}$ are the standard errors of the tangential force $F_{t}$ and the normal force $F_{n}$ . Contact height is corrected for the lag of the contact detector, stiffness is the least-squares slope of normal force over penetration, and insertion advance carries a flag that says whether the part dropped in or jammed. Sec. B.2 of the supplementary material gives every quantity.


#### 3.6 Model in the loop

Figure 2 shows one turn of this loop from a real USB-plug insertion. At every turn the model reads the instruction, the tool menu, the call history, and the latest observations, and it answers with one tool call. Instructions are written by hand for each task and never reveal the hidden variables of an episode. A task usually starts without force, as the model points at a target in the image, locate turns that pixel into a 3D point from depth, and position moves bring the hand close. The model then switches to force primitives and sets their parameters from what it has seen and read so far. Each primitive stops on its own, and its force comes back translated into physical quantities as the tool result. From these quantities the model judges what happened, for example whether a part has seated or needs another attempt, and it chooses the next call until it ends the episode. Calibration and safety stay beneath the model, because the bottom layer keeps the force zeroed by the rules of Sec. 3.2 and the fixed guard limits force, speed, and workspace for every primitive whatever the model asks.


### 4 The HiddenPhys benchmark

Public force benchmarks such as ForceBench [ 14 ] are built to train and compare learned policies. We complement them with HiddenPhys, a benchmark of seven manipulation tasks in robosuite on MuJoCo. Its tasks come from real-world contact scenarios and add fine manipulation that ForceBench does not cover.


##### Tasks.

Each task hides one physical variable that decides success, as listed in Table 2 . In Weight and CoM the robot must judge a load that it is holding, and in Wipe and Stack it must find a surface whose height is uncertain. Peg asks it to find a hole by touch, while Latch and Lock ask it to operate a mechanism without damaging it. The instruction states the range of the hidden variable but never its value, which is drawn from the episode seed, so every method faces the same episodes.

##### Setting and references.

A Panda arm runs operational-space control, and force is the wrist sensor reading with added noise and a per-episode bias. To isolate force from visual perception, a frozen model reads object poses as noisy text instead of images, while the real robot and ForceBench cover camera input. HiddenPhys ships with three kinds of reference. The expert force controller is a hand-written state machine for each task, and the position-only controller makes the same motions without using force. The learned policies are Diffusion Policy [ 6 ] and $\pi_{0.5}$ [ 24 ] , each trained with and without force input, together with a controlled multi-task variant of ForceVLA [ 39 ] , and all of them learn from about 200 expert demonstrations per task. Besides success, HiddenPhys counts damage, an episode in which the force on a handle passes its damage threshold. Sec. C of the supplementary material gives the task cards, success checks, and seeds, and Sec. G gives the training recipes.

##### Results.

In Table 3 , ForceHarness raises GPT-6 Astra from 2/60 without force to 54/60 across all six tasks, while GPT-6.1 Sol reaches 47/60 under the same prompts. On bottle sorting and telling soft from hard, RGB-D images give no cue to mass or stiffness, leaving the no-force control at 0/10 on both. On the sensorless FR3, joint-torque estimates drift with arm pose and misstate absolute grams, yet taring at rest, settling before reading, and comparing within-episode changes keep relative order reliable. This lets Astra score 10/10 on both tasks and Sol score 8/10 and 10/10, even when no probing method is given for soft versus hard.

On the four contact tasks, wrist depth localizes targets only to about 1 to 2 cm, so position-only steps either miss the surface or hit the collision guard without knowing the contact load, succeeding in only 2 of 40 episodes. With ForceHarness, force acts both inside and between primitives, as Figure 1 illustrates. Inside each call, guarded moves and admittance control absorb height and pose error, holding steady contact across the flat board or curved eggplant and spiraling at 1 N until a resistance drop guides the USB plug into its port. Between calls, tool results and images guide adaptation: remaining ink after an 8 N wipe prompts Astra to retry at 10 N, rising side load during screwing triggers an in-place release before binding, and a 2.77 N mean force over a 51 mm peel tells Astra to keep its 6 N target. Calibrated joint-torque sensing, self-terminating primitives, and physical quantities thus make zero-shot contact manipulation reliable without a wrist sensor.

#### 5.2 ForceBench


##### Experimental setting.

ForceBench [ 14 ] is a public Isaac Lab suite of eight contact-rich tasks, where we keep its evaluator and run its first ten seeds. Our harness serves as the policy with the same primitives, estimators, and a few suite-specific tools, reading two RGB views, object sizes, and key heights without object coordinates. Instructions state task facts that demonstrations expose, such as the two mass classes, and the no-force control adds a height-following turn tool so it lacks force rather than motion. GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol run at low reasoning effort, and Claude Fable 5.2 runs with ForceHarness at high effort.


##### Results.

In Table 4 , Astra and Sol reach 87.5% with ForceHarness and Fable reaches 75.0%, above 35.31% for the best trained policy. Since Astra and Sol already score 70.0% and 65.0% without force through the model, harness, and instructions, that gap is not from force alone. Within our harness, force brings paired gains at McNemar’s $p$ of 0.009 and 0.001 mainly on Drawer Pull from 0/10 to 10/10, while other tasks do not separate at 10 episodes, showing clean transfer when subtle resistance decides success.

Two protocol notes apply. First, tools and perception thresholds were tuned on eight of the ten seeds, and Stove Press and Nut Thread were revised after initial runs. Second, on Faucet Turn, Stove Turn, and Drawer Pull the public evaluator omits checks such as release and clearance.

### 6 Conclusion

We presented ForceHarness, a three-layer harness that lets an API-only frozen frontier model use wrist force with no demonstrations or weight updates. Across a real arm without a wrist sensor, ForceBench, and HiddenPhys, keeping force calibration, admittance control, and event termination beneath the model while returning calibrated physical quantities lets frozen models match hand-written expert controllers and surpass policies trained on demonstrations. Ablations show that self-terminating primitives keep contact safe without force values, physical quantities help smaller models judge a held load, and calling the model after each primitive lets it recover from failures.


##### Limitations.

Model queries add seconds between primitives and preclude reactive coordination, a fixed menu limits new motion laws, and our ablations run only in simulation.

### Appendix A Self-terminating force primitives

This section specifies the force primitives of Sec. 3.3 and Table 1 of the main paper and every other tool of the menu. The backends are MuJoCo, the robosuite Panda of HiddenPhys, ForceBench, the Isaac Lab arm behind that benchmark’s evaluator, and the real arm, the FR3 of Sec. 5.1 of the main paper. The constants are taken from the code of each backend.

Throughout, $p$ is the measured tool center point and $p^{*}$ the target, so $p^{*}-p$ is the lead. $F$ is the force that the environment applies to the hand, in the base frame, with the zero and the held-load tare $b_{c}$ removed, and $F_{\mathrm{raw}}$ is the same force before $b_{c}$ is removed. A unit normal $n$ points into the surface, so that $F_{n}=-F\cdot n$ is positive while the hand presses. The slow and fast target speeds are $v_{\mathrm{near}}$ and $v_{\mathrm{free}}$ , and one tick lasts $\Delta t$ . #### A.1 The shared loop

Every tool that moves the robot is a small control law plus one call of the loop in Figure A1 . The loop starts from $p^{*}=p$ , so no lead carries over from the previous call, and with a closed gripper it first re-estimates the held-load tare from a still, load-like reading. At every tick the law reads the tared force and the pose and returns an increment of the target. The loop clamps the lead to the limit $L$ of the tool, which bounds the force that any primitive can build, sends the target to the impedance controller, and runs the event detector. Force and torque caps stop every call, while the other events stop a call only if the tool asked for them. After a stop on an event, the target returns to within a tenth of its lead, which unloads the spring, and the hand then holds still while the model thinks. The loop keeps at most 160 evenly spaced rows of the trace for the estimators of Appendix B , and the peak force is tracked before thinning.

A reading $f$ counts as load-like when $f_{z}<-1$ N, $\lVert f\rVert<15$ N, and $\lVert f_{xy}\rVert\leq\max(1\,\mathrm{N},\,0.5\,|f_{z}|)$ . The tare $b_{c}$ is cleared by grasp and release , set by lift from its still window, and replaced at the end of any still, completed call that holds a load-like reading. On the real arm, the runner also reads the force still for a nominal 0.5 s after each call, which gives the still reading of Sec. 3.2 of the main paper.

#### A.2 Force from lead, and contact events

No backend offers a force command at this level. Each accepts a position target and behaves as a spring between hand and target. MuJoCo runs an operational-space law with pose-dependent stiffness, ForceBench uses inverse kinematics with fixed joint gains, and the real arm runs its stock joint-impedance controller with fixed gains, so all compliance comes from the outer loop and the lead. With the hand blocked along a direction $u$ ,

[Tableau] $$F_{u}\approx K\,(p^{*}_{u}-p_{u}),\qquad\lVert p^{*}-p\rVert\leq L,$$ (A1)

so the lead limit bounds the force at $KL$ . On the real arm the measured $K$ is 0.75 to 1 N/mm, because pressing with a 15 mm lead read 10 to 11 N and free-air steps gave about 1 N/mm, so 8 mm of lead holds at most about 8 N and 25 mm about 18 N.

To hold $F_{d}$ along $u$ , the primitives run admittance control on top of this spring,

[Tableau] $$p^{*}_{k+1}=p^{*}_{k}+u\,\mathrm{clip}\big(k_{f}(F_{d}-F_{u,k}),\,\pm v_{\max}\big)\,\Delta t,$$ (A2)

with $F_{u}=-F\cdot u$ . The target integrates the force error, so the steady error is zero while the lead stays below $L$ . Unsaturated and blocked, the error shrinks by a factor $1-\lambda$ per tick with $\lambda=Kk_{f}\Delta t$ . Computed from the constants rather than measured, $\lambda$ for push is 0.17 to 0.23 on the real arm, 0.09 to 0.14 in MuJoCo, and 0.15 to 0.37 in ForceBench, so the force approaches its target without oscillation. On the real arm each tick therefore corrects about a fifth of the error and moves the target at most 0.2 mm, and the force rises at 3 to 4 N/s.

Two real-arm primitives, screw and wiggle , press with a fixed lead instead of a force loop. They keep the target a fixed lead $\ell=F_{d}/K_{z}$ below the hand, with $K_{z}=750$ N/m. Both turn the wrist, which shifts the zero of the force estimate, and a fixed lead presses with about $K_{z}\ell$ independently of that shift.

On the real arm, the position moves, the turns, lift , and hold are guarded against collisions, so the detector there uses $f_{c}=8$ N and the runner also watches $\lVert F_{\mathrm{raw}}\rVert$ , which a held load cannot hide.

##### Finding and seating.

search presses lightly and moves the target on an Archimedean spiral of pitch $a$ about its start until the part sinks, which Sec. 3.4 of the main paper walks through and Figure A5 details. It first descends and arms on surface contact, ending with no_surface after 3 cm without one. When $F_{n}$ falls below $0.3F_{d}$ , the part is over a hole, so the spiral pauses and the target falls, and a part that is already deeper than drop and blocked sideways also counts as dropped . In simulation a lateral force above 6 N for 0.3 s means a corner caught on the rim, so the part lifts, moves 6 mm along the wall’s reaction, and restarts the spiral there. On the real arm, once the advance gains over 0.25 mm on its recent median, the target thrusts down with the 25 mm lead toward $\max(F_{d},15\,\mathrm{N})$ , limited so that no force estimate would reach 23 N within 0.3 s, and 1.5 mm of gain after 2 s means dropped . Besides dropped and completed , the call can end stuck , out_of_range , or lost_contact . A hop mode, the default in simulation, probes the spiral points one at a time instead of dragging the part, and a probe deeper than earlier ones by drop means dropped .

##### Moving by position.

move_to moves the tool center on a straight line to a goal $g$ with the orientation unchanged, and move_by does the same toward $p_{0}+[dx,dy,dz]$ , as Figure A13 shows. In simulation the speed drops to $v_{\mathrm{near}}$ in contact. On the real arm joint friction can stop the hand short of $g$ , so a stuck hand near $g$ gets a target bias that grows up to the 8 mm lead and retracts within 1 mm of $g$ . Both stop on contact at $f_{c}$ , or at the collision limits on the real arm, where they can also end stalled_short . They report kinematics and a note if the call ended over 5 mm short. point_tool turns the fingers toward a direction with the tool center fixed, on Latch, Lock, Drawer Pull, and the real arm. In simulation the force zero is converted every tick as the tool turns.

### Appendix B Physics translation layer

This section specifies the physics translation layer of Sec. 3.5 of the main paper, which HiddenPhys, ForceBench, and the FR3 share, so that each number in a tool result can be recomputed from the primitive’s log.


#### B.1 Common recipe

Five estimators, whose constants per backend are in Table B1 , turn the log of one primitive into the quantities of Sec. B.2 by the recipe of Sec. 3.5 of the main paper. The log holds the force, torque, and tool position of every tick and is downsampled uniformly beyond 160 rows. Each estimator removes the zero and any held load, $F_{i}=F_{i}^{\mathrm{raw}}-c$ , where $F^{\mathrm{raw}}$ is the zeroed force and the tare $c$ is set by lift and cleared by grasp and release . Payload mass and the FR3 pull skip this tare, since the load or the resistance is their signal. Within the window where it has one meaning, a quantity is by default a mean with its standard error, while payload mass and pull force add the residual zero bias $\sigma_{b}$ , a ratio propagates relative errors, and a slope takes its least-squares error,

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\bar{x}$ $\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{n}\textstyle\sum_{i\in W}x_{i},\qquad\sigma=s/\sqrt{n},$ (B1) $\displaystyle\sigma_{m}$ $\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{g}\sqrt{s_{z}^{2}/n+\sigma_{b}^{2}},\quad\sigma_{\mathrm{pull}}=s/\sqrt{n}+\sigma_{b},$ (B2) $\displaystyle\sigma_{\mu}^{2}/\mu^{2}$ $\displaystyle=\sigma_{t}^{2}/\overline{\lvert F_{t}\rvert}^{2}+\sigma_{n}^{2}/\bar{F}_{n}^{2},$ (B3) $\displaystyle\sigma_{k}$ $\displaystyle=\sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{i}e_{i}^{2}/(n-2)}\,\Big/\sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{i}(\delta_{i}-\bar{\delta})^{2}},$ (B4)

where $W$ is the window of $n$ rows and $s$ the sample standard deviation. $\sigma_{t}$ and $\sigma_{n}$ are the standard errors of the two means, $e_{i}$ the fit residuals, and $\delta_{i}$ the penetrations. The flags, given with each quantity in Sec. B.2 , are class bands fixed across tasks, warnings, and outcomes translated from how the primitive ended, and no step holds a task threshold.

#### B.2 Every quantity

This subsection gives every quantity that a tool result can carry and its flags, grouped by the primitives that report it. Each paragraph states the window and the $\sigma$ of a quantity. Here $u$ is the call’s direction, or else its start-to-end direction, and $\hat{n}$ its normal. The tool position $p$ starts at $p_{0}$ , $t_{c}$ is the time of contact, and $F$ is tared unless marked raw. Endings that no rule below translates, such as stuck or timeout , pass through by name.


##### Held load.

lift and hold report the payload mass $m=\lvert\bar{F}^{\mathrm{raw}}_{z}\rvert/g$ with the gripper closed and $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^{2}}$ , averaged over the last hold_time or else the last half of the call, with $\sigma_{m}$ of ( B2 ). When $m\geq 0.03$ kg and the load hangs, they also report the center-of-mass offset over the same window, printed as $\lvert r\rvert$ and the nearest base axis. A load of weight $mg$ at horizontal lever $r$ gives the torque $(-mg\,r_{y},\ mg\,r_{x},\ 0)$ , and a sign constant $s_{\tau}$ set on the simulator’s true lever maps the sensor’s raw torque rows to $r$ ,

[Tableau] $\displaystyle r_{x}$ $\displaystyle=s_{\tau}\frac{\bar{T}_{y}}{mg},\quad r_{y}=-s_{\tau}\frac{\bar{T}_{x}}{mg},\quad s_{\tau}=-1,$ (B5) $\displaystyle\sigma_{r}$ $\displaystyle=\sqrt{(\sigma_{\tau}/mg)^{2}+(|r|\,\sigma_{m}/m)^{2}},$ (B6)

with $\sigma_{\tau}=\sqrt{(s_{x}^{2}+s_{y}^{2})/n}+\tau_{b}$ , where $s_{x}$ and $s_{y}$ are the standard deviations of the two torque axes and $\tau_{b}=0.01$ N m is the torque bias. The lever is taken about the sensor’s vertical axis. Both quantities appear in MuJoCo and ForceBench. A reading is flagged not_settled if the height varies over 3 mm and unsteady if $s_{z}>3\sigma_{\mathrm{noise}}+0.5$ N. The mass is flagged no_load if $m<2\sigma_{m}$ and pushing_up_not_hanging if $\bar{F}_{z}>0$ without no_load , and the offset is flagged within_noise if $\lvert r\rvert<2\sigma_{r}$ . The FR3 reports weight from the still reading of Appendix I instead of payload mass, which would use one zero point for every pose, and it omits the offset.


##### Contact.

A call whose contact event passes the contact gate reports four quantities on all three backends. The gate admits an event outside grasp , release , and pull that lasts at least 0.15 s and peaks at or above $0.8f_{c}$ within 0.3 s, where $f_{c}$ is the contact threshold. The contact height is $z_{c}=z(t_{c})-u_{z}\ell$ if $\lvert u_{z}\rvert>0.7$ , and otherwise the contact point is $s_{c}=(p(t_{c})-u\ell-p_{0})\cdot u$ , both taken at the tick of $t_{c}$ with $\sigma=\ell/2+v\Delta t$ , where $\ell$ is the detector lag of Table B1 . The contact normal $\bar{F}/\lvert\bar{F}\rvert$ and its tilt $\arccos(\lvert\bar{F}_{z}\rvert/\lvert\bar{F}\rvert)$ use the 6 rows from $t_{c}$ , and the impact peak is $\max\lvert F\rvert$ from $t_{c}$ to $t_{c}+0.3$ s, both without $\sigma$ . The stiffness $k$ is a least-squares fit $F_{u}=k\delta+b$ with $\delta=(p-p(t_{c}))\cdot u$ and $F_{u}=-F\cdot u$ , on at least 4 of the 12 rows from $t_{c}$ that have $\delta>0.5$ mm, and it is reported if $k>0.05$ N/mm with $\sigma_{k}$ of ( B4 ). The impact is labeled gentle below 5 N, moderate below 15 N, and hard above, and the stiffness soft below 1 N/mm, firm below 10 N/mm, and rigid above.


##### Pressing and sliding.

push and slide report the normal force, the mean of $F_{n}=-F\cdot u$ , or of $-F\cdot\hat{n}$ on slide , over the rows of the last 80% of the call with $F_{n}>0.5f_{c}$ , with $\sigma$ of ( B1 ). On the same rows slide adds the friction coefficient $\mu=\overline{\lvert F_{t}\rvert}/\bar{F}_{n}$ with $F_{t}=F\cdot u$ and $\sigma_{\mu}$ of ( B3 ), and the stick-slip index $\mathrm{std}\lvert F_{t}\rvert/\overline{\lvert F_{t}\rvert}$ . All three backends report them. If fewer than 3 rows qualify, the result says lost_contact and gives the mean of all rows with no $\sigma$ and no friction line, and an index above 0.5 sets stick_slip .


##### Insertion.

search and insert report the advance $d=(p_{\mathrm{end}}-p_{0})\cdot\hat{n}$ over the whole call and the lateral force $\lvert\overline{F-(F\cdot\hat{n})\hat{n}}\rvert$ over its last 5 rows, without $\sigma$ , on all three backends. search ends dropped_in on a drop or a resistance drop, no_hole_found at full radius, and jammed if stalled. insert ends seated at depth, and after under 1 mm of advance in 1 s it ends jammed with $F_{n}$ at least half of max_force or stalled_no_progress with less, and otherwise not_seated . Both give jammed_hard at the force cap and lateral_load if the lateral force at the end exceeds $f_{c}$ .


##### Pulling.

pull reports the pull force, the mean of $-F\cdot d_{i}$ along the pull direction $d_{i}$ of each tick, and the mean sideways force over the last 80%, with $\sigma$ of ( B2 ), or on the FR3 the mean of $-F^{\mathrm{raw}}\cdot u$ . After 3 cm of travel it adds the path turn $\mathrm{atan2}(q_{1}\times q_{2},\,q_{1}\cdot q_{2})$ between the first and last 1.5 cm of path $q_{1}$ and $q_{2}$ , counter-clockwise positive, the radius and pivot of an algebraic circle fit if $\lvert\mathrm{turn}\rvert\geq 4^{\circ}$ , and the free direction as the heading of $q_{2}$ . They carry a $\sigma$ of $2^{\circ}$ , or the fit RMS plus 5 mm for the fit, in MuJoCo and ForceBench. The call ends mechanism_stopped when it stalls, under 3 mm in 1 s with a mean pull force over 0.5 s of at least 95% of target, or on the FR3 under 0.2 mm in 1 s with a mean resistance of at least 95% of target, and a turn under $3^{\circ}$ is flagged straight_line .


##### Turning on ForceBench.

The quantities of this paragraph and the next appear only under ForceHarness. On ForceBench, $\theta$ is the commanded turn about the axis $a$ through the pivot $p_{v}$ , and $T$ is the torque row. press_turn reports the resisting torque $\tau=\mathrm{sgn}(\theta)T_{z}$ , positive against the turn, as its mean over the second half with $\sigma=s/\sqrt{n}$ and its peak over the turn. On Stove Press it reports instead the resistance per side, the median of $\tau$ on each side of the start angle over rows moving away from it, 8 to 25 ∘ out and since the last grasp, from 4 rows on, with $\sigma=1.253s/\sqrt{n}$ . It also reports the press force, the mean upward reaction while turning, and the depth, the start minus the end tool height, both without $\sigma$ . turn can also report the resisting moment $M=\mathrm{sgn}(\theta)\big[a\cdot T-a\cdot((p-p_{v})\times F)\big]$ , smoothed over 4 rows, as its mean over the second half, its peak over the call, and its value at the last row, without $\sigma$ , but this was off in all reported runs.


##### On the FR3 only.

Every call reports the still reading of Appendix I and, while the hand holds a load, the weight $\hat{m}=-\Delta F_{z}/g$ above 20 g, with $\Delta F$ the change since grasp ended. The reading takes 0.5 s after a 0.8 s relax, nominally, and carries a fixed $\pm 400$ g on grams. Every call also reports the net force peak $\max\lvert F\rvert$ over the whole call. screw reports the advance $\Delta z=z_{\mathrm{start}}-z_{\mathrm{end}}$ , split into the sink while pressing and while turning, the advance per turn $\Delta z_{\mathrm{turn}}/\lvert\theta\rvert\times 360^{\circ}$ if $\lvert\theta\rvert\geq 10^{\circ}$ , and as side load the size of the untared change in $\bar{F}_{xy}$ between its first 3 and last 5 rows, without $\sigma$ . It ends advancing , rising , or not_advancing for a sink while turning above 0.2 mm, below $-0.2$ mm, or in between. The FR3 prints no quantity when the force calibration is not valid for the pose.


#### B.3 Rendering levels

L1 prints each value with its unit and any fixed geometry, and L2, the default of ForceHarness, adds $\pm\,\sigma$ and a line with the window and flags, as Figure F2 shows.


#### B.4 Accuracy

We check the estimators against the ground truth of the simulators. On HiddenPhys test episodes, the mass error against simulator truth has a mean of 0.004 kg and a standard deviation of 0.03 kg, which matches the printed $\sigma$ , and the direction cosine of the center-of-mass offset is at least 0.97. On ForceBench, masses of 0.06 and 0.18 kg read 0.058 and 0.177 kg. The printed $\sigma$ covers measurement noise only.

#### G.3 Code-as-Policies

In the program rows of Sec. 5.4 of the main paper, GPT-6 Astra at low reasoning effort writes one Python program per episode in a single call without examples, and the program runs with no further model calls. The force row thus measures a single zero-shot program written over our force interface, closer in spirit to code generation over compliant primitives [ 4 , 22 ] than to the original Code-as-Policies [ 20 ] , which uses few-shot examples, hierarchical generation, and no force.

### Appendix I Real-robot system and tasks

This section summarizes how the harness runs on the real robot of Sec. 5.1 of the main paper, a Franka FR3 without a wrist force sensor, and every constant here belongs to that arm.


##### System.

The stock joint-impedance controller of Polymetis runs unchanged at 1 kHz, with joint gains at four times the default and eight times on joint 7, and we move only its joint target. Beside it on the real-time computer, our guard runs at a nominal 50 Hz and enforces the hard limits of Sec. A.5 . The harness on the workstation runs the primitives and estimators and sends the guard one step per tick, at a median rate of 14.9 Hz. The frozen model is called once per turn and answers with one tool call.


##### Force estimate.

Polymetis forwards the filtered external joint torques that libfranka estimates but not a wrench, so the guard solves Eq. 1 of the main paper on every tick by least squares with the flange Jacobian of the controller’s own kinematic model. It also checks the joint torque that no flange wrench explains, and a residual above 3 N $\cdot$ m or a Jacobian near a singularity ends the episode with a fault. The estimate has little noise, 0.03 to 0.05 N at rest, but its zero shifts by a few newtons with the approach direction, joint friction in motion, and the wrist angle. Filtering cannot remove such an offset, so we calibrate only the offset and read force by the three rules of Sec. 3.2 of the main paper. The harness tares with the gripper open before each empty-hand primitive, and after every primitive it relaxes the target onto the measured pose for 0.8 s and then averages the wrench over 0.5 s. Relaxing lowers the median difference between consecutive still readings from 1.07 N to 0.08 N over 999 empty-hand windows, because a target that leads the joints pushes against joint friction, which the estimate reports as external force. Each still reading is reported relative to the same reading at the start of the episode, and with an object in the hand also as the change since the grasp. Lifted weights are therefore compared within an episode rather than read in grams.


##### Safety.

Beneath the guard of Sec. A.5 , the harness adds collision stops at 8 N, or 12 N when holding, in the guarded tools of Appendix A , and it ends an episode after the number of actions in Table I1 . An operator stands at the emergency stop in every episode.


##### Localization.

The exterior camera is uncalibrated, so every coordinate comes from fixed geometry on the wrist depth image, with no learned detector. A pixel maps to the base frame through the flange pose at capture and the hand–eye calibration of the wrist camera. Each turn, the harness groups points about 1 cm or more above the fitted table plane into objects and lists their center, top, and long axis with its size, and the model picks targets itself with locate . The hand–eye calibration has a median error of 4.1 mm and 0.28 ∘ on 6 held-out views. The model is told that coordinates are good to about 1 to 2 cm, so vision alone cannot reach a port, and its last millimeters are left to search .


##### Tasks and conditions.

Table I1 describes each task as in the final runs, and Sec. 5.1 of the main paper gives the success criteria. ForceHarness offers all 22 tools of Appendix A except wiggle and press_turn , USB insertion adds wiggle , and screw driving swaps lift for wiggle . The no-force control keeps the 11 tools that move by position only or not at all, and its tool results report only kinematics. In peeling it also gets move_until_contact , which stops at a fixed 5 N and reports no force.


### Appendix J Limitations and reproducibility


#### J.1 Limitations

Querying a remote frontier model adds seconds of latency between primitives, which suits quasi-static contact tasks but precludes fast reactive replanning during dynamic motion. Both the primitive menu and the physics estimators are specified by hand, and each task relies on a hand-written instruction, leaving automatic primitive synthesis and open-ended task specification to future work. Finally, our evaluation focuses on single-arm tabletop manipulation with a parallel gripper across models from two API providers, and our component ablations are conducted in simulation rather than repeated on real hardware.


#### J.2 Reproducibility

HiddenPhys uses robosuite 1.5.1 and MuJoCo 3.2.7, ForceBench runs on Isaac Lab with the changes of Sec. H.1 , and the real robot runs stock Polymetis with its hybrid joint impedance controller. Code and configurations will be released upon publication, together with logs and task assets.

Model settings are those of Sec. 5 of the main paper, and the GPT-5.x models use chat completions with reasoning off when tools are present.
