## Don’t Just Trust Your Memory: Evidence-Linked Memory Reduces Memory-Induced Degradation in LLM Agents

External memory enables LLM agents to improve from experience by distilling trajectories of past tasks into concise lessons for new tasks. However, memory can also lead to memory-induced degradation , where an agent fails on tasks it would solve without memory, often because it follows a lesson that is incorrect, or misapplies or misreads a valid one. This is because lessons are inferred from trajectories labeled only with task success or failure, yet are typically provided without the trajectories they came from, leaving the agent no way to check them. To address this, we introduce WikiTrace , an evidence-linked memory system designed to let agents check each lesson against its original trajectory. Specifically, motivated by the finding that a trajectory often still holds the evidence needed to catch an erroneous lesson, WikiTrace stores each lesson as a concise insight linked to a summary of why its trajectory succeeded or failed and to the raw trajectory itself. Then, on a new task, the agent starts from all insights and, whenever one lacks details needed to act, conflicts with another, or comes from a failed task, descends through its links with terminal tools to fill in missing details or correct the insight before acting. We validate WikiTrace on five agentic benchmarks, showing that it consistently outperforms existing memory approaches and a baseline given the same insights alone, while also reducing memory-induced degradation. Our code is available at https://github.com/yukyeongleee/wikitrace .


### 1 Introduction

Large language model (LLM) agents increasingly solve multi-step tasks by interacting with an environment Yao et al. (2023) , and external memory allows them to improve from experience without updating their parameters Sumers et al. (2024) ; Hu et al. (2025b) ; Luo et al. (2026) . Specifically, memory distills trajectories of past tasks, namely their sequences of actions and observations, into concise lessons that are reused on new tasks, allowing the agent to avoid repeated exploration and to follow procedures specific to its environment. Prior work captures such lessons in diverse forms, including verbal reflections, executable skills, instruction manuals, workflows, and reasoning strategies Shinn et al. (2023) ; Zhao et al. (2024) ; Wang et al. (2024) ; Chen et al. (2024) ; Wang et al. (2025) ; Ouyang et al. (2026) ; Zhang et al. (2026b) .

> Figure 1: Memory-induced degradation and WikiTrace. (a) The agent solves a task without memory but fails with memory that holds a wrong insight. (b) With WikiTrace, the agent follows the wrong insight’s links to its source run, revises it, and solves the task.

However, memory does not always help and can even lead to memory-induced degradation , where an agent fails on tasks it would solve without memory Zhang et al. (2026a) ; Xiong et al. (2025) ; Hu et al. (2026a) (Figure 1 a). Reliable experience reuse therefore requires solving more tasks while preserving those the agent already solves, but average accuracy cannot tell the two apart. We thus measure them separately, as Gain , the rate at which memory turns failures into successes, and Regression , the rate at which it turns successes into failures. As shown in Figure 5 , existing memory methods exhibit Regression comparable to, or even higher than, that of rerunning the agent without memory.

To understand this degradation, we analyze tasks that the agent consistently solves without memory but fails with it, and find that 52% of the failures trace to a memory item the agent followed (Figure 4 ), most often a wrong one. Wrong items arise because a trajectory comes with only a single label, success or failure, which does not reveal which of its many actions caused that outcome. Any method that learns lessons from such a trajectory therefore has to guess which actions matter and under which conditions, and each wrong guess yields a lesson that misleads the agent on new tasks. Yet, existing approaches provide lessons without the trajectories they came from, leaving the agent no way to check them, even though the evidence needed to catch an erroneous lesson often remains in that trajectory.

To this end, we present WikiTrace , an evidence-linked memory system that keeps each lesson together with a path back to its original trajectory (Figures 1 b and 2 ), combining the strengths of both: a lesson concisely tells the agent what to do, whereas the trajectory faithfully records what actually happened but is too long to reread for every decision. Specifically, WikiTrace organizes its memory into three layers linked from top to bottom: each lesson is stored as a concise insight , which links down to a compilation that summarizes why its trajectory succeeded or failed, and further down to the raw trajectory itself. Then, on a new task, the agent starts from all insights and, whenever an insight lacks details needed to act, conflicts with another, or comes from a failed trajectory, descends through its links with terminal tools to recover missing details or revise the insight before acting.

We validate WikiTrace on five agentic benchmarks comprising eight suites, spanning simulated text worlds Shridhar et al. (2021) ; Wang et al. (2022) ; Dagan et al. (2025) and tool-use environments Yao et al. (2025) ; Barres et al. (2026) ; Trivedi et al. (2024) . WikiTrace achieves the best performance on every suite, improving over the agent without memory by 10.7 points on average and outperforming a baseline given the same insights alone by 3.2 points, while reducing memory-induced degradation (Regression from 6.5% to 4.5%). Further analyses show that the agent consults the linked evidence more often on harder tasks, where its advantage is largest, and gains most when the evidence changes its plan. These gains hold under another backbone.


### 2 Related Work


#### 2.1 Experience-Driven Procedural Memory

Language agents increasingly convert past execution experience into procedural memory that can guide future decisions. Prior methods retain verbal feedback or successful trajectories Shinn et al. (2023) ; Zhao et al. (2024) , accumulate executable skills Wang et al. (2024) , or distill experience into reusable guidelines, manuals, workflows, modular procedures, and multigranular scripts Fu et al. (2024) ; Chen et al. (2024) ; Wang et al. (2025) ; Han et al. (2026) ; Fang et al. (2026) . Continual approaches further update reasoning memories and playbooks with new successful and failed experience Zhou et al. (2025) ; Ouyang et al. (2026) ; Zhang et al. (2026b) ; Cao et al. (2026) . These approaches develop mechanisms for distilling, selecting, and updating experience for reuse. WikiTrace focuses on reassessing the resulting guidance during task execution, linking each lesson to its source evidence so that the agent can examine its basis and applicability to the current task.

> Figure 2: Overview of WikiTrace. (a) A compiler turns each past run into a compilation, a diagnosis of why the run succeeded or failed, and insights, each a concise lesson. All layers stay linked. (b) Each layer’s documents follow a fixed template. (c) The agent starts from every insight and descends through the links under the conditions shown.


#### 2.2 Reliable Reuse of Unverified Memory

Reusing past experience does not always improve an agent’s performance. Agents ignore or misinterpret relevant condensed experience Zhao et al. (2026a) , encounter retrieval competition and negative transfer during continual learning Hu et al. (2026a) , and exhibit amplified unsafe behavior when reusing execution-oriented experience collected from benign tasks Zhao et al. (2026c) . These findings motivate evaluating the failures introduced by memory alongside the tasks it helps solve. We explicitly separate Gain from Regression to highlight memory-induced degradation that aggregate accuracy obscures. Research on memory admission and revision addresses reliability by controlling which experiences and lessons are retained. PREPING validates synthetic practice trajectories before storage Choi et al. (2026) , SkillOpt admits skill updates through held-out evaluation Yang et al. (2026) , and ReMe updates or removes memories using subsequent utility Cao et al. (2026) . WikiTrace complements these approaches by focusing on how stored lessons inform decisions during task execution. This perspective treats reliable memory reuse as a question of both which lessons to retain and whether those lessons are justified in the situations where they are applied.


#### 2.3 Memory Structure and Access

Agent memory design concerns both how past experience is organized and how agents access it during task execution. Hierarchical approaches organize experience at multiple levels of detail for compression, planning, and retrieval Hu et al. (2025a) ; Zhang et al. (2025) ; Fang et al. (2026) ; Yang et al. (2025) ; Gao et al. (2025) ; Hu et al. (2026b) , while HORMA connects filesystem summaries to raw interaction history and learns to select a minimal sufficient context Hsu et al. (2026) . Beyond memory organization, active-access methods determine when and what to retrieve during reasoning Zhang et al. (2026c) ; Zhao et al. (2026b) ; Cai et al. (2026) . Direct Corpus Interaction (DCI) provides a general approach to corpus access through terminal tools Li et al. (2026) . WikiTrace brings these two concerns together around the reassessment of procedural lessons: its structure preserves the connection between a lesson and its source execution, and its access mechanism lets the agent consult that execution when deciding how to apply the lesson.


### 3 WikiTrace

We first define the problem setting and examine memory-induced degradation to motivate WikiTrace. We then describe its memory construction and test-time evidence access (Figure 2 ).


#### 3.1 Problem Setting

We consider an agent that reuses a fixed corpus of past runs collected without memory,

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}=\{(x_{i},r_{i},s_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}.$$

Each run consists of a task $x_{i}$ , a trajectory $r_{i}$ recording the agent’s actions and observations, and a binary outcome $s_{i}\in\{0,1\}$ indicating task success or failure. No gold solutions or expert explanations are available, so a run’s outcome does not exactly tell us why it succeeded or failed. Thus, an insight drawn from it is not always valid.

We construct memory $\mathcal{M}$ from this corpus before any test task runs, with no additional run to validate an individual memory item. Once constructed, the memory remains fixed and read-only for an agent solving new tasks. Our objective is to improve task performance through experience reuse while limiting memory-induced degradation on tasks the agent solves without memory.

To understand how memory breaks tasks the agent can already solve, we examine tasks that the agent solves in all three repeated runs without memory but fails with memory. An LLM judge, gpt-5.6-sol at medium reasoning effort, compares each failed run with a no-memory run of the same task, locates the first turn where the two runs depart, and classifies the cause into one of five kinds (Figure 4 ; Appendix D ).

In 52% of these cases, the failure traces to a memory item the agent followed (A to D). Most often the item itself was wrong (A, 20%). Otherwise, a valid item was applied outside its scope (B), misused by the agent (C), or followed while memory held a better one (D). 1 1 A cause is often implicit, and an agent acts differently across runs even without memory. Many cases therefore show no link to any memory item (E). Checking the original run a lesson was drawn from can reveal a wrong or out-of-scope item. Figure 4 shows an example: a wrong rule from ExpeL Zhao et al. (2024) fails a task the agent solves without memory, although the run behind the rule contradicts it.

These observations motivate preserving a path from each insight back to its source run. With this path, the agent can recover the observations an insight omits and check whether the insight is supported and whether it applies to the current task before following it. WikiTrace realizes this through source-linked memory (Section 3.3 ) and test-time evidence access (Section 3.4 ).

#### 3.3 Constructing Source-Linked Memory

WikiTrace constructs memory $\mathcal{M}=(R,C,I,E)$ , consisting of raw trajectories $R$ , compilations $C$ , insights $I$ , and source links $E$ . Each run is processed independently so that every extracted lesson remains connected to the experience it came from.


##### Compilation and insight extraction

A compiler reads a task $x_{i}$ , the agent’s trajectory $r_{i}$ on that task, and a binary signal $s_{i}$ of its success or failure, producing a compilation $c_{i}$ and a set of insights $I_{i}$ :

[Tableau] $$(c_{i},I_{i})=\mathrm{Compiler}(x_{i},r_{i},s_{i}).$$

The compilation summarizes the run’s key decisions and supporting observations, using separate templates for successful and failed runs Ouyang et al. (2026) ; Yang et al. (2026) . For successful runs, it describes decisions associated with success; for failed runs, it records plausible explanations for failure. These accounts remain interpretations of the run rather than verified explanations of its outcome. The compiler also extracts at most three insights per run. Each insight is a separate document that says what to do, when it applies, which run it came from, and whether that run succeeded. We generate insights independently for each run, so each insight has a clear source, and we never update or delete them once written. The insight pool may therefore contain redundant or conflicting insights, and deciding which one applies to the current task is left to the agent.


##### Source preservation and linking

WikiTrace preserves each raw trajectory unchanged: the task metadata, each turn’s reasoning, action and observation, and whether the run succeeded. Each insight links to both its source compilation and raw trajectory. Thus, when reassessing an insight, the test-time agent can see which run it came from and how that run was interpreted to derive it. To help the agent navigate, each layer also has an index. Appendix B provides the corpus layout, document templates, and construction algorithm.


#### 3.4 Revisiting Source Evidence at Test Time

WikiTrace lets the agent start from the insights and descend to their source runs when an insight may not hold or does not say enough to act on now.


##### Initial input

The agent first receives all insights, each stating what to do, when it applies, and the id of its source run. The message then lists the three past runs whose tasks are most similar to the current one, with the ids and titles of the insights drawn from each. These runs give the agent starting points for its search.


##### Descent to source runs

To let the agent examine an insight before following it, the prompt asks it to descend from the insight to its source run when the insight lacks the details needed to act on it, comes from a failed run, or disagrees with another insight. It asks for another descent whenever an action fails. The source-linked memory is mounted as read-only files, and the prompt encourages the agent to search them with read and bash for the fact that settles the question, such as a command format. Figure 27 in Appendix F gives the full prompt.

#### 4.1 Experimental Setup


##### Benchmarks

We evaluate on eight suites across five benchmarks. Three are simulated text worlds, where the agent issues one text command at a time: ALFWorld Shridhar et al. (2021) (household tasks), ScienceWorld Wang et al. (2022) (science experiments) and PlanCraft Dagan et al. (2025) (Minecraft crafting). Two are tool-use environments: AppWorld Trivedi et al. (2024) poses everyday tasks through application APIs, and $\tau^{3}$ -bench Retail Yao et al. (2025) ; Barres et al. (2026) combines conversation with a simulated customer and tool calls over an order database. AppWorld includes Test-Normal and Test-Challenge; the latter requires unseen applications. Appendix A.1 details suite construction and splits.


##### Baselines

No Memory is the same agent with nothing added to its prompt or files. AWM Wang et al. (2025) abstracts successful trajectories into reusable workflows. ExpeL Zhao et al. (2024) keeps a list of free-form rules and reviews the whole list against each successful trajectory to add, edit or remove rules. ReasoningBank Ouyang et al. (2026) distills successful and failed trajectories into reasoning strategies and retrieves them by task similarity. We adapt their official implementations to our offline protocol (Appendix A.3 ). Insights Only gives the same insights as WikiTrace but mounts no source compilations or raw trajectories and omits the prompt to descend, so the two differ only in access to source runs.


##### Protocol

We run three trials per suite and method. In each trial, every method builds its memory once from the same No Memory runs of the training tasks and is tested on the same test tasks. No method may make additional runs to validate its memory. All methods use the same agent: a Pi terminal agent Earendil Works (2025) given each benchmark’s ReAct instruction. The agent and memory builders use deepseek-v4-flash 2 2 The 2026-04-23 release. with high reasoning effort. We report each benchmark’s original metric, averaged over the three trials. See Appendix A.2 for implementation details.


#### 4.2 Main Results

Table 1 shows that WikiTrace achieves the highest performance on all eight suites, averaging 90.2 compared with 79.5 for No Memory. It also exceeds Insights Only by 3.2 points on average, demonstrating the benefit of source evidence and the descent prompt beyond the same insights alone. Notably, WikiTrace is the only memory method that matches or exceeds No Memory on every suite, suggesting that revisiting source evidence helps mitigate memory-induced degradation.

#### 4.3 Gain and Regression

Aggregate performance does not distinguish improvements on previously failed tasks from failures on previously solved tasks. We therefore report Gain and Regression separately.

Figure 5 reports both rates averaged across suites, with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals over tasks (Appendix C.1 ). To account for variation across runs, we evaluate three additional No Memory trials against the same reference outcomes $B_{i}$ . The dashed line shows this rerun baseline, measuring outcome changes without memory.

WikiTrace achieves a Regression of 4.5%, compared with 6.5% for Insights Only and 9.7% for the rerun baseline. Its Regression is lower than Insights Only on seven of the eight suites, and it is the only method whose Regression confidence interval lies entirely below the rerun band. This reduction accompanies higher Gain: WikiTrace exceeds the rerun baseline by 35 percentage points, compared with 27 for Insights Only and 7 to 14 for existing memory methods. Because WikiTrace and Insights Only differ only in access to source runs, the higher Gain and lower Regression are attributable to that access. Relative to Insights Only, wrong or out-of-scope items cause half as many of WikiTrace’s regressions (Figure 4 ). Appendix C provides results for individual suites.


### 5 Analysis


#### 5.1 When Does Descent Help?

Insights Only gives the agent the same insights, so comparing it with WikiTrace on the same task and trial shows what access to source runs adds. We split WikiTrace’s test runs in the three text-world benchmarks into Descent runs, which open a compilation or raw trajectory, and Stay runs, which do not (Figure 6 ). WikiTrace descends more often on harder tasks, in 22% of runs on Easy tasks and 44% on Hard ones. Descent runs lead Insights Only by 1.6 to 4.7 points more than Stay runs at every difficulty and on every benchmark. The gap between Descent and Stay grows with difficulty, from 1.8 points on Easy tasks to 3.7 on Medium and 4.1 on Hard ones. These results suggest that source runs help more on harder tasks. The agent chooses when to descend, however, so Descent and Stay runs differ in more than the descent.

Stay runs also lead Insights Only, by 0.6 to 12.4 points. In Stay runs, WikiTrace reports 19 to 28% fewer insights as used, 3 3 After each run, the agent names the insights it applied. which suggests more selective use of memory. The reduction is larger in Descent runs, 38 to 57%.

#### 5.2 Why and How Does WikiTrace Descend?

Section 5.1 shows when descent helps, but not why the agent descends or what the opened run changes. To find out, an LLM judge reads the agent’s reasoning around each descent in the three text-world benchmarks and labels three things: its trigger , what prompted the descent; its target , what the agent looked for in the opened run; and its effect , what the run contributed to the agent’s next actions (Figures 9 and 9 ). The judge, gpt-5.6-sol at medium reasoning effort, agrees with a second judge, GLM-5.3 , at Cohen’s $\kappa$ of 0.77 on trigger and 0.63 on target. Effect is harder to judge, so its label is the majority of three judges (Appendix E.1 ).

> Figure 9: How WikiTrace uses evidence. WikiTrace’s score lead over Insights Only by effect, with descent shares in gray.


##### Why it descends and what it seeks

WikiTrace’s advantage over Insights Only concentrates on descents made after a problem. Descents that follow a blocked attempt, an observation that blocked the agent’s plan, or a doubt about an insight make up 36% of descents and lead Insights Only by 10.4 points, against 3.7 points for descents made with nothing gone wrong (Figure 9 ). The largest advantage, 17.3 points, comes when a blocked attempt sends the agent after a specific fact in past runs, which Insights Only has no way to look up. Routine checks are the most common descent (38%), and 90% of them read a similar run’s procedure, with a small advantage of 3.4 points.


##### How it uses source evidence

The opened run changes the agent’s plan in 43% of descending runs, by revising an insight (24%), adding a detail an insight leaves out (14%), or supplying a fact no insight covers (4%). These runs lead Insights Only by 8.3 points, against 4.4 points when the opened run only confirms the plan or goes unused (Figure 9 ). Figure 11 illustrates such a revision, with additional cases in Appendix E.2 .

Failed runs contain useful experience, but the lessons drawn from them depend on how the failure is interpreted. To examine their value for memory, we compare WikiTrace and Insights Only using insights from successful runs, failed runs, or both on PlanCraft and ScienceWorld. Figure 7 shows that both methods perform better with failed runs alone than with successful runs alone, and perform best with both. Failed runs therefore add guidance beyond what successful runs give.

However, this benefit accompanies a greater risk of regression when the extracted insights are provided alone. Insights Only has its highest Regression with failed runs alone, reaching 9.3% on PlanCraft and 9.4% on ScienceWorld. With the same insights, WikiTrace reduces these rates to 7.1% and 8.9%. These findings support keeping failed runs with their source evidence, so that agents can revisit the basis of a lesson before reusing it.


#### 5.4 How Cost-Effective Is WikiTrace?

> Figure 11: Qualitative example: revising a wrong insight (PlanCraft). Insight-0083 leads agents into the auto-craft mistake. Insights Only follows it and fails, but WikiTrace revises it through descent and succeeds.

To examine whether WikiTrace’s advantage comes from greater memory construction cost, we increase existing methods’ construction budgets by running their builders over the same training trajectories nine times for AWM and three times for ExpeL and ReasoningBank. Figure 10 shows that WikiTrace retains the highest average performance even with these larger baseline budgets. At test time, WikiTrace costs 1.17 times as much per task as Insights Only, averaged across suites, while improving average performance by 3.2 points. These results suggest that WikiTrace improves performance with modest additional test-time cost.


#### 5.5 Does WikiTrace Generalize?

WikiTrace also achieves the highest performance on all three evaluated suites with GLM-5.3-flash (Table 2 ). We evaluate all methods on PlanCraft and ScienceWorld at medium reasoning effort, using training executions generated by the same model to construct memory. WikiTrace averages 92.7, exceeding Insights Only by 1.8 percentage points and No Memory by 21.5. Each existing memory method falls below No Memory on at least one suite. This pattern mirrors the DeepSeek results, supporting the benefit of source-linked memory across the two models.


#### 5.6 Resolving Conflicting Insights

Figure 11 illustrates how WikiTrace resolves conflicting insights by revisiting source evidence. In PlanCraft, an insight from a failed run incorrectly rules out a valid procedure that another recommends. The successful run’s observations show that the procedure works. WikiTrace revises the insight and succeeds, whereas Insights Only, given identical insights, follows the wrong guidance and fails. This case shows how raw trajectories enable agents to reassess interpretations embedded in insights and compilations.


### 6 Conclusion

Lessons extracted from past executions are not validated by their outcomes alone, and reusing them without scrutiny can cause memory-induced degradation. We introduced WikiTrace, which links insights to source compilations and raw trajectories so that agents can recover missing details and reassess their guidance. Across five benchmarks, WikiTrace achieves the highest performance on every suite while increasing Gain and reducing Regression compared with injecting the same insights alone. These findings suggest that agent memory should preserve not only reusable lessons, but also the evidence needed to reassess them.


### Limitations

Memory is built from binary trajectory outcomes, so a compilation’s diagnosis of a failure, and any insight drawn from it, may be wrong. A source link lets the agent check an insight against its run but does not verify the insight. WikiTrace places every insight in the first message so that the agent never misses one, and the prompt therefore grows with the memory. A memory too large to place in full would need the agent to find the insights it needs, a retrieval problem this work leaves open. The causes of degradation (Section 3.2 ) and the descent labels (Section 5.2 ) come from an LLM judge that reads the agent’s reasoning, so they describe what that reasoning states, not what produced the outcome.

### Appendix A Experimental Details


#### A.1 Benchmark Suite Construction

Table 3 lists the suites. Every draw is seeded.


##### ALFWorld

Training holds 50 games of the training split, eight or nine per task type, in rooms that also appear in valid_seen. valid-seen is the full set of 101 valid_seen games filtered to rooms visited by the 50 training games, and valid-unseen uses all 134 valid_unseen games, set in four rooms absent from training.


##### ScienceWorld

We use ten of the 30 task types, boil, melt, freeze, change-the-state-of-matter-of, use-thermometer, measure-melting-point-known-substance, measure-melting-point-unknown-substance, test-conductivity, test-conductivity-of-unknown-substances, and chemistry-mix. Each meets three conditions.

- • Its variations pose distinct tasks.

- • Its training and test descriptions are largely disjoint.

- • Its reference solutions fit the simulator’s 100-step limit.

Training holds five variations per type from the training split, and test holds 100 variations from the test split whose description does not occur in training.


##### PlanCraft

We use solvable examples only, weighted toward medium and hard ones. Training holds 50 examples of the training split with distinct target items that also occur in the test split. plancraft-seen holds 100 test examples whose target is a training target, and plancraft-unseen 100 whose target occurs in no solvable training example.


##### AppWorld

A scenario groups three tasks and is solved only when all three pass, so every suite keeps scenarios whole. We use difficulty 2 and 3 only. Training holds all 18 such training scenarios (54 tasks), test-normal all 37 of the Test-Normal split (111 tasks), and test-challenge a draw of 33 of the 65 difficulty-3 scenarios of the Test-Challenge split (99 tasks). TGC counts tasks and SGC scenarios.


##### $\tau^{3}$ -bench (Retail)

We set aside the authors’ splits and divide the 114 tasks into 38 training and 76 test tasks, grouped by customer, so every customer with several tasks appears on both sides. The simulated user is gpt-4.1 , and the judge of conversation assertions is gpt-5.4-mini at medium reasoning effort.

### Appendix C Gain and Regression with Confidence Intervals


#### C.1 Confidence Intervals

Table 6 counts the tasks that each method moved per benchmark, the numerators and denominators behind the rates. Figure 13 draws the rates of Section 4.3 for each suite with 95% confidence intervals and the No Memory floor.


##### Intervals.

We resample tasks with replacement within each suite 10,000 times. A resampled task keeps its three No Memory trials and its three trials with each method, and every rate is recomputed on the same resample, so the methods stay paired. The suite average is taken within each resample, and its interval spans the 2.5th to 97.5th percentiles of these averages. 4 4 Resamples with no eligible ALFWorld-seen task for Gain (4.8%) are dropped.


##### No Memory floor.

$B_{i}$ is the majority of three No Memory trials, so a fresh run without memory can disagree with it by chance. We ran three further No Memory trials and score them against the same $B_{i}$ exactly as a method: the Regression floor is the share of their trials that fail a task with $B_{i}=1$ , and the Gain floor the share that solve a task with $B_{i}=0$ . Its interval comes from the same task resamples, in which each task keeps all six No Memory trials. A method’s excess is its rate minus the floor, with the interval of that difference over the resamples. A positive excess is added Gain, or, for Regression, added harm.

### Appendix E How WikiTrace Uses Its Evidence


#### E.1 Descent Judge

The judge behind Figures 9 and 9 reads one WikiTrace run in a text-world benchmark and labels every descent in it, where a descent is a stretch of consecutive memory calls that opens a compilation or a raw run and ends at the next game action. It receives the system prompt of Figure 21 and three user messages: the insights the agent saw, the agent’s full transcript with each descent marked, and the prompt of one axis (Figures 22 and 23 ). Each axis is a separate call. The trigger and the target are read from the agent’s reasoning before the descent, and the effect from what the agent does after it. Every label carries a verbatim quote from the agent’s messages, and a checker sends an answer back once if a quote is not found there.

gpt-5.6-sol labels the trigger and the target, and GLM-5.3 labels them too, agreeing at Cohen’s $\kappa$ of 0.77 and 0.63. The two judges agree on the effect only at $\kappa=0.46$ , so the effect label is the majority of these two and Claude Opus 5.5 (Fleiss’ $\kappa=0.49$ ). All three judges run at medium reasoning effort. Of the 508 descents, 46 have no majority. The judges label 508 descents in the 430 descending runs; one descent has no trigger or target because its answer failed the checker twice. The prompt calls a Ag blocked attempt a setback ; the other labels in the text are the prompt’s codes.
