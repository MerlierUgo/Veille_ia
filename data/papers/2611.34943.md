## Tracking Any Point with Agentic Visual Reasoning

A general-purpose Vision-Language Model (VLM) has surpassed expert vision models in many domains such as object detection and video recognition. However, point correspondence is known to be challenging for VLMs since it goes beyond object-level recognition: it requires pixel-level localization of the same physical surface point across observations, even when appearance changes or the point disappears and later reappears. In this paper, we introduce AgenTAP , a VLM-based agent that directly predicts point coordinates and visibility and actively reasons over videos. Starting from its own estimate, the agent autonomously chooses what to inspect and uses the resulting evidence to verify point identity and refine its predictions. With GPT-6 Astra, it surpasses expert vision models in both point localization and visibility prediction, without correspondence-specific fine-tuning. We further use AgenTAP to re-identify physical points after disappearance and reconnect fragmented tracks, constructing EgoTAP-4D , a dataset of 3D point trajectories in long real-world egocentric videos. On sampled reappearing points, its predictions approach human agreement, and adding EgoTAP-4D to D4RT training improves average 3D tracking performance .

### 2 Introduction

Visual correspondence is a fundamental capability in computer vision [ 51 , 19 , 24 ] . This task is traditionally tackled in two lines of research. Image matching [ 76 , 80 , 23 , 24 ] establishes correspondences across viewpoints, while point tracking [ 34 , 20 , 41 , 35 ] follows moving surfaces through time. Both rely on expert vision models trained to exploit appearance and temporal cues. As a result, preserving point identity remains challenging when appearance changes dramatically or a point disappears and later reappears.

> Figure 1: Tracking Any Point with Agentic Visual Reasoning. AgenTAP takes a top-down approach: a VLM agent hypothesizes point identity and visibility, selects visual tools to test its hypotheses, and refines predictions from the returned evidence. Here, AgenTAP locates a reappearing point that an expert vision model ( e.g. , AllTracker [ 35 ] ) incorrectly predicts as occluded.

Vision-language models (VLMs) have emerged as powerful general-purpose systems for visual understanding, reasoning, and interaction, demonstrating broad capabilities in interpreting complex scenes [ 46 , 48 ] , solving visual reasoning problems [ 36 ] , grounding instructions [ 102 , 71 ] , and autonomously operating computers [ 92 , 28 ] . However, visual correspondence remains an underexplored frontier for VLMs. Their understanding of objects and spatial relationships could help preserve point identity across appearance changes, occlusion, and reappearance. Our findings reveal a turning point: with agentic inference, GPT-6 Astra [ 58 ] becomes the first VLM in our evaluation to outperform expert vision models on the real-video benchmark. Fable 5.2 [ 2 ] performs on par, while other VLMs have yet to cross this threshold. We view this result as an early sign of a broader shift and expect more VLMs to follow as their capabilities advance. This crossover in turn motivates a broader question:

How far have general-purpose VLMs progressed toward reliable pixel-level physical correspondence?

We systematically investigate this question using TrackingBench (Sec. 4 ). We curate 2,000 queries from 272 real-video clips across six domains, selecting visually diverse scenes and balancing visibility, motion, and frame gap. An additional synthetic evaluation and independently annotated held-out videos probe distribution shifts and generalization. Without tools, substantial gaps remain: VLMs fall far behind expert vision models on synthetic videos, and even GPT-6 Astra often identifies the right region without locating the exact point. It places 87.1% of visible points within 16 pixels but only 23.8% within one pixel. Other errors are semantically plausible but physically wrong, such as selecting the same part of a similar-looking instance, echoing prior findings on VLM hallucination [ 47 , 10 , 32 ] .

This gap reflects a limitation of direct prediction: a VLM reasons over two full images without the ability to inspect finer details or verify a candidate location. We introduce AgenTAP , a VLM agent that turns an initial correspondence estimate into a prediction verified against visual evidence (Fig. 1 ; Sec. 5 ). The VLM first predicts point coordinates and visibility before seeing optional proposals from expert vision models. Its understanding of the scene then guides which magnified views, visual comparisons, and geometric measurements it requests, giving it access to the evidence needed for precise localization. Expert outputs serve as additional evidence: the VLM can revise both coordinates and visibility and depart from every proposal, including by recovering a point that a tracker declares invisible. Agentic inference improves correspondence for every backbone evaluated with AgenTAP , most at strict localization tolerances. With GPT-6 Astra, AgenTAP surpasses all evaluated expert vision models on all four metrics under matched two-image and full-video input settings (Tab. 1 ). Some GPT-6 Astra predictions locate the queried physical point more accurately than the reference human annotations [ 19 ] .

This also enables continuous point trajectories in long real-world videos. Growing efforts in robot learning use point tracking for manipulation [ 90 ] and policy learning from egocentric human demonstrations [ 42 , 50 ] . Reliable 3D trajectory annotations could turn egocentric recordings into beneficial supervision for learning how humans interact with the physical world. However, existing trackers often fail to reconnect points after disappearance, fragmenting trajectories and limiting supervision for long-horizon interactions. Our pipeline uses trackers to follow visible points and AgenTAP to recover the same physical points when they reappear, connecting their earlier and later tracks. On a manually annotated sample of reappearing points, AgenTAP with GPT-6 Astra achieves 76.9 APD versus 40.1 for the best evaluated tracker (Tab. 5 ). The resulting EgoTAP-4D provides 3D trajectory annotations for egocentric recordings substantially longer than the clips in existing real-video datasets (Sec. 7 ; Tab. 3 ). Adding EgoTAP-4D to D4RT [ 100 ] training improves average 3D tracking performance (Tab. 4 ).

In summary, our work makes three contributions: (1) TrackingBench , a diverse cross-domain benchmark that characterizes the emerging crossover between general-purpose VLMs and expert vision models, together with the remaining gaps in localization, visibility, and generalization; (2) AgenTAP , a VLM agent that improves correspondence across all evaluated backbones and, with GPT-6 Astra, surpasses all evaluated expert vision models on all four metrics; and (3) EgoTAP-4D , a 3D point tracking dataset from long real-world videos that improves D4RT performance, together with its AgenTAP -based annotation pipeline.


### 3 Related Work

> Figure 2: Example queries from TrackingBench . The first six columns show real-video domains [ 62 , 90 , 19 , 6 , 106 ] , and the last shows synthetic data used in the analysis. Each column pairs a reference frame (top) with a target frame (bottom). Red crosshairs mark the same physical surface point. Visual correspondence. Image matching establishes correspondences across views using visual descriptors and spatial context [ 51 , 76 , 80 , 23 , 24 ] , and features of large vision models yield correspondences without matching-specific training [ 83 , 79 ] . Point tracking additionally exploits temporal information to maintain physical surface-point identity and estimate visibility through motion and occlusion. PIPs [ 34 ] and TAPIR [ 20 ] combine per-frame matching with temporal refinement, CoTracker3 [ 41 ] jointly updates tracks across points and time, and AllTracker [ 35 ] extends tracking to dense correspondences between a reference frame and each video frame. TAPNext [ 105 , 39 ] casts tracking as next-token prediction, and Track-On [ 4 ] tracks online with a memory. Pseudo-labels from real videos improve generalization [ 21 , 41 ] , and a learned verifier can select reliable ones among tracker predictions [ 5 ] . Benchmarks on long egocentric and dynamic videos show that points leaving and re-entering the view remain a key failure mode [ 15 , 18 ] . In AgenTAP , the VLM forms its own correspondence hypothesis, actively selects image regions and visual operations to test it, and revises its prediction. This verification happens at inference and re-identifies points after they reappear, which we use to build EgoTAP-4D .

Spatial grounding in VLMs. VLMs ground language to image regions [ 102 , 71 ] and predict points for object and affordance localization [ 17 , 99 ] . Molmo2 [ 13 ] extends pointing to video and object tracking, while MolmoPoint [ 12 ] improves localization through dedicated grounding tokens. Evaluation spans language-guided pointing [ 11 ] , candidate correspondence selection [ 26 , 98 ] , and semantic matching across object instances [ 22 ] . CrossPoint-Bench [ 94 ] evaluates physical point correspondence and visibility in indoor scenes, scoring pointing by whether a prediction falls within the target mask. Its CroPond model is fine-tuned on geometrically generated correspondence pairs, whereas AgenTAP reaches precise correspondence with general-purpose VLMs through agentic inference alone.

Agentic visual reasoning. Visual programming connects language reasoning to executable vision tools [ 82 , 33 ] , while Visual Sketchpad [ 36 ] enables iterative reasoning with visual feedback. V* [ 96 ] , ZoomEye [ 78 ] , and DeepEyes [ 104 ] make visual inspection adaptive through search, zooming, or learned policies. ACTrack [ 7 ] coordinates expert vision models for object tracking, invoking a VLM under persistent disagreement to arbitrate between candidate boxes. AgentSTAR [ 53 ] combines VLM reasoning with numerical optimization for shape tracking: the VLM guides pose searches and inspects renderings, while an optimizer produces precise poses. In AgenTAP , the VLM itself predicts and revises the point’s coordinates and visibility. Tools only supply evidence, so its answer can depart from any proposal.


### 4 TrackingBench

TrackingBench contains 2,000 two-frame point-correspondence queries selected from public point-tracking benchmarks, covering 272 video clips across six real-video domains (Fig. 2 ). Because public videos may appear in VLM training data, we also capture 15 new videos and annotate a held-out set of 1,814 queries to check that results on TrackingBench do not reflect data leakage (Sec. 4.2 ).


#### 4.1 Problem Setup

Each query consists of a source image $I_{s}$ , a marked visible point $p_{s}$ , and an unmarked target image $I_{t}$ . A method predicts whether the same physical surface point is visible in $I_{t}$ , giving a visibility label $\widehat{v}_{t}\in\{0,1\}$ . If predicted visible, it also returns the point’s 2D image coordinates $\widehat{p}_{t}$ . Otherwise, no position is required. Both the camera and surface may move, so the task requires locating the same surface point, not merely recognizing the same object or part. A point is invisible if it is occluded, lies outside the image, or belongs to a surface facing away from the camera, even when the object itself remains visible.


#### 4.2 Benchmark Curation

Source videos. We pool clips from public point-tracking benchmarks [ 19 , 31 , 45 , 6 , 15 , 73 , 62 , 18 , 91 , 90 , 106 , 49 ] to cover diverse scenes and motions. Uniform sampling would overrepresent larger sources and their recurring recording conditions. We therefore allocate source quotas based on visual diversity measured with DINOv3 embeddings [ 79 ] . Within each source, we cluster clips by appearance, motion, and visibility, then select those nearest the cluster centers.

Benchmark queries. We construct a pool of 14,896 queries: 12,496 from real videos and 2,400 from synthetic videos. For each annotated track, we pair its first visible observation and, when available, one randomly sampled later visible observation with later labeled target frames. We include invisible targets, exclude missing annotations, and balance displacement, frame gap, and target visibility. The synthetic queries are used only for real–synthetic comparisons in the analysis. To reduce the computational cost and runtime of evaluation, we select 2,000 queries from the real-video pool as the main benchmark, stratified by domain, target visibility, whole-track motion, and frame gap. Across evaluated VLMs, scores and rankings on the 2,000-query benchmark closely agree with those on the full 12,496-query real-video pool.

Held-out annotations. We independently capture and annotate 15 in-the-wild videos in three stages: (1) an annotator selects a visible source point and labels its position or invisibility in target frames; (2) a second annotator labels the same frames without seeing the first answers; (3) a third annotator labels disputed targets without seeing either earlier answer. Two labels agree if both mark the point invisible or both mark it visible within four pixels at the evaluation scale. For disputed targets, the third label and an agreeing earlier label determine the final annotation. We average agreeing visible coordinates and exclude unresolved targets and unusable tracks, retaining 1,814 queries. Videos and labels remain hidden.


#### 4.3 Evaluation Protocol

All main comparisons and ablations use the same 2,000 queries. Two-image methods receive only the source and target images, while full-video methods can also access other frames. In direct prediction, a VLM reasons over the inputs and answers in a single call without tools, whereas agents may inspect images and use tools over multiple steps. Missing or invalid answers count as failures.

> Figure 3: AgenTAP . (1) Initial prediction: the VLM agent inspects both views and records its own prediction. (2) Optional expert consultation: expert vision models propose candidate locations. (3) Iterative visual verification: the agent selects visual tools to test its hypothesis, revises the point’s location and visibility, and re-checks as needed.

Metrics. Following TAP-Vid [ 19 ] , we evaluate on a normalized $256\times 256$ grid. We report APD for localization accuracy, AJ for joint localization and visibility, and OA for visibility accuracy. APD and AJ are averaged over distance thresholds of 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 pixels. Unlike TAP-Vid’s position-only accuracy, our APD additionally requires a visible prediction. EPE averages position errors on ground-truth-visible targets, capped at 32 pixels. All metrics are pooled across queries. Curation, annotation, and evaluation details appear in supplementary Sec. A .


### 5 AgenTAP


#### 5.1 Overview

Human visual reasoning draws on an understanding of surfaces and motion to guide where to look and which details to compare. Motivated by this interaction between reasoning and observation, AgenTAP lets a VLM independently predict point coordinates and visibility, then actively test its prediction (Fig. 3 ). The VLM selects visual tools (Sec. 5.2 ) to inspect image regions, compare local details, and refine the point’s location and visibility. Each observation informs both the prediction and what to inspect next. Expert vision models optionally provide candidate locations for verification, while the VLM determines the final correspondence. The procedure requires no correspondence-specific VLM fine-tuning .

#### 5.2 Visual Tools

The VLM receives descriptions of the tools in Fig. 3 and chooses which to invoke and their inputs.

Image inspection. With view , the VLM chooses regions and scales to inspect scene context, texture, and boundaries, zooming in or out and enhancing contrast as needed. Grids and point overlays use original image coordinates. The marks tool returns source-marker coordinates.

Correspondence checking. The check tool displays source and candidate target crops side by side and as an overlay. Landmarks and boundaries help assess surface identity and spatial offsets. Rechecking revised coordinates tests whether refinement preserves the queried surface point.

Coordinate estimation. The VLM can call fit with landmark correspondences it identifies in the source and target images. The tool fits an affine transformation or homography to these pairs and maps the source point to a target coordinate. This estimate assumes that the landmarks and query point share a local transformation. Fitting residuals and a visualization help the VLM assess the resulting estimate. The match tool uses template matching or SIFT [ 51 ] within a VLM-selected target region, returning candidates for further visual verification .

Temporal inspection. With full-video input, frame shows VLM-selected intermediate frames and regions. Surface motion and orientation help resolve point identity and visibility. Supplementary Sec. B provides tool descriptions and additional visualization options .


#### 5.3 Agentic Inference

Initial prediction. In Stage 1 of Fig. 3 , the agent inspects source and target overviews, identifies the queried surface, and records a position or an invisible decision. This prediction precedes expert proposals and intermediate-frame inspection, preserving the agent’s independent estimate.

Optional expert consultation. When enabled, expert proposals provide alternative correspondence hypotheses for visual verification (Stage 2 of Fig. 3 ). The VLM may accept, refine, or reject these proposals, and its final answer need not match any supplied candidate .

Iterative visual verification. The VLM tests correspondence hypotheses against visual evidence, choosing subsequent inspections based on what it observes. The prompt specifies the verification objective and available tools, and the VLM selects each operation and its inputs.

For a visible candidate, the agent compares its position relative to nearby landmarks with that of the source point, as in Stage 3 of Fig. 3 . If the surface correspondence appears correct but the position remains uncertain, the VLM can adjust the coordinate directly or use fit with landmark correspondences. Crops and geometric estimates retain native image coordinates. A location obtained from a magnified view or landmark fit can then be marked on the target and checked again. In the illustrated example, a nearby side marking guides the correction, which the agent then re-checks .

If the visual evidence instead suggests the wrong object, part, or surface, the VLM can choose to revisit the source image and search for a different correspondence. The marked source point remains fixed, and the VLM revises its interpretation of the surface carrying that point. Position and visibility are reconsidered together: further inspection may recover a missed point or reveal that the queried surface is hidden despite a plausible match on a visible surface.

The agent returns a target coordinate or an invisible decision after verification. Supplementary Sec. B details the protocol, prompts, and example agent sessions .

#### 6.1 Direct Prediction

Visibility and localization. VLMs that localize the point better also judge more reliably whether it is still visible (Fig. 5 ), suggesting that both draw on the same scene understanding. Visibility is the harder half. VLMs rarely call a point hidden: most mark close to nine in ten queries visible, although one in five targets is hidden. Answering “visible” every time would already reach 79.0 OA, and only six VLMs in Fig. 5 beat this by more than two points. The two skills can still come apart: Inkling, Inkling-Small, and Qwen3.5-9B reach about this OA yet rarely find the exact point, whereas Molmo2, which sees the video, localizes better than its visibility decisions suggest. The strongest VLM, GPT-6 Astra, already beats every two-image expert model in APD and OA by direct prediction, though not yet in AJ (Tab. 1 ).

Model scaling. Within the Qwen3.5 dense family, larger models do better, although APD plateaus from 9B to 27B (Fig. 4 a). Across families, Gemma-4-31B-it beats the much larger Qwen3.5-122B-A10B, so total parameter count alone does not predict correspondence ability.

Held-out generalization. Across models, rankings on independently annotated held-out videos closely match those on TrackingBench (Fig. 4 b). This agreement supports the benchmark as a reliable evaluation of correspondence ability and eases concerns that strong performance reflects data leakage. TrackingBench is also harder: nine of the eleven models score lower on it than on the held-out set.


#### 6.2 Agentic Inference

AgenTAP gives the same VLMs visual tools: they still reason, but can now inspect, compare, and verify their answers.

AgenTAP helps across backbones. AgenTAP improves precision for all backbones (Tab. 1 ). Seven of the eight then beat every two-image expert model in APD, and GPT-6 Astra, from only two frames, beats every full-video tracker in APD and OA. Given the same input as the expert models, AgenTAP with GPT-6 Astra beats all of them on all four metrics, including high-resolution AllTracker, whose own predictions it receives as proposals. Claude Fable 5.2 gives the best APD and EPE in both settings. Applied frame by frame, AgenTAP turns two-frame correspondences into trajectories that follow the ground-truth motion and keeps points that AllTracker loses (Fig. 7 ).

AgenTAP narrows the backbone gap. AgenTAP helps weaker backbones more. Among the six strongest, it adds 30.8 APD to the weakest, Inkling 2 Small, but only 12.0 to the strongest, GPT-6 Astra. Their gap shrinks from 26.0 APD under direct prediction to 7.3 with AgenTAP : given tools, a much weaker VLM nearly catches up.

Localization precision. Without tools, GPT-6 Astra already places 87.1% of visible points within 16 pixels, but only 23.8% within one pixel (Fig. 4 d): it finds the right region, not the exact pixel. AgenTAP mainly fixes the last few pixels. Its improvement is small at 16 pixels (+3.9) but large at one- and two-pixel thresholds (+16.4 and +18.9), as magnified views and side-by-side checks reveal details that are lost when the VLM reasons over the full images alone.

Temporal separation. All methods lose accuracy as the frame gap grows, but high-resolution AllTracker loses the most, so AgenTAP ’s lead over it widens at longer gaps (Fig. 4 c). When a hidden point reappears in the target frame, full-video trackers call it invisible about three times as often as two-image AgenTAP (supplementary Sec. C ).

Robustness of AgenTAP . AgenTAP fixes many wrong initial answers while rarely breaking correct ones. From each session’s first prediction, made before any expert proposal, to its final answer, corrections by AgenTAP with GPT-6 Astra far outnumber regressions at every threshold, mostly by moving a visible point to the right location (Fig. 4 f).

#### 6.3 Ablations

Tab. 2 adds AgenTAP ’s components one at a time, with GPT-6 Astra and the open-weight Inkling 2 Small.

Agentic tool use. Agentic tool use helps every model. A fixed zoom harness, which shows the VLM preset close-ups around its direct prediction, barely helps. Letting the VLM choose its own inspections instead, still without proposals, improves both backbones and already lets GPT-6 Astra beat every two-image expert model on all metrics.

Expert proposals. Expert proposals improve the weaker backbone, Inkling 2 Small, more than GPT-6 Astra. Both models usually retain a supplied proposal, but GPT-6 Astra is more reliable when revising them: its changes usually improve localization, whereas Inkling 2 Small’s help in 53.5% of cases and hurt in 38.7%. To assess whether the agents go beyond proposal selection, we evaluate an oracle that chooses the better-localized RoMa v2 or AllTracker proposal for each point while using AllTracker’s visibility. This oracle reaches 59.7 APD. GPT-6 Astra exceeds it even without proposals, demonstrating strong independent correspondence ability. Inkling 2 Small exceeds it only with proposals, indicating greater reliance on external candidates. Even with the true visibility, it reaches only 53.1 AJ and 67.3 APD, below AgenTAP ’s 54.9 and 71.4.

Temporal evidence. The VLM can exploit video, mainly to judge whether a point is visible: with two images, AgenTAP with GPT-6 Astra adds 12.0 APD and 0.8 OA over direct prediction, and with the full video OA reaches 88.2. With the full video, AgenTAP improves both backbones and, with GPT-6 Astra, surpasses high-resolution AllTracker, whose predictions it receives as proposals. When both call a point visible, AgenTAP almost always keeps the tracker’s position. Where they disagree about visibility, AgenTAP is right four times out of five, mostly on visible points the tracker marks invisible. The frames between the endpoints add 1.6 AJ and 1.1 OA (supplementary Sec. C.3 ).


#### 6.4 Analysis

> Figure 6: EgoTAP-4D annotation pipeline. From a stereo egocentric video with camera poses and hand motion capture, we recover metric depth and camera poses, label each region as static (blue), moving (orange), or hand (green), and build 2D trajectories from the fused scene geometry, AllTracker, and MANO. When an object returns after leaving the view, AgenTAP with GPT-6 Astra locates its query points again (Fig. 9 , first row). All trajectories are then lifted to 3D in one world frame.

Real vs. synthetic. With two-image input, GPT-6 Astra’s direct predictions outperform AllTracker in several real-video domains. On synthetic videos, however, AllTracker clearly outperforms GPT-6 Astra and Opus 5.5 (Fig. 4 e). Models that perform well on real videos can therefore struggle with synthetic scenes, possibly due to differences in training data.

Improvement by domain. AgenTAP with GPT-6 Astra improves least in driving scenes (+4.8 APD), where direct prediction is strongest, and most in biomedical scenes and sports (+14.1 and +13.7). It also leads the hardest domain, animals and nature, with 63.5 APD against 49.5 for two-image AllTracker (Tab. 1 ).

Semantic-part hallucinations. Like humans, VLMs can confuse similar body parts or similar-looking objects as they move. Even after visual inspection, the agent may place the point on the other eggshell half or gripper finger ( supplementary Sec. C ). Surrounding details may also look alike, so a closer look may not reveal the mistake.

Computational cost. AgenTAP reads 141K–184K input tokens per query, and sharing a session among 16 points or three frames saves 56–70% or 44–52% of them with similar accuracy (supplementary Secs. B.4 and C.4 ).


### 7 EgoTAP-4D

Egocentric video is a stress test for correspondence: as the wearer moves and handles objects, surfaces leave the view, disappear behind hands, and return seconds later. Expert vision models often lose such points (Sec. 6.2 ), and existing real-video 3D point tracking datasets span only a few seconds per clip (Tab. 3 ). EgoTAP-4D fills this gap with 3D point trajectories over long egocentric recordings, using a VLM to recover each point when it reappears.

After a per-recording calibration of the Xperience-10M stereo depth, its median error stays within $\pm$ 1% up to 3 m (supplementary Sec. D.2.1 ). Geometry handles static surfaces: GMOS object masks [ 97 ] separate them from moving ones, and every point on the fused static surface projects into every frame, so static points can be sampled anywhere, at any density. A tracker handles moving surfaces while they stay in view: AllTracker [ 35 ] follows them and bridges gaps of up to a second, and motion capture provides hands where available. A VLM handles what both miss. When an object returns after at least three seconds, AgenTAP with GPT-6 Astra locates 10 of its points from before the gap in a frame after the return. We accept a prediction only if it lies on the same object, has valid depth, and is not on a hand, and an AllTracker cross-check on a second post-return frame rejects inconsistent matches. Of 14,091 reappearance image pairs with 10 points each, 7,057 pass filtering, and 23,072 of their 70,570 query points pass these checks and enter the dataset (supplementary Sec. D.2.4 ). ### 9 Conclusion

Finding the same physical point across views and time is a longstanding problem in computer vision. We approached it as visual reasoning: with AgenTAP , a general-purpose VLM forms a hypothesis about the point, inspects the images to test it, and revises its answer. With GPT-6 Astra, AgenTAP surpasses expert vision models on TrackingBench , especially on reappearing points. This shows that VLM reasoning has advanced from objects to individual pixels. We used AgenTAP to reconnect reappearing points in long egocentric videos for EgoTAP-4D . Applied at scale, it can provide 3D training data for expert vision models and for robots that learn from human demonstrations. We hope the community will build on agentic tracking to develop models with stronger spatial intelligence.

### Appendix A TrackingBench

TrackingBench is built in three steps. First, we select video clips from public point-tracking benchmarks (Sec. A.1 ). Second, we build a pool of 14,896 queries from their released tracks (Sec. A.2 ). Third, we sample the 2,000-query main benchmark from the 12,496 real-video queries of this pool (Sec. A.3 ). Because public videos may appear in VLM training data, we also annotate 1,814 held-out queries on 15 new videos (Sec. A.4 ). Sec. A.5 details the evaluation protocol.


#### A.1 Source Videos

We draw 7,172 candidate clips from public point-tracking benchmarks (Tab. S1 ), allocate a quota to each source, and select representative clips within each source.

We represent each clip by the mean DINOv3 ViT-L [ 79 ] features of 16 frames, normalized to unit length. A greedy covering procedure [ 27 ] counts how many representative clips are needed to cover each source within a distance of 0.7. Each quota grows with the square root of this count, so visually diverse sources receive more clips while large sources do not dominate. Every source contributes at least eight clips when available, and DriveTrack and ADT together contribute at most 50.

Within each source, we cluster the appearance features together with five tracking attributes: camera rotation, visible fraction, points per labeled frame, clip length, and dynamic fraction. The attributes add variation in tracking conditions that appearance alone misses. We log-transform points per labeled frame and clip length, standardize all five attributes, scale them by 0.5, and concatenate them with the appearance features. With a fixed seed, we then select the unused clip nearest to each cluster center (Fig. S1 ).

We select 300 clips, remove the 8 STIR clips because their landmarks lack verified temporal identities [ 77 ] , and add 20 TAP-Vid Kubric validation clips for synthetic analysis [ 19 , 31 ] , giving 312 clips. The 28 Kubric and RGB-Stacking clips are synthetic and stay out of the main benchmark.

#### A.2 Query Pool

Each query pairs a reference observation of a released track with a target frame, as described in Sec. 4.2 . Query points thus inherit their identities from the released tracks, as in TAP-Vid [ 19 ] . To avoid concentrating on nearby frames and nearly static points, we sample reference–target pairs across strata of displacement, frame gap, target visibility, and intermediate invisibility. The smallest-displacement stratum receives half weight. At most four targets per reference and 40 track–reference pairs per clip limit repeated queries. This yields 14,896 queries from 5,958 tracks, 12,496 of them from real videos and 2,400 from synthetic ones.


#### A.3 Main Benchmark

To reduce the computational cost of evaluation, all main comparisons use 2,000 queries sampled from the 12,496 real-video queries. We fix the sampling rule before evaluation, then check that the sample represents the pool and examine its labels.

We stratify by content domain, target visibility, whole-track motion (static or dynamic, defined below), and frame gap (1–16, 17–64, 65–256, and more than 256 frames). Each of the 85 nonempty strata receives a share of the 2,000 queries proportional to its size in the pool. We sample uniformly within each stratum with seed 0. The rule and the seed were fixed before any score on the benchmark was observed.

The benchmark covers 272 clips from 13 real-video sources and six content domains: everyday activities, sports and performances, robotic manipulation, driving and street scenes, animals and nature, and biomedical scenes. Domains describe scene content, so one source can span several domains. The benchmark has 1,579 visible and 421 invisible targets, and 1,296 queries on dynamic tracks and 704 on static ones. A track is dynamic if any of its visible annotations lies on an object that GMOS [ 97 ] identifies as moving, and we review these labels manually. The label thus describes the whole track rather than the queried interval. Frame gaps count frames rather than seconds, since frame rates differ across sources.

The benchmark matches the pool in domain, frame gap, visibility, and motion (Fig. S2 ). Scores match as well. Across the 29 systems scored on both sets, APD rankings on the benchmark and the pool have Kendall $\tau=0.995$ , and APD differs by at most 1.1 points (Fig. S3 ). Models run only on the benchmark, including all agents, GPT-6.1 Sol, Claude Fable 5.2, and Inkling 2 Small, have no pool scores and are not included. Over 200 alternative sampling seeds, the 95% range of a system’s APD stays within $\pm 1.3$ points of its pool score for the median system and within $\pm 2.3$ points for every system.

#### A.4 Held-out Annotations

To check that results on TrackingBench do not reflect data leakage, we capture 15 new videos, totaling 33.7 minutes of animals and human activities, and annotate them in the three stages of Sec. 4.2 . Four annotators take part, and three of them contribute videos.

The uploader selects a reference frame and a physical surface point, then labels the point in about 20 target frames before and after the reference. A visible point is clicked, and a hidden one is marked occluded or outside the image. This gives 96 tracks and 1,870 targets.

A second annotator labels the same targets without seeing the first answers and may reject an unsuitable reference point. One rejection removes 19 targets. The two annotators agree on 89.6% of the remaining 1,851 targets. Where both mark the point visible, their median distance is 0.42 pixels on the $256\times 256$ grid.

A third annotator labels the 192 disputed targets without seeing either earlier answer, and the final label comes from the third label and the earlier label it agrees with (Sec. 4.2 ). We exclude 18 unresolved targets and a second unsuitable reference point with its 19 targets. The final set has 1,814 queries on 94 tracks, with 1,364 visible targets and 631 targets before the reference. The median gap between reference and target is 5.5 seconds, and the 90th percentile is 59.7 seconds. The videos and labels remain hidden. Fig. 4 b of the main paper compares direct prediction on this set with the benchmark.


#### A.5 Evaluation Protocol

All methods are scored with the protocol of Sec. 4.3 . This section defines its metrics and the settings of the expert vision models.

Each query $q$ has a ground-truth visibility $v_{q}\in\{0,1\}$ and, if $v_{q}=1$ , a position $p_{q}$ in the native pixels of a $W_{q}\times H_{q}$ target image. A method returns a predicted visibility $\hat{v}_{q}$ and, if $\hat{v}_{q}=1$ , a position $\hat{p}_{q}$ . A missing or unreadable answer counts as a wrong visibility decision. When $v_{q}=\hat{v}_{q}=1$ , the error is measured on the $256\times 256$ grid of TAP-Vid [ 19 ] ,

[Tableau] $$d_{q}=\left\lVert\operatorname{diag}\!\left(\tfrac{255}{W_{q}-1},\tfrac{255}{H_{q}-1}\right)\left(\hat{p}_{q}-p_{q}\right)\right\rVert_{2},$$

and otherwise $d_{q}=\infty$ . This scaling does not constrain the image resolution given to a model.

A query is a hit at threshold $x$ if $v_{q}=\hat{v}_{q}=1$ and $d_{q}<x$ . With the thresholds $X=\{1,2,4,8,16\}$ ,

[Tableau] $$\delta^{x}=\frac{\#\{\text{hits at }x\}}{\#\{q:v_{q}=1\}},\qquad\mathrm{APD}=\frac{1}{|X|}\sum_{x\in X}\delta^{x}.$$

AJ is the average Jaccard of TAP-Vid,

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{AJ}=\frac{1}{|X|}\sum_{x\in X}\frac{\mathrm{TP}_{x}}{\mathrm{TP}_{x}+\mathrm{FP}_{x}+\mathrm{FN}_{x}},$$

where $\mathrm{TP}_{x}$ counts hits, $\mathrm{FP}_{x}$ counts queries predicted visible that are not hits, and $\mathrm{FN}_{x}$ counts visible targets that are not hits. A missing answer on an invisible target also counts as a false positive. OA, the occlusion accuracy, is the share of queries whose predicted visibility matches the ground truth, with missing answers counted as wrong. EPE averages $\min(d_{q},32)$ over the visible targets, so a visible target predicted invisible or left unanswered contributes 32 pixels. All metrics pool the queries of a set rather than averaging over clips.

Tab. S2 rescores the same predictions in native pixels, with $d_{q}$ computed without the rescaling above and the same thresholds and EPE cap. These scores depend on image resolution, so we compare them only within that table. AgenTAP still achieves the best score on every metric in both input settings, and with GPT-6 Astra it surpasses all expert vision models on all four metrics.

RoMa v2 [ 24 ] runs at its default precise setting and predicts the point visible when its overlap probability is at least 0.05. AllTracker [ 35 ] uses its official evaluation settings at $384\times 512$ , and its high-resolution variant processes each clip with the long side at 1,024 pixels. It predicts the point visible when the product of its visibility and confidence is at least 0.6. CoTracker3 [ 41 ] runs offline at $384\times 512$ and predicts the point visible when its visibility is at least 0.6. With full video, the trackers receive every frame from the reference frame to the end of the clip, and with two images only the reference and target frames.

BootsTAPIR [ 21 ] and TAPNext++ [ 39 ] in Table 1 use the released PyTorch checkpoints at $256\times 256$ in FP32, without fine-tuning. For TAPNext++, this is the public PointOdyssey/Kubric-1024 checkpoint, not the final model of its paper trained with DynHumans. Both run on the full video. BootsTAPIR uses offline temporal refinement, and TAPNext++ runs causally, carrying its state across 16-frame chunks. BootsTAPIR predicts visible when $(1-\sigma(o))(1-\sigma(d))>0.5$ , where $o$ and $d$ are its occlusion and expected-distance logits. TAPNext++ thresholds its visibility probability at 0.5. Predictions are mapped back to native coordinates and scored like all other methods.


### Appendix B AgenTAP

In AgenTAP , a VLM answers each query by calling a small image tool and reasoning over what it returns. This section describes the tool (Sec. B.1 ), the staged protocol and its prompt (Sec. B.2 ), how each backbone is run (Sec. B.3 and Tab. S4 ), its computational cost (Sec. B.4 and Tab. S4 ), and example sessions (Sec. B.5 ).


#### B.1 Tools

Each query runs in its own container, which holds only the images of the query and the tool, with no network access and no dataset or ground-truth annotations. The agent calls the tool from the command line (Fig. S5 ). The tools of Sec. 5.2 are commands of this one program, zoom.py . Two further commands, commit and frames , run the staged protocol and list the intermediate frames. Most commands return an image, which we call a view, overlaid with a coordinate grid in native image pixels (a 0–1000 grid for the two locally served models of Sec. B.3 ). view can mark a candidate position on the target or enhance contrast, and check shows the reference point next to a candidate position, side by side, blended, and as a checkerboard. fit and match return proposed coordinates that the agent can inspect, revise, or discard.

marks

Returns the exact centers of the markers drawn on the reference image.

$ marks

$\rightarrow$ {"center": [257.0, 310.5], …}

view

Renders a chosen region of the reference or the target under a grid in native pixel coordinates.

$ view reference --center 200 280 --size 300

view --mark

The same command can mark candidate coordinates ( --mark ) or enhance the contrast ( --enhance ).

$ view target --center 251 310 --size 80 --mark 251 310

commit

Records the initial prediction and returns it next to the expert proposals.

$ commit 230 332

check

Compares the patches around A and around a candidate: side by side, blended, and as a checkerboard.

$ check --ref 257 310.5 --tgt 251 310 --size 64

fit

Maps A through an affine transform or a homography fitted to landmark pairs chosen by the agent.

$ fit --pair 229.7 285.4 229.4 285.1 … --point 257 310.5

match

Searches a target box for the patch around A, by template matching or SIFT.

$ match --ref 257 310.5 --size 48 --search 150 230 360 400

frames , frame

List the intermediate frames and render one of them, or a region of it.

$ frames

$\rightarrow$ {"available_frames": [1, 2, …, 22], …}

$ frame 21 --center 370 155 --size 100

#### B.2 Protocol

The protocol follows the three stages of Fig. 3 .

The agent opens at most two overviews, each covering at least a third of the image in both dimensions, and records its initial prediction with commit .

If the initial prediction is visible, commit returns one view that shows it next to the RoMa v2 and AllTracker proposals. An expert’s proposal is shown only if that expert predicts the point visible and inside the image. The agent receives no other information about the experts’ visibility decisions. After an invisible initial prediction, no proposals are shown.

The agent then has five more views to verify its answer and may change both the position and the visibility. These limits count only views rendered by the tool, and the agent can also open the two original images. The final answer is a target position or an invisible decision, and a missing or invalid answer counts as a failure (Sec. A.5 ).

The prompt is the agent instructions (Fig. S6 ) followed by the task text of the query (Fig. S7 ). Direct prediction receives only the task text.

With full video, the RoMa v2 proposal stays the same, the AllTracker proposal comes from its high-resolution prediction on the whole video, and after the initial prediction the agent can also inspect intermediate frames with frames and frame . These views count toward the five verification views. The intermediate-frame ablation of Sec. C.3 keeps this proposal but shows only the two endpoint images.


#### B.3 Settings

Tab. S4 lists how each backbone runs. Every backbone uses the same inspection commands with at most eight rendered views per session, and a session that ends without a valid answer counts as a failure. The six hosted backbones follow the staged protocol above. The two open-weight models served locally, Qwen3.8-27B and Gemma-4-31B-it, follow the same protocol with the same proposals on a 0–1000 coordinate grid, with longer time limits because a local model takes longer per step.


#### B.4 Runtime and Computational Cost

Tab. S4 reports tokens and time per query for GPT-6.1 Sol, GPT-6 Astra, and Inkling 2 Small. GPT-6 Astra opens on average 4.3 views per two-image session and 4.5 with video access, and GPT-6.1 Sol opens 5.2. The expert proposals add less than 7 GPU-seconds per query and are not included. For the free-form GPT-6.1 Sol agent of Sec. C.4.1 , sharing a session among 16 points lowers the mean tokens per query by 56–70%. Sessions are independent, so they run in parallel. With 8 concurrent sessions, GPT-6 Astra with video access answers all 2,000 queries in 4.0 hours, about 500 queries per hour. While this computational cost is high, distilling the agent into an efficient model, such as a compact VLM or AllTracker, is a natural next step and could enable real-time tracking with AgenTAP .

#### C.4 Scaling Reference Points and Target Frames

For each clip, we select one reference frame and 16 points that remain visible at target frames 18, 36, and 54 frames later. We choose points farthest-first, keeping them at least 5% of the image width apart so that their markers do not overlap. ### Appendix D EgoTAP-4D

Fig. 6 of the main paper summarizes how geometry, tracking, and VLM reconnection produce EgoTAP-4D annotations. This section describes the data sources (Sec. D.1 ), the annotation pipeline (Sec. D.2 ), the annotation quality (Sec. D.3 ), and the D4RT experiments (Sec. D.4 ).


#### D.1 Dataset Sources

We combine Xperience-10M [ 75 ] and Ego-Exo4D [ 30 ] , processing both at 10 fps. From Xperience-10M, we use 3,740 episodes totaling 253.1 h, with the left camera view, calibrated stereo depth, SLAM camera poses, and hand motion capture where available. From Ego-Exo4D, we use 1,659 takes totaling 44.1 h, which provide no hand motion capture. In total, EgoTAP-4D contains 5,399 videos totaling 297.2 h, with an average duration of 198 s.


#### D.2 Annotation Pipeline

We annotate each recording in five steps (Fig. 6 ). We first recover metric depth and camera poses for every frame. We then label each object as static or moving in every frame and use these labels to build trajectories separately for static surfaces, moving objects, and hands. Next, we reconnect the tracks of objects that leave the field of view and return. Finally, we lift the trajectories to 3D, remove unreliable 3D positions, and store the annotations.


##### D.2.1 Metric Depth and Camera Poses

For every frame, we need a metric depth map $d_{t}$ and a camera-to-world pose $T_{t}^{w}$ , with all poses of a recording in a common world coordinate system. A pixel $p_{t}$ of track $a$ is then back-projected to the 3D point

[Tableau] $$X_{a}(t)=T_{t}^{w}\,\Pi^{-1}\!\left(p_{t},d_{t}(p_{t})\right),$$ (2)

where $\Pi^{-1}$ is the inverse projection with the camera intrinsics. Xperience-10M provides stereo depth and SLAM camera poses, whereas Ego-Exo4D provides camera poses from Aria Machine Perception Services (MPS) [ 25 ] but no dense depth. We therefore process the two sources separately.

We downsample the original 20 fps rectified left video to 10 fps. For camera poses, we use the SLAM trajectory provided with the dataset. We obtain the camera-to-world transform $T_{t}^{w}$ of each frame by composing the headset pose with the camera-to-headset extrinsics. To detect frames with inaccurate poses, we back-project background pixels outside the GMOS masks to 3D with the stereo depth and project them into frames at least one second away. A projection is inconsistent if it lies in front of the observed surface or falls inside an object mask. Frames with an unusually high fraction of inconsistent projections are marked unreliable and excluded from annotation.

We find that the stereo depth provided with Xperience-10M systematically overestimates distance, and the error grows with distance. The bias differs across recordings and is consistent with imperfect rectification of the stereo pair. Such errors perturb the disparity, which is inversely proportional to depth. We therefore correct the stereo depth $d$ of each recording with an affine model in inverse depth,

[Tableau] $$\frac{1}{z}=\frac{a}{d}+b,$$ (3)

where $z$ is the corrected depth. We fit $a$ and $b$ to sparse 3D points that do not rely on the stereo depth: points triangulated from Kanade–Lucas–Tomasi (KLT) tracks [ 52 ] with the SLAM poses, and the SLAM landmarks provided with the dataset. We use half of these points for fitting and hold out the other half for evaluation. On the held-out landmarks, the stereo depth overestimates distance by 1–2% within 1 m and by 7% at 1.5–3 m, with a quarter of the recordings exceeding 16% (Tab. S13 ). After calibration, the median error stays within $\pm$ 1% in every depth range up to 3 m, and the held-out KLT points give consistent results.

We take every third frame of the Aria RGB video to obtain 10 fps and undistort each fisheye frame to a $512\times 512$ pinhole image. Camera poses come from the MPS closed-loop trajectory [ 25 ] . Since Ego-Exo4D provides no dense depth, we combine monocular metric depth from MoGe-3 [ 44 ] , stereo depth from FoundationStereo [ 95 ] on two stereo pairs of Aria cameras, and the semi-dense MPS points. For static regions, we correct the monocular depth in inverse depth as in the Xperience-10M calibration (Eq. 3 ), but fit the correction separately for each frame to the MPS points visible in it. For moving objects, which the MPS points do not cover, we rescale the monocular depth to match nearby stereo depth and refine the depth along each point track by optimizing stereo, multi-view photometric, motion smoothness, and monocular prior terms.

We discard low-confidence stereo depth, depth near object boundaries, and depth outside 0.15–3.5 m. For both sources, we fuse the static scene of each recording with TSDF fusion [ 14 ] . We treat pixels outside objects that move during the recording as static and integrate their depth from all frames with reliable poses into a single volume. We then render the fused surface into each frame and use the rendered depth for static pixels, leaving static pixels without a valid fused depth empty. Moving objects, people, and hands keep their per-frame depth. The fused depth reduces per-frame noise, fills small holes, and gives each static point a consistent 3D position across frames.


##### D.2.2 Motion Segmentation

We obtain object instance masks from GMOS [ 97 ] and label each instance as static or moving in every frame, based on multi-view depth consistency, hand contact, and whether the instance is a person. We detect hand contact from SAM 3.1 hand masks [ 8 ] and, where available, hand motion capture. We label people, and objects whose motion is uncertain, as moving. While an object is out of view, we assume that it remains in place if it was static when it left, unless later frames show that it has moved.


##### D.2.3 Surface Trajectories

We obtain trajectories for static surfaces from scene geometry, for moving objects from point tracking, and for hands from motion capture.

For static surfaces, we project each 3D point of the fused scene into every frame with the camera poses. We mark a static point visible in a frame when its projected depth matches the depth map.

We track points on moving instances with AllTracker [ 35 ] and sample new query points when the existing tracks no longer cover the instance. We sample no query points on hands. Each AllTracker run covers at most 5 s before and 10 s after its query frame. AllTracker also predicts a visibility and a confidence score for each point, and a point is visible when its visibility is at least 0.5. We keep a visible point only if its confidence is at least 0.5 and it lies on its own object mask, has valid depth, and is not on a hand, so that a track does not jump onto an occluding hand. Otherwise, we keep its 2D position but mark it invalid. Depth errors, for example on transparent surfaces, can make a static object appear to move. We therefore also triangulate the tracks of moving objects from the camera poses alone, without depth. If an object’s tracks over frames labeled as moving triangulate to fixed 3D points with a reprojection error of at most 3 pixels, the object is in fact static in these frames, and we relabel them as static. If the depth in these frames differs from the triangulated depth by more than 10%, we use the triangulated points as the 3D positions, and frames without a triangulated point have no 3D position.

We fit MANO [ 74 ] meshes to the hand motion capture, whose 778 vertices per hand give exact correspondences over time. We project the vertices into each frame and compare them with the depth map to label each vertex as visible, occluded, self-occluded, or inconsistent with the depth. Frames without motion capture have no hand annotations.


##### D.2.4 Reconnecting Reappearing Points

After an object leaves the field of view, GMOS sometimes assigns its instance identity to a different object that appears later. Before reconnecting tracks, we show GPT-6.1 Sol the object before and after the gap and ask whether it is the same object. If it is not, we give the later mask a new instance identity.

When an object or a person leaves the field of view and returns, we try to continue its existing tracks after the gap. We bridge gaps of up to one second with AllTracker and keep a bridge when its forward–backward error is at most 3 pixels. By design, we leave gaps between one and three seconds unconnected and use the VLM for longer ones.

For gaps of at least three seconds, we ask AgenTAP with GPT-6 Astra to locate 10 query points from before the gap in several frames after the object returns (Fig. 9 and Fig. S14 ). The query points are the object’s existing tracks or textured points that we sample in the last frame before the gap in which the object is large enough. Each image pair shows the frame of the query points and one frame after the return. Sharing one session among several points keeps accuracy and lowers token usage (Sec. C.4 ). We do not query the VLM when the object is static on both sides of the gap, stays in view for less than one second after returning, or never appears large enough to sample query points. Of the 14,091 image pairs with 10 points each, 7,057 pass filtering, with 70,570 query points. Of these points, 23,072 pass the checks below and enter the dataset.

We accept a VLM prediction only if the VLM finds the queried point and the prediction lies on the same object, has valid depth, and is not on a hand. We then track each accepted prediction with AllTracker to a second frame after the return and compare it with the VLM prediction in that frame. Matching predictions verify the reconnection, and conflicting ones are rejected. Predictions that cannot be cross-checked are kept but marked unverified. We do not fill in positions during the gap.


##### D.2.5 3D Lifting, Filtering, and Annotation Format

We back-project every valid track point to 3D with Eq. 2 . Stereo noise can make a back-projected point jump along the viewing ray. We invalidate the 3D position of a track in frames where it spikes or deviates from its neighboring frames along the ray by more than 2 cm or 4% of its distance, whichever is larger, while keeping its 2D position. When at least half of an object’s tracks jump in the same frame, we discard the depth of the entire object in that frame.

Each track stores its 2D and 3D positions, object instance, temporal segments, per-frame visibility state, and annotation source. The source indicates whether a position comes from AllTracker, a short-gap bridge, a verified or unverified VLM reconnection, or triangulation. The visibility state is one of visible, masked, occluded, out of frame, object absent, or position missing. A point may have a valid 2D position but no valid 3D position.
