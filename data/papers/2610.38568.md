## $\bm{\mathcal{A}}$ -RSI : Adaptive Task Refresh for Zero-Data Multimodal Self-Improvement

Recursive self-improvement requires models not only to acquire useful training experience, but also to decide when their current experience has ceased to be useful. Existing methods often focus on what experience to generate next, while paying less attention to when fresh experience should be introduced. Many also construct training targets through self-consistency or majority voting, making supervision depend on the model’s own uncertain predictions. We address these two questions separately: training dynamics determine when to acquire new experience, while the answers to new tasks remain independently checkable . $\bm{\mathcal{A}}$ -RSI monitors reward variation and accuracy in training logs to refresh a task pool when its learning signal declines, without consulting validation or test scores. A static library of model-authored programs produces multimodal questions and derives their reference answers; we specify the task-generation contract, automatically verify chosen properties of the reward and refresh rules, and separately audit the generated answers. The training loop uses no external training examples or human-written answer labels. On the primary eleven-benchmark suite, $\mathcal{A}$ -RSI raises the performance average from 57.77 to 61.05 with 800 fresh tasks.

### 1 Introduction

Recursive self-improvement depends on both acquiring useful experience and recognizing when the current experience is no longer useful. Recent methods have emphasized what to generate next, such as harder problems or tasks near the model’s estimated competence. Yet a system can continue spending updates on a pool whose learning signal has declined, while refreshing too often consumes new tasks that may not be needed. This makes the timing of replacement a distinct decision from the choice of replacement tasks.

The answers attached to self-generated tasks raise a separate reliability question. When training targets come from self-consistency or majority voting, errors in the model’s predictions can become labels for its next update. We therefore separate the source of a refresh decision from the source of answer correctness: training dynamics can indicate when to draw new experience, while the new tasks should have independently checkable answers.

We implement this separation in $\bm{\mathcal{A}}$ -RSI (Adaptive Recursive Self-Improvement). In group-based reinforcement learning, prompts whose sampled responses receive different rewards provide nonzero relative-advantage signal. $\mathcal{A}$ -RSI monitors how many distinct prompts retain this signal, together with logged answer accuracy, and refreshes the active pool when the specified training-log condition fires. This is an operational indicator of declining learning signal, not a proof that the pool has been mastered. The controller does not consult validation or test scores.

A fixed library of model-written programs supplies the new multimodal tasks. Each program constructs an image and question from sampled parameters and computes a reference answer from the same underlying configuration. We specify this shared-configuration contract, machine-check selected reward and refresh properties, and independently audit generated answers. These checks support the use of program-computed supervision without asserting that every rendered question has been formally proved correct.

The loop trains the Solver on the current pool, reads its training logs, and either retains the pool or activates a prepared, unused replacement. A refresh keeps the learned model weights; the task templates and sampling quotas remain fixed. The training stage uses no external training examples, human-written answer labels, or evaluation scores. In the tested Qwen comparisons, difficulty-aware and competence-aware replacement rules do not outperform random sampling under the fixed task-family shares.

On the primary eleven-benchmark suite, Qwen improves from 57.77 at the post-initialization checkpoint $S_{t}$ to 61.05 after eight continuation windows and 800 fresh tasks. MiMo improves from 59.08 at its public base model $S_{0}$ to 60.71. On the same eleven-benchmark protocol, $\mathcal{A}$ -RSI scores 61.05 with 800 fresh tasks, while every-window refresh scores 60.64 with 3,200. At the matched sixth continuation window, $\mathcal{A}$ -RSI scores 60.40 against 58.35 for continued training on the old pool. Figure 1 also motivates our response-level audit: a score can rise because more answers finish within the output budget or because already completed answers are corrected. We report these outcomes separately.

Our contributions are threefold:

- • A training-log refresh rule. We formulate a pool-replacement rule using the decline in prompts with within-group reward variation, without consulting evaluation scores.

- • Programmatically verified experience. Model-written programs instantiate multimodal tasks and compute their reference answers, allowing the training loop to operate without external training examples or human-written answer labels.

- • Matched comparisons and output-level analysis. We compare refresh policies at matched training progress across full benchmarks and distinguish gains linked to response completion from corrections among completed answers. The Qwen results identify the benefit of the implemented replacement policy and the limits of the timing claim.


### 2 Related Work

Recursive self-improvement research studies how a system can produce useful experience and use it to improve. Prior work has changed the system around a model, as in STOP and the Darwin Gödel Machine, or updated model weights from generated rationales, filtered samples, self-play, or model-based judgments ( Zelikman et al., 2024 , Zhang et al., 2025 , Zelikman et al., 2022 , Singh et al., 2024 , Chen et al., 2024 , Yuan et al., 2024 , Wang et al., 2023 ) . We focus on a control question within weight-based improvement: how can a system tell, without evaluation feedback, when its current training pool has stopped supplying useful signal? Our refresh decision comes from training logs; it does not adapt the task-generation rules during training. Table 1 compares how related systems obtain supervision and change their training data.


##### Zero-data self-improvement and verifiable rewards.

Absolute Zero uses an executor to validate code-generated tasks and answers, while R-Zero trains a Challenger and Solver to co-evolve around the Solver’s current ability ( Zhao et al., 2025 , Huang et al., 2026 ) . Multimodal work extends self-play through generated visual games or multi-role systems, including Vision-Zero and MM-Zero ( Wang et al., 2026 , Li et al., 2026 ) . These methods primarily develop how tasks are proposed, solved, or adapted. $\mathcal{A}$ -RSI instead fixes a library of task-generating programs and studies when to draw a fresh pool. Each sampled instance has a program-computed answer, so answer correctness does not depend on a model vote. This distinction complements reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), where methods such as GRPO and DeepSeek-R1 optimize against outcome checks rather than human-written reasoning traces ( Shao et al., 2024 , DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025 ) . DAPO’s dynamic sampling also removes prompts whose sampled responses all receive the same outcome, since they yield no within-group policy gradient ( Yu et al., 2025 ) . We use the related signal for a different decision: $U_{w}$ aggregates informative prompts across a window and helps determine whether the entire pool should be refreshed. It is a pool-level diagnostic, not a replacement for batch-level sampling.


##### Procedural tasks and task selection.

Programmatic generation provides an open-ended source of varied tasks, as demonstrated by Procgen for reinforcement-learning environments and by language-model resources such as Reasoning Gym and SynLogic for verifiable reasoning ( Cobbe et al., 2019 , Stojanovski et al., 2025 , Liu et al., 2025a ) . Curriculum and adaptive-sampling methods address a related but distinct problem: they prioritize examples or environments according to estimated difficulty or learning value. Examples include Prioritized Level Replay and PAIRED in reinforcement learning, and online difficulty filtering, bandit-based curricula, and distribution-level sampling for language-model training ( Jiang et al., 2020 , Dennis et al., 2020 , Bae et al., 2026 , Chen et al., 2025 , Wang et al., 2025b ) . ThinkLite-VL is especially close to our setting: it uses search-based difficulty estimates to select a smaller, challenging subset from a fixed collection of visual reasoning examples ( Wang et al., 2025a ) . Those studies motivate testing selection, but they do not settle how to choose a replacement pool after a training plateau. We compare difficulty-frontier, difficulty-ladder, and rollout-based selection with random draws under fixed family shares. In our experiments, the selection strategies do not outperform random replacement; this result is limited to our generators, model, and tested selection rules.


##### Training duration and evaluation budgets.

The apparent benefit of continued reasoning can depend on how generations are terminated and scored. Dr. GRPO analyzes response-length bias, DAPO includes overlong-reward shaping to address truncation, and L1 explicitly trains length-budget control ( Liu et al., 2025b , Yu et al., 2025 , Aggarwal and Welleck, 2025 ) . This work motivates a separate audit in our setting: we check whether a measured gain persists under a longer generation budget and format-corrected scoring, including when evaluating released MM-Zero weights (Section 7.1 ). The audit is not a new reward or refresh rule; it tests whether an apparent improvement reflects better task performance or only a greater chance of completing within the evaluation limit.

### 3 Method

$\mathcal{A}$ -RSI supports continued multimodal reinforcement learning through a programmatic task pipeline and a training-log controller. Figure 2 organizes the workflow into three parts. Before training, fixed, model-written templates generate images, questions, and program-computed answers; the pipeline checks candidates and prepares disjoint replacement pools under fixed family quotas (Figure 2 (a); Section 3.1 ). During training, the Solver learns on the active pool with GRPO using correctness, format, and termination rewards (Figure 2 (b); Section 3.2 ). After each accepted window, the controller reads training-side accuracy and group-relative advantage signals to decide whether to retain the current pool or activate the next unused one (Figure 2 (c); Section 3.3 ). A refresh replaces the whole active pool while retaining the latest accepted model weights (Section 3.4 ). Training experience therefore changes the timing of data replacement; it does not update the generator or the task-sampling distribution.

> Figure 2 : Overview of $\mathcal{A}$ -RSI . Task templates, the candidate reservoir, and replacement pools are fixed before training. The controller reads training logs to decide when to activate a fresh pool; only the Solver policy is optimized.

Let $\theta_{w-1}$ be the model parameters and $\mathcal{P}_{w}$ the active pool at the start of window $w$ . A window consists of $H$ GRPO updates ( Shao et al., 2024 ) , producing parameters $\theta_{w}$ and a rollout log $\mathcal{L}_{w}$ . In the main Qwen experiment, $\theta_{0}=S_{t}$ is obtained by training the public Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct checkpoint on the initial task pool. The index $w$ counts subsequent continuation windows, whereas $S_{0}$ denotes the public model before this initialization. We report the resulting model states with pool-indexed notation $S_{ij}$ , defined in Section 4 . Each replacement introduces $B$ unused tasks; concrete pool sizes and optimization settings are given in Appendix 27.3 . Algorithm 1 specifies this main training loop; Section 4 describes the comparison arms and MiMo experiments.


#### 3.1 Programmatic task construction

Template construction takes place once, before training. Starting from seed task types in geometry, charts, tables, and counting, the base model generates reusable problem structures and writes their parameterized programs. For each structure, the model specifies the admissible parameters, the conditions and quantity to ask for, and the procedures for sampling values, drawing an image, composing a question, and computing its answer. The resulting library $\mathcal{T}$ stores these executable templates and stays fixed throughout training.

Task construction then executes the frozen templates. A generator selects $\tau\in\mathcal{T}$ and samples parameters $z$ from its distribution $p_{\tau}$ . The resulting instance produces an image $I$ , a question $q$ , and a reference answer $a$ :

[Tableau] $$z\sim p_{\tau},\qquad(I,q,a)=G_{\tau}(z).$$ (1)

The program first constructs the underlying data or geometric configuration, then uses it to produce all three outputs. For example, a chart template can sample category values $v_{A}=18$ and $v_{B}=11$ , render their bars, ask how much larger $A$ is than $B$ , and compute $a=v_{A}-v_{B}=7$ from the stored values. The learner receives only $(I,q)$ , rather than the latent values or answer, and its response is scored against $a$ . Replacement tasks require no further model call to generate their questions or reference answers.

Admission checks are specific to each generator: they test the sampled configuration, the answer computation, and applicable rendering and ambiguity constraints. Freshness and contamination screens then reject flagged candidates, and held-out diagnostic items are excluded from training. Retained records associate the image, question, and answer with a generator identity and seed for reconstruction. These checks and independent answer audits are detailed in Appendix 27 ; program-computed supervision remains subject to the correctness of the template and its implementation.

The generators build a reservoir before continuation training. Candidates that pass the construction, freshness, and contamination checks and lie within the configured training ladders form the eligible set $\mathcal{C}$ , after excluding diagnostic holdouts. This set supplies the replacement pools. The initial pool comes from earlier geometry and chart batches with model-assisted question rewriting and checks. Generator allocations and reservoir sizes are given in Appendix 27.2 .


#### 3.2 Rewards and policy optimization

For each sampled task, the model produces $m$ responses. We score each response $y$ against the program-computed answer $a$ and check its format and stopping condition:

[Tableau] $$r(y,a)=r_{\mathrm{ans}}(y,a)+\lambda_{\mathrm{fmt}}\,r_{\mathrm{fmt}}(y)+\lambda_{\mathrm{stop}}\,r_{\mathrm{stop}}(y).$$ (2)

The answer reward $r_{\mathrm{ans}}$ is 1 when the extracted answer matches $a$ and 0 otherwise. The format reward $r_{\mathrm{fmt}}$ is 1 when the response follows the required boxed-answer format and 0 otherwise. The stopping term $r_{\mathrm{stop}}$ is 0 when decoding ends normally with a final boxed answer and $-1$ otherwise. Reaching the response limit incurs the stopping penalty even if an earlier boxed expression is present. All normally completed responses with a final boxed answer receive the same stopping reward, irrespective of length; there is no per-token length penalty or separate reward for hop count.

We update only the Solver policy with GRPO, using relative advantages computed from these rewards within each response group. The templates and controller remain fixed. The coefficients $\lambda_{\mathrm{fmt}}$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{stop}}$ are fixed throughout training. Their values, the response limit, and optimizer and sampling settings appear in Appendix 27.3 .


#### 3.3 Detecting pool saturation

A response group with identical total rewards has zero GRPO advantages and provides no reward-derived policy-gradient signal. As training changes the response distribution, previously informative prompts may cease to provide within-group reward differences. We monitor the number of prompts with nonzero advantages alongside response accuracy to obtain a training-side heuristic for deciding when to refresh the pool. Here, “saturation” refers to this operational rule, rather than a measured plateau in held-out performance.

Let $\mathcal{G}_{w}$ contain the response groups logged after dynamic resampling, with each generation batch counted once. For group $g$ , let $x_{g}$ be the full prompt, $A_{g,j}$ the logged advantage of response $j$ , and $c_{g,j}$ its answer reward. We compute

[Tableau] $\displaystyle U_{w}$ $\displaystyle=\left|\left\{x_{g}:\ g\in\mathcal{G}_{w},\ \max_{1\leq j\leq m}|A_{g,j}|>\varepsilon\right\}\right|,$ (3) $\displaystyle a_{w}$ $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m|\mathcal{G}_{w}|}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{w}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_{g,j},$

where $\varepsilon>0$ is a numerical tolerance. $U_{w}$ counts distinct observed prompts with nonzero advantages, and $a_{w}$ measures how often the logged responses are correct. Advantages use the full reward in Eq. ( 2 ), so differences in formatting or stopping can also contribute to $U_{w}$ .

At the end of each window, we compare these signals with their values $\ell$ windows earlier. Let $n_{w}$ be the number of accepted consecutive windows on this pool, $n_{\min}>\ell$ the minimum observation period, $\rho\in(0,1)$ the signal-retention threshold, and $\eta>0$ the required accuracy increase. The decision to refresh is

[Tableau] $$\delta_{w}=\begin{cases}1,&n_{w}\geq n_{\min}\ \text{and}\ \bigl(U_{w}\leq\rho U_{w-\ell}\ \text{or}\ a_{w}-a_{w-\ell}\geq\eta\bigr),\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}$$ (4)

Either a relative decline in $U_{w}$ below the retention threshold or a sufficient increase in $a_{w}$ triggers replacement. The first condition detects fewer observed prompts supplying relative-advantage signal. The second uses increased accuracy as a proxy for growing familiarity with the pool. The minimum observation period $n_{\min}$ ensures that both measurements come from the same pool. It does not prescribe a refresh interval: when neither condition holds, the controller retains the pool and checks again after the next accepted window. The lag and thresholds remain fixed across stages and models, while the decision depends on each model’s current training feedback.

Neither condition establishes that the model has mastered the pool or stopped improving on external tasks. Uniformly incorrect responses and a concentrated response distribution can also remove within-group variation, while dynamic resampling affects which prompts are observed. Accordingly, $U_{w}$ describes the logged training signal, rather than an exhaustive estimate of the pool’s remaining learnability. The usefulness of this refresh rule is assessed by the continuation experiments, separately from these signal definitions. The shared controller settings and reward-component replay are reported together in Appendices 27.3 and 14 .


#### 3.4 Fresh replacement and continued training

When $\delta_{w}=1$ , we replace the entire pool with unused tasks from $\mathcal{C}$ . Let $\mathcal{H}_{w}$ contain all tasks introduced so far. We draw without replacement under fixed family quotas $\mathbf{k}=(k_{\mathrm{geo}},k_{\mathrm{chart}},k_{\mathrm{table}},k_{\mathrm{count}})$ , with $\sum_{f}k_{f}=B$ :

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{P}_{w+1}=\begin{cases}\operatorname{Draw}_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{H}_{w}),&\delta_{w}=1,\\ \mathcal{P}_{w},&\delta_{w}=0.\end{cases}$$ (5)

Here $\operatorname{Draw}_{\mathbf{k}}$ takes tasks in a fixed pseudorandom order within each family. The ordering does not depend on model answers or difficulty estimates. We execute these draws before training to prepare an ordered list of disjoint replacement pools, and the detector decides when to activate the next one. Replacing the entire active pool does not reset the model: $\theta_{w}$ carries forward experience from all preceding windows. The within-pool window count returns to zero after a refresh. Each new window starts a fresh optimizer and scheduler while inheriting the accepted model weights, whether or not the pool changes.

The method follows a training-feedback loop: train on an active pool, observe training-side signals, retain or replace the pool, and continue from accepted model weights. Only accepted windows contribute to the detector’s history. Training-time health checks identify interrupted attempts, whose weights are discarded. Algorithm 1 specifies the training and refresh procedure used to define the method.

In the main training loop, a failed window is retried once from the preceding accepted checkpoint on the same pool and seed; a second failure stops the run. If a refresh is requested but the declared replacement pools are exhausted, the controller refuses to start the next window. It does not silently reuse a pool or change the sampling rule. No replacement is needed after the final budgeted window.

### 4 Experimental Setup


##### Arms.

Figure 3 compares five strategies for refreshing the training pool. The old pool contains 500 tasks. Each window consists of 100 GRPO updates, and each refresh replaces the entire pool with 400 unused tasks. Random sampling draws unused tasks under fixed task-family quotas. Frontier selection uses accuracy on held-out synthetic tasks to find the easiest difficulty level the model does not yet solve reliably, then draws tasks from that level and its neighbors.

- • $\mathcal{A}$ -RSI refreshes when the saturation detector fires, using random sampling.

- • Frontier uses the same detector, but selects new tasks by frontier selection.

- • Periodic uses frontier selection and refreshes every two windows.

- • Continuous refreshes every window and adjusts the selected difficulty up or down according to held-out accuracy.

- • No-Refresh keeps the old pool throughout training.

Appendix 27.4 specifies the difficulty thresholds and pool construction rules.


##### Training.

The main experiments use Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct. All five arms start from $S_{t}$ , obtained after 100 GRPO updates on the old pool, and train for up to eight additional windows with the same optimization settings. We also evaluate the method on MiMo-VL-7B-SFT.


##### Checkpoint notation.

$S_{0}$ denotes the public starting model. In our main trajectory, $S_{ij}$ is the accepted model after the $j$ th continuation window on the $i$ th data pool. Each continuation window contains 100 updates. The Qwen and MiMo main trajectories use three windows on each of their first three pools, so continuation indices $w=1,2,3$ correspond to $S_{11},S_{12},S_{13}$ , indices $w=4,5,6$ to $S_{21},S_{22},S_{23}$ , and indices $w=7,8,9$ to $S_{31},S_{32},S_{33}$ . This correspondence describes the observed refresh decisions, rather than imposing a fixed three-window rule. Qwen additionally has the 100-update initialization from $S_{0}$ to $S_{t}$ before these continuation windows. In comparisons between refresh strategies, column and horizontal-axis labels refer to the training progress of the main trajectory; control arms are evaluated after the same number of continuation updates and need not use the same pool or within-pool count. Earlier independent experiments retain their original window indices because their refresh schedules differ.

##### Evaluation protocols.

The Qwen and MiMo main trajectories, refresh-policy and replacement-selection comparisons, and earlier-chain benchmark results use the same eleven full test sets. #### 5.3 Training signals identify repeated pool saturation

We first examine the executed refresh decisions in the Qwen run. On the old pool, the number of tasks with nonzero group advantages falls from 239 to 195 over three windows, giving $U_{w}/U_{w-2}=.816$ and triggering replacement. On the first new pools, the detector fires again after three windows, with ratios .832 for $\mathcal{A}$ -RSI and .826 for Frontier . The accuracy condition never fires alone. The first new random pool starts at training accuracy .35, compared with .57 at the end of the old pool, exposing tasks the model has not yet mastered.

Keeping the old pool eventually exhausts its useful signal. At $S_{31}$ , No-Refresh records 35 zero-signal warnings and only 94 of 100 normal gradient steps. Its health gate fails twice and training halts. Before that halt, at the matched window-6 budget, $\mathcal{A}$ -RSI scores 60.40 against 58.35 for continued reuse under the eleven-benchmark protocol. The observed refreshes coincide with a three-window schedule. Section 6.1 separates what the policy comparison establishes from the timing question.

#### 6.3 Adaptive decisions across stages and models

The controller adapts through its decisions while keeping the observation period and thresholds fixed. Figure 4 (b) compares the first and third windows on each pool. For MiMo, the distinct-prompt advantage count falls from 245 to 153 on the initial pool and from 199 to 143 on the first replacement pool. On the second replacement pool, the count remains nearly unchanged at 152 to 150, retaining 98.68%, while accuracy rises by 6.63 points. The final-round replay therefore keeps this pool. Equal periods of reuse produce different decisions as MiMo’s training feedback changes.

The same rule also distinguishes the two models at the same stage. On Qwen’s second replacement pool, the count falls from 181 to 152, retaining 83.98%, while accuracy rises by 6.59 points. The replay requests replacement for Qwen and continued reuse for MiMo. A fixed three-window schedule would replace both. Thus, the minimum observation period makes a decision eligible, while the observed training state determines the action. The first two decisions for each model were executed during training. ### 7 Analysis

We examine reward shortcuts and pipeline validity through controlled reward scoring, independent checks of generated answers, and qualitative correction examples across eleven benchmarks. The generated-task audit $\mathcal{A}$ contains 1,000 actually consumed training tasks for independent answer checks and controlled reward-scoring tests. The external examples illustrate changes in completed answers; aggregate effects are assessed separately using the full-suite and outcome-independent output audits. The generated-task and reward-check protocols are given in Appendix 16 .


#### 7.1 Reward hacking and non-truncated corrections


##### A controlled reward-scoring stress test.

For every generated task in $\mathcal{A}$ , we construct seven response variants, including correctly and incorrectly boxed answers, the same answer with different finish reasons, and unboxed answers. We score all 7,000 variants with the actual correctness, format, and termination functions used by training. The task reference and response content are held fixed where appropriate, so format and termination changes can be isolated (Table 6 ). These are counterfactual scoring probes, not generated model responses or a reward-removal training ablation.

##### Adversarial answer-checker tests.

We extend the same 1,000 references to 18 response variants, with expected correctness specified for 16 variants. The production checker fails 220 of the 16,000 specified checks: on 110 choice questions, it accepts both a gold-prefixed ambiguous answer and a gold option followed by unrelated text. An isolated candidate that requires a complete terminal option passes all 16,000 checks. On 16,000 actual logged responses from five training windows, 3,760 are choice responses; the candidate changes one score. These results expose a parser vulnerability without establishing widespread exploitation by the trained model. The candidate has not been substituted into the reported training runs. Appendix 17 gives the checks and prospective training comparison.


##### Auxiliary rewards cannot reverse the correctness ranking.

Let $r_{c},r_{w}$ receive correctness rewards one and zero, respectively. Since the auxiliary reward lies in $[-\lambda_{\mathrm{stop}},\lambda_{\mathrm{fmt}}]$ , Eq. ( 2 ) gives

[Tableau] $$R(r_{c})-R(r_{w})\geq 1-\lambda_{\mathrm{fmt}}-\lambda_{\mathrm{stop}}>0,$$ (6)

where the final inequality follows from our fixed coefficients. Thus, auxiliary terms cannot make a response with zero correctness reward outrank one with unit correctness reward, assuming the reference and answer checker are valid. This ranking property does not prevent optimization within the all-wrong class: the stress test also confirms that formatting or committing a wrong answer can increase reward without improving correctness. We therefore examine non-truncated corrections separately rather than equating higher reward with better reasoning.


##### MiMo: qualitative examples of non-truncated correction.

The MiMo examples compare $S_{0}$ with $S_{32}$ . Both responses finish normally, while the earlier incorrect answer becomes correct under both original and format-corrected answer checks. Such a transition illustrates a change beyond simply finishing a previously truncated response. It does not by itself establish that the reasoning steps are valid or exclude guessing; those judgments require the original image and full response. These examples are used qualitatively, without estimating how frequently this change occurs or presenting the automated checks as completed human annotations.

Full-suite output decompositions, an outcome-independent random sample, and fixed-weight length-budget comparisons are reported in Appendices 15 , 18 , and 12 , respectively. Those analyses retain both corrections and regressions.


##### Training gains persist at a larger output budget.

When the generation limit doubles from 4096 to 8192 tokens, $\mathcal{A}$ -RSI retains 80% of its gain over base, compared with 59% for MM-Zero v5 (Figure 5 ). Each trained checkpoint is compared with the base model at the same limit on the eleven-benchmark suite. At 8192 tokens, $\mathcal{A}$ -RSI remains 3.25 points above base, versus 2.45 for MM-Zero v5. Its training gain is thus less sensitive to the output budget.

#### 7.2 Pipeline validity: verifiable data and experience-driven refresh


##### An independent check of generated answers.

For $\mathcal{A}$ , we randomly sample 200 tasks without replacement from each of five supported generator groups in actually consumed replacement pools, fixing membership before recomputing any answers. Independent solvers agree with all 1,000 reference answers. Training gold/solution values agree with the selected source records, and image-file hashes agree with the recorded hashes on all 1,000 tasks. The five groups cover geometry, charts, tables, and counting. The group-level counts and sampling protocol are given together in Appendix 16 , which also states the excluded generator groups.


##### A conditional specification for task validity.

For a template $\tau$ and sampled parameters $z$ , let $s_{\tau}(z)$ be the underlying configuration, $E_{\tau}$ its answer executor, $R_{\tau}$ its renderer, and $Q_{\tau}$ its question constructor. The generation contract is

[Tableau] $\displaystyle I$ $\displaystyle=R_{\tau}(s_{\tau}(z)),\qquad q=Q_{\tau}(s_{\tau}(z)),$ (7) $\displaystyle a$ $\displaystyle=E_{\tau}(s_{\tau}(z)).$

Thus, image, question, and answer share one underlying configuration. Let $V_{\tau}(I,q,a,z)$ denote the generator-specific construction checks, $F(x)$ the freshness check, $C(x)$ the contamination screen, and $H$ the diagnostic holdout. The admission rule can be specified as

[Tableau] $$x\in\mathcal{C}\ \Longrightarrow\ V_{\tau}(x)=1\ \land\ F(x)=1\ \land\ C(x)=1\ \land\ x\notin H.$$ (8)

We use an SMT solver to check four properties of the specified model: correctness-ranking protection, within-pool lag indexing, bounded one-step difficulty updates, and freshness across successive admitted pools (Appendix 17 ). These are machine-checked properties of the specification, not verification of the full Python implementations or renderer. It certifies only the properties tested by those checks. Interpreting $a$ as the answer to the rendered question additionally assumes that the executor is correct and that rendering and wording preserve the configuration without ambiguity. Independent recomputation tests that assumption; repeating the original answer function would not provide an independent audit.

For the finite audit set $\mathcal{A}$ , the checks establish agreement with independently recomputed answers and consistency of source labels and image hashes. Every audited record satisfies those checks. Interpreting the recomputed answer as the answer to the rendered question still relies on rendering and wording preserving the underlying configuration. The broader generator audits and remaining ambiguities are retained in Appendix 14 . All 1,000 image files also decode, meet the minimum-dimension check, and are nonblank; none of the task IDs overlaps the diagnostic holdout. Independent audit checks detect all 4,000 injected reference, image-byte, source-question, and truncated-image defects. These injections test the audit checks rather than all production admission paths.


##### Rendered-task consistency on the same fixed sample.

Additional task-specific checks pass for all 1,000 records, including target uniqueness, admissible values, and option consistency where applicable. Replaying the frozen generators reproduces 800 whole images byte for byte under the recorded audit environments. For the remaining 200 referring charts, the plot pixels and the category and numeric-axis labels agree with the source, although whole-image reproduction remains unresolved because of font differences. These checks strengthen the finite quality audit without certifying every visual or linguistic property (Appendix 17 ).


##### Does fresh-pool training improve the learner?

The complete eleven-benchmark format-corrected scoring comparison in Table 4 evaluates the downstream consequence of the implemented refresh policy: at matched $S_{23}$ progress, fresh-pool training scores 60.40 against 58.35 for original-pool continuation, a $+2.05$ -point difference. Both arms share the trajectory through $S_{13}$ and receive the same number of subsequent updates. Pool replacement also changes the active pool from 500 to 400 tasks, so this is evidence for the combined policy in one run rather than an isolated effect of freshness, validated labels, or detector timing. The output-stratum analysis uses these same eleven-benchmark outputs and decomposes this Macro11 difference into a $-0.60$ -point contribution from answers completed by both arms and $+2.66$ from the other completion strata. Appendix 13 gives the definitions and comparison scope.


##### Qwen: qualitative examples of same-progress fresh-pool correction.

The Qwen examples compare old-pool continuation with fresh-pool training at the same $S_{23}$ training progress. The arms share the trajectory through $S_{13}$ , use the same number of subsequent updates, and are evaluated at 8192 tokens. In these examples, both responses finish normally and the incorrect old-pool answer becomes correct under both scoring rules. They illustrate where fresh-pool training changes a completed answer favorably, without identifying which individual training task caused the correction or estimating an overall advantage. The complete-test-split continuation comparison is reported separately in Appendix 13 .


##### Difficulty and update rules have distinct roles.

Dependency depth, required reads, and reading mode describe task construction. The main policy samples unused tasks under fixed family quotas and uses training-side signals to decide when to replace the pool. Equation ( 4 ) requires enough accepted windows on that pool; because $n_{\min}>\ell$ , the lagged comparison stays within the pool. Replacement resets the history and continues from accepted model weights. These properties make the realized update decision reproducible, but do not prove that reward-signal decline is answer mastery or optimal timing. The component replay in Appendix 14 distinguishes total-reward variation from correctness-only variation. Exhaustive integer-threshold tests, bounded difficulty-update tests, and the actual-window replay check conformance to these rules; they do not establish that a trigger implies mastery or that the selected timing is optimal (Appendix 17 ).


##### Connecting cases to mechanisms.

For the retained external cases, the review procedure checks the reference against the original image, locates the earlier error, and asks whether the later response repairs it with valid steps. Candidate mechanisms include visual reading, omitted conditions, composition, arithmetic, and answer presentation. These mechanism categories remain unassigned for the qualitative examples. The generated-task audit supports the checked data properties, the scoring probes establish reward behavior, and the external examples expose favorable answer changes; none identifies which individual training task caused a downstream correction.

### 9 Discussion


##### The simple version of the loop wins.

The cheapest policy we found for keeping a zero-data loop alive is to let the model’s training log say when the pool is spent and then supply random fresh tasks. Neither of the refinements we tried helped, and one made things worse. We read this as a statement about this setting, in which the tasks are programmatic and the checkpoint is a plateau.


##### Measured state is for diagnosis.

The ladder is useful for something other than selection. Because difficulty is measured, we can say what the model has learned, which tiers saturate first, and where a mismatch occurs: for example, three of six ladders never reached the promotion threshold, and hard multi-hop tasks are largely length exhaustion. Self-assessed difficulty (AUC near 0.50) could not support these statements.


##### Adaptation through observable training state.

The controller connects experience reuse to the model’s ongoing optimization. MiMo’s changing decisions across pools and the divergent Qwen–MiMo final-round replay show why elapsed training alone does not specify the action. A common fixed rule can respond to these differences through model-generated training feedback, without changing its thresholds or consulting evaluation performance.

Limitations are discussed in Appendix 31 .


### 10 Conclusion

An RSI loop stalls first where its data runs out, and its gains can look larger than they are. $\mathcal{A}$ -RSI combines program-based generators whose answers are computed, a detector that reads only the model’s own training log and says when a pool of 400 tasks is spent, a draw of 400 fresh random tasks when it fires, and audits of length-budget sensitivity, answer formatting, and completed responses. On our main model, eight continuation windows raise Macro11 from 57.77 to 61.05 with 800 new tasks. Under that same eleven-benchmark protocol, the tested difficulty-based, controller-based, and rollout-based selectors do not outperform random selection in their matched comparisons. The length-budget comparisons use the eleven-benchmark suite and matched output limits. Part of a zero-data gain at a tight generation limit is learning to finish, as the untrained base and the released MM-Zero weights show. MiMo’s random-refresh endpoint is 60.71 versus 60.01 for continued reuse, a $+0.70$ -point difference, and exceeds every-window and periodic refresh by $+0.02$ and $+0.18$ , respectively; these small single-run margins do not establish a timing advantage. Each arm was trained once. The next steps are replication across seeds, a fixed-schedule random control for detector timing, 16k evaluation of this run, and a refresh policy for low-entropy models.

##### An untrained model at a long limit matches the trained one at a short limit.

On four datasets, raising only the base model’s limit to 16384 without any training gives 51.12, comparable to MM-Zero after training under 4096 (51.08). This is a cross-limit comparison on a subset, so it supports the point about budgets but not a precise ranking.


##### No training contribution beyond the budget was detected in a paired test.

We reproduced the published scores (base 50.27 against the paper’s 50.03, v5 54.25 against 54.18) and the training procedure locally (three rounds: v1 52.25, v2 53.68, v3 53.07). On 7,257 fixed items with an independent selector, only raising the base model’s limit to 8192 gives $+1.92$ ( $p<0.0001$ ), while the two earlier MM-Zero versions we could test gave $+0.69$ ( $p=0.138$ ) and $+0.37$ ( $p=0.434$ ), so no contribution of training beyond termination was detected. “Not detected” is not “absent”, and these are not the v5 weights. Among base-model failures that did not finish, 4,006 tasks went from 0.30 to 29.18 accuracy after MM-Zero training. On ChartQA the gain is entirely in machine-templated questions (augmented $+6.12$ , human $-0.47$ ).


##### Potential proxy-reward vulnerabilities.

The source audit identifies potential mismatches between proxy rewards and externally verified correctness. The training label is the solver’s own majority vote, not a program’s answer, and the generator’s answer agrees with it only 5–12% of the time. The self-consistency denominator drops truncated responses. No check requires that an image be necessary for the answer, and about 16% of the released images are almost entirely black. The difficulty reward favors self-consistency near 0.5, where a majority-vote label has a small vote margin and need not be correct. This is a tension between difficulty selection and label reliability, rather than a logical contradiction. We have not shown that any of these causes the effect in Table 11 ; each is a pathway by which a gain at a short limit could reflect termination behaviour rather than visual reasoning. A longer list is in Appendix 30 .


##### Our own reward is not exempt.

Our reward includes a termination term. The first window of fresh random tasks at 4096 tokens shows its effect: the arm gains $+1.22$ , almost entirely from its truncation rate falling from 17.2% to 12.3%, while numeric-answer items are flat across arms. Moreover $\mathcal{A}$ -RSI retains about 80% of its gain over the base model when the limit grows from 4096 to 8192, and truncation at 8192 is still 5.4% (every-window random refresh), 9.6% (ladder control), and 11.9% (failure-prioritized sampling) at window 4 of the earlier experiments. We therefore treat any 4096-token result as suspect, use 8192 as the main setting, and report a stable-format view. Only the longest-trained earlier chain (fixed-pool continuation) retains its entire gain across limits.


##### What we do not claim.

MM-Zero’s gain is not spurious: at 8192 the same weights are still $+2.45$ above the base model. We withdraw an earlier accusation of ours that it hurts items the base model already answers; once a mean-reversion baseline is added, the change is $+1.98$ and there is no harm. The comparison of fixed-pool continuation with MM-Zero v5 at 8192 is between two fixed sets of weights, each trained once, and fixed-pool continuation belongs to the earlier lineage in which a model snapshot writes tasks, not to $\mathcal{A}$ -RSI . $\mathcal{A}$ -RSI itself exceeds MM-Zero by $+0.79$ (58.16 against 57.37), which is within the estimated training noise and establishes neither superiority nor non-inferiority.


##### A practical recommendation.

Zero-data self-improvement results should report, at a minimum, the base model at the same relaxed limit, the truncation rate of every model, and the gain at two or more limits. A gain that falls sharply with the limit is partly a gain in finishing.

##### Difficulty has separate construction and empirical meanings.

The construction records quantities such as dependency depth, required reads, and reading mode. A hop count certifies program structure when the later computation uses an earlier result; it does not certify a particular model error rate. We therefore measure accuracy and truncation on held-out synthetic tasks. In the earlier multi-hop ladder, accuracy falls from 99.7% at one hop to 21.2% at five, but truncation reaches 75% at five hops. This supports a model- and budget-dependent difficulty ladder, with a substantial length component. It does not establish that additional hops produce better transferable training data. The historical twelve-benchmark comparison of multi-hop and parallel-reading tasks gives $-0.37$ points with interval $[-1.50,+0.74]$ , providing no detected transfer advantage (Appendix 21 ).


##### Checks on actually consumed replacement pools.

The two Qwen random replacement pools contain 800 tasks. ##### The refresh controller has checkable invariants.

Equation ( 4 ) requires enough accepted windows on the same pool and compares only that pool’s logged signals. Hence a normal refresh cannot fire before $n_{\min}$ windows, and its lagged comparison cannot cross a pool boundary because $n_{\min}>\ell$ . Upon a successful refresh, the replacement draw uses unused task identities under fixed family quotas; the next window inherits the last accepted weights. These are conditional consequences of the specified rule and its pool bookkeeping. In the recorded Qwen run, refreshes occur at $S_{13}$ and $S_{23}$ , and the decline in $U_{w}$ supports both. The accuracy condition never fires alone. This verifies the realized decision path, without proving that the detector finds a benchmark plateau or the optimal refresh time. The detector reads no benchmark scores at runtime; the fixed family mix and vocabulary were nevertheless informed by evaluation-set metadata, as disclosed in Appendix 27.2 .


##### Reward-component replay of the controller.

We recompute 38 controller receipts from 28 distinct training logs. Shared windows reuse their corresponding log. Reconstructed advantages and signal counts agree with the recorded values. On the main random-refresh arm, prompts with nonzero total-reward advantages decline from 196 to 163 between the first and third windows of the first replacement pool, triggering the retention rule. Counting only prompts with within-group correctness variation gives 166 to 155, a ratio of 0.934; the accuracy increase is 0.0946875. Neither reaches the corresponding trigger threshold. Holding the observed rollouts fixed, the second refresh therefore depends on auxiliary-reward variation being included in the signal. This is a deterministic replay of those observations, not a prediction of a policy retrained with only correctness feedback.

### 17 Adversarial Checks and Machine-Checked Specification Properties


##### Fixed references and adversarial responses.

We retain the generated-task audit’s 1,000 references without replacing any failing task. The 18 variants include canonical correct and incorrect answers, both orders of conflicting boxes, a quoted answer followed by a different final answer, gold-prefixed disjunctions, unrelated suffixes, negated answers, empty outputs, and different finish reasons. Expected binary correctness is specified for 16 variants per task; the remaining two diagnose unboxed and truncated correct answers. The production checker passes 15,780/16,000 specified checks. The 220 failures consist of two variants on each of 110 choice tasks. The candidate passes 16,000/16,000 by requiring a complete terminal option in its choice branch; other answer kinds retain their production checks. This finite adversarial set is not exhaustive.

Re-scoring five accepted replacement-pool windows reproduces every logged choice correctness value on 3,760 choice responses out of 16,000 responses. The candidate changes one score: a response had an earlier answer box followed by unfinished reasoning. This is a difference in terminal-answer enforcement, not evidence that the model intentionally exploited the checker. A prospective matched training fork retains or removes the termination coefficient from a common accepted checkpoint, using the same pool, update count, and a fixed outcome-independent 1,000-question evaluation frame. It evaluates both arms at 8192 and 16384 tokens. Its training results are pending and are not included in the reported outcomes. Because the termination weight also affects dynamic group survival, that fork measures the combined reward-and-sampling intervention.


##### Specification properties.

Z3 checks the negation of each of the following properties and returns unsatisfiable:

- 1. Given $C\in\{0,1\}$ , $F\in[0,1]$ , and $T\in[-1,0]$ , unit correctness outranks zero correctness by at least $0.6$ with the fixed coefficients.

- 2. If the current pool has $j\geq n_{\min}>\ell$ accepted windows, the lagged comparison remains within that pool.

- 3. For a valid difficulty pointer and probe rate in $[0,1]$ , an update stays within the ladder and moves by at most one step.

- 4. If each successive pool is drawn outside the accumulated history and holdout, successive pools are disjoint from that history, the holdout, and each other.

The saved SMT queries reproduce these results on reloading. These proofs depend on the stated data types, bounds, and admission construction; renderer correctness and all Python execution paths are outside the model.


##### Implementation conformance and negative controls.

The production detector matches the specified rule in 321,605 threshold and initialization cases, including every pair of integer signal counts in $[0,400]$ at two accuracy boundaries. The difficulty controller passes 11,935 valid pointer/probe-count combinations, and all 38 accepted-window receipts reproduce. These are finite implementation tests, separate from the SMT properties. The stronger statement that firing implies at least 80% accuracy is satisfiable in negation: a model with zero accuracy and zero signal fires. A signal collapse accompanied by declining accuracy also fires. The detector therefore identifies the specified refresh condition, not mastery.


##### Empirical difficulty calibration on the same tasks.

The fixed collection contains 600 tasks on four ordered axes: geometry F (92), geometry S (108), referring charts (200), and counting (200). The other 400 chart/table tasks do not define an ordered axis in this check. Using the frozen eight-response probes at a 4096-token limit, task-level Spearman correlations between rung and correctness are $-0.591$ , $-0.515$ , $-0.124$ , and $-0.239$ , respectively. An exploratory permutation check with 9,999 draws and correction across four axes supports three trends; referring charts do not pass that check (adjusted $p=0.3364$ ). Geometry’s high-rung error rates coincide with substantial truncation: on S5, accuracy is 1.39% and truncation is 88.89%. Thus the construction provides empirical difficulty variation in this collection, with a length component; these diagnostics do not establish a causal effect of hops or a training advantage from difficulty-aware selection.


##### Task-precondition and representation checks.

We additionally check the same fixed 1,000 tasks for applicable construction constraints: agreement between encoded configurations and independent text parsers; exact table headers including units; nonnegative counts and stated differences; unique in-bounds referents; positive geometry targets; and unique choice labels and option values. All 1,000 pass the applicable checks. Coherent ambiguity injections are separate from simple file corruption: the checks reject 110 duplicated correct options, 106 tied table extrema or ranks, and 105 ambiguous base referents, as well as 1,000 configuration/target mismatches. These 1,321 cases test the independent audit, not every production admission path.


##### Checks against actual image content.

Replaying the frozen generators on the selected keys reproduces the configuration, question and answer for all 200 geometry and 200 single-series chart tasks. With an explicit Fontconfig profile for the applicable Cairo renderers, the original PNGs are reproduced byte-for-byte on 200 geometry, 200 table and 200 counting tasks; all 200 chart images also reproduce in their recorded rendering branch. No original image is replaced. Independent SVG inspection verifies table values and layout, and counting-category counts, canvas bounds and absence of filled-shape overlap. Fixed OCR on the original table PNGs matches every row name and all numeric columns on 192/200 tasks; eight numeric OCR disagreements are retained with image crops, rather than counted as completed human annotations.

For the remaining 200 referring-chart tasks, source render receipts and configuration records match; independent coordinate read-back agrees with the configured values. All 200 original plot regions match the reconstructed geometry, and OCR of the original category labels and numerical axis ticks agrees on all 200. Their whole-image reproduction remains 0/200, with generic-font rendering differences unresolved. Thus the audit provides 800 exact whole-image reproductions plus 200 plot-region and key-text checks, not 1,000 exact whole-image reproductions. The recorded font profile reproduces the applicable groups but is not claimed to uniquely recover the historical machine configuration. These finite checks strengthen data binding without certifying every rendered property.


##### Quality checks on the fixed 1,000 tasks.

All tasks pass independent reference recomputation, source consistency, image-file hashes, PNG decoding, minimum dimensions, nonblank-image checks, and diagnostic-holdout ID exclusion. Four defects are injected per task: a changed reference, modified image bytes, a changed source question, and a truncated PNG. The corresponding independent audit checks detect all 4,000 injected defects. This establishes detection of those specified defects on these tasks; it does not prove that all original admission paths enforce these checks or that every rendered question is semantically unambiguous.

### 20 Why Random Wins: Mechanisms

We offer three mechanisms. They are supported by logs but not isolated by ablations.

- 1. A random pool keeps the tasks the model cannot finish. Choosing tasks moves the pool away from the full difficulty mix and removes tasks the model cannot finish within the limit. Those are exactly the tasks on which the termination term gives its strongest signal (in a random pool 21.9% of training answers were truncated, and the correlation between length and advantage was $-0.31$ ; in selected pools truncation was 17%). The consequence is visible at evaluation: on the eleven-benchmark suite at 8192 tokens, truncation at window 8 is 4.16% for $\mathcal{A}$ -RSI and 8.14% for Frontier ; at window 4 of the earlier selection experiment it is 6.56% for every-window random refresh, 12.70% for learnable-band sampling, and 14.30% for failure-prioritized sampling.

- 2. Selection changes the answer types. The pool built by failure-prioritized sampling has no multiple-choice tasks, whereas every-window random refresh has 222 and learnable-band sampling has 227, and failure-prioritized sampling scores 72.81% on the 114 retained yes/no items (WATCH under the recomputed sentinel). This mixes format effects into the comparison.

- 3. Random pools do not saturate within 4–8 windows. The number of tasks with training signal per window is 209, 180, 189, and 183 for every-window random refresh, flat after window 2, so the data do not support the idea that longer training would let a controller overtake random.

Part of the “difficulty” of the frontier is also not difficulty. In the first 6,000 probed candidates, 39% of geometry answers were truncated at 4096, so much of what looks hard in multi-hop geometry is the model running out of length. Some difficulty is created by format: the model often writes the option’s numeric value in the answer box instead of the letter, which the reward scores 0. In the first 12,000 candidates, a lenient grader found 317 tasks (2.6%) that a strict grader marks wrong at least three times more often, 311 of them multiple-choice multi-hop geometry.

### 26 Evaluation Protocol and Noise Analysis


##### Benchmark suites.

The main suite contains nine benchmarks shared with earlier diagnostic evaluations: ChartQA ( Masry et al., 2022 ) , MMMU ( Yue et al., 2024 ) , MathVerse ( Zhang et al., 2024 ) , MathVision ( Wang et al., 2024 ) , VisNumBench ( Weng et al., 2025 ) , MMSI ( Yang et al., 2026 ) , and the 4-option, 10-option, and vision versions of MMMU-Pro ( Yue et al., 2025 ) . The primary eleven-benchmark suite adds PhyX-MC and SCEMQA-MC, for 22,042 questions. The historical twelve-benchmark diagnostic suite instead adds MathVista ( Lu et al., 2024 ) , HallusionBench ( Guan et al., 2024 ) , and MM-Vet ( Yu et al., 2024 ) , for 20,371 questions. Both use complete test splits. Macro11 and Macro12 are equal-weight means over their respective benchmarks, rather than pooled item accuracies.


##### Evaluation schedule and metrics.

Main trajectories, refresh-policy and selection ablations, endpoint audits, and the earlier-chain and extension benchmark comparisons use the eleven-benchmark suite and format-corrected scoring. The primary output limit is 8192 tokens; the per-window and earlier-chain tables additionally identify 4096-token results. Data efficiency divides the gain over $S_{t}$ under the same suite, scoring rule, and limit by thousands of new training tasks. The fresh-pool completion decomposition uses all eleven benchmarks with the same equal-benchmark weights. The fixed 1,000-question sample and qualitative cases retain their stated item scopes. The length-budget comparisons use the same eleven-benchmark suite at matched output limits; earlier 16k and multi-hop diagnostics retain their explicitly stated historical scopes.


##### Auxiliary views.

Stable format keeps letter-, number-, and expression-gold items, applies format-corrected scoring to those same outputs, and equally averages the eleven benchmark subset accuracies (21,415 items). The sentinel is recomputed on the 114 yes/no items retained in the eleven-benchmark suite: COLLAPSE means accuracy below 70%; WATCH means accuracy below 74% or a numeric-box share at least 10 points above the two-run $S_{t}$ reference; otherwise it reports OK. Visual-type statistics use the original fixed labels restricted to retained benchmarks and exclude the unannotated science sets.


##### Scoring and generation.

Each evaluation samples one response per item at temperature 0.6 and top- $p$ 0.95. The prompt and original answer checker follow the public MM-Zero evaluation code ( Li et al., 2026 ) . The original matching procedure combines answer matching, unambiguous option-value matching, and Math-Verify equivalence for non-letter gold answers. The format-corrected accuracy reported in the main and ablation tables retains those accepted answers and additionally accepts non-truncated unboxed final answers and normalized option values. ChartQA numeric answers receive a 5% tolerance, except for year answers or questions asking for a year. Truncation is recorded separately; a length-limited response retains credit when the checker accepts its answer. Format checks reuse the same fixed responses, and each evaluation uses complete test splits at its stated output limit.


##### LLM-judged accuracy.

LJ uses Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct with the MM-Zero judging prompt, answer extraction, and verdict parser. Questions of at most 400 characters retain their existing judge verdicts; longer questions are re-evaluated with the complete question. The judge receives the extracted final answer, falling back to the first 600 characters of the response when extraction is empty. Judging uses temperature 0 and a 16-token output limit. Reported LJ scores equally average the eleven full benchmark sets.


##### Measured variability on the eleven-benchmark protocol.

Each policy arm was trained once. The four ladder-control runs with identical first-window data and seed score 54.74, 55.27, 54.45, 54.03 at 4096 tokens (range 1.25, sample sd 0.52). At window 2 and 8192 tokens, the ladder and truncation-aware runs, whose first two pool files hash identically, score 58.14 and 59.60 (difference 1.46). The two evaluations of the same $S_{t}$ weights at 4096 score 54.55 and 54.72, differing by 0.17; their science outputs are shared, so this pair measures decoding variation only on the nine common benchmarks. These examples show variability but do not provide replicated uncertainty for each endpoint policy difference.


##### Interpreting differences.

We report descriptive single-run point estimates and stable-format checks. The available repeats do not estimate variation across seeds for each policy. Shared-prefix runs are not independent replications, and endpoint differences alone do not establish significance, equivalence, or superiority.


### 27 Task Construction and Training Details


#### 27.1 Template synthesis and task instantiation


##### From seed types to templates.

Given seed task types in geometry, charts, tables, and counting, the base model generates reusable problem structures and their executable implementations. For each structure, it determines the conditions and question target, chooses the variable parameters and their admissible ranges, and writes the procedures for sampling an instance, drawing it, composing its question, and computing its answer. These components form a template $\tau$ . The resulting library $\mathcal{T}$ is fixed before GRPO training.


##### From templates to tasks.

The seven generators in Table 30 select structures from the library and sample their parameters. Geometry parameters determine the points, relations, and given quantities. Chart and table parameters determine categories, labels, series, and numerical values. Counting parameters determine the objects and queried attributes. Each generator uses the sampled instance both to render the image and to construct the question and answer. For multiple-choice tasks, the program also constructs distractors and assigns the correct option its answer label.


##### Execution and checks.

Each candidate undergoes the checks implemented by its generator. Geometry checks compare the derived answer with the constructed relations or coordinate measurements. Chart checks compare the rendered marks with the underlying values and axis specification. Further checks reject ambiguous or invalid questions, and freshness and contamination screens decide whether an accepted candidate can enter the reservoir. Retained records include the image, question, answer, generator identity, and seed. Algorithm 2 summarizes the pipeline.

##### A concrete geometry example.

An initial-pool geometry template draws parallel lines $AB$ and $CD$ , with a transversal meeting them at $G$ and $H$ . The segment $GM$ bisects $\angle BGH$ and meets $CD$ at $M$ . The program samples the given angle $\beta=\angle BGH$ from $40^{\circ},42^{\circ},\ldots,150^{\circ}$ and constructs the point coordinates. When the question asks for the acute angle $\angle GMH$ , the answer is $\beta/2$ . Sampling $64^{\circ}$ therefore produces a question with answer $32^{\circ}$ . The program recomputes the angle from the coordinates and rejects the instance if the two answers disagree.

#### 27.3 Initial pool and optimization


##### Initial pool.

The initial 500 tasks come from three earlier generation batches, two for charts and one for geometry. The retained pool contains 204 geometry questions and 296 chart questions. Programs supply the answers, while the earlier pipeline uses an API-served Qwen3-VL-8B for question processing and checks. Of the retained questions, 342 use model-rewritten wording, 117 keep the original program wording, and 41 revert to it after rewriting. Pool selection uses eight-response probes and filters candidates by correct-answer count and truncation, with constraints on the question-type mix and reuse of each image. This pool is separate from the later 16,000-task reservoir. Two questions in the initial pool were later identified as configuration isomorphs of MathVerse items.


##### Initialization and optimization.

The starting checkpoint $S_{t}$ follows 100 GRPO updates of Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct on the old pool. In the preceding plateau check, another 300 steps on the same pool changed the benchmark average by $+0.45$ , $-0.27$ , $-0.38$ , and $-0.63$ across four training seeds. The continuation experiments start from $S_{t}$ and retain that pool until their respective replacement rules act.

The five-arm comparison reuses accepted checkpoints where the runs coincide. All arms except Continuous share the first two windows on the old pool, and $\mathcal{A}$ -RSI and Frontier also share the third. The first four windows of Continuous come from an earlier run of the same difficulty-control strategy, with fresh pools used from window 1.

Training uses FSDP2 and colocated vLLM, with 100 optimizer updates per window and a constant learning rate of $10^{-6}$ . Each window starts with a fresh optimizer and scheduler, retaining the preceding model parameters. Each prompt has eight rollouts, sampled at temperature 1.0 and top- $p$ 1.0 with a 4,096-token response limit. A generation batch contains eight prompt groups and serves two optimizer steps. The detector counts each batch once, giving 50 distinct batches and 3,200 responses per window. The GRPO objective uses group-normalized advantages, a clipping threshold of 0.2, and no KL penalty. Dynamic resampling checks variance in correctness plus termination reward and excludes the format term, with at most six resampling rounds. The final optimization reward and logged advantages include all three terms. The detector reads those logged advantages after resampling.

The reward coefficients in Eq. ( 2 ) are $\lambda_{\mathrm{fmt}}=0.1$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{stop}}=0.3$ , with unit weight on answer correctness. These coefficients stay fixed across windows.

The stopping term in Eq. ( 2 ) uses the decoder’s recorded finish reason and the final boxed answer. A response that reaches the token limit receives $-1$ even if it contains a boxed expression earlier in the text. Once a response ends normally with a final boxed answer, this term stays at 0 regardless of the response length. Its effect on truncation and benchmark scores is examined in Sections 20 and 7.1 .


##### Refresh-controller settings.

The controller in Eq. ( 4 ) uses lag $\ell=2$ , minimum within-pool observation period $n_{\min}=3$ , signal-retention threshold $\rho=0.85$ , and accuracy-increase threshold $\eta=0.10$ . The numerical tolerance in Eq. ( 3 ) is $\varepsilon=10^{-9}$ . Thus, after at least three accepted windows on the same pool, a decline of at least 15% in the distinct-prompt advantage signal or a rise of at least ten percentage points in logged accuracy, relative to two windows earlier, triggers replacement. The initial pool contains 500 tasks, each replacement contains $B=400$ , and each window has $H=100$ updates with $m=8$ responses per prompt. Replacement-family quotas are $\mathbf{k}=(194,131,54,21)$ , as detailed in Appendix 27.2 . All controller settings are fixed before training.


#### 27.4 Diagnostic difficulty tiers

For diagnosis we organize tasks into six controlled ladders: two multi-hop geometry ladders of five tiers, plane geometry of five, chart reading of ten cells, referring multi-hop of three, and counting of three. Each ladder orders related tasks from easy to hard. Accuracy is measured on held-out synthetic tasks that are excluded from training. Models’ own predictions of which questions they will get wrong give AUC about 0.50, a program-based predictor 0.58–0.61, and measured accuracy of other models 0.72–0.82. At $S_{t}$ , the pass rate falls monotonically with tier, for example .94, .57, .32, .14, and .07 from tier 1 to 5 on one multi-hop ladder. The comparison arms build frontier pools from these measurements (Section 6.2 ).

For Frontier and Periodic , the frontier is the easiest tier in each ladder with a held-out pass rate of at most 70%. If none qualifies, we select the hardest tier. The selected tier supplies 50% of the ladder’s quota, and each adjacent tier supplies 25%. Continuous re-measures pass rates after every window and moves the selected tier up one step at a pass rate of at least 80%, down one step at a pass rate of at most 20%, and otherwise keeps it unchanged. It uses the same allocation across the selected and adjacent tiers. These measurements do not affect random replacement or its refresh trigger.


### 28 Training Health Gate

In the main Qwen experiment, a window is accepted when its checkpoint export is complete, at least 50 generation batches are present, the learning rate remains $10^{-6}$ for all 100 updates, finish reasons are recorded, the training source has not changed, and the weights have moved. The weight check uses the median relative change of three sampled tensors, with a threshold of $3.69\times 10^{-5}$ . At least 97 of the 100 updates must have finite, nonzero gradient norms. A failed window is retried once from the same preceding checkpoint with the same pool and seed. A successful retry enters the detector normally. A second failure halts the run.

The trainer can also stop an attempt before all 100 updates complete: if six consecutive generation batches each contain at least one zero-advantage group after resampling, the attempt is aborted. This incomplete attempt fails the window gate and enters the same-pool retry procedure. The condition concerns the presence of a zero-advantage group in each batch, even when other groups carry signal; it does not establish that every task in the pool has become uninformative.

The detector, controller, probes, and pool builder use access guards that block benchmark scores and evaluation outputs. The legacy contamination guard runs separately and returns a flag. An independent checker logged file accesses during the guarded processes and recorded zero test-set accesses.

The earlier experiments required at least 99 of 100 updates to have normal gradients. Seven attempts failed at 98/100 across five windows. Fixed-pool continuation failed once at each of windows 10 and 11, and original-pool continuation failed once at window 3. Their retries passed, and the 8192-token comparison with MM-Zero uses the retrained fixed-pool continuation weights. Partial-pool refresh failed twice at window 12, and one ladder-control run failed twice at window 3. Both runs stopped. Each failure arose from a single generation batch in which all eight groups had zero variance. The new experiments pre-registered a threshold of 97/100. In them, No-Refresh failed twice at window 7 with 94/100 normal steps, 35 zero-signal warnings, and training accuracy .728.


### 29 Engineering Notes

- • File timestamps on the shared disk have a one-second resolution and can lag. A pre-registered ordering check based on modification times blocked arm No-Refresh at window 5 (two files written in the same second). The order is now proven by a content hash chain, with modification times as a coarse check with five seconds of tolerance, and the check is rehearsed with same-second mutants. The first version of the experiment was frozen and rebuilt as sagpd2 under the rule that each experiment is frozen only once.

- • Quota exhaustion halted construction for about 11 hours once and left five machines idle; long dependency chains are now queued ahead of time.

- • A pre-registered health gate does block runs. Readouts are written as “no reading” and the gate is not relaxed afterwards.

- • Command arguments must be quoted (an unquoted argument disabled depth feedback in one experiment), and base port plus stride times shard count must stay under 65,535.

- • Zero-data discipline. Using the 70,000 public ThinkLite tasks as a candidate pool was considered and dropped before any download, since it would violate the zero-data rule.


### 30 Further Documented Issues in the MM-Zero Reproduction

The following are observations in our reproduction setting and are not claims about the correctness of the released system.

- • Reproduction. Published base and v5 scores reproduce (50.27 against 50.03, and 54.25 against 54.18). Local training reproduces three rounds (v1 $+2.67$ , v2 $+4.05$ , v3 $+3.30$ over base).

- • Gain is mostly finishing, by four routes. Paired per-item analysis (4,006 base failures that did not finish, from 0.30 to 29.18); strict reproduction (7,257 finishing items, v1 $+0.69$ , v2 $+0.37$ , p-values above); an untrained base at 16384 matching MM-Zero at 4096 (51.12 against 51.08); and the pre-registered 8192 comparison (59% retained).

- • Source and paper review. The two equations for the difficulty reward and the training label contradict each other; no image-necessity check (the figure-1 example can be answered without the image); text and table disagree (56.6% against 54.5%; the 4B block average differs from its cells by up to 0.89; 10 against 60 training steps; 5 against 8 rollouts; the same 8B base evaluated twice differs by 0.4 with no error bars); the central claim that three roles beat two has no experiment (the coder was never removed); one equation branch can never fire and another affects 0.03% of batches yet is credited with 2.9 points; labels have no program-based ground truth; the denominator drops truncated responses; about 16% of released images are almost entirely black.

- • A retracted accusation. We had earlier reported that MM-Zero lowers accuracy on items the base model already answers ( $-1.28$ ). With a zero-effect baseline (the same model compared with itself gives $-3.26$ ) the change is $+2.0$ and there is no harm. That claim is withdrawn.

- • Not done. A 16k fair-budget comparison (about four node-hours), and mechanistic ablations of MM-Zero were not run.


### 31 Limitations

- • Replication and evaluation horizon. Each arm was trained once. The main comparison spans eight continuation windows and has no 16k evaluation. Small score differences lack significance testing. No-Refresh halted at $S_{31}$ , leaving the pre-registered $S_{32}$ endpoint unobserved.

- • Refresh timing. The first refresh point was set using existing logs. Without a random-refresh control on a fixed three-window schedule, the contribution of history-dependent timing remains entangled with the use of fresh tasks.

- • Cross-model transfer. MiMo has a positive fresh-versus-retained endpoint difference in one run, with small margins over Continuous and Periodic . Their pools were selected at Qwen’s difficulty frontier, limiting conclusions about transfer across model families.

- • Generator scope. The fixed programmatic library covers geometry, charts, tables, and counting, but omits function plots, numerical estimation, and spatial tasks. Task-family shares and 19 thematic terms were informed by the evaluation benchmarks; diversity beyond these templates remains unmeasured. The initial task pool also contains model-rewritten questions with program-computed answers.
