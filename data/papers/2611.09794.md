## Seeing What Matters: Benchmarking Task-Conditioned Actionable Spatial Belief for Manipulation

When should a robot look at the cup, and when at the portafilter? Spatial-reasoning benchmarks evaluate whether a model can describe a scene, but manipulation needs something more specific: the ability to acquire the particular evidence that makes the current command executable. A view that is informative for one task can be useless for another, even at the same workstation. We introduce Seeing What Matters, a benchmark for task-conditioned actionable spatial belief in simulated tabletop manipulation. Across three domains—coffee workstation, drawer/cabinet, and microwave/appliance—the benchmark provides 860 frozen evaluation cases; expanding across methods, budgets, and inference repeats yields more than 115,000 evaluator episodes under two protocols: closed-loop actionability and same-object functional out-of-distribution generalization. Each case pairs a set of candidate RGB views with a manipulation command, shared scripted skills, and hidden postconditions, so the benchmark directly measures whether a view-selection policy acquires the observations its command actually needs. We also provide a diagnostic reference pipeline that maintains a spatial–functional belief state and ranks views by missing evidence. At primary budget settings, this pipeline reaches 98.8% closed-loop and 98.9% functional-OOD success; ablating the functional or command-binding components collapses performance to near zero. Seven vision-language models, evaluated on the same cases, reach only 26–43%. Replacing the reference pipeline’s coverage metadata with frozen RGB-derived scores preserves 98.5%/98.9% success; shuffling the visual scores collapses it to 15%/11%, confirming that the result depends on image content. These gaps suggest that spatial belief for manipulation should be evaluated not by scene-level completeness, but by whether it acquires the evidence the current command requires.

### 1 Introduction

An embodied agent can look at the right scene and still acquire the wrong evidence. Consider a coffee workstation: tamping requires observing the portafilter basket, puck surface, and tamper alignment; cup placement requires cup–spout alignment, support, and clearance; grinding requires the hopper, chute, receiver, and relevant controls. The physical workspace may be unchanged, yet the observation that makes one command executable can be irrelevant to another. Seeing more is not necessarily seeing what matters.

Recent work has expanded spatial reasoning from passive question answering toward active belief construction and embodied interaction [ 10 , 39 ] . Theory of Space studies whether agents can acquire, maintain, and revise spatial beliefs through exploration [ 49 ] ; ESI-Bench evaluates spatial intelligence through perception–action loops [ 12 ] ; and BEHAVIOR-Spatial connects spatial understanding and instruction following to downstream robot behavior [ 6 ] . In parallel, vision-language-action policies and affordance-centered methods increasingly ground language in object parts, action regions, and manipulation trajectories [ 4 , 19 , 29 , 25 , 48 ] , while active-vision systems learn where to look before acting [ 2 , 1 , 44 , 18 , 21 , 32 , 41 ] .

These directions motivate a narrower but consequential question: given a command, can an agent acquire the particular spatial-functional evidence required to execute it? Existing evaluations may measure the accuracy of a spatial answer, the quality of a global representation, or the final success of a learned policy. Final success alone, however, does not reveal whether an agent selected the correct evidence, relied on a shortcut, or was rescued by the controller. Conversely, a geometrically accurate belief may contain many facts while omitting the one relation that makes the next action safe and executable. A controlled evaluation should therefore hold the scene, candidate observations, and downstream skill fixed, vary the command and observation budget, and test whether the acquired evidence supports a hidden task outcome.

We introduce Seeing What Matters , a frozen RGB benchmark for task-conditioned actionable spatial belief ( Fig. 2 ). The benchmark spans three simulated tabletop domains: a five-task coffee workstation (500 cases covering tamping, portafilter insertion, cup placement, serving, and grinding), a drawer/cabinet interface (180 cases emphasizing handle access, openness, and reachability), and a microwave/appliance interface (180 cases emphasizing cavity support and control state)—860 evaluation cases in total ( Figs. 4 and 4 ). Each case pairs a command with twelve frozen candidate-view RGB observations, a shared scripted skill interface, and hidden simulator-state postconditions; the resulting base contains 6,000 candidate-view records. Expansion across evaluated methods, observation budgets, and repeated runs produces more than 115,000 evaluator episodes, including 60,000 closed-loop and 55,500 same-object functional-OOD episodes from the primary coffee domain. The functional-OOD episodes preserve object identity while changing command-conditioned functional requirements, testing whether a selector follows the requested task rather than an object- or scene-level shortcut.

The evaluation separates two sensing contracts. Under the primary sequential protocol, every policy starts from the same frozen candidate set and initial state, acquires observations under a common budget $H$ , and passes the resulting evidence to the same scripted skill. Under a separate strict-clean VLM stress test, a model previews all twelve frozen RGB candidates and must return exactly three ranked view identifiers; this second experiment evaluates full-candidate visual ranking rather than an equivalent active-acquisition policy. Both protocols reserve simulator-state postconditions for evaluation: hidden state is never supplied to the view selector. This separation makes it possible to attribute failures to evidence acquisition without conflating them with learned low-level control.

We additionally provide a transparent, metadata-assisted reference pipeline. A Spatial–Functional Belief Ledger records task-relevant pose, relation, function, provenance, confidence, and staleness fields. A rule-based View Belief Map binds the current command to required predicates and ranks candidate views by their expected gain over missing evidence; it uses the command, public candidate-view metadata, and the current ledger state, but not hidden postconditions or labels from unselected observations. Optional Action-Induced Belief Revision compares predicted and observed postconditions after execution, localizes potentially stale ledger entries, and requests targeted re-observation. The purpose of this pipeline is not to establish a general learned vision policy but to provide an auditable reference whose command-to-evidence decisions can be inspected directly.

The results expose a substantial gap between task-conditioned evidence selection and generic visual ranking. At the primary settings, the diagnostic reference pipeline reaches $98.8\%$ closed-loop success at $H\!=\!5$ and $98.9\%$ functional-OOD success at $H\!=\!3$ . Removing functional belief fields reduces functional-OOD success to $0.0\%$ ; removing command binding reduces it to $19.7\%$ . Under the more restrictive $H\!=\!1$ initial-selection setting, closed-loop and functional-OOD success fall to $19.0\%$ and $58.6\%$ , respectively. In the separate full-candidate stress test, seven strict-clean VLMs achieve only $26.0\%$ – $42.9\%$ postcondition success despite previewing every candidate image ( Fig. 1 ): models frequently detect target-related cues without consistently selecting evidence that supports the hidden task outcome. These findings suggest that actionable belief requires more than scene recognition: it requires binding the current command to the evidence needed for execution.


##### Contributions.

Our contributions are threefold:

- • We formulate task-conditioned actionable spatial-functional belief , evaluating a spatial belief by whether it exposes the parts, relations, and functional states required by the current command. We introduce a frozen RGB benchmark spanning three simulated tabletop domains (five-task coffee workstation, drawer/cabinet, and microwave/appliance) with 860 evaluation cases, 6,000 candidate-view records, shared scripted skills, hidden simulator-state postconditions, and controlled closed-loop and functional-OOD evaluation.

- • We provide an auditable metadata-assisted reference pipeline comprising a Spatial–Functional Belief Ledger, a rule-based View Belief Map, and optional Action-Induced Belief Revision, while explicitly separating public selection information from hidden evaluator state.

- • We evaluate budget-matched baselines, ablations, no-image and shortcut controls, paired scene-group bootstrap estimates, observation-cost behavior, and seven strict-clean VLMs, accompanied by manifests, raw records, table-generation artifacts, and traceable admission checks.

### 2 Related Work


##### Spatial intelligence and embodied evaluation.

Spatial-reasoning evaluation has advanced across complementary layers. Benchmarks now test spatial memory and measurement from video, egocentric embodied cognition, and reference frames and spatial relations in 2D/3D observations [ 45 , 9 , 35 ] ; diagnostic and comprehensive evaluation suites probe spatial-reasoning breadth [ 40 , 42 ] ; and vision-language methods train spatial reasoning, optimize 3D layouts, and support agentic spatial understanding [ 5 , 36 , 23 ] . EmbodiedQA and Habitat established goal-directed navigation and interactive evaluation in simulated environments, while ENACT evaluates egocentric interaction through world modeling [ 10 , 30 , 39 ] . XVR trains cross-view correspondence, verification, and localization and transfers the resulting representations to manipulation [ 15 ] . Theory of Space asks whether an exploring agent forms and revises an internal spatial belief, connecting modern foundation-model evaluation to classical planning under partial observability [ 49 , 17 ] . These works principally score answers, representations, or broad agent behavior. We focus on a narrower controlled interface: whether observations selected before execution expose the command-specific evidence required for a shared manipulation skill to satisfy a hidden postcondition.


##### Manipulation benchmarks and policies.

RLBench, ManiSkill2, and CALVIN provide standardized task suites for learning and evaluating diverse or long-horizon manipulation policies [ 14 , 11 , 24 ] ; CLIPort and VIMA further test language- or multimodal-prompt-conditioned tabletop generalization [ 33 , 16 ] . ALFRED and TEACh evaluate language-grounded household task execution, with TEACh additionally requiring interactive dialogue [ 34 , 26 ] . SAPIEN, Habitat, and BEHAVIOR-1K provide complementary simulation infrastructure and activity suites for controlled embodied evaluation [ 43 , 30 , 20 ] . These settings primarily vary the learned policy and score end-to-end task completion or generalization. Our benchmark instead fixes the downstream skill and hidden evaluator, isolating whether a method first acquires the command-specific spatial-functional evidence that makes execution possible; this decomposition complements, rather than replaces, evaluation on broad manipulation suites. Within the learned-policy literature, Where2Act localizes actionable regions on articulated objects, SeqAfford grounds sequential 3D affordances, and RT-2, OpenVLA, SpatialVLA, and $\pi_{0}$ map visual-language context to robot actions or spatially grounded action representations [ 25 , 48 , 4 , 19 , 29 , 3 ] . ActiveVLA and SaPaVe explicitly incorporate critical-region localization or active view–pose reasoning [ 22 , 21 ] . PhysVLM focuses on physical reachability, while RoboGround grounds manipulation with vision-language priors [ 51 , 13 ] . GraspGPT and ThinkGrasp use language-model or vision-language reasoning for task-oriented grasping and strategic part grasping in clutter [ 37 , 28 ] .


##### Active perception for embodied action.

Classic active-vision and next-best-view work selects sensing actions to reduce uncertainty or improve reconstruction [ 2 , 1 , 8 , 27 , 31 ] . Recent robotic systems learn task-directed gaze, camera motion, or base placement: Vision in Action learns human-like search and focus from demonstrations; EyeRobot couples gaze with hand actions; Observe Then Act coordinates camera next-best-view selection with gripper next-best-pose planning; and Mobi- $\pi$ chooses robot base poses that make a downstream policy effective [ 44 , 18 , 38 , 46 ] . VISO-Grasp and Observer-Actor further couple active view planning or sparse-view perception to manipulation [ 32 , 41 ] . WoMAP likewise uses learned world models for active open-vocabulary localization under partial observability [ 47 ] . We share their task-directed sensing motivation, but target a different evaluation question: whether a fixed candidate-view selector, under matched budgets and with the same downstream skill and evaluator for every method, acquires the right evidence.


##### Closest concurrent benchmarks.

FUSE studies learned semantic–geometric evidence acquisition for functional affordance grounding, ESI-Bench broadens embodied spatial evaluation to perception–action loops, VABench studies visual demonstrations, active perception, and metric control, and BEHAVIOR-Spatial connects spatial understanding and instruction following to robot behavior [ 7 , 12 , 50 , 6 ] . These concurrent efforts broaden the learned-policy or agent setting. Seeing What Matters contributes a controlled complement: frozen RGB candidates, explicit sensing contracts, command-shift interventions, a fixed skill interface, hidden simulator-state postconditions, and a metadata-assisted reference selector whose decision trace can be audited. This narrower design is intended to isolate which evidence was acquired and why , not to establish a general-purpose active-vision policy.


### 3 Benchmark and Method


##### Task interface.

Each episode contains a hidden simulator state $s$ , a manipulation command $\tau$ , a set of candidate views $\mathcal{V}$ , and RGB observations acquired under budget $H$ . The agent sees the command, acquired RGB frames, candidate-view metadata, and its own ledger state; it never sees hidden predicates, unselected endpoint renders, evaluator labels, contact logs, or postcondition ground truth before acting. The five task families—tamp, insert, place, serve, and grind—each have 50 scene groups and two variants, yielding 500 frozen cases and 6,000 rendered candidate-view RGB records. All downstream execution uses the same shared scripted skill library, so differences in task outcome trace to evidence acquisition rather than controller quality.


##### Evaluation protocols.

The benchmark defines two protocols that isolate different aspects of evidence acquisition. Protocol C.1 ( closed-loop actionability , primary budget $H{=}5$ ) tests sequential view selection: the agent acquires up to five views one at a time, updating its ledger after each observation, then passes the accumulated evidence to the scripted skill. Protocol C.2 ( same-object functional OOD , $H{=}3$ ) holds object identity fixed across commands and varies only the functional requirements—a cup that must be placed needs alignment and clearance evidence, whereas the same cup in a serve command needs fill-level and stability evidence. This split exposes whether a selector follows the commanded task or falls back on an object- or scene-level shortcut. In both protocols, hidden simulator-state postconditions are evaluated only after execution: the evaluator checks whether the physical outcome satisfies the command’s requirements without revealing those conditions to the agent beforehand.


##### Spatial–functional belief ledger.

The Spatial–Functional Belief Ledger stores entries

[Tableau] $$B_{t}=\{(p_{i},\hat{y}_{i},q_{i},E_{i},\kappa_{i},\rho_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N_{t}},$$

where $p_{i}$ is a spatial or functional predicate, $\hat{y}_{i}$ is its estimated value, $q_{i}$ is confidence, $E_{i}$ records visual provenance, $\kappa_{i}$ marks staleness, and $\rho_{i}$ marks task relevance. Predicates include part visibility, relative pose, support, containment, action affordance, functional cue, and predicted postcondition state. In the reference implementation, $\rho_{i}$ is instantiated from command templates, $E_{i}$ stores the selected view IDs that support an entry, $q_{i}$ is a three-level confidence flag (observed, inferred, or unknown), and $\kappa_{i}$ is raised only by actions that may invalidate the corresponding predicate type. The benchmark evaluates whether a policy acquires the command-relevant evidence it needs; the ledger’s role is to make that evaluation transparent and traceable, not to serve as an optimal learned state estimator.


##### View Belief Map.

For each candidate view $v$ , the agent estimates which missing target predicates the view may resolve:

[Tableau] $$J(v;B_{t},\tau)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}_{t}|}\sum_{p_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{t}}g(v,p_{i},\tau)-\lambda\,c(v),$$

where $\mathcal{M}_{t}$ is the set of missing, uncertain, or stale target-relevant predicates, $g$ is an ordinal evidence-gain estimate, and $c(v)$ is normalized view cost. The selected views form a discrete View Belief Map over the candidate camera set—a command-conditioned score over executable observations, not a dense point-cloud value field.

The key implementation detail is that $g$ is a deterministic ordinal score, not a learned RGB scorer or privileged predicate checker. It uses the command template $R(\tau)$ , the ledger’s missing/stale set $\mathcal{M}_{t}$ , and public candidate metadata including semantic view type, camera relation, and nominal functional-part coverage. This metadata is a strong design aid and is disclosed as part of the reference method; it does not include hidden state, success labels, future postconditions, contact logs, or unselected RGB content. The resulting pipeline—hereafter the diagnostic reference selector —therefore serves as an auditable metadata-assisted anchor that future learned view selectors can be measured against. ##### Optional action-induced belief revision.

After a scripted skill executes, the evaluator returns a postcondition observation. Action-Induced Belief Revision compares predicted and observed postconditions, localizes the stale predicate type, lowers confidence in contradicted ledger entries, and selects a local repair view. Recovery is charged in the view/step budget and counted only when the final simulator postcondition passes. Because successful view selection makes mismatches rare in the primary protocol, Action-Induced Belief Revision is treated as a repair diagnostic and stress-test component rather than the main source of the benchmark’s performance gains.


### 4 Experimental Setup


##### Setup.

The experimental unit is 500 frozen cases (five tasks, 50 scene groups, two variants each), each paired with twelve candidate RGB views. We freeze task, postcondition, method, candidate-view, and failure-code manifests before evaluation. All non-privileged baselines share the same initial RGB view, candidate-view list, camera metadata, initial budget, scripted skill library, simulator reset distribution, and hidden postcondition evaluator. Hidden simulator predicates, ground-truth postconditions, evaluator labels, and unselected endpoint renders are never exposed before action. The supplement specifies each method’s sensing contract explicitly. Applying multiple methods, budgets, and inference repeats yields 60,000 closed-loop scripted-skill episodes and 55,500 functional-OOD episodes.

All raw records and SHA-256 manifests are retained.


##### Statistical reporting.

At the primary budget, each task-method cell contains 300 episodes (50 scene groups $\times$ 2 variants $\times$ 3 inference repeats); each aggregate method row contains 1,500 episodes across five tasks. We treat frozen cases—not repeated answers from the same case—as the conceptual unit for generalization. The supplement reports paired scene-group bootstrap intervals over 500 case clusters, where each cluster is a task, group, and variant with three inference repeats averaged before resampling. Budget-curve bands reproduce source-provided episode-level normal-approximation 95% intervals and are descriptive. The appendix retains the full per-budget, per-task, bootstrap, and failure-diagnostic plots so that large aggregate gains can be checked against task-level and case-level behavior.


##### Baselines.

Random samples valid views uniformly; Fixed scan follows a deterministic camera order; Pose coverage chooses geometry-revealing views regardless of command; History active reuses previously useful views without an explicit target ledger; Target ledger stores target predicates but does not score views by missing functional evidence; Privileged diagnostic uses privileged view labels under a fixed diagnostic selector. This privileged diagnostic is a privileged oracle-map/headroom anchor: it does not perform adaptive postcondition repair and may be affected by fixed single-policy tie-breaking. Success is the percentage of episodes whose scripted skill satisfies the hidden postcondition.


##### Diagnostic QA versus actionability.

VLM question answering verifies whether selected images contain answerable spatial-functional information. It is not the main performance claim. The main evidence is whether selected observations make the downstream action pass simulator-state postconditions.

##### Closed-loop actionability.

Table 1 shows that task-conditioned view selection turns spatial belief into executable action. The diagnostic reference selector with optional Action-Induced Belief Revision reaches 98.8% average success, improving over History active by 45.9 points and over Target ledger by 86.4 points. The gain is not explained by memory alone: the ledger must expose which missing predicate should determine the next view. The budget curves ( Fig. 9 in the supplement) confirm that the gap is not an artifact of choosing a single favorable budget; the advantage of the diagnostic reference selector persists across $H\!\in\!\{1,3,5,8\}$ in both the closed-loop and functional-OOD protocols.

Shaded bands are episode-level normal-approximation intervals.

The near-ceiling primary results should not be read as the benchmark being solved under every observation regime. The supplement adds a hard $H{=}1$ budget stress split re-summarized from the frozen episode logs. At $H{=}1$ , recovery-capable methods may still acquire additional charged observations after mismatch, so the constraint limits the initial selection, not the total observation cost. Even with that allowance, the diagnostic reference with Action-Induced Belief Revision drops to 19.0% in C.1 and 58.6% in C.2, exposing remaining difficulty that is hidden by the main $H{=}5$ and $H{=}3$ settings.


##### Same-object functional OOD.

Table 1 evaluates the harder same-object setting. A method may observe the coffee machine and still fail if it observes the wrong functional part for the command. The diagnostic reference selector with optional repair reaches 98.9% success and 70.0 functional selectivity; category shortcuts and command-agnostic affordance policies fail despite seeing plausible object parts. The functional-evidence visualization ( Fig. 13 in the supplement) shows the dissociation: target-functional cue observation tracks success, whereas wrong-command cue observation does not.


##### Foundation-model stress test.

We upgrade the foundation-model view-scoring protocol from a 500-row endpoint stress check to a 1,500-case strict-clean benchmark. A VLM is admitted only when all 1,500 raw responses have HTTP 200/endpoint-OK status, strict JSON parses, unique exact- $H{=}3$ selections drawn from the twelve frozen candidates, complete evaluator records, exact case order, and no fallback parsing. Seven models pass this gate. Their postcondition success ranges from 26.0% to 42.9%: Grok 4.3 leads at $42.9\!\pm\!2.5\%$ , followed by Grok 4.6 at $42.5\!\pm\!2.5\%$ , Amazon Nova Pro at $40.3\!\pm\!2.5\%$ , Qwen3-VL-8B at $39.4\!\pm\!2.5\%$ , Gemma 3 27B at $32.5\!\pm\!2.4\%$ , Llama 4 Maverick at $28.3\!\pm\!2.3\%$ , and Sonar Pro at $26.0\!\pm\!2.2\%$ (normal-approximation 95% intervals; $n{=}1{,}500$ per model). All seven strict-clean VLMs cluster within a 16.9-point band across multiple provider and model families. Even the strongest model remains 55.9 points below the diagnostic reference selector at the primary setting, suggesting a systematic gap in command-conditioned view selection rather than an idiosyncratic weakness of one provider. The gap is not a controlled architecture comparison—the methods use different priors and sensing contracts—but it shows that current prompted ranking does not recover the benchmark’s command-to-evidence rules.

The task-level pattern is consistent across models: Tamp and Place, whose target predicates involve large, visually salient parts (portafilter basket, cup–spout alignment), admit near-perfect scores from several VLMs, whereas Insert, Serve, and Grind require observing smaller or partially occluded functional chains (hopper–chute path, drip-tray clearance, grinder–receiver contact) that prompted VLMs fail to prioritize. This suggests that the gap is driven by the models’ inability to bind the command to the specific functional-part evidence chain, not by a blanket failure to parse candidate images.

Only models passing the full 1,500-case strict-clean gate enter the reported table; the supplement documents the gate criteria and lists models whose endpoints were unavailable.


##### Sanity checks.

The sanity-checks table ( Tab. 5 in the supplement) consolidates the shortcut and leakage sanity checks. These controls are intentionally conservative: they reuse the same frozen same-object functional-OOD cases rather than introducing easier auxiliary tasks. Category shortcuts, command-agnostic affordance viewing, removing functional fields, and removing target conditioning all fail under the same hidden evaluator. This pattern argues against a leakage explanation in which success comes from object category, hidden postconditions, or a template rule that ignores the command.


##### Ablation.

The ablation table ( Tab. 15 in the supplement) confirms that the decisive components are command binding, functional fields, and target conditioning. Removing functional fields does not harm the easier C.1 closed-loop protocol, but it collapses C.2 functional-OOD success to 0.0%; the distinction supports the claim that functional evidence matters specifically when object category is an unreliable shortcut. Removing Action-Induced Belief Revision has little effect in the primary functional-OOD split because mismatches are already rare after selecting the correct functional evidence; the recovery stress test ( Fig. 14 in the supplement) isolates the setting where repair matters. The supplement reports the full task-level ablation matrices.


##### RGB-derived coverage.

To test whether the reference pipeline depends on simulator-derived view metadata at inference, we replace its nominal view-to-predicate coverage mapping with VBM-Vision . A frozen CLIP-B/32 encoder first converts each candidate RGB image into a task-agnostic visual signature, and a lightweight per-predicate linear head estimates functional-part visibility. The head is trained with visibility labels on training scene groups and evaluated five-fold out of fold; every reported evaluation image is scored by a model that did not observe its scene group. At inference, the visual stage receives neither the command nor camera/view identifiers, simulator state, success labels, evaluator outputs, or nominal coverage metadata. The unchanged command-to-predicate binding is applied only after visual coverage prediction. With the evaluator, scripted skills, and postconditions fixed, VBM-Vision reaches 98.5% C.1 success at $H{=}5$ and 98.9% C.2 success at $H{=}3$ , with zero postcondition failures in both protocols. The remaining 1.5% and 1.1% failures are collision events from the shared scripted skills. Shuffling the RGB-derived coverage rows within each candidate set reduces success to 14.9% and 11.4%, respectively, confirming that the result depends on candidate-image content rather than simulator-derived coverage metadata at inference.

### 6 Discussion and Conclusion

Seeing What Matters reframes spatial reasoning for simulated embodied manipulation as actionability: can the agent see what matters for the current command before a shared skill executes? The evidence is strongest in the same-object functional-OOD split, where generic object views and wrong-command affordances are insufficient—a distinction that separates the benchmark from scene-level spatial QA and category-level affordance recognition. The Spatial–Functional Belief Ledger and diagnostic reference pipeline show that command-bound evidence selection converts spatial-functional belief into closed-loop task success under equal observation budgets, while optional Action-Induced Belief Revision diagnoses recovery after postcondition mismatch.

The main empirical finding is not that one foundation model fails, but that seven strict-clean VLMs cluster within a 16.9-point band and remain 55.9 points below the diagnostic reference selector under the same primary information contract.

The benchmark is deliberately scoped: it evaluates information selection and belief revision with frozen RGB candidate views, scripted skills, and simulator-state postconditions. The diagnostic reference scorer uses semantic view names and nominal functional-part coverage—an auditable anchor, not a learned perception model or an optimal view-selection policy. The current benchmark is not a physical robot deployment, a learned dexterous controller, or a claim of household-scale generality; it extends the original Theory-of-Space probing question from scene-level spatial belief to manipulation-conditioned actionability.

VBM-Vision eliminates simulator-derived coverage metadata at inference but retains predicate-visibility labels for training the coverage head; a fully unsupervised variant remains future work.

The 500 evaluation cases come from one simulated coffee workstation; episode totals reflect method, budget, and repeat combinations over these cases, not additional domains. The Drawer/Cabinet and Microwave/Appliance interfaces provide small sanity checks, not evidence of household-scale transfer. The primary splits are close to saturation for the reference selector, which is why the shortcut controls, no-image controls, paired scene-group bootstrap, and hard $H{=}1$ stress split are important. The VLM ranking test previews all candidates simultaneously, so its gap with the sequential reference selector is not a controlled architecture comparison.

The Drawer/Cabinet and Microwave/Appliance interfaces provide small-scale cross-domain sanity checks (180 cases each); broader evaluation with more object families, real robot execution, and learned view scorers remains future work.

The results suggest that future VLM/VLA manipulation benchmarks should evaluate not only what a model knows about a scene, but whether it can acquire the evidence that the next action requires.

### Appendix B Benchmark Construction and Information Boundary

The benchmark is a task-conditioned analogue of spatial-belief probing. Instead of asking whether a model can build a complete scene-level cognitive map, each episode asks whether a policy acquires the spatial and functional evidence needed by the current operation. The five primary coffee-task families are Tamp , Insert , Place , Serve , and Grind . Each family contains 50 scene groups and two variants, giving 100 variants per task and 500 variants per protocol before method and inference-repeat expansion.

Each episode contains a hidden simulator state, a command, a fixed set of candidate views, and an observation budget. The agent receives the command, acquired RGB frames, view metadata, and its own belief state. It does not receive hidden simulator predicates, evaluator labels, unselected endpoint renders, contact logs, or postcondition truth before acting. The same scripted skill library is used across methods, so differences in closed-loop success come from information selection and belief revision rather than from changing the low-level controller.

### Appendix D Protocols, Metrics, and Baseline Fairness


##### Protocols.

C.1 is the closed-loop actionability protocol. Its primary budget is $H=5$ , and it evaluates whether selected observations support successful execution of the shared scripted skill. C.2 is the same-object functional-OOD protocol. Its primary budget is $H=3$ , and it evaluates whether methods observe the command-relevant functional cue rather than a plausible cue for a different command. Recovery experiments evaluate postcondition mismatch handling after an initially stale or contradicted belief.


##### Metrics.

Task success is the percentage of episodes whose shared scripted skill satisfies the hidden simulator-state postcondition. Collision rate and postcondition-failure rate are reported separately. Average charged views count all acquired views, including recovery observations. Primitive steps count scripted skill steps. Unknown/abstain is tracked separately from failed execution. In C.2, target-functional hit measures whether selected views expose the functional cue required by the current command, wrong-command hit measures whether selected views expose a cue for another command, and command selectivity is their difference in percentage points.

##### VBM-Vision method details.

We compute a frozen CLIP-B/32 image-text signature for each of the 18,000 candidate RGB records and fit one class-balanced logistic-regression head per functional predicate. We use five scene-group folds (150 groups total; 120 training and 30 held-out groups per fold), fixed $C{=}1.0$ , the liblinear solver, and no evaluator-success-based hyperparameter tuning. All reported coverage scores are strictly out of fold. The coverage head is trained using nominal predicate-visibility labels from the training folds, but at inference it receives only frozen RGB-derived features and candidate-relative normalizations. It does not receive task or command identity, view or camera identifiers, simulator state, success labels, evaluator outputs, or the nominal view-to-predicate coverage mapping. The command is bound to predicted predicates only after this task-agnostic visual stage.

### Appendix L Limitations of the Supplementary Evidence

The supplementary evidence strengthens auditability but does not over-broaden the paper’s claim. The experiments evaluate simulated information selection and belief revision with frozen RGB candidate views, shared scripted skills, and hidden simulator-state postconditions. The direct VLM stress check and Drawer/Microwave sanity check address two reviewer concerns–whether a prompted foundation model can be evaluated under the same contract and whether the formulation is confined to one coffee object family–but they do not demonstrate physical robot deployment, learned dexterous control, or household-scale generality. The main bootstrap table reports paired scene-group intervals over frozen cases, while budget-curve bands remain descriptive episode-level intervals. A future version should add independent environment seeds and real-robot trials where possible. Finally, diagnostic VLM QA remains a support check: it indicates whether selected images contain answerable spatial-functional information, but the main performance claim is closed-loop simulator postcondition success.
