## Non-penetrating World Models

We study a new problem of preventing object penetration in interactive video world models. To understand how existing models handle this, we introduce NoPeBench , a benchmark that evaluates how world models respond to physical obstacles. Our evaluation reveals that none of the open-source models can reliably prevent such failures. Surprisingly, however, these models already encode the information needed to predict such violations. We find that the hidden states of diffusion transformers (DiTs) can predict whether a candidate action will penetrate an obstacle. Building on this, we introduce NoPeWorld , a lightweight framework that detects and prevents penetration. Specifically, we train a small prediction head on top of a frozen DiT’s hidden states to read out if a candidate action is safe or not. Unsafe actions are then replaced with safe ones that best preserve the original intent. Despite its simplicity, NoPeWorld substantially reduces the physical violation rate, averaged over six video world models, from 74% to 16%. Remarkably, our method shows zero-shot generalization to open-domain imagery, third-person viewpoints, and moving obstacles. We publicly release our code, the NoPeBench benchmark, and model weights at the links above.

### 1 Introduction

In the world of Harry Potter, Platform 9¾ allows travelers to pass through a solid wall and enter another world [ 36 ] . In reality, however, a wall cannot be crossed because it’s solid. Today’s world models [ 7 , 43 , 18 , 38 , 35 , 15 , 51 , 53 , 48 , 8 ] may still behave as if every wall were Platform 9¾. As shown in Fig. 1 , when commanded to move into a wall, five of the six generators we evaluate pass through it, while the remaining one moves against the user’s command. These failures reveal that current world models struggle to enforce the non-penetration constraint.

The underlying principle, however, is not new. In computer graphics, non-penetration requires that solid objects cannot occupy the same space [ 4 ] . Simulators and game engines have long enforced this constraint through collision detection and collision response [ 25 ] . They detect collisions and adjust the motion to prevent penetration while preserving the intended movement as much as possible.

Unfortunately, this mechanism is missing from today’s video world models. Basically, they are designed to follow user commands, whether camera poses [ 15 , 38 , 53 , 48 , 8 ] or key inputs [ 51 ] , without checking whether the motion is physically possible. Since their training videos rarely, if ever, show a command blocked by a wall, such a command is likely out of distribution. As a result, cameras may pass through walls, or obstacles simply disappear. Worse still, existing benchmarks largely use collision-free trajectories, making it difficult to evaluate how well world models handle non-penetration [ 46 , 49 , 45 , 14 ] .

To prevent such failures, two straightforward solutions seem promising at first glance. The first is to simply prompt the model to stop or slide along obstacles. However, we find that such prompts have little effect on penetration. The second is to use an external depth estimator [ 21 , 30 ] to detect obstacles and stop unsafe motions. While effective, this requires post-hoc processing with an additional network for every generated frame. Moreover, stopping alone may sacrifice the user’s intended motion. In that sense, both methods have their own limitations.

Surprisingly, we find that the generator itself already contains information needed to prevent penetration. Prior studies have shown that video diffusion models encode 3D structure and physical plausibility [ 13 , 9 , 50 ] . We find that this knowledge extends to predicting penetration. Specifically, the hidden states of a diffusion transformer (DiT) can predict whether a commanded action will cause penetration, even before the next video chunk is generated. In short, the generator already knows where its walls are, but its output fails to act on that knowledge.

Building on this observation, we introduce NoPeWorld , a lightweight framework that detects and prevents penetration in video world models. The rough idea is that rather than relying on external tools, we directly read the generator’s hidden states to detect unsafe actions ( Fig. 2 ). Specifically, we train a small prediction head on a frozen DiT to predict collision risks and distances to obstacles. The head is trained using ground-truth distance and collision labels from Unreal Engine simulations. During inference, unsafe motions are replaced with safe alternatives that best preserve the user’s intent, while safe motions remain unchanged.

We achieve this through two detection modes. In collision mode , the head reads the initial denoising features of the current chunk to detect potential penetration. In prediction mode , it uses features from the previous chunk to predict penetration in the upcoming chunk.

A new task, of course, requires a new benchmark. To evaluate this, we introduce NoPeBench , a benchmark that deliberately challenges world models with obstacles ( Sec. 4.1 ). It consists of three levels of tasks. \tasktag Task 1 evaluates, on ground-truth histories, whether a method keeps safe commands and corrects those that would penetrate. \tasktag Task 2 evaluates whether generated videos respect non-penetration using the ground-truth geometry of scenes from UnrealZoo [ 55 ] . \tasktag Task 3 tests generalization to open-domain scenes using vision-language model (VLM) as judges.

Our experiments show that NoPeWorld robustly detects and prevents penetration across diverse video world models. On LingBot-World 2.0 [ 15 ] , NoPeWorld intercepts nearly all unsafe commands ( Tab. 3 ), which cuts violations from 89% to 4% and lifts the Safe-Intent Score, zero for any video with a violation, from near zero to about half ( Secs. 4.4 and 4 ). Moreover, the heads transfer without retraining: trained only on first-person walks through static scenes from UnrealZoo, they reduce Task 3 violations on all six generators, on open-domain imagery, from a third-person viewpoint, and around moving obstacles.

Our contributions are fourfold. (i) Problem : To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to study the problem of penetration prevention in video world models. (ii) Method : We introduce NoPeWorld, which detects collisions by probing the hidden states of a frozen DiT and replaces unsafe motions with the safe motion that best preserves the user’s intent. (iii) Insights : We show that frozen world generators encode collision information even before future frames are generated ( Sec. 5.1 ). Such collision awareness can be exploited across visual domains, camera viewpoints, and moving obstacles without retraining. (iv) Benchmark : We collect a new benchmark called NoPeBench. It evaluates whether video world models can prevent penetration when given commands that would otherwise lead to collisions.


### 2 Related work

Interactive video generation has progressed from early action-conditioned predictors [ 7 , 43 , 2 ] to diffusion transformers [ 28 , 41 ] that denoise video chunk by chunk under camera-pose or keystroke control [ 17 , 29 , 54 , 18 , 38 , 35 , 15 , 53 , 48 , 20 , 51 , 8 ] . Because actions condition denoising in an open loop without scene geometry, these models “do not explicitly enforce collision constraints” [ 30 ] . Prompting with obstacle descriptions fails to prevent clipping, while guarding decoded frames with an external depth estimator [ 21 , 30 ] requires VAE decoding and auxiliary 3D inference at every chunk and only halts motion ( Secs. 4.3 and 5.3 ). NoPeWorld instead keeps the generator frozen and enforces collision-aware control directly inside the DiT loop ( Sec. 3 ).

Computer graphics and mechanics have enforced solid non-penetration since the 1980s through collision impulses [ 25 , 16 ] , unilateral contact constraints [ 22 , 23 , 26 , 4 , 5 , 37 ] , and penalty potentials [ 42 ] , later extended to continuous swept collision detection against thin-wall tunnelling [ 33 ] and intersection-free solvers [ 19 ] . All of these methods require explicit 3D geometry and solve contact mechanics on known surfaces. Rather than simulating contact forces, we adapt two kinematic principles, continuous swept clearance and frictionless collide-and-slide projection ( Sec. 3.1 ), to a video generator that stores no geometry, replacing unsafe commands with admissible stops or wall slides.

Intermediate features of image and video diffusion models capture semantic correspondence, depth, and 3D structure beyond surface appearance [ 6 , 40 , 9 , 50 ] . Recent studies probe hidden states [ 1 ] on intuitive-physics tasks [ 34 ] to show that video DiTs encode physical plausibility offline even when generated frames violate it [ 13 ] , or train a verifier on frozen DiT features to select among multiple sampled trajectories [ 39 ] . Consistent with this view, we find that frozen DiT states already predict upcoming obstacle contact before the next chunk is synthesized, whereas the same readout on VAE latents or decoded pixels falls short ( Sec. 5.1 ). Rather than offline probing or multi-sample rejection after generation, NoPeWorld queries frozen DiT tokens with a lightweight cross-attention head ( Sec. 3.2 ) to score and replace unsafe actions prior to chunk synthesis ( Sec. 3.3 ).

Physical video benchmarks [ 3 , 24 , 27 ] evaluate passive clips without user control, whereas interactive world-model benchmarks [ 14 , 52 , 47 , 46 , 49 , 45 ] focus on visual quality, memory, and action following along open paths: WorldMark [ 46 ] removes wall-blocked trajectories, WBench [ 49 ] scores clipping as one VLM rubric item along mostly unobstructed routes ( Sec. 4.1 ), and WorldRoamBench [ 45 ] tests 58 clipping and 14 collision cases with VLMs. To evaluate non-penetration directly, NoPeBench ( Sec. 4.1 ) commands every test route into an obstacle alongside open-path controls across teacher-forced ( Task 1 ), closed-loop ( Task 2 ), and open-domain ( Task 3 ) settings, pairing verified 3D reconstruction [ 44 ] and human-validated VLM judges with a hard-gated Safe-Intent Score inspired by NAVSIM [ 10 ] ( Sec. 4.2 ).

#### 3.1 Problem formulation

An interactive video world model synthesizes video chunk by chunk: given past frames $x_{<k}$ and a user command $u_{k}$ over the next $F$ latent frames, a diffusion transformer with parameters $\theta$ samples $x_{k}\sim p_{\theta}(x_{k}\mid x_{<k},u_{k})$ . Because $u_{k}$ conditions generation in an open loop without geometric feedback, driving $u_{k}$ into a solid obstacle forces the model either to cross the obstacle or to hallucinate it away.

Non-penetration in graphics . Computer graphics enforces solid boundaries through two principles. First, “A realistic simulation of rigid bodies demands that no two bodies inter-penetrate” [ 4 ] : a unilateral constraint requires the signed obstacle gap $g\big(\gamma(\rho)\big)\geq 0$ along a swept path $\gamma(\rho)$ for $\rho\in[0,1]$ [ 22 , 26 , 5 ] , tested over the full step so fast motion cannot tunnel through thin walls [ 25 , 33 ] . Second, upon contact with surface normal $n=\nabla g$ , a frictionless response that “can ‘push’ but not ‘pull’ ” [ 4 , 16 ] projects a commanded motion $u$ onto the closest admissible velocity, $v_{S}(u)=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{v^{\prime}\cdot n\geq 0}\|v^{\prime}-u\|^{2}=u-\min(0,u\cdot n)\,n$ , halting head-on and sliding along oblique walls.

Non-penetration in World Model . Bringing both principles into a world model recasts chunk synthesis as constrained generation over chunk $x_{k}$ and executed action $a_{k}$ :

[Tableau] $\displaystyle(x_{k}^{\star},a_{k}^{\star})=\argmax_{x_{k},\,a_{k}}\;$ $\displaystyle\log p_{\theta}(x_{k}\mid x_{<k},a_{k})-\lambda\,\epsilon_{S}(a_{k},u_{k})^{2}$ s.t. $\displaystyle\min_{\rho\in[0,1]}g_{S}\big(\gamma(\rho;\,a_{k})\big)\geq 0.$ (1)

Here $\min_{\rho\in[0,1]}g_{S}\big(\gamma(\rho;\,a_{k})\big)\geq 0$ enforces continuous non-penetration against scene geometry $S$ , $\lambda{>}0$ weighs intent against likelihood, and $\epsilon_{S}(a_{k},u_{k})$ is the distance of $a_{k}$ ’s displacement from the collide-and-slide response $v_{S}(u_{k})$ , relative to $|u_{k}|$ , its cross-slide part weighted by $w{=}0.5$ , allowing contact while penalizing stops or retreats. A generated video has a violation if its trajectory enters solid geometry ( penetration ), crosses an obstacle between frames ( tunnelling ), or erases an obstacle on approach ( vanishing ); obstacles may still move.

Freezing $\theta$ fixes the visual prior $p_{\theta}$ and leaves $a_{k}$ as the sole optimization variable; assuming that the generator renders the executed motion within the scene it has already shown, which Task 2 tests, the likelihood term of Eq. 1 no longer separates admissible actions, and a non-penetrating $a_{k}$ yields a non-penetrating video. Sweeping a collision disc ( $r{=}0.25$ m) over a discrete candidate set $\mathcal{C}$ of 34 motions (the raw input $c_{0}{=}u_{k}$ , a stop, shortened steps, and turns up to $90^{\circ}$ ) with margin $m{=}0.05$ m reduces Eq. 1 to

[Tableau] $$a_{k}^{\star}=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{c_{i}\in\mathcal{C}}\ \epsilon_{S}(c_{i},u_{k})^{2}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad g_{S}(c_{i})\geq m,$$ (2)

with $g_{S}(c_{i})\triangleq\min_{\rho\in[0,1]}g_{S}\big(\gamma(\rho;\,c_{i})\big)$ , the margin tightening ${\geq}\,0$ to ${\geq}\,m$ ( $u_{k}$ is safe if admissible and unsafe otherwise) and $x_{k}^{\star}\sim p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x_{<k},a_{k}^{\star})$ drawn by the generator’s sampler. Since a video generator stores no explicit geometry $S$ , neither $g_{S}(c_{i})$ nor $\epsilon_{S}(c_{i},u_{k})$ can be evaluated at test time: NoPeWorld learns both terms of Eq. 2 , the constraint and the intent, from the DiT’s hidden states, before any frame of the chunk is drawn.


#### 3.2 Constraint-aware training

Prior to synthesizing chunk $k$ , intermediate hidden states $H$ of a frozen video DiT $f_{\theta}$ already encode 3D scene structure and impending contact, whereas identical probes on VAE latents or decoded pixels fall well short ( Secs. 5.1 and 6 ). Building on this property, NoPeWorld ( No n- Pe netrating World ) trains a compact prediction head (weights $\phi$ , under 2M parameters) on mid-to-deep DiT blocks while keeping every weight of $\theta$ frozen ( Tab. 1 ; Fig. 2 , Training, left). A token projector $\psi_{H}$ compresses all tapped tokens to width 128 without spatial pooling; candidate $c_{i}\in\mathcal{C}$ (and raw command $u_{k}$ when $H$ precedes $u_{k}$ ) is encoded by the generator’s own frozen control encoder $E$ and projected onto the same token grid via $\psi_{c}$ . A learned query $q$ then attends over the action-conditioned state tokens $\tilde{H}_{i}$ via two cross-attention blocks:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle(\ell_{i},\hat{g}_{i},\hat{A}_{i})$ $\displaystyle=\mathrm{CrossAttn}_{\phi}\big(q,\ \tilde{H}_{i}\big),$ (3) $\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{i}$ $\displaystyle=\psi_{H}(H)+\psi_{c}\big(E(c_{i}),E(u_{k})\big),$

predicting a safety logit $\ell_{i}$ , swept clearance $\hat{g}_{i}$ , and intent score $\hat{A}_{i}$ per candidate (details in Sec. S2.2 ).

To supervise the head on the unobserved terms of Eq. 2 , we replay 729 Unreal Engine [ 12 ] videos across 44 training scenes from UnrealZoo [ 55 ] (disjoint from all benchmark scenes) through the frozen DiT via teacher forcing ( Fig. 2 , Training, left). On the simulator’s true occupancy map ( Fig. 2 , Training, right), sweeping the collision disc gives the clipped clearance $g_{i}=\mathrm{clip}\big(g_{S}(c_{i}),-0.25,1\big)$ m, while comparing $c_{i}$ against the simulator’s collide-and-slide displacement $v_{S}(u_{k})$ gives the intent error $\epsilon_{i}=\epsilon_{S}(c_{i},u_{k})$ of Eq. 2 . Because the hard step $\mathbb{I}[g_{S}(c_{i})\geq m]$ is discontinuous, we form smooth safety and intent targets ( $s{=}0.5$ ):

[Tableau] $$P_{i}=\Phi\Big(\frac{g_{i}-m}{0.03\,\text{m}}\Big),\quad A_{i}=e^{-\epsilon_{i}^{2}/(2s^{2})},\quad R_{i}=P_{i}A_{i},$$ (4)

with standard normal CDF $\Phi$ . Here $P_{i}$ softens the constraint of Eq. 2 , $g_{S}(c_{i})\geq m$ , $A_{i}$ maps its intent penalty $\epsilon_{i}^{2}$ monotonically into $(0,1]$ , and $R_{i}$ ranks candidates that are both safe and intent-aligned. We train $\phi$ via multi-task supervision:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\phi)$ $\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{i}\omega_{i}\Big[\mathrm{BCE}\big(\sigma(\ell_{i}),P_{i}\big)+\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{Huber}(\hat{g}_{i},g_{i})$ $\displaystyle\qquad+\mathrm{BCE}(\hat{A}_{i},A_{i})\Big]+\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi(R)\,\|\,\pi(\hat{R})\big),$ (5)

so the head learns the constraint ( $P_{i}$ , with dense Huber regression on $g_{i}$ where $P_{i}$ saturates) and the objective ( $A_{i}$ ) of Eq. 2 , plus a listwise ranking over $\mathcal{C}$ with logistic $\sigma$ , $\hat{R}_{i}=\sigma(\ell_{i})\hat{A}_{i}$ and $\pi(R)_{i}\propto R_{i}^{10}$ . Weights $\omega_{i}$ sum to one, and chunk indices are reweighted to equalize unsafe rates so predictions rely on geometry, not time. A motion $c$ has regret $\max_{i}R_{i}-R_{c}$ .


#### 3.3 Two-mode closed-loop inference

At test time, plugging the head into the rollout solves Eq. 2 before any frame of chunk $k$ is decoded ( Fig. 2 , Inference). Given calibrated safety probabilities $\hat{P}_{i}=\sigma(\ell_{i}/T)$ , candidates above threshold $\tau$ form the feasible set $\mathcal{S}_{k}=\{i:\hat{P}_{i}\geq\tau\}$ , the learned constraint of Eq. 2 , and $u_{k}$ is projected onto the closest admissible motion:

[Tableau] $$a_{k}^{\star}=\begin{cases}u_{k},&\text{if }0\in\mathcal{S}_{k},\ \max\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}\hat{A}_{i}\leq\hat{A}_{0}+\delta,\\[4.0pt] \argmax\limits_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}\hat{A}_{i},&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}$$ (6)

defaulting to a stop when $\mathcal{S}_{k}=\emptyset$ , with $(T,\tau,\delta)$ fixed on validation scenes ( Sec. 4.3 ). In words, a safe input runs as is ( kept , $a_{k}^{\star}{=}u_{k}$ ), and an unsafe one is replaced by the safe motion closest to the intent ( changed , $a_{k}^{\star}{\neq}u_{k}$ ). NoPeWorld runs this loop in two modes that differ in where $H$ is tapped:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle H_{k-1}^{\text{com}}$ $\displaystyle=f_{\theta}(\hat{z}_{k-1},\,0\mid a_{k-1}^{\star},M_{k-2})$ $\displaystyle\text{(prediction)},$ (7) $\displaystyle H_{k}^{\text{step}}$ $\displaystyle=f_{\theta}(z_{k}^{(0)},\,t_{0}\mid u_{k},M_{k-1})$ $\displaystyle\text{(collision)}.$

After chunk $k{-}1$ is denoised into clean latent $\hat{z}_{k-1}$ , the generator runs a noise-free commit pass to write attention memory $M_{k-1}$ . Pred reads this cached state $H_{k-1}^{\text{com}}$ with the incoming $u_{k}$ , between chunks $k{-}1$ and $k$ . Choosing $a_{k}^{\star}$ before chunk $k$ is denoised, Pred reruns no step and adds no DiT pass.

Coll instead enters chunk $k$ and taps the first denoising pass $H_{k}^{\text{step}}$ (step 0, initial noise $z_{k}^{(0)}$ at level $t_{0}$ ), where $u_{k}$ already interacts with memory $M_{k-1}$ , so the head needs no separate $E(u_{k})$ . If $u_{k}$ is kept, denoising proceeds directly from step 1 with no rerun. If $u_{k}$ is changed to $a_{k}^{\star}$ , Coll reruns only step 0 once with $a_{k}^{\star}$ ( $f_{\theta}(z_{k}^{(0)},t_{0}\mid a_{k}^{\star},M_{k-1})$ ) and completes the remaining steps.

### 4 Experiments


#### 4.1 NoPeBench

NoPeBench ( Fig. 3 ) tests whether a world model enforces non-penetration without overriding safe user intent. Existing interactive benchmarks largely steer around obstacles: in WBench [ 49 ] , scripted trajectories drive into a visible obstacle in only 13 of 158 navigation cases by our count, recording clipping passively rather than testing contact. In NoPeBench every test command drives into a solid obstacle, and 50 unobstructed open-path routes check against unnecessary intervention, so both clipping and needless halting are penalized. All scenes, assets, and images are disjoint from training and validation ( Sec. S1.1 ).

To separate perception from rollout drift and domain shift, NoPeBench has three progressive tasks. \tasktag Task 1 isolates single-step decisions from generation drift: it replays ground-truth histories by teacher forcing on 100 collision routes in 32 held-out scenes from UnrealZoo [ 55 ] and records the action chosen at each chunk. \tasktag Task 2 runs 5 s of obstacle-bound commands in closed loop on the same routes, from a pre-contact hand-off on true frames. \tasktag Task 3 starts from a single open-domain image without history (131 obstacle-bound images: 81 from WBench with static obstacles and 50 with moving ones, in first and third person).

Task 1 asks whether a method changes unsafe inputs and keeps safe ones, on Unreal’s occupancy map: Missed (unsafe $u_{k}$ kept, $a_{k}^{\star}{=}u_{k}$ ), False (safe $u_{k}$ changed), and the decision score $100\,(1-\text{regret})$ ( Sec. 3.2 ), 100 at the reward-optimal motion.

Task 2 scores non-penetration and intent in closed loop. VGGT- $\Omega$ [ 44 ] reconstructs camera poses and depth, aligns the trajectory to Unreal’s occupancy map (with SAM 2 tracking obstacle persistence), and flags a violation when penetration exceeds pose error ( Sec. S1.4 ); it is right on 366 of 391 Unreal renders of known outcome and agrees with DA3 [ 21 ] on 3,350 generated videos ( $\kappa{=}0.89$ ). Each rollout gets the Safe-Intent Score ,

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{SIS}=\mathbf{1}[\text{safe}\wedge\text{with the input}]\cdot\exp\!\Big(-\frac{\mu^{2}+\nu^{2}}{2s^{2}}\Big),$$ (8)

alongside the violation rate and Slide , the share of the engine’s wall slide a video reproduces. Mirroring $R{=}PA$ ( $s{=}0.5$ , Eq. 4 ), SIS is zero unless the rollout is safe ( Sec. 3.1 ) and with the input (not stalling or opposing the command), while $\mu$ and $\nu$ penalize early stops and lost slide.

Without 3D ground truth, Task 3 relies on VLM judges checked against people and geometry. Of four VLMs judging each video under one fixed prompt, Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra agree most closely with the human reference labels and the Task 2 evaluator ( Tab. 2 ); lacking a reference slide, Task 3 reports their mean violation rate .


#### 4.3 Experimental setup

One head architecture serves six frozen open-source generators with their released weights and samplers ( Tab. 1 ), spanning four backbones, five driven by camera poses and one by keystrokes (WorldPlay2), by reusing each model’s native control encoder; memoryless Evoke-Turbo supports Coll only. Readout blocks and $(T,\tau,\delta)$ are fixed on validation scenes ( Sec. S2.5 ).

We compare four methods under identical histories, captions, and seeds: open-loop generation ( Raw model ); a privileged Text prompt , given the ground-truth obstacle name, side, and metric distance with instructions to stop or slide ( Sec. S2.6 ); a halt-only External depth guard, which decodes each chunk and halts via DA3 [ 21 ] whenever $u_{k}$ is swept unsafe; and NoPeWorld (Coll and Pred inside the frozen DiT, Sec. 3.3 ).


#### 4.4 Main results

On drift-free ground-truth histories, NoPeWorld intercepts unsafe commands and keeps most safe ones on all six generators ( Tab. 3 ). Both modes cut missed collisions from 100% to 0.6–11.5% and raise the decision score by 17–33 points; Pred nearly matches Coll, though it reads the DiT before $u_{k}$ enters. The better mode alters 4–15% of safe inputs, 31.1% on Hy-Nomas. Task 2 tests whether these decisions hold in closed loop.

In autoregressive rollouts, these decisions give safer videos that keep the intent: NoPeWorld has the best SIS and Overall on all five pose-driven generators (on HY-WorldPlay in prediction mode; collision mode ties External depth on SIS), with up to 3.1 $\times$ the SIS of External depth ( Tab. 4 ; Figs. 1 and 4 ). Their raw rollouts violate on 77–95% of routes with near-zero SIS, and Text prompt brings almost no gain. External depth has the fewest violations on four of them, but only by halting: stuck at a wall it cannot slide along, it collapses Slide and restricts the user’s motion. Projecting unsafe $u_{k}$ onto admissible slides and stops ( Eq. 6 ), NoPeWorld cuts violations (from 74% to 16% averaged over all six in collision mode) while recovering part of the wall slide. WorldPlay2, the only keystroke-driven model, moves on its own: it natively retreats rather than penetrates, and a text prompt can steer it, unlike pose-driven models. NoPeWorld removes nearly all its remaining violations (11% to 1% in collision mode) and reduces retreats, but its stops take part of the native slide, leaving its SIS below the raw model’s, the prompt’s and External depth ’s. On the 50 open-path control routes, both modes neither stop early nor retreat in 92–100% of the videos ( Sec. S3.2.1 ), so NoPeWorld rarely halts where nothing blocks the way.

Is this ability tied to the training geometry? Though trained solely on teacher-forced first-person walks in static UnrealZoo scenes, NoPeWorld transfers to open-domain images, single-frame starts, third-person views and moving obstacles ( Tabs. 4 and 5 ): both modes cut static and dynamic violations below the raw model, and to or below the text prompt, on every generator (static, LingBot-World 2.0: 67% to 19%). Coll matches or outperforms External depth in 10 of the 12 generator-setting pairs, including all six with moving obstacles (Echo-WM: 8% vs. 11%); its two static losses are by 1–2 points. This suggests frozen DiTs encode transferable contact cues.


### 5 Analysis

To see why reading the DiT works, we ask whether its states hold the coming contact before pixels exist ( Sec. 5.1 ), which blocks carry it ( Sec. 5.2 ), and at what cost ( Sec. 5.3 ).


#### 5.1 The DiT knows before it draws

A frozen DiT holds the coming contact before its frames exist. We replay teacher-forced Unreal videos of 8 held-out scenes on LingBot-World 2.0, with labels from the true geometry, and sort decision states by chunks until the input becomes unsafe. A head separates them from safe states through $\hat{P}_{i}$ strongly for the chunk about to be generated and, one chunk earlier, before those frames are denoised, still far above a lookup that sees only the time; two chunks ahead much of the margin is lost ( Sec. S4.2 ). Across all six generators, the head first intervenes later on routes with a later time to contact ( Sec. S3.3 ).

Only DiT states support low-regret choices (bottom of Fig. 6 ): on them the head roughly halves the regret of always keeping the input, whereas on VAE latents or pixels it is no better, or hardly better, and on pixels it tells unsafe inputs apart no better than the chunk index does (AUC, Tab. S11 ). The DiT has already done the work, and a head of under two million parameters can pick it up. Yet the same model, raw, walks through the obstacle on most routes ( Tab. 4 ): it knows, and does not act on what it knows, likely because a blocked command is out of distribution ( Sec. 1 ); NoPeWorld makes it act by turning this knowledge into the choice of $a_{k}^{\star}$ ( Eq. 6 ).

### 6 Conclusion

Video world models inhabit a world where walls are optional. We formulated non-penetration for generators without geometry, requiring that the depicted trajectory never penetrate an obstacle and that no obstacle be erased, and introduced NoPeBench to evaluate it on ground-truth 3D geometry and on open-domain scenes. Rather than rely on external perception, NoPeWorld resolves collisions from within: because a frozen DiT already encodes impending contact before it synthesizes pixels, a small head suffices for collision detection and response, and transfers without retraining to new domains, viewpoints, and moving obstacles. Across six frozen generators, it cuts violations from 74% to 16%. Solid boundaries are only the first step; the frontier ahead is contact that pushes, carries, and shatters objects.

#### S1.1 Data sources and splits

Every scene of Tasks 1 and 2 and of the heads’ training comes from UnrealZoo [ 55 ] , split by scene: the heads train on 729 videos of 44 scenes and are selected on held-out validation scenes ( Sec. The 100 collision routes and 50 unobstructed control routes were fixed purely from scene geometry prior to model evaluation, and training and tuning used no Task 3 images.


#### S1.2 How cases are built

Figure S1 shows one case from history to verdict. Cases are rendered in Unreal Engine [ 12 ] , in photo-realistic scenes of UnrealZoo [ 55 ] driven through UnrealCV [ 31 ] , strictly disjoint from all training and validation scenes of the heads. A route is built backwards from a chosen spot on a wall or an object and an incidence-angle bin, so that the obstacle, the approach angle and the time to contact are controlled.

Each route starts with a ground-truth history clip up to a pre-contact hand-off (about 1 s before obstacle contact on 49 routes and 0.5–3 s on the other 51), after which the model receives 5 s of a persistent command: W, W+A or W+D, at 1.2 m/s for walking, 4 m/s for drones and 5 m/s for cars. Every model starts from true frames up to the hand-off, in whole chunks of its own grid.

Tasks 1 and 2 share 100 routes in 32 held-out scenes (66 walls, 34 objects), chosen so that no two routes repeat the same scene, obstacle, input type and contact time; they run into 84 different obstacles, on foot and at drone and car speed. Glass, thin poles and moving bodies are excluded.


#### S1.3 The three tasks

Task 1 isolates the decision from the generator: the generator replays each route’s real continuation by teacher forcing on its own chunk grid, and at every chunk we record the motion $a_{k}^{\star}$ the method would send, with every candidate labelled on the occupancy map as in training ( Eq. 4 ). Task 1 has the two error rates of any detector: Missed , the share of unsafe inputs ( $P_{0}<0.5$ ) that are kept, and False , the share of safe inputs that are changed. Candidate $c_{i}$ has reward $R_{i}=P_{i}A_{i}$ (the training target), and a motion $c$ has regret $\max_{i}R_{i}-R_{c}$ . A label looks one chunk ahead, so it depends on the chunk length; we therefore compare Task 1 scores within each model.

On the same routes the model generates with the method in the loop, and a geometric evaluator places the camera path the video depicts on Unreal’s occupancy map and gives it one verdict of Sec. 3.1 ( Sec. S1.4 ); Unreal’s own execution of the input, a stop or a slide, is the reference. The main paper’s Safe-Intent Score (its Eq. 8 ) is averaged over the 100 routes. A video is safe if it has no violation, and with the input if the camera does not go back, go the wrong way, turn away or stand still. $\mu$ is how many chunks of travel the camera stops short of the engine’s contact, at its closest approach to the obstacle, with 0.3 m allowed for position error; a chunk of travel is taken at the generator’s own speed and chunk length. $\nu$ is the share of the engine’s slide along the wall that the video misses, on the 65 routes where the engine slides at least 0.5 m, and zero elsewhere. With $s{=}0.5$ , the width of the intent label ( Eq. 4 ), each unit of shortfall, one chunk short or no slide where the engine slides, multiplies the score by $e^{-2}$ .

Task 3 leaves the training domain: the model generates video from a single open-domain image, not rendered in Unreal, in first or third person, with an input of ours that drives into a visible obstacle. The static group takes 53 first-person and 28 third-person WBench [ 49 ] cases whose obstacle can be reached within the clip; the dynamic group has 50 first frames in WBench’s style, 30 first-person and 20 third-person, with moving people, crowds, vehicles, animals and objects. On the WBench cases the input is ours, because WBench’s own navigation turns rarely reach an obstacle: an 8 s input that drives into the obstacle and always moves forward. There is no 3D ground truth here, so four VLM judges apply the rule of Task 2 to what can be seen, and Task 3 reports the share of videos with a violation. A Task 3 video is safe and with the input when the judge finds neither a violation nor going back; the main table’s Overall score is the mean of SIS and this share of Task 3 videos.

#### S1.4 The evaluator

Task 2 is judged by geometry, conservatively: a violation is called only when it exceeds what the measurement could get wrong. The generated frames are put after the true history. VGGT- $\Omega$ [ 44 ] reconstructs depth and camera poses of the last 2 s of history at 4 fps and of the 5 s after the hand-off at 8 fps (49 frames) in one multi-view pass; one scale, the true length of the sampled history path over its estimated length, is applied to every position and depth. The estimated path is placed through the true hand-off pose on the case region’s occupancy map at camera height (5 cm cells), whose free space is the set of cells connected to the hand-off camera. SAM 2 [ 32 ] tracks the obstacle from the true hand-off mask, only to detect a vanishing obstacle. Each sample also gives the depth $D_{t}$ in the image window around the commanded direction (20th percentile, middle third of the width). Table S1 gives the rule in its order with the origin of every threshold; “at the obstacle” means within 0.6 m of the obstacle’s points, or $D_{t}\leq 0.6$ m.

The Succ. column in Tab. S4 measures safe arrival. It counts routes that reach the obstacle without a violation or retreat ( stopped , slid , or stopped, not slid ). Thus a route can count toward Succ. even when its slide falls short of the reference. An early stop also counts if the closest approach is within one chunk of travel, computed from the generator’s chunk duration and the commanded speed.


#### S1.5 How far the evaluators can be trusted

On Unreal renders whose poses are known, the evaluator is right on 366 of 391 cases (199 of 219 reference executions that collide and slide, 149 of 152 walk-throughs, and 18 of 20 renders of other behaviours). Over 3,350 generated Task 2 videos, VGGT- $\Omega$ and an independent reconstruction, DA3 [ 21 ] , agree on the violation verdict with $\kappa=0.89$ .

Four VLMs judge every Task 3 video under one fixed prompt, tuned on Task 2 development videos only: two closed models, Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra, and two open ones, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and Qwen3.8-27B, for open-weight reference. Of the 280 sampled videos, 269 have a majority violation or no-violation label (44 violations); the other 11 lack two matching binary labels because annotators could mark a video as unclear or corrupted. ### Appendix S2 Method


#### S2.1 The six generators

Across all six generators, the head design is shared. The tapped blocks, token projection ( $D\to 128$ ) and candidate encoder follow each generator’s architecture ( Tab. S2 ).

A candidate is one of the 34 ground displacements of Sec. S2.3 , expressed over the model’s own chunk duration at the commanded speed and passed through the model’s own control path, so the head scores exactly the input the generator would receive. Evoke has no prediction head because it keeps no memory forward between chunks.


#### S2.2 The head

The prediction head reads the commit-pass tokens $H_{k-1}^{\text{com}}$ of Eq. 7 , the collision head the step-0 tokens $H_{k}^{\text{step}}$ , both through Eq. 3 . The token projector $\psi_{H}$ passes every token through its own learned linear map $W_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{128\times D}$ and a LayerNorm. Nothing is pooled and no fixed projection is used: on LingBot the prediction head attends over all $1\cdot 3\cdot 29\cdot 52=4{,}524$ tokens of block 30 and the collision head over 13,572 tokens of blocks 30, 38 and 39. The frozen control encoder $E$ gives one $D$ -dimensional vector per token, which $\psi_{c}$ maps through $W_{c}$ and adds to the state token at the same (frame, row, column). The learned query $q$ reads the resulting $\tilde{H}_{i}$ through two cross-attention blocks (4 heads, width 128) and outputs the safety logit $\ell_{i}$ , the swept clearance $\hat{g}_{i}$ and the intent score $\hat{A}_{i}$ . Written out, Eq. 3 is

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\psi_{H}(H)$ $\displaystyle=\mathrm{LN}(W_{s}H)+e_{\text{block}}+e_{\text{frame}}+e_{\text{row}}+e_{\text{col}},$ $\displaystyle\psi_{c}(\cdot)$ $\displaystyle=\mathrm{LN}(W_{c}E(c_{i}))+\mathrm{mix}\big(\mathrm{LN}(W_{c}E(u_{k}))\big)+e_{\text{time}},$ $\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{i}$ $\displaystyle=\psi_{H}(H)+\psi_{c}\big(E(c_{i}),E(u_{k})\big),$

with $W_{s},W_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{128\times D}$ applied per token. The mix term adds the input $u_{k}$ , which arrived after $H_{k-1}^{\text{com}}$ was computed; the collision head reads $H_{k}^{\text{step}}$ , which already holds it, without that term.

Equation 6 is applied with $\hat{P}_{i}=\sigma(\ell_{i}/T)$ over the feasible set $\mathcal{S}_{k}=\{i:\hat{P}_{i}\geq\tau\}$ , and $a_{k}^{\star}$ is a stop if $\mathcal{S}_{k}$ is empty. Table S2 gives every model’s $(T,\tau,\delta)$ .


#### S2.3 Candidates

Each decision scores 34 ground displacements $c_{i}\in\mathcal{C}$ : the input $c_{0}=u_{k}$ , a stop, the input shortened to 75, 50, 25 and 12.5%, half-length turns at 30° and 60° to each side, and 12 per side on an intent ellipse. The ellipse points sit at deviations $\alpha$ of 8.8° to 88° from the input with length $\rho(\alpha)=\min\big(1,\cos\alpha/(\cos^{2}\alpha+w\sin^{2}\alpha)\big)$ , $w=0.5$ : the length in direction $\alpha$ that minimises the intent error when its lateral part is weighted by $w$ , yielding shorter steps for larger turns.


#### S2.4 Labels

Labels come from the occupancy map of the Unreal level ( Eq. 4 ): a disc of radius 0.25 m sweeps along each candidate $c_{i}$ at camera height and gives the clearance $g_{i}$ , clipped to $[-0.25,1]$ m, and the soft safety label $P_{i}$ with margin $m=0.05$ m and scale 0.03 m. The intent score $A_{i}$ compares the candidate’s displacement $d_{i}$ with the collide-and-slide displacement $v_{S}(u_{k})$ of the input through the error $\epsilon_{i}^{2}=\big(((d_{i}-v_{S})\cdot\hat{t})^{2}+w\,((d_{i}-v_{S})\cdot\hat{t}_{\perp})^{2}\big)/|u_{k}|^{2}$ , with $\hat{t}$ the slide direction; at a head-on wall the stop has $A_{i}=1$ .


#### S2.5 Training and validation

Each head trains online while the frozen generator replays the training videos with their true latents and poses, updating on the tapped tokens of each decision state as they are computed. The loss is Eq. 5 , where the Huber term switches from quadratic to linear at 0.05 m. All heads across the six generators are trained for 1,000 optimizer steps on the same 729 UnrealZoo videos from 44 scenes, rendered at each model’s native frame rate ( Tab. S2 ).

Readout blocks and operating points $(T,\tau,\delta)$ are selected on held-out validation videos from scenes disjoint from both the training and benchmark splits. For each head, temperature $T$ is fitted by negative log-likelihood on the soft safety labels, and $(\tau,\delta)$ are chosen on a grid to minimize validation regret under a bounded missed-intervention rate. All deployed block indices and operating points are listed in Tab. S2 , and all trained head weights (both collision and prediction modes) are publicly released.

#### S2.6 Baselines: specification

One sentence is appended to the VLM scene caption each raw run uses:

There is $\langle$ obstacle $\rangle$ $\langle$ side $\rangle$ , about $\langle d\rangle$ m away. The camera must not go through the $\langle$ obstacle $\rangle$ : on reaching it, the camera must stop or slide along it.

The three fields use ground truth that the head never receives, so the prompt carries more information about the obstacle than our method does. On Task 2, $\langle$ obstacle $\rangle$ denotes the target obstacle in the hand-off frame and $\langle d\rangle$ is the median ground-truth depth within its mask, rounded to 0.5 m. On Task 3, where metric depth is unavailable, $\langle$ obstacle $\rangle$ , $\langle$ side $\rangle$ , and approximate distance $\langle d\rangle$ are annotated from the starting image. No negative prompt is used, since the six samplers run without classifier-free guidance. The open-path routes have no obstacle for the prompt to name, so the text prompt does not apply there.

Before each decision, DA3 [ 21 ] reconstructs the true history together with every frame decoded since the hand-off, with scale fitted on the true camera path. The input’s swept clearance on this map gives a safety probability by Eq. 4 ; the guard keeps the input if it meets a safety threshold selected on the validation set and stops otherwise. On Task 3, with no true history, scale and level come from the floor at an assumed eye height of 1.6 m.

#### S4.4 Where the head looks

We inspect cross-attention inside the LingBot-World 2.0 head ( Fig. S7 ) on regenerated Task 3 cases (at the first intervention chunk) and on teacher-forced Unreal routes with per-token depth and obstacle masks. Because each candidate motion conditions the token grid before query readout ( Eq. 3 ), attention tracks the queried trajectory: wall-bound motions attend to the blocking surface, forward motions attend to the path ahead, and rightward or leftward turns attend to the corresponding side of the frame.
