## Reading, Writing, Sharing, and Interacting with the World through Video

Video foundation models have advanced rapidly in understanding and generating visual sequences, while video world models seek to capture the underlying dynamics of the world they depict. Despite their different objectives, both concern the relationship between video observations and an evolving world. This survey adopts world modeling as a unifying lens for examining these developments. Viewing an observed video as a partial, lossy, and temporally ordered projection of a latent temporal world, we organize the literature around four complementary operations: reading infers world properties from observed video; writing synthesizes video from specified or learned world conditions; sharing integrates understanding and generation through common modeling mechanisms; and interacting predicts and updates world states under actions and evolving observations. This perspective distinguishes temporal coherence in observed or generated sequences from consistency in the underlying world state. Across tasks, representations, models, datasets, and benchmarks, we trace a shift in emphasis from clip-level recognition and perceptual fidelity toward persistent state, controllable evolution, physical and causal validity, and evaluation grounded in interaction and downstream decisions, and we distill the open challenges that follow.

### 2 Introduction

Video occupies a distinctive position among the modalities available to machine intelligence. Unlike static images that capture individual moments, text that conveys selectively described information, or audio that primarily records acoustic dynamics, video directly captures how visible entities, their relations, and actions evolve across space and time, often together with synchronized audio, speech, and text. This rich spatiotemporal information makes video essential to a wide range of applications, including communication, entertainment, autonomous driving, robotics, healthcare, and education. Consequently, video has long been a central subject of computer vision and multimedia research, progressing from spatiotemporal architectures for action recognition Kay et al., 2017 ; Carreira & Zisserman, 2017 ; Feichtenhofer et al., 2019 to self-supervised pretraining for transferable video representations Tong et al., 2022 ; Wang et al., 2024d . More recently, advances in large language models (LLMs) and large-scale generative modeling have driven a shift from task-specific video models toward general-purpose video foundation models, expanding their capabilities along three complementary directions. For video understanding , Video-LLMs such as Video-LLaVA Lin et al., 2024 enable language-based interaction with visual sequences, while Qwen3-VL Bai et al., 2025 extends multimodal context to 256K tokens to accommodate longer video inputs. For video generation , models such as Sora Brooks et al., 2024 , Wan Wan et al., 2025 , HunyuanVideo Kong et al., 2024 , Movie Gen Polyak et al., 2024 , and Seedance 2.0 Team Seedance et al., 2026 demonstrate increasingly capable synthesis under diverse conditions, with Sora and Loong Wang et al., 2024f further exploring minute-scale generation. Toward the unification of understanding and generation , emerging models Wang et al., 2024b ; Xie et al., 2025a ; Wei et al., 2026 seek to integrate these capabilities through shared token representations, latent spaces, or modeling interfaces, bringing video perception and synthesis into a common foundation-model framework.

Beyond video understanding and generation, a broader research direction is emerging around modeling how the physical world evolves , rather than merely interpreting or synthesizing its visual observations. Although language models acquire extensive knowledge through text, their representations are only indirectly grounded in physical experience, motivating the pursuit of world models that can predict how an environment changes over time and support reasoning and planning LeCun, 2022 ; Chen et al., 2026e . This idea has roots in model-based reinforcement learning, where agents learn predictive dynamics to simulate possible futures and evaluate candidate actions before acting Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018 ; Hafner et al., 2025 . Video provides a natural and scalable foundation for extending this paradigm beyond task-specific environments: by capturing continuous changes in objects, relations, and actions, large-scale video data offers rich supervision for learning world dynamics without requiring manually annotated labels, although actions and underlying physical states often remain unobserved. Building on this opportunity, recent research has explored several complementary approaches to video-based world modeling. Generative models such as Sora Brooks et al., 2024 have motivated the prospect of learning world dynamics through video synthesis, while Genie Bruce et al., 2024 demonstrates how interactive environments can be learned from unlabeled videos. Action- and condition-aware models such as GAIA-1 Hu et al., 2023 and Cosmos NVIDIA et al., 2025 extend predictive generation toward driving and physical AI, whereas V-JEPA 2 Assran et al., 2025 and Dreamer 4 Hafner et al., 2025a explore predictive representations and imagined trajectories for planning and control. Together with progress in interactive simulation Yang et al., 2024 , egocentric and robotic learning Grauman et al., 2022 ; Wu et al., 2024a , and long-horizon prediction Xiao et al., 2025a , these developments are expanding the role of video from a medium for perception and generation toward a foundation for predicting and interacting with dynamic environments. Figure 1 traces milestones of these lines of work.

These two lines of progress are increasingly converging around a common challenge: connecting video observations with the dynamics of the world they depict . Video world models must infer underlying states from observed sequences before predicting how those states evolve, and often translate predicted states back into visual observations for simulation, evaluation, or planning. Conversely, video generators are increasingly incorporating action- and state-dependent controls, while unified video models explicitly couple visual understanding with synthesis. Despite their different objectives, these approaches therefore share a fundamental question: what relationship does a model learn between an observed video and the evolving world that gives rise to it?

To examine this question systematically, we adopt world modeling as a unifying perspective on video foundation models. Specifically, we view an observed video $V$ as a partial, lossy, and temporally ordered observation of an underlying latent temporal world $W$ . This distinction provides a common basis for organizing four complementary research paradigms: reading , which infers world properties from observed video; writing , which synthesizes video observations from specified or learned world conditions; sharing , which integrates both directions within a unified modeling framework; and interacting , which updates or predicts world states in response to actions and evolving observations. These paradigms characterize different ways in which models connect video observations to the underlying world, rather than merely categorizing models by their input and output modalities. This perspective enables a systematic comparison of their objectives, capabilities, and evaluation criteria while preserving the distinctions between visual plausibility, temporal coherence, and consistency of the modeled world. Section 3 formalizes this framework and clarifies how these properties differ across the four paradigms.

Existing relevant surveys have examined related developments from different perspectives, including general world models organized around predictive state representations across modalities Ding et al., 2026 , interactive video world models centered on online prediction and control Liu et al., 2026 ; Luo et al., 2026a , 3D and 4D world modeling grounded in explicit geometric representations Kong et al., 2025 , multimodal language models emphasizing the integration of visual encoders and language reasoning Yin et al., 2024 , long-form video generation focusing on temporal extension and consistency Li et al., 2024 , and audio-visual foundation models spanning complementary modalities Qin et al., 2026a . While these surveys provide valuable insights into individual research directions, they do not collectively establish a unified account of how video models connect observations to evolving world states across understanding, generation, unification, and interaction. Our survey addresses this gap by taking the relation between video observations and the underlying dynamic world as its central organizing principle , not any single architecture, task, or modality.

Under this perspective, we systematically examine the four paradigms of reading, writing, sharing, and interacting through a common analytical framework. For each paradigm, we review the tasks, datasets, and evaluation protocols that define its objectives; the representations and model architectures that support its capabilities; the reasoning and control mechanisms that extend its functionality; and the applications that demonstrate its practical utility. Beyond reviewing individual paradigms, we identify several cross-cutting questions: how models represent and preserve world states beyond immediate observations, how actions and external conditions influence state evolution, and how predictive capabilities translate into effective reasoning and decision-making. These questions also motivate a closer examination of evaluation practices, particularly the distinction between conventional metrics of recognition accuracy or perceptual fidelity and emerging criteria concerning state persistence, controllability, physical and causal consistency, and downstream utility. Within the interacting paradigm, we further distinguish video world models along two complementary dimensions: prediction space , ranging from observation-space to latent-state prediction, and transition interface , ranging from passive prediction to action-conditioned rollout and closed-loop interaction. This taxonomy separates what a model predicts from how its predictions are conditioned, updated, and used.

Our review covers publicly available research that contributes relevant architectural, methodological, or evaluation evidence. We retain foundational studies where necessary to establish conceptual and technical developments, while interpreting recent advances in the context of their specific tasks, training data, and evaluation protocols. Throughout the survey, we distinguish between the capabilities supported by a model’s design, the supervision provided by its training data, and the empirical evidence demonstrated by its evaluation, avoiding assumptions that strong performance on individual video tasks necessarily implies consistent or generalizable world modeling.

Specifically, this survey makes three main contributions:

- A world-modeling lens. The four-operation framework of reading, writing, sharing, and interacting relates video understanding, video generation, unified video models, and video world models in one account, plus a two-axis classification of world models by prediction space and transition interface.

- A comprehensive scope with insights. Across tasks, representations, models, systems, datasets, and benchmarks, we compare how the four operations represent state, learn from video, accept control, preserve memory, and are evaluated. The synthesis distills recurring design patterns and failure modes into explicit insights, and identifies open directions, including long-horizon consistency, physical and causal grounding, measurable synergy between understanding and generation, and standardized evaluation of world properties.

- Open resources. A public reading list, taxonomy, codes, data, and resources are all categorized and available at project page .

### 3 The World-Modeling Lens

This section defines the world and its video (Section 3.1 ), two forms of consistency used later for evaluation (Section 3.2 ), and the four operations that organize Sections 5 – 8 (Section 3.3 ). Table 1 lists the notation.


#### 3.1 World, Video, and Represented State

We distinguish three objects: the evolving world, the video through which it is observed, and the representations that a model constructs from available information. The following definitions provide a conceptual abstraction:

Latent temporal worldworld The latent temporal world is a stochastic process $W=(w_{t})_{t\geq 1}$ over a state space $\mathcal{W}$ . Each state $w_{t}\in\mathcal{W}$ encompasses entities, their attributes and relations, and relevant physical and dynamic variables. Assuming that $w_{t}$ contains sufficient information for predicting subsequent evolution, its dynamics can be expressed as

[Tableau] $$w_{t+1}\sim\mathcal{T}(\,\cdot\mid w_{t},a_{t}),$$

where $a_{t}$ denotes an optional intervention or control input, with uncontrolled evolution recovered when no such input is supplied.

Observed videovideo An observed video $V=(x_{1},\dots,x_{T})$ is a temporally ordered sequence of visual observations generated by a sensing process, $x_{t}\sim\mathcal{O}(\,\cdot\mid w_{t},\pi_{t})$ , where $\pi_{t}$ is the camera configuration. We refer to an observed or generated video $V$ as a trace . This trace is partial : $x_{t}$ depends only on the part of $w_{t}$ visible under $\pi_{t}$ , which changes with camera motion, shot changes, or multiple cameras; lossy : sensing, resolution, compression, and editing remove information, so distinct world states may produce indistinguishable observations; and temporally ordered : frame order and duration carry evidence about motion and state transition, and thus about $\mathcal{T}$ .

Represented statestate A model with parameters $\theta$ may construct an internal representation $s_{t}\sim q_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x_{\leq t},a_{<t})$ , inferred from the observations and actions available up to time $t$ ; a deterministic encoder $s_{t}=f_{\theta}(x_{\leq t},a_{<t})$ is the special case of a point mass, and a transition model may instead sample $s_{t+1}$ from its own predictions (Definition ). In general, $s_{t}\neq w_{t}$ , and its information about the underlying world may be incomplete or inaccurate.

Remark. Definitions – describe a partially observable dynamical system, closely related to the environment model underlying a partially observable Markov decision process. Such formulations are widely used in model-based reinforcement learning Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018 ; Hafner et al., 2025 , where agents infer predictive states from observations and simulate possible futures under candidate actions. For general video foundation models, however, the represented state may be implicit or constructed from an entire sequence rather than updated online.


#### 3.2 Two Forms of Consistency

Because video observes the world only partially (Definition ), a trace can be consistent at two levels.

Temporal coherence and world consistencyconsistency Let $\hat{V}=(\hat{x}_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{T})$ be a trace that a model generates or predicts. $\hat{V}$ is temporally coherent if neighboring frames agree locally, $d(\hat{x}_{t},\hat{x}_{t+1})\leq\delta$ for all $t$ , where $d$ penalizes implausible local motion, appearance change, or flicker. $\hat{V}$ is world-consistent relative to specified dynamics and constraints, if its observations are compatible with at least one admissible world trajectory:

[Tableau] $$\exists\,w_{1:T}\in\mathcal{C},\ \pi_{1:T}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\hat{x}_{t}\in\operatorname{supp}\mathcal{O}(\cdot\mid w_{t},\pi_{t}),\quad\forall t,$$

where $\mathcal{C}$ contains the trajectories that satisfy the assumed dynamics, identity persistence, spatial relations, and relevant physical and causal constraints under the specified interventions. When the camera path is specified as a condition, $\pi_{1:T}$ is fixed to it instead of quantified.

Temporal coherence is a property of the trace; world consistency is a property of the world the trace implies. A recorded video is world-consistent by construction, because a real trajectory produced it, so the distinction matters for traces that a model writes or predicts. World-modeling evidence must therefore test world consistency, not only temporal coherence. As $\mathcal{O}$ and $\mathcal{C}$ are unobserved, Definition is an evaluation target, not a metric; benchmarks in Sections 6.1.2 and 8.4 probe it via revisits, occlusion, and interventions.


#### 3.3 The Four Operations

Four operationsoperations

- Reading $V\rightarrow W$ (video comprehension) . Reading infers selected properties of the world from observed video, $\hat{\phi}_{t}\sim r_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s_{t})$ , an estimate of $\phi(w_{t})$ , where $s_{t}$ is constructed from the available observations and $\phi$ ranges over semantic, spatial, temporal, physical, and causal properties. It is evaluated by accuracy against $\phi(w_{t})$ and by calibrated uncertainty for occluded or off-screen content.

- Writing $W\rightarrow V$ (video generation) . Writing samples a trace from a condition, $\hat{x}_{1:T}\sim p_{\theta}(x_{1:T}\mid c)$ , where $c$ (text, an image or video prefix, audio, camera motion, trajectories, or layouts) specifies the world only in part and the generator resolves the rest. It is evaluated by perceptual quality, adherence to $c$ , and world consistency of $\hat{V}$ (Definition ).

- Sharing $V\leftrightarrow W$ (unified comprehension and generation) . Sharing couples a reading map with parameters $\theta_{\mathrm{R}}$ and a writing map with parameters $\theta_{\mathrm{W}}$ through at least one shared component: parameters ( $\theta_{\mathrm{R}}\cap\theta_{\mathrm{W}}\neq\varnothing$ ), tokens or representations, an interface, or a training objective. It is evaluated at three levels: capability coverage, architectural sharing, and functional synergy, that is, measurable improvement of reading, writing, or both under matched resources.

- Interacting $W\circlearrowleft$ (video world modeling) . Interacting rolls out a transition model, $s_{t+1}\sim p_{\theta}(s_{t+1}\mid s_{\leq t},a_{t},c_{t})$ , with $\hat{x}_{t+1}=g_{\theta}(s_{t+1})$ when visible output is needed. The state $s_{t}$ may be frames, video tokens, or latent features, and the actions $a_{t}$ and conditions $c_{t}$ are fixed before an open-loop rollout or supplied online. It is evaluated by whether predicted changes follow these inputs and stay world-consistent (Definition ), beyond the quality of rendered frames.

Three labels describe the recorded content: its default form, its modalities, and its viewpoint. Regular short video , the standard setting for representation learning and clip-level understanding, consists of passive third-person clips of seconds to minutes, whose audio is usually ignored. Its datasets annotate action classes Kay et al., 2017 , actions defined by temporal change Goyal et al., 2017 , or captions Xu et al., 2016 , but do not test memory, online response, or control. Sounding video treats synchronized audio as evidence, with tasks that range from audio-event recognition and source localization to generating events whose sound and appearance must agree Gemmeke et al., 2017 ; Chen et al., 2020 ; Tian et al., 2018 . Its benchmarks must rule out visual or language shortcuts. Egocentric video is recorded from a wearer’s or agent’s viewpoint, so hands, manipulated objects, gaze, and body-coupled camera motion enter the stream Grauman et al., 2022 ; Damen et al., 2022 ; Grauman et al., 2025 ; Mangalam et al., 2023 . The task shifts from recognizing actions to tracking object state and intent across long activities with frequent occlusion.

Three further labels describe how the model accesses observations and whether it can change them. In streaming and online video , frames arrive incrementally, so a model must update its state and respond within bounded latency, without access to future frames. Typical tasks are online question answering and streaming captioning, which may require timestamped or proactive responses Niu et al., 2025 ; Yang et al., 2025f . The same recording can be processed offline in full or presented as a stream. Long-form video spans minutes to hours: the full recording is usually available, but not every frame can be processed at full resolution, and the evidence for a query is sparse and spread across distant segments Zhou et al., 2025b ; Wang et al., 2025c ; Fu et al., 2025 ; Wu et al., 2024 . A model must therefore decide which evidence to keep and link events that lie far apart in time. Interactive video lets a human, policy, or agent supply actions that change what is observed next Arai et al., 2024 ; Ye et al., 2026 ; Shang et al., 2026 . Unlike in a passive stream, the future then depends on the interaction history; interactive world models (Section 4.4 ) target this setting.

Domain-specific video cuts across all of these labels: driving Caesar et al., 2020 ; Sima et al., 2024 , surgery Twinanda et al., 2017 , sports Giancola et al., 2018 , and instructional video Miech et al., 2019 ; Tang et al., 2019 impose their own event structure, terminology, and costs of error. These domains test whether general video representations transfer to rare events and specialized outputs.


#### 4.2 Video Large Language Models

Connecting video to an LLM. A Video-LLM feeds video into a pretrained large language model (LLM), usually an autoregressive Transformer Vaswani et al., 2017 ; Touvron et al., 2023 trained by next-token prediction (Equation ( 2 )). The LLM learns to follow instructions through supervised instruction tuning Wei et al., 2022a , which visual instruction tuning extends to images Liu et al., 2023 , and is further aligned with human or model feedback Ouyang et al., 2022 and verifiable rewards Lambert et al., 2025 ; Guo et al., 2025 . Inside a video system, it understands and generates language, answers questions, and sometimes acts as a controller that decomposes tasks, selects tools, and reasons over several steps. In a common division of labor, a video encoder supplies perceptual features, the LLM interprets or organizes them, and a decoder, generator, or world model produces pixels or state transitions. To connect video, a video encoder $E_{v}$ maps frames or clips $x_{1:T}$ to visual features, and a connector $P$ converts these features into a language-compatible sequence:

[Tableau] $$z_{1:L}=P\!\left(E_{v}(x_{1:T})\right).$$ (1)

Connectors range from linear projectors Liu et al., 2023 to learned-query resamplers Jaegle et al., 2021 ; Li et al., 2023 and gated cross-attention Alayrac et al., 2022 ; Section 5.3 compares them and the video-specific training recipes.

Video tokens and context. In the Video-LLM literature, a video token usually denotes one of the continuous embeddings in $z_{1:L}$ passed to the LLM. Autoregressive video generators often use the same term for discrete codebook indices, a different representation (Section 6 ). Frame sampling, temporal pooling, and the connector set the number $L$ of embeddings kept from a video. A larger $L$ preserves finer spatial and temporal evidence but costs more context, memory, and attention; stronger compression is cheaper but can remove brief events, small objects, or long-range relations. Because the context window is fixed and also holds the instructions, earlier dialogue, and the output, long-video models must split the remaining budget between temporal coverage and visual detail.


#### 4.3 Generative Modeling Foundations

Conditional generation. Whereas the LLM mainly produces language, generative models produce the visual output of a video system. Writing samples a trace $\hat{x}_{1:T}\sim p_{\theta}(x_{1:T}\mid c)$ (Definition ). Video generators usually work in the latent space of a variational autoencoder (VAE) Kingma & Welling, 2014 , whose decoder may be trained with an adversarial loss Goodfellow et al., 2020 : an encoder maps the video to a latent $z$ , the generator models $p_{\theta}(z\mid c)$ , and the decoder maps a sampled latent back to frames. We write $z$ for whichever target is modeled, whether continuous latents, discrete video tokens, or, for pixel-space models, the frames themselves. In general, a condition may specify content, such as a prompt or a reference image, or control how the state evolves, such as actions, trajectories, or camera paths. The same modality can play different roles: an image may specify appearance, provide the initial observation, or serve as a spatial reference. Action-conditioned and interactive world models (Section 4.4 ) require the second kind.

Autoregressive and masked modeling. When the target is a sequence of ordered units $z_{1:N}$ , an autoregressive model factorizes the conditional distribution as

[Tableau] $$p_{\theta}(z_{1:N}\mid c)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{\theta}(z_{i}\mid z_{<i},c)$$ (2)

and generates the units in order, each sampled unit joining the context for later predictions. The units may be discrete codes or continuous latent blocks, ordered by time alone or by a space–time serialization, and Transformer decoders are the common implementation Vaswani et al., 2017 . Masked modeling instead hides a subset of units and predicts them in parallel from the visible context and the condition. For discrete targets, with sampled masked positions $M$ and visible units $z_{\bar{M}}$ , a common objective is the token cross-entropy

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mask}}=\mathbb{E}_{(z,c),M}\!\left[-\sum_{i\in M}\log p_{\theta}(z_{i}\mid z_{\bar{M}},c)\right].$$ (3)

MaskGIT Chang et al., 2022 turns these predictions into samples through iterative filling and refinement. Continuous pixel or feature targets instead use a reconstruction loss such as squared error He et al., 2022 .

Diffusion and flow matching. A diffusion model learns to reverse a forward process that gradually corrupts data with noise Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015 ; Ho et al., 2020 . Let $(z_{0},c)\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}$ be a clean training latent and its condition, and let $k\in\{1,\dots,K\}$ index the denoising step; the subscript $k$ is diffusion time, distinct from video time $t$ . In the noise-prediction form, $z_{0}$ is corrupted to $z_{k}=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{k}}\,z_{0}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{k}}\,\epsilon$ , where $\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})$ and $\bar{\alpha}_{k}$ decreases toward $0$ as $k$ increases, and the network learns to recover the noise:

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diff}}=\mathbb{E}_{(z_{0},c),\epsilon,k}\!\left[\big\lVert\epsilon_{\theta}(z_{k},k,c)-\epsilon\big\rVert^{2}\right].$$ (4)

Sampling starts from $z_{K}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})$ and applies denoising updates $z_{K}\to\dots\to z_{k}\to z_{k-1}\to\dots\to z_{0}$ , after which $z_{0}$ is decoded into video; networks may equivalently predict the clean sample or the velocity. Flow matching instead trains a vector field that transports a simple base distribution to the data along a chosen probability path Lipman et al., 2023 . Here $k$ is continuous: for the linear path $z_{k}=(1-k)z_{0}+k\epsilon$ with $k\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]$ , the target velocity is $\epsilon-z_{0}$ :

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fm}}=\mathbb{E}_{(z_{0},c),\epsilon,k}\!\left[\big\lVert v_{\theta}(z_{k},k,c)-(\epsilon-z_{0})\big\rVert^{2}\right].$$ (5)

Sampling integrates $dz_{k}/dk=v_{\theta}(z_{k},k,c)$ from noise at $k=1$ to data at $k=0$ , in practice over a discrete grid of steps. Diffusion and flow matching can share backbones and samplers. Section 5 uses masked prediction to learn representations, Section 6 covers generators built on these objectives, and Section 8 adapts them to action-conditioned online rollout.


#### 4.4 Taxonomy of Video World Models

A video world model performs the interacting operation of Definition with a video-centered state. Unlike a general video generator, which aims at plausible content, it learns how that state evolves. We exclude models whose primary persistent state is an explicit 3D structure, such as a neural field, 3D Gaussians, or an occupancy grid, as well as purely symbolic or text-based world models; models that keep 3D structure only as auxiliary memory for a video-centered state remain in scope (Section 8.2.2 ). World models from model-based reinforcement learning are included only when their state is learned from video.

We classify video world models along two independent axes. On the first axis, the prediction space , observation-space models predict future frames, video tokens, or generative latents decoded to video, as GAIA Hu et al., 2023 ; Russell et al., 2025 and EVA Chi et al., 2024 do. Although VAE latents are not pixels, predicting them for decoding to video still counts as observation-space prediction. Latent-state models instead predict future video-derived features that need not be decoded to video: V-JEPA Bardes et al., 2024 and V-JEPA 2 Assran et al., 2025 learn such features by masked feature prediction, which serves as pretraining for latent transition models (Section 8 ), and DINO-WM Zhou et al., 2025a and V-JEPA 2-AC add action-conditioned transitions for planning. The second axis, the transition interface , describes how controls reach the model. Passive models predict from observations alone, while action-conditioned models receive a control sequence fixed before rollout, such as the ego trajectories of Vista Gao et al., 2024a or the robot actions of IRASim Zhu et al., 2025 . Interactive models accept new controls during rollout, so each input can change what follows, as in GameNGen Valevski et al., 2025 , Genie 2 Parker-Holder et al., 2024 , and related playable simulators Alonso et al., 2024 ; Bruce et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2025g ; He et al., 2025a . Section 8 reviews each cell of the resulting $2\times 3$ grid (Figure 10 ).


### 5 Video Comprehension: Reading the World

Video comprehension performs the reading operation $V\rightarrow W$ of Section 3 : given a trace $V$ , the model estimates properties of the latent world $W$ that produced it. From a limited viewpoint and interval, it must recover which entities and events are present, where they are, how they change, which constraints govern that change, and what may have caused it. Occlusion, camera motion, editing, sparse sampling, and missing modalities leave much of this state unobserved, and the viewing scenarios of Section 4.1 change how much evidence must be selected and remembered. Sections 5.1 – 5.5 cover tasks and benchmarks, encoders, Video-LLMs, agents, and applications in turn. Tang et al. Tang et al., 2026a survey Video-LLMs in more detail; here we organize the evaluation of reading by the world properties it tests (Section 5.1 ) and review Video-LLMs as one stage of the reading pipeline.


#### 5.1 Tasks, Data, and Evaluation

We organize video-understanding tasks by the main dimension of world state they require: semantic (entities, actions, events), spatial (location, identity, layout, viewpoint), temporal (order, duration, boundaries, long-range dependencies), physical (contact, gravity, containment, material behavior), and causal (effects, interventions, counterfactuals). The dimensions interact, as when tracking combines identity with temporal continuity, so Table 4 places each benchmark under the dimension it mainly tests, even when the text introduces it elsewhere. Output format is a separate axis: a label, span, mask, or sentence can probe any dimension, and what a benchmark measures depends on the evidence needed to produce its output.

##### 5.1.1 Semantic Understanding

Semantic understanding identifies the entities, actions, events, and relations that make a video meaningful. Action recognition is the most established semantic task: UCF101 Soomro et al., 2012 and HMDB51 Kuehne et al., 2011 test closed-set recognition in trimmed clips, Kinetics Kay et al., 2017 scales up the number and diversity of classes, and Something-Something Goyal et al., 2017 makes labels depend on how objects change under manipulation. Language moves the output beyond fixed categories through retrieval (MSR-VTT Xu et al., 2016 ), captioning (VATEX Wang et al., 2019 , YouCook2 Zhou et al., 2018 ), and video question answering (VideoQA: ActivityNet-QA Yu et al., 2019 , TVQA Lei et al., 2018 , TGIF-QA Jang et al., 2017 ).

Recent Video-LLM benchmarks mix semantic questions with temporal, spatial, and knowledge-intensive items (Video-MME and Video-MME-v2 Fu et al., 2025 ; Fu et al., 2026 , MMVU Zhao et al., 2025a , Video-MMMU Hu et al., 2026c ; Luo et al., 2026 ). Hallucination benchmarks test whether the reported content is supported by the video at all: VideoHallucer Wang et al., 2024g ; Luo et al., 2026c uses adversarially paired questions, EventHallusion Zhang et al., 2024 probes events suggested by language priors, and ARGUS Rawal et al., 2025 scores hallucination and omission in free-form captions.


##### 5.1.2 Spatial Understanding

Spatial understanding asks where entities are, how their extent changes, and whether their identity persists as viewpoint and visibility vary. Spatiotemporal grounding links a referring expression to a region tube over an interval (VidSTG Zhang et al., 2020 ). Video object segmentation tracks pixel masks (DAVIS Pont-Tuset et al., 2017 , YouTube-VOS Xu et al., 2018 ), referring variants select the target by language or motion (Ref-YouTube-VOS Seo et al., 2020 , MeViS Ding et al., 2023 ; addressed by ReferFormer Wu et al., 2022 ), and MOTChallenge Milan et al., 2016 and LaSOT Fan et al., 2019 test identity across occlusion and reappearance. Memory-based and promptable trackers such as XMem Cheng & Schwing, 2022 , AOT Yang et al., 2021 , DEVA Cheng et al., 2023 , and SAM 2 Ravi et al., 2025 keep earlier evidence as the target changes appearance or disappears.

Spatial reasoning benchmarks go beyond masks and tracks: VSI-Bench Yang et al., 2025b and MMSI-Video-Bench Lin et al., 2025a test relative position, viewpoint, and layout, STI-Bench Li et al., 2025j asks for quantitative pose and motion, and DSI-Bench Zhang et al., 2025i separates observer motion from object motion. A newer group tests state that is out of view: SCP-Bench Zhao et al., 2026b infers unseen past or future layouts, Beyond3D Ma et al., 2026 asks where relocated objects now are, and OVO-S-Bench Li et al., 2026i answers from the video prefix seen so far. These tasks test the object permanence and revisit consistency that world models must maintain (Section 8.2.2 ).


##### 5.1.3 Temporal Understanding

Temporal understanding recovers when an event occurs, how long it lasts, and how evidence at different moments is ordered or connected. Temporal action detection locates predefined actions in untrimmed video (THUMOS Idrees et al., 2017 , ActivityNet Heilbron et al., 2015 ), and moment retrieval returns the segment that matches a language query (Charades-STA Gao et al., 2017 , QVHighlights Lei et al., 2021 , DiDeMo Hendricks et al., 2017 ). Dense event captioning localizes and describes possibly overlapping events (ActivityNet Captions Krishna et al., 2017 , addressed end to end by Vid2Seq Yang et al., 2023 ), and anticipation predicts an action not yet observed (e.g., Ego4D forecasting Grauman et al., 2022 ).

Fine-grained and long-form benchmarks stress subtle temporal differences and sparse evidence. TemporalBench Cai et al., 2024 and TempCompass Liu et al., 2024d test ordering, speed, and direction, TimeBlind Li et al., 2026 pairs videos that differ only in temporal structure, and Video-MME-Logical Kwan et al., 2026 separates logical complexity from scene recognition. MLVU Zhou et al., 2025b , LVBench Wang et al., 2025c , and LongVideoBench Wu et al., 2024 pose heterogeneous questions over long videos, and VideoOdyssey He et al., 2026 extends this to ultra-long, omni-modal video, measuring how much context stays usable as evidence length grows.

Streaming benchmarks make state maintenance directly observable. SVCBench Liu et al., 2026d asks for running object and event counts at many points in a video, TRACE Ma et al., 2026a scores answer quality together with latency, false alarms, and missed responses under causal input, and StreamingEval Tang et al., 2026 adds encoding throughput, time to first token, and a fixed memory budget.


##### 5.1.4 Physical Understanding

Physical understanding concerns how a visible state can change under constraints that are often only partly observed. Plausibility benchmarks ask whether an observed transition is possible: IntPhys Riochet et al., 2022 and IntPhys 2 Bordes et al., 2025 test object permanence and intuitive physics, ImplausiBench Motamed et al., 2025 contains videos that violate expected physical behavior, and Physion Bear et al., 2021 instead asks whether objects will make contact. Other benchmarks ask for next states, interaction outcomes, or the mechanism behind a change. CLEVRER Yi et al., 2020 asks descriptive, predictive, and explanatory questions about collisions, while PhysicsMind Mak et al., 2026 , PhysBench Chow et al., 2025 , and PAI-Bench-U Zhou et al., 2026 broaden coverage to object properties, commonsense constraints, task completion, and next actions. APT Wu et al., 2026a further labels timestamped transitions by mechanism (contact, gravity, or friction).

Two benchmarks check that answers rest on physical evidence rather than priors. MVP Krojer et al., 2025 pairs near-identical videos that require opposite answers, and VisPhyWorld Liang et al., 2026a asks the model to rebuild the scene as executable simulator code so that its physical hypothesis can be checked. Physics benchmarks for generated video, such as Physics-IQ Motamed et al., 2026 and VideoPhy Bansal et al., 2025 ; Bansal et al., 2026 , are covered in Sections 6.1.2 and 8.4 .


##### 5.1.5 Causal Understanding

Causal understanding asks why an observed event occurred and how the outcome would change if an action, object, or condition were different. On synthetic collisions, CLEVRER Yi et al., 2020 adds counterfactual questions to its explanatory ones. Real-world and procedural benchmarks link actions to effects that may appear much later: NExT-QA Xiao et al., 2021 and STAR Wu et al., 2021 ask about event relations and reasons, Video-Holmes Cheng et al., 2026 and Causal2Needles Li et al., 2025f spread the clues far apart, and CaST-Bench Zhang et al., 2026h covers more varied settings and causal structures. Counterfactual benchmarks test altered outcomes directly: CausalVQA Foss et al., 2025 asks counterfactual, hypothetical, and planning questions about real egocentric video, and DMC-CF Zhang et al., 2026g pairs such questions about real videos with causal-graph interventions, whose regenerated variants also limit data contamination.


#### 5.2 Video Representation Learning and Encoding

Video representation learning maps frames to features that can be reused across recognition, retrieval, grounding, reasoning, and multimodal modeling. Convolutional backbones such as I3D Carreira & Zisserman, 2017 and SlowFast Feichtenhofer et al., 2019 established supervised spatiotemporal processing, and TimeSformer Bertasius et al., 2021 , ViViT Arnab et al., 2021 , and Video Swin Liu et al., 2022 carried the Vision Transformer Dosovitskiy et al., 2021 to video at different spatial and temporal scales. The pretraining objective decides what these backbones retain, and we group objectives by their dominant supervision signal into the four families of Figure 3 . The families overlap: masked feature prediction, for instance, is both masked and predictive. Variational autoencoders and tokenizers built for synthesis are covered in Section 6 .

##### 5.2.1 Contrastive Learning

Contrastive learning pulls positive pairs together in embedding space and pushes mismatched pairs apart, commonly with an InfoNCE-style objective. In video-only learning, CVRL Qian et al., 2021 and $\rho$ MoCo Feichtenhofer et al., 2021 draw positives from augmentations or clips of the same video, and the sampling distance sets a trade-off: views too close give little contrast, while views too far apart may show different events.

Video–text contrastive learning pairs clips with language. Among dual-encoder retrieval models, Frozen in Time Bain et al., 2021 trains a space–time Transformer jointly on image–text and video–text pairs, and CLIP4Clip Luo et al., 2022 transfers the CLIP image–text encoder and adds temporal aggregation, while VideoCLIP Xu et al., 2021 , X-CLIP Ma et al., 2022 , ViCLIP Wang et al., 2024c , and Finsta Fei et al., 2024b develop video-specific alignment. Foundation encoders combine contrastive pretraining with masked or generative losses (InternVideo Wang et al., 2022a , InternVideo2 Wang et al., 2024d ), distillation into a masked-video student (VideoPrism Zhao et al., 2024 ), or later alignment stages for language and dense prediction (Perception Encoder Bolya et al., 2025 ). Global alignment can drop fine-grained detail: the World Embedding Benchmark Liu et al., 2026j shows that alignment and recoverable physical state can diverge, as physics-specific contrastive tuning improves video–text matching but weakens physical-quantity regression from frozen features; the added losses aim to retain it.


##### 5.2.2 Masked Learning

Masked learning removes much of the spatiotemporal input and trains the model to recover it, an idea that VideoBERT Sun et al., 2019 applied early to quantized video tokens and speech transcripts jointly. VideoMAE Tong et al., 2022 reconstructs pixels behind tube masks, with a high mask ratio that exploits the redundancy of adjacent frames, and VideoMAE-V2 Wang et al., 2023 scales it with dual masking. Feature targets replace raw pixels: MaskFeat Wei et al., 2022 reconstructs handcrafted or learned features, and UMT Li et al., 2023a distills from an unmasked teacher that sees the full clip. Learned or teacher targets shift capacity from low-level appearance toward the semantics and motion that the teacher encodes, but they also inherit its biases.


##### 5.2.3 Predictive Learning

Predictive learning trains a model to predict the latent representation of hidden or future content, without pixel reconstruction or negative examples. V-JEPA Bardes et al., 2024 predicts the features of masked regions against a slowly updated target encoder, V-JEPA 2 Assran et al., 2025 scales this to larger and more diverse data, and V-JEPA 2.1 Mur-Labadia et al., 2026 improves dense features. InternVideo-Next Wang et al., 2026 bridges the masked and predictive families, combining masked reconstruction with latent prediction of its first-stage targets, without video–text data. Adding an action-conditioned transition model to such a representation yields the action-conditioned latent-state world models of Section 8 .


##### 5.2.4 Generative Learning

Generative pretraining shapes a representation by requiring it to produce missing or future content. Visual autoregressive objectives Yan et al., 2021 predict future frames, patches, or tokens, which rewards sensitivity to appearance and motion but can spend capacity on reconstruction detail that recognition does not need. Vision-language generation outputs text instead: GIT-style Wang et al., 2022 training generates captions or answers from visual features, and VideoCoCa Yan et al., 2022 combines captioning with contrastive alignment. In both cases, the representation is shaped only by what captions describe. Large video diffusion models offer a third route: Gen4U King et al., 2026 reuses a frozen generator’s intermediate activations for classification, geometry, and captioning, and GenCeption Wang et al., 2026c adapts a diffusion backbone into a text-steered model for depth, normals, pose, segmentation, and keypoints (Section 7.2 ).


#### 5.3 Video-Language Foundation Modeling

Video-LLMs extend language foundation models to video for open-ended description, dialogue, question answering, grounding, and reasoning. They take visual features from encoders such as those in Section 5.2 and must decide which frames to observe, how to encode time, and how many visual tokens the language model can afford. We review their architectures, training, and test-time scaling in turn.


##### 5.3.1 Architectures

Visual front end. Many systems apply an image encoder such as CLIP Radford et al., 2021 or SigLIP Zhai et al., 2023 ; Tschannen et al., 2025 to sampled frames, a route that is cheap and semantically strong but leaves temporal modeling to later modules. Native-video encoders Assran et al., 2025 ; Zhu et al., 2026 model local motion before fusion, and direct patch or visual-token inputs keep finer spatial detail at a higher context cost. No encoder can recover an event from an unsampled frame.

Time and position. Frame order may be encoded by temporal embeddings, timestamps, or multidimensional rotary positions. Qwen2.5-VL Bai et al., 2025a uses M-RoPE to coordinate temporal and spatial indices, Qwen3-VL Bai et al., 2025 adds textual timestamps so that answers can cite moments, and the InternVL series Wang et al., 2025d advances visual positions in fractional steps so that long sequences stay within the trained positional range. Temporal Transformers and state-space models such as Mamba Gu & Dao, 2024 can also aggregate frame sequences before they reach the LLM.

Vision-language connector. The connector maps visual features into the language backbone’s embedding space and token budget. Linear or MLP projectors turn each visual feature into a prefix token, as in LLaVA-style systems Liu et al., 2023 , while Q-Former and resampler designs Li et al., 2023 extract a fixed number of query tokens, as in early Video-LLaMA Zhang et al., 2023 and VideoChat Li et al., 2025e . Gated cross-attention, as in Flamingo Alayrac et al., 2022 , instead lets language layers consult visual features at selected depths. Structure-preserving compressors downsample while keeping spatial layout, through convolutional abstractors (Honeybee Cha et al., 2024 ), spatial-temporal convolution (VideoLLaMA 2 Cheng et al., 2024 ), or pixel unshuffle (InternVL Wang et al., 2025d ). Projectors keep the most direct token path, resamplers and cross-attention give the most control over how much visual information enters the LLM, and compressors trade some directness for locality-aware reduction.

Compression and memory. Video token count grows with both the number of frames and the tokens per frame, and pooling, merging, or resampling reduce it at the risk of erasing small objects, short events, or precise timestamps. Some designs spend tokens unevenly over time: LLaVA-Video Zhang et al., 2025h keeps a few frames dense and pools the rest, Keye-VL-1.5 Yang et al., 2025 routes frames with large visual change to a high-resolution pathway, and LLaVA-OneVision-2 An et al., 2026a groups tokens adaptively like a video codec. Others compress each unit: Chat-UniVi Jin et al., 2024 merges tokens into dynamic multi-scale units, LLaMA-VID Li et al., 2024c reduces each frame to as few as two tokens, and Video-LaVIT Jin et al., 2024a pairs keyframes with compact motion vectors. For long video, LongVA Zhang et al., 2025d and LongVILA Chen et al., 2025f rely on long-context transfer and token budgeting, and Video-XL Shu et al., 2025 adds structured compression. One finding bounds this design space: a few tokens carry most of the discriminative information, but finding them without an oracle is hard Hao et al., 2025 . Memory solves a different problem from compression: MovieChat Song et al., 2024 and MA-LMM He et al., 2024 maintain a memory of past segments, which decides what remains available after the original frames leave the context (agent memory is treated in Section 5.4 ).

Language backbone and output. An instruction-following LLM combines the retained evidence with the query and produces dialogue, descriptions, explanations, or structured predictions. Early systems extended image-language models to video with adapters and compressed frame tokens Zhang et al., 2023 ; Li et al., 2025e ; Maaz et al., 2024 ; Lin et al., 2024 ; Li et al., 2024c , and later open models add larger multimodal mixtures, more efficient video tokens, and grounded or reasoning outputs Li et al., 2025 ; Bai et al., 2025a ; Bai et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2025d ; Yan et al., 2026 ; Yao et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2026g . Boxes, timestamps, and masks can be emitted as special tokens or by attached heads, which makes the evidence behind an answer easier to inspect. Closed systems such as Gemini Team et al., 2024 show strong long-context video and audio results but disclose few design details. Supervised instruction tuning. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) then trains on VideoQA and dialogue data for question-conditioned answers, temporal grounding data for timestamps Qian et al., 2024 , and spatial grounding data for coordinates or other structured outputs. Which components are updated varies: early Video-ChatGPT Maaz et al., 2024 tunes only its projector, Video-LLaVA Lin et al., 2024 also updates the LLM, and VideoLLaMA3 Zhang et al., 2025 and VideoChat3 Li et al., 2026g adapt both the visual and language components (Table 5 ). The mixture of image, short-video, long-video, optical character recognition (OCR), and grounding data decides whether a model mainly describes clips or can locate and reason over the evidence behind an answer.

Preference optimization and reinforcement learning. Post-training can then optimize preferences or task outcomes. Reinforcement learning (RL) from human feedback Ouyang et al., 2022 and direct preference optimization, which Qwen2.5-VL Bai et al., 2025a and VistaDPO Huang et al., 2025 apply after SFT, improve visual faithfulness only insofar as the feedback separates grounded content from fluent but unsupported answers. Reasoning-oriented methods instead use verifiable rewards, often with group relative policy optimization (GRPO) Guo et al., 2025 . Video-R1 Feng et al., 2025 adds a temporal reward that compares rollouts on ordered and shuffled frames, LongVILA-R1 Chen et al., 2025e scales RL to long videos through its data and distributed training, and OneThinker Feng et al., 2026a broadens the task mixture and normalizes advantages with running per-task reward statistics.

Task-specific rewards target the output directly: Time-R1 Wang et al., 2025e rewards timestamp intersection-over-union for temporal grounding, VideoChat-R1 Li et al., 2025h jointly rewards spatiotemporal tasks without hurting general QA, and VideoP2R Jiang et al., 2026a rewards perception and reasoning separately. Others modify GRPO itself: DeepVideo-R1 Park et al., 2025 recasts it as advantage regression with difficulty-aware augmentation, and GRPO-CARE Chen et al., 2026f adds a bonus if a slowly updated reference model reproduces the answer from the reasoning trace.

Data and scaling. Data and curriculum matter as much as model size. Current recipes such as InternVideo3 Yan et al., 2026 , Qwen3-VL Bai et al., 2025 , and VideoLLaMA3 Zhang et al., 2025 train jointly on image, video, and language data and widen task coverage with synthetic instructions; InternVideo3 and Qwen3-VL also add long-context curricula and use on-policy distillation to supply responses from states the model actually visits. Apollo Zohar et al., 2025 studies this design space systematically, measuring how frame sampling, encoder choice, and token allocation interact across model scales. Newer corpora add finer temporal supervision: OneStreamer-1M Zeng et al., 2026a times its captions and QA to the evidence available at each response point.


##### 5.3.3 Test-Time Scaling

Test-time scaling spends extra computation after training to read the available video better. Multimodal chain-of-thought Fei et al., 2024a ; Luo et al., 2024 spends tokens on intermediate reasoning, which helps only if those steps stay grounded in the frames. Query-aware frame selection instead spends compute on choosing the input: SeViLA Yu et al., 2023a and VideoTree Wang et al., 2025g rank, retain, or hierarchically organize frames for the query. Iterative “thinking with video” methods such as FrameThinker He et al., 2026d and VideoChat-A1 Wang et al., 2026g alternate between reasoning and revisiting frames over several steps. Search and aggregation select among several reasoning paths by self-consistency, best-of- $N$ , or verifier and process-reward models, while V-Reason Sridhar et al., 2026 directly modifies the model’s inference-time behavior instead of sampling more outputs. Each route trades latency and repeated visual computation for broader exploration or a second look at ambiguous evidence.

The boundary with agentic modeling is gradual: thinking-with-video methods already revisit frames, and once a model issues explicit operations to fetch new crops, frames, retrieval results, or persistent memory, it enters the agentic setting of Section 5.4 .


#### 5.4 Agentic Video Modeling

Agentic video modeling turns evidence acquisition into a sequential decision problem: an LLM or Video-LLM forms subquestions, chooses a temporal region or visual detail to inspect, invokes an operation, and updates its reasoning from the result until it can answer. Its actions change access to the recording by locating segments, sampling frames, cropping regions, querying tracks, or retrieving memory; actions that change the depicted world belong to the interactive rollouts of Section 8 .

##### 5.4.1 Video Agents

Early video agents separate the controller’s reasoning state from the full video stream (Table 7 ). Two systems named VideoAgent take different routes: VideoAgent (long-form) Wang et al., 2024a has an LLM iteratively gather a small set of question-relevant frames, and VideoAgent (memory) Fan et al., 2024 builds event and object-tracking memory and exposes segment localization and memory queries as tools. VideoAgent2 Zhi et al., 2025 adds uncertainty-aware replanning, so that new observations can revise the search and intermediate evidence is verified. Later controllers refine the observation policy: VCA Yang et al., 2025e explores unexamined segments out of curiosity, and VideoMind Liu et al., 2026i splits the loop into planner, grounder, verifier, and answerer roles, all chain-of-LoRA experts on one backbone.

Agentic search extends adaptive observation from frame selection to a structured video collection. Deep Video Discovery Zhang et al., 2025f segments long video into a multi-granular database and lets the controller choose search tools and parameters, and VideoExplorer Yuan et al., 2025 poses sub-questions inside the reasoning loop and locates the moments that answer them.

Continuous streams add another decision: when to observe and when to speak, possibly before the user asks. StreamAgent Yang et al., 2025a anticipates task-relevant events and shifts attention before they arrive, and AViLA Zhang et al., 2025a answers asynchronous queries once the accumulated evidence is sufficient.

Memory determines what an agent retains between observations. Flat caption lists are cheap to query but lose temporal scope and visual detail. VideoLucy Zuo et al., 2025 instead backtracks through a coarse-to-fine memory hierarchy and MM-Mem Lian et al., 2026 builds a sensory, episodic, and schema-level memory pyramid, while InternVideo3 Yan et al., 2026 accumulates observations, reasoning, and tool actions in one evolving context. Memory-centric agents aim for competence that grows with history instead of resetting at every query: M3-Agent Long et al., 2026a consolidates an entity-centric long-term memory from continuous audio-visual input, and WorldMM Yeo et al., 2026 maintains a dynamic multimodal memory.

Multi-agent collaboration divides the loop across specialized roles. VideoMultiAgents Kugo et al., 2025 coordinates vision, text, and graph specialists, LongVideoAgent Liu et al., 2026e pairs a reasoning master with grounding assistants, and Think, Then Verify Wang et al., 2026f makes candidate answers survive adversarial checking. MAPA Xu et al., 2026d pairs lightweight perception agents with action agents to avoid redundant computation.

Other agents are trained, not only prompted: SAGE Jain et al., 2026 learns with RL how far ahead to look, EVA Zhang et al., 2026l makes end-to-end RL of a video agent efficient, and CASE Du et al., 2026 learns when to stop searching. Trained policies can lower tool cost and improve stopping, but they tie the agent to the tools seen in training.

Agent quality depends jointly on the backbone, observation policy, tool outputs, and memory: a wrong first hypothesis can steer later calls to the wrong interval, and noisy captions or detections propagate through the reasoning trace. Agents are trained on tool-use traces for SFT and questions for RL (Ego-R1 Data Tian et al., 2026a , LongVT’s VideoSIAH Yang et al., 2026f , VideoThinker’s synthetic trajectories Li et al., 2026b ). Benchmarks (Table 8 ) test sparse-evidence search (VideoSIAH-Eval Yang et al., 2026f ), multi-hop retrieval with evidence-necessity checks and tool-call cost (LongVidSearch Yu et al., 2026 ), trajectories against evidence milestones (AgentVidBench An et al., 2026 ), and combined video and web search (VideoGAIA Zhang et al., 2026 ). Others test proactive and spoken audio-visual interaction (IPIBench Li et al., 2026d , OmniInteract Lu et al., 2026a ) and cross-session memory with cases where the needed history is missing (APM-Bench Huang et al., 2026 ). Beyond answer accuracy, evaluation should report whether the retrieved evidence supports the answer and what the search costs; REPAIR AlHamidi et al., 2026 adds repeated paired runs to separate run-to-run variance from method effects and counts the operations and latency hidden behind visible tool calls.

##### 5.4.2 Tool Use and Retrieval Augmentation

Tools supply observations that the base model cannot obtain reliably from its current context: detectors and trackers for entity state, temporal grounders for event boundaries, croppers for spatial detail, and OCR, speech recognition, or captioners for other modalities. In modular agents, a prompted controller composes external tools: VisProg Gupta & Kembhavi, 2023 and ViperGPT Surís et al., 2023 generate executable programs over vision tools without training. Native tool-calling models instead learn when to call a tool, with which parameters, and how to use the result. LongVT Yang et al., 2026f surveys a long video, predicts a relevant interval, and crops and densely resamples it before continuing to reason, and VideoThinker Li et al., 2026b learns temporal retrieval and zoom from tool trajectories synthesized in caption space and grounded back to video. Ego-R1 Tian et al., 2026a trains a chain of tool-thought with RL over day- and week-long egocentric recordings. Tool use can also be distilled away: Agent-of-Thoughts Distillation Shi et al., 2025 distills agent traces into a single Video-LLM as chain-of-thought supervision, trading flexibility for one-pass inference.

Retrieval augmentation moves evidence into an external index. One VideoRAG Jeong et al., 2025 retrieves query-relevant videos and keeps both their visual and textual content, while another Ren et al., 2026 combines graph-based text grounding with multimodal encoding to search very large collections. The retrieval unit shapes what can be recovered: fixed-length chunks may split an event, so SceneRAG Zeng et al., 2026 indexes narrative scenes and adds an entity-relation graph for multi-hop retrieval, at the cost of errors in segmentation and graph construction. Tools and retrieval address different bottlenecks: a cropper or grounder fetches finer local evidence on demand, whereas an index narrows the search across long videos or corpora. Both still require the system to decide what to request, detect missing or conflicting evidence, and trace the answer back to its source clips.


#### 5.5 Applications

Applications pose these problems under domain-specific viewpoints, vocabularies, and event structures.


##### 5.5.1 Embodied and Egocentric Perception

Egocentric video records activity from the wearer’s viewpoint, so the camera moves with the body, hands occlude manipulated objects, and key events are separated by long stretches of routine activity. Standard resources include EPIC-KITCHENS-100 Damen et al., 2022 , Ego4D Grauman et al., 2022 , EgoSchema Mangalam et al., 2023 , and the paired first- and third-person views of Ego-Exo4D Grauman et al., 2025 (Section 4.1 ). An egocentric assistant has to track the manipulated object and its state, the current task step, and the earlier event that explains the present situation, even when the object is hidden behind the hand or out of view.

Newer benchmarks target specific settings. EgoTools Tian et al., 2026 tests understanding of physical tools and their effects (not software-tool calls), covering affordance, grounding, procedure, and spatial reasoning, and EgoPolice Gonzalez-Diez et al., 2026 tests police body-worn camera footage, where consequential events are rare and visibility is poor.


##### 5.5.2 Autonomous Driving

Driving video must be read as a dynamic scene of road geometry, traffic controls, vehicles, pedestrians, and their relations. BDD100K Yu et al., 2020 supplies diverse annotated road video, and nuScenes Caesar et al., 2020 and the Waymo Open Dataset Sun et al., 2020 add synchronized camera and range-sensor sequences with 3D annotations. Language-based benchmarks ask which participants and relations matter: DriveLM Sima et al., 2024 links key objects and their interactions to prediction and planning questions through graph-structured VideoQA, and the frame-level NuScenes-SpatialQA Tian et al., 2025 tests spatial relations in driving scenes. OmniDrive Wang et al., 2025b aligns language reasoning with 3D driving tasks and adds counterfactual questions about how alternative actions would change an outcome. The resulting scene state links video reading to the trajectory prediction, action selection, and closed-loop control of Section 8 .


##### 5.5.3 Domain-Specific Video Analysis

Medical and surgical video. Surgical video is structured by procedure phases, so recognizing visible instruments is not enough. EndoNet Twinanda et al., 2017 introduced Cholec80 for phase and tool recognition, CholecT50 and Rendezvous Nwoye et al., 2022 refine the output into instrument–verb–target triplets, and SurgVLM Zeng et al., 2025 combines perception, temporal analysis, and question answering across surgical tasks. Evaluation must check phase, anatomy, and action against the structured labels of clinical workflows, not the fluency of a description.

Sports analytics. A match contains long stretches of routine play broken by brief, rule-dependent events. SoccerNet Giancola et al., 2018 defined action spotting in broadcast soccer; SoccerNet-v2 Deliege et al., 2021 adds camera-shot segmentation and replay grounding, which require precise localization and knowledge of broadcast structure.

Film and edited video. In edited footage, shot size, cut type, and transitions are deliberate choices. VEU-Bench Li et al., 2025a tests recognition, reasoning, and judging of editing attributes within and across shots, and finds that general Video-LLMs handle them poorly, in some cases below chance.

Education and instructional video. Instructional video follows a procedure from an initial state through intermediate steps to a goal. HowTo100M Miech et al., 2019 supplies narrated demonstrations, and CrossTask Zhukov et al., 2019 and COIN Tang et al., 2019 annotate steps for temporal localization. Reading such video means tracking the progress of the procedure: separating narrated intention from visible operation, recovering omitted or repeated steps, and reasoning about order and prerequisites.


### 6 Video Generation: Writing the World

Video generation is the writing operation $W\rightarrow V$ of Section 3 : its conditions fix some entities, relations, and dynamics of $W$ and leave the rest to the generator. Text may specify an event, an image may fix its initial appearance, audio may set visible rhythm or articulation, and trajectories camera or object motion.

This chapter covers models whose output and training target are the video itself, judged by fidelity, condition adherence, temporal coherence, and controllable revision. Persistent state under occlusion, revisits, long rollouts, and actions is treated in Section 8 . We cover tasks and evaluation, latent representations, generative paradigms, control strategies, and applications. Xing et al. Xing et al., 2025 review video diffusion models in depth.


#### 6.1 Tasks, Conditions, and Evaluation

A video-generation task is defined by the information fixed before synthesis: text, images, source videos, audio, reference identities, or prescribed motion. Evaluation measures the visual and temporal quality of the result, its adherence to the condition, and the stability of the entities, events, and physical relations it shows. ##### 6.1.1 Generation Tasks by Condition

Text, image, and video conditions. Text-to-video (T2V) maps a description of content, style, or events to a video and leaves most appearance and motion to the model, as in Sora Brooks et al., 2024 , Wan Wan et al., 2025 , and HunyuanVideo Kong et al., 2024 . Image-to-video (I2V) fixes the initial state or reference appearance and must add plausible motion without losing that structure and identity, as in Stable Video Diffusion Blattmann et al., 2023 and the human-animation systems Animate Anyone Hu, 2024 and MagicAnimate Xu et al., 2024a . Video-to-video (V2V) starts from a source video and covers transformations with different preservation requirements. Editing changes selected content and should stay local, as in Tune-A-Video Wu et al., 2023 , Video-P2P Liu et al., 2024a , FateZero Qi et al., 2023 , and TokenFlow Geyer et al., 2024 . Translation maps the source to another appearance or domain while keeping its content. Interpolation connects observed endpoints, and extension continues the sequence beyond its last frame, as in NUWA-XL Yin et al., 2023a , SEINE Chen et al., 2024f , and StreamingT2V Henschel et al., 2025 .

Audio and audio-visual conditions. In audio-driven generation, a speech, music, or sound track sets visible dynamics such as lip motion, expression, gesture, or rhythm, as in Wav2Lip Prajwal et al., 2020 , SadTalker Zhang et al., 2023b , EMO Tian et al., 2024 , and Hallo Xu et al., 2024 . Joint audio-video generation instead produces both streams and must keep their events synchronized as well as each stream plausible, as in JavisDiT Liu et al., 2026a , JavisDiT++ Liu et al., 2026b , and Ovi Low et al., 2025 .

Geometric, motion, and identity conditions. Camera, motion, and trajectory conditions prescribe how the viewpoint or scene elements evolve. Camera poses and paths control the observation process, as in CameraCtrl He et al., 2025 , while object trajectories, human poses, and action sequences constrain motion within the scene, as in MotionCtrl Wang et al., 2024i , DragNUWA Yin et al., 2023 , VideoComposer Wang et al., 2023a , and Wan-Move Chu et al., 2025 . Identity- or subject-conditioned generation, as in ConsisID Yuan et al., 2025a , fixes which person, object, or subject persists over time, and that subject must stay stable under pose, viewpoint, and occlusion changes.

Any-conditioned and unified control. Any-conditioned systems accept a variable subset of text, image, audio, structure, motion, and reference inputs, as in FullDiT Ju et al., 2025 , UNIC Ye et al., 2026a , and Any2Caption Wu et al., 2025b . They must bind each cue to the right entity, region, time span, or modality and reconcile cues of different granularity. Section 6.4 describes how such conditions enter the model.

Training supervision. Each task needs training pairs that expose its condition–output correspondence (Table 9 ). Caption corpora such as Panda-70M Chen et al., 2024d and UltraVideo Xue et al., 2025 serve T2V, and image conditioning and extension can be learned by keeping selected frames or prefixes of a clip Jiang et al., 2025 . Editing needs source–instruction–target triplets, as in OpenVE-3M He et al., 2026a . Camera control uses posed video such as RealEstate10K Zhou et al., 2018a , motion control uses tracked point trajectories Chu et al., 2025 , and audio-visual generation needs synchronized tracks such as VGGSound Chen et al., 2020 . Identity control pairs references and targets from different contexts or shots, as in Phantom-Data Chen et al., 2026g , MuSS Zhang et al., 2026b , and Omni-R2V Li et al., 2026e , so the model cannot simply copy the reference’s pose or background into the output.

##### 6.1.2 Evaluation and Metrics

Video-generation quality has several dimensions, and the right profile depends on the task: T2V emphasizes semantic alignment, editing requires source preservation and locality, audio-driven generation requires synchronization, and motion control requires trajectory adherence. A single aggregate score hides the failure that limits a system, so we group metrics by the property they measure (Table 10 ) and benchmarks by the cases they test (Table 11 ).

Visual fidelity and temporal quality. FID Heusel et al., 2017 compares frame-feature distributions and ignores temporal order. The Inception Score Salimans et al., 2016 rewards confident, diverse classifier predictions without reference to real data, which limits its meaning outside the classifier’s domain Barratt & Sharma, 2018 . FVD Unterthiner et al., 2018 extends the comparison to video features, but it depends on the feature extractor and sample count and can under-penalize temporal corruption in favor of static samples Ge et al., 2024 . JEDi Luo et al., 2025 instead uses V-JEPA features and a maximum mean discrepancy, avoiding FVD’s Gaussian assumption and needing fewer samples. PSNR, SSIM Wang et al., 2004 , and LPIPS Zhang et al., 2018 suit reconstruction and source preservation but penalize valid alternative futures. FVMD Liu et al., 2024 and STREAM Kim et al., 2024 target motion consistency and spatiotemporal quality, and VMBench Ling et al., 2025 checks whether motion strength matches the prompt. Diversity needs repeated samples under one condition: DiVid Hu et al., 2026a measures variation in semantics, style, subject, scene, motion, and camera alongside quality and faithfulness.

Condition alignment and compositionality. Condition-aware evaluation checks whether the video follows its condition. CLIPScore Hessel et al., 2021 measures image–caption similarity, and its frame-averaged video variants ignore temporal order and motion. cFreD Koo et al., 2026 adds text–visual cross-covariances to a conditional Fréchet distance, so it penalizes videos swapped among prompts, which a marginal distance cannot detect. FETV Liu et al., 2023a and T2V-CompBench Sun et al., 2025 separate objects, attributes, relations, actions, and temporal composition. Structured conditions have their own tests: MultiRef-Compass Zhang et al., 2026k checks how several references bind to audio-visual content. OmniVBench Li et al., 2026e applies case-specific checklists for reference fidelity and binding, and MoveBench Chu et al., 2025 measures trajectory endpoint error. For source-conditioned tasks, V2V-Bench Liu et al., 2026f scores what the instruction should change separately from what the source should preserve.

Physical and world consistency. Realistic local motion can still show impossible interactions, unstable objects, or broken persistence. VideoPhy Bansal et al., 2025 and VideoPhy-2 Bansal et al., 2026 test physical commonsense, the latter in harder action-centric interactions. PhysicsMind Mak et al., 2026 and VideoScience-Bench Hu et al., 2026d test mechanics and basic scientific phenomena, and PhyAVBench Xie et al., 2025b tests whether generated sound changes with paired physical conditions. PhyProbe Ku et al., 2026 instead trains a physical-violation scorer on frozen visual features and shows that prior learned evaluators depend on their annotation distributions, though it retains some dataset dependence itself. Persistence under revisits and occlusion is tested by the world-model benchmarks of Section 8.4 .

Human preference, learned evaluators, and suites. Human comparisons capture coherence, visual appeal, and instruction adherence that fixed feature distances miss. Learned evaluators model such judgments: VideoScore He et al., 2024a predicts several human-rated properties, VideoScore2 He et al., 2026c adds physical and commonsense scores with rationales, and MJ-VIDEO Tong et al., 2025 trains a reward model on fine-grained preferences. All three inherit the coverage and biases of their annotations, which for VideoScore2 are partly model-generated. Suites such as VBench Huang et al., 2024a and EvalCrafter Liu et al., 2024c combine perceptual, semantic, and temporal dimensions, and VBench-2.0 Zheng et al., 2025 extends them to human fidelity, controllability, creativity, physics, and commonsense.

Multi-shot and audio-visual generation. Multi-shot and audio-visual outputs also need evaluation across shot boundaries and modalities. MSVBench Shi et al., 2026 scores story alignment, cross-shot consistency, visual quality, and motion, while the Cinematic Narrative Benchmark of MuSS Zhang et al., 2026b separates fidelity to external references from identity persistence across shots and detects reference copying. JavisBench Liu et al., 2026a tests joint generation of scenes with multiple or overlapping sound events, T2AV-Compass Cao et al., 2026 standardizes text-to-audio-video evaluation, and LongAV-Compass Liu et al., 2026g extends it to minute-scale settings where identity, narrative continuity, and synchronization can fail at different times. At the metric level, Fréchet Audio Distance Kilgour et al., 2019 compares audio-feature distributions but ignores the video, LSE-D and LSE-C Prajwal et al., 2020 measure speech–mouth alignment, and JavisScore Liu et al., 2026a measures audio-video correspondence in broader sounding scenes.

#### 6.2 Video Latent Representation Learning

Many current video generators work in a compressed latent space: an encoder maps video to a shorter representation, the generator models its distribution, and a decoder reconstructs the frames. Compression lowers training and sampling cost, especially for long videos, while the bottleneck and decoder decide whether fine texture, fast motion, and small objects survive. Figure 4 contrasts the four families: continuous latents for diffusion and flow objectives, discrete tokens for autoregressive or masked prediction, hybrids that mix continuous and discrete forms or pair a tokenizer with a diffusion decoder, and semantically aligned representations that also serve understanding.

> Figure 4: Latent representations for video generation. Continuous, discrete, hybrid, and semantically aligned designs differ in how tokenizers and VAEs shape reconstruction fidelity, compression, sequence modeling, and unification with understanding. Semantic alignment can be applied to either continuous or discrete representations.


##### 6.2.1 Continuous Latents

Per-frame image VAEs. Latent diffusion Rombach et al., 2022 established the value of training a generative model in the continuous space of a pretrained image autoencoder, and the simplest video extension encodes each frame independently. VideoLDM Blattmann et al., 2023a and AnimateDiff Guo et al., 2024 reuse image autoencoders and add temporal layers to image diffusion backbones. This keeps the image prior and eases reuse of text-to-image backbones. However, the latent sequence still grows linearly with the number of frames, and temporal coherence must be learned by the generator or by temporal layers in the decoder.

Spatiotemporal and Causal VAEs. Video VAEs use 3D or factorized spatiotemporal operations to compress both resolution and time. Temporal compression shortens the sequence, lowers attention cost, and exploits redundancy between nearby frames, but an aggressive bottleneck can smooth brief events or fast motion. CV-VAE Zhao et al., 2024a keeps its video latent space compatible with a pretrained image VAE so that image-generation priors remain usable, whereas CogVideoX Yang et al., 2025g and Cosmos NVIDIA et al., 2025 use dedicated 3D tokenizers for high spatial and temporal compression. A causal 3D VAE computes the latent at time $t$ without future frames and encodes the first frame without preceding context, so one tokenizer handles both images and videos and streaming or autoregressive generators see no future leakage.


##### 6.2.2 Discrete Tokens

From vector quantization to codebook-free schemes. VQ-VAE Oord et al., 2017 replaces each encoder output with the index of a learned codebook entry, producing categorical symbols that next-token or masked-token models can predict. VQGAN Esser et al., 2021 improves high-resolution reconstruction with perceptual and adversarial losses, and RQ-VAE Lee et al., 2022 stacks residual codes to increase precision without enlarging the token grid. Learned codebooks, however, may be used unevenly or collapse to a small active subset. Later quantizers drop the learned codebook: FSQ Mentzer et al., 2024 rounds a few scalar dimensions to fixed levels, LFQ in MAGVIT-v2 Yu et al., 2024 uses binary dimensions to obtain a very large implicit vocabulary, and BSQ Zhao et al., 2025b projects embeddings onto a hypersphere before binary quantization. They differ in optimization stability, vocabulary size, and distortion at a given token budget.

Video tokenization. The video encoder also fixes the spatiotemporal extent of each token and hence the sequence length. C-ViViT in Phenaki Villegas et al., 2023 factorizes spatial and temporal encoding into causal discrete tokens, MAGVIT-v2 pairs a causal tokenizer with LFQ for both autoregressive and masked generation, and HiTVideo Zhou et al., 2026e shortens the sequence with hierarchical codes that place semantics and fine detail in different layers. Fixed grids, however, spend as many tokens on a static shot as on a fast-changing scene. Adaptive tokenizers allocate the budget by content: EVATok Xiong et al., 2026 predicts how many tokens each segment needs, and VideoFlexTok Atanov et al., 2026 emits a variable-length coarse-to-fine sequence whose early tokens carry semantics and motion and whose later tokens add detail.


##### 6.2.3 Hybrid and Semantically Aligned Representations

Hybrid tokenizers combine continuous and discrete forms or change the decoder. OneVAE Zhou et al., 2026d initializes a discrete video VAE from a continuous one and trains both in one network, so continuous latents can feed diffusion or flow models and discrete codes can feed sequence models. Divot Ge et al., 2025 and DiCoDe Li et al., 2024d instead decode with a diffusion model. Divot learns continuous features for LLM-based understanding and reconstruction, and DiCoDe compresses video into deep tokens that an LLM predicts autoregressively.

Semantic alignment adds a further requirement. A reconstruction-optimal latent may keep texture and local motion without organizing them into the objects, events, and relations needed for understanding, while a highly semantic latent may discard the detail that generation needs. VILA-U Wu et al., 2025e trains one discrete tokenizer for both reconstruction and text alignment, and the SEED tokenizer Ge et al., 2024a gives a language model a semantically organized visual code. PyraTok Susladkar et al., 2026 quantizes features at several spatiotemporal scales against one text-guided codebook, so coarse semantics and fine detail occupy different levels. Section 7 pursues this question in models that share LLMs, parameters, or objectives across understanding and generation.

Representation autoencoders start from the semantic side: they freeze a pretrained encoder as the tokenizer and train only a projector or pooler and a decoder. V-RAE Guo et al., 2026 compares image and video encoders such as DINOv3 and V-JEPA 2.1 and finds that reconstruction ranking does not consistently predict generation quality, so a semantic latent must be tested inside the target generator.


#### 6.3 Generative Video Foundation Modeling

Video generators differ in their generative objective, the order in which space-time units are predicted, and the system that organizes synthesis. Diffusion, autoregressive or masked prediction, and flow matching define model-level generation processes (Figure 5 ), whereas LLM/MLLM-assisted and agentic systems add planning, tool use, and revision around a visual generator that may use any of the three. The levels combine in practice: a system may denoise each chunk while rolling out autoregressively in time, and an agent may repeatedly call a flow-based generator. Preference optimization is applied on top of these generators. VideoReward Liu et al., 2025a aligns a flow-based generator with Flow-DPO on a multidimensional preference signal, and LiFT Wang et al., 2024e applies reward-weighted fine-tuning with a reward model learned from human annotations. World-R1 Wang et al., 2026d uses feedback from pretrained 3D and vision-language models to reinforce geometric consistency through Flow-GRPO. ##### 6.3.1 Diffusion-Based Generation

Diffusion models learn to reverse a gradual corruption process: a clean video latent is perturbed according to a noise schedule, and the model learns to predict the noise, a velocity, or an equivalent denoising target at any noise level Ho et al., 2020 ; Rombach et al., 2022 . The $\epsilon$ - and $v$ -prediction objectives and EDM-style schedules are alternative parameterizations of this problem (Section 4.3 ); they affect optimization and sampling, not the video architecture. Flow-matching generators (Section 6.3.3 ) share these backbones, so the backbone and long-video designs below apply to both.

Backbone and attention. Early video diffusion systems, including Video Diffusion Models Ho et al., 2022a , Imagen Video Ho et al., 2022 , and Make-A-Video Singer et al., 2023 , extended convolutional U-Nets with temporal convolutions or factorized spatial and temporal attention. Lumiere Bar-Tal et al., 2024 instead generates the whole clip in one pass with a space-time U-Net that downsamples in both space and time, avoiding keyframe generation followed by temporal super-resolution. Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) Peebles & Xie, 2023 then made it easier to scale attention over compressed space-time tokens. CogVideoX Yang et al., 2025g , Mochi Genmo Team, 2024 , and LTX-Video HaCohen et al., 2024 pair a causal or highly compressed video VAE with a DiT. CogVideoX uses an expert Transformer with separate text and video normalization, Mochi gives the visual stream more capacity in an asymmetric DiT, and LTX-Video compresses aggressively so that full spatiotemporal attention stays affordable. Joint audio-video backbones couple two modality streams through bidirectional cross-modal attention, so timing and semantics are exchanged throughout synthesis. LTX-2 HaCohen et al., 2026 extends LTX-Video with separate modality VAEs and asymmetric audio and video streams, and Ovi Low et al., 2025 couples matched audio and video DiTs trained with flow matching.

The main backbone trade-off concerns attention. Factorized attention separates spatial and temporal interactions to save cost, whereas full spatiotemporal attention lets all space-time tokens interact at a higher memory cost. Sparse and linear attention cut this cost for long or high-resolution clips. HunyuanVideo 1.5 Wu et al., 2025 uses selective and sliding tile attention within a unified T2V and I2V training framework, and SANA-Video 2.0 Chen et al., 2026c interleaves gated linear attention with periodic softmax layers and routes block summaries across depth. Conditions enter the backbone through cross-attention, adaptive normalization such as AdaLN, joint text-video attention, or channel and token concatenation (Section 6.4 ).

Streaming and long videos. Standard diffusion denoises a fixed-length clip as a whole, whereas streaming variants generate causally, chunk by chunk. Diffusion Forcing Chen et al., 2024 gives each frame its own noise level, MAGI-1 Teng et al., 2025 denoises chunks autoregressively, CausVid Yin et al., 2025 distills a bidirectional model into a few-step causal generator, Self-Forcing Huang et al., 2025a trains on the model’s own rollouts, and Causal Forcing Zhu et al., 2026a distills from an autoregressive teacher (Section 8.2.2 , Table 17 ). S2PD Hu et al., 2026b instead changes the sampling order within a clip, generating blocks autoregressively at high noise, then refining the sequence in parallel at low noise.

Long videos raise a different problem: repeated local generation keeps short-term motion but gradually loses appearance and global structure. StreamingT2V Henschel et al., 2025 carries anchor frames and short- and long-term memory across windows, FreeLong Lu et al., 2024 blends low-frequency global features with high-frequency local ones during denoising without training, and FramePack Zhang et al., 2025c compresses past frames by importance to bound the context. Other methods change training or the history representation. LongTake Park et al., 2026 supervises later frames from long real-video prefixes before self-rollout distribution matching, and its evaluation separates sustained motion from appearance quality, since a nearly static continuation can score well on consistency. Test-time training Dalal et al., 2025 stores context in MLP weights that a self-supervised loss updates while the sequence is processed, and has been demonstrated on one-minute, storyboard-conditioned cartoons.


##### 6.3.2 Autoregressive and Masked Token Generation

Autoregressive video models factorize the distribution over an ordered sequence of video units. With discrete tokens, this resembles language modeling: VideoGPT Yan et al., 2021 paired a VQ-VAE with a Transformer prior over video codes, CogVideo Hong et al., 2023 scaled the recipe to text-conditioned generation, and VideoPoet Kondratyuk et al., 2024 applied it in a multimodal sequence model. Loong Wang et al., 2024f places text and video tokens in one sequence and uses short-to-long training and inference-time re-encoding to limit error accumulation in minute-level videos. InfinityStar Liu et al., 2025b extends discrete autoregression to 720p image, video, and long-video generation, reporting about 30 times lower latency than Wan 2.1-14B in its own single-GPU setting. The route offers a standard causal likelihood and flexible length, at the cost of long decoding chains, dependence on tokenizer quality, and errors that propagate through the prefix.

Masked generation relaxes the one-token-at-a-time order: the model predicts sets of masked positions from visible tokens, often refining coarse-to-fine over several rounds. Phenaki Villegas et al., 2023 applies a bidirectional masked Transformer to causal video tokens, MAGVIT-v2 Yu et al., 2024 improves the tokenizer and the masked generation framework, and Lumos-1 Yuan et al., 2026 keeps frames causal while decoding each frame in parallel with mask-based discrete diffusion. Continuous tokens support the same factorization. MAR Li et al., 2024b introduced a diffusion loss for the conditional distribution of a continuous image token, and NOVA Deng et al., 2025a and VideoMAR Yu et al., 2025 carry it to video, predicting frames causally in time and continuous tokens within each frame with a diffusion loss.


##### 6.3.3 Flow-Based Generation

Flow matching Lipman et al., 2023 trains a time-dependent vector field that transports samples from a simple base distribution to the data along a prescribed probability path, and generation integrates the corresponding ordinary differential equation. It differs from the usual diffusion account in its training target and sampling path: rectified-flow formulations favor nearly straight paths, which can reduce the number of solver steps, and the same DiT or multimodal DiT (MMDiT) can parameterize either target. Stable Diffusion 3 Esser et al., 2024 made the rectified-flow and MMDiT combination prominent for images, and video systems adapted it to space-time latents. HunyuanVideo Kong et al., 2024 , Step-Video-T2V Ma et al., 2025 , Goku Chen et al., 2025c , and Wan Wan et al., 2025 scale flow matching with DiT backbones and video VAEs, HunyuanVideo with a dual-stream-to-single-stream Transformer. Pyramid Flow Jin et al., 2025 changes the path itself, linking spatial pyramid stages and compressing full-resolution history with a temporal pyramid so that one DiT is trained end to end across resolutions.

Normalizing flows are a distinct, likelihood-based route that should not be confused with flow matching. STARFlow-V Gu et al., 2026 applies invertible autoregressive transformations to pretrained video-VAE latents, with a global–local decomposition and a light causal denoiser, so the likelihood is tractable in latent space.

##### 6.3.4 LLM/MLLM-Assisted Generation

LLM/MLLM-assisted generation covers systems in which a language model plans, expands, captions, or structures the conditions for a separate visual sampler. A language model that predicts video tokens directly belongs to autoregressive generation instead. In the common arrangement (Figure 6 ), the language model turns an underspecified request into scenes, shots, layouts, or refined prompts that a diffusion, flow, or autoregressive generator consumes. VideoDirectorGPT Lin et al., 2024a produces a grounded video plan before layout-conditioned generation, and LLM-grounded Video Diffusion Lian et al., 2024 and Dysen-VDM Fei et al., 2024 draft dynamic scene layouts or scene graphs that guide the denoiser, so the language model handles composition and timing while the video model handles appearance and motion. Any2Caption Wu et al., 2025b interprets text, image, video, region, motion, and camera cues and translates them into dense structured captions for a downstream generator.

The language model and the generator can also be coupled more tightly than through a written prompt. MetaCanvas Lin et al., 2025 lets an MLLM plan in spatial or spatiotemporal latent spaces connected to diffusion backbones, including image-to-video and editing settings. TV2TV Han et al., 2026 couples the two more tightly, interleaving language next-token prediction with video flow matching in one framework.


##### 6.3.5 Agentic Generation

Agentic generation turns planning into an executable loop: a controller decomposes a request, calls specialized models or tools, inspects intermediate results, and revises the plan or conditions when the output falls short (Figure 6 ). The recurring stages are scripting, storyboarding, visual generation, evaluation, and revision.

Current agents mainly decompose long-form creation or coordinate tools. MovieAgent Wu et al., 2025d assigns screenplay, directing, storyboard, and scene construction to cooperating agents, and Anim-Director Li et al., 2024e has an MLLM write a storyline and script, produce consistent scene references, and select outputs from generative tools. Mora Yuan et al., 2024a routes among generation and editing skills in a multi-agent workflow.

##### 6.4.1 Attention-Based Control

Attention-based methods reuse the correspondences that a text-conditioned generator already forms: cross-attention links prompt tokens to spatial features, and self-attention relates visual features to one another. Prompt-to-Prompt Hertz et al., 2023 injects cross-attention maps from a source prompt into the denoising run for an edited prompt, keeping the layout while changing a word, an attribute, or a concept’s strength.

Video editing also needs the change to stay stable across frames. Video-P2P Liu et al., 2024a and FateZero Qi et al., 2023 combine attention control with inversion of the source video. Video-P2P adds separate guidance for source and target prompts, and FateZero fuses source and target cross- and self-attention maps recorded during inversion. TokenFlow Geyer et al., 2024 instead propagates diffusion features between frames through feature-space correspondences, so an image editor produces temporally consistent edits without training.


##### 6.4.2 Adapter-Based Control

Adapters add a trainable path for external structure while keeping most or all of the pretrained generator frozen. ControlNet Zhang et al., 2023a copies the encoder blocks of a frozen image diffusion model into a conditional branch joined by zero-initialized convolutions, whereas T2I-Adapter Mou et al., 2024 adds features from a small condition encoder at several resolutions. They represent the two main variants: a heavy side network that mirrors the backbone and a light adapter that produces residual features.

Video adapters must also encode how a condition changes over time. MotionCtrl Wang et al., 2024i represents camera motion with poses and object motion with trajectories so that the two can be composed. VideoControlNet Hu & Xu, 2023 brings ControlNet to video-to-video translation with motion guidance that keeps the injected structure aligned across frames. Unified adapters train several controls together. VACE Jiang et al., 2025 encodes reference frames, source video, and masks in a Video Condition Unit and injects them into a frozen DiT through a context adapter, supporting reference generation, editing, and masked editing with shared adapter parameters.


##### 6.4.3 Latent-Space Control

Latent-space methods act on the noisy sample or the denoising trajectory itself. Partial noising, as in SDEdit Meng et al., 2022 , perturbs an input to an intermediate noise level and denoises it under a new condition, so the noise level trades fidelity against edit strength. Inversion maps an observed video to a noise trajectory from which the generator approximately reconstructs it, often through deterministic DDIM sampling Song et al., 2021 ; editing then changes the condition or selected latents along the reverse path.

A method may also construct latent conditions directly. Wan-Move Chu et al., 2025 projects dense point trajectories into latent space and propagates first-frame features along them, giving an image-to-video model a motion condition without a separate motion encoder. VLIPP Yang et al., 2025d has a vision-language model plan coarse object motion, renders the plan as a synthetic motion video, and converts its optical flow into structured noise that initializes a motion-controllable image-to-video model (Section 8.2.4 ).


##### 6.4.4 Concatenation and In-Context Conditioning

The most direct route places the condition in the model input. Channel concatenation appends aligned condition latents to the noisy video latent, as in the image-to-video variants of Stable Video Diffusion Blattmann et al., 2023 and CogVideoX Yang et al., 2025g and in VideoComposer Wang et al., 2023a , which fuses spatial and temporal conditions before concatenation. Token concatenation instead appends condition embeddings to the sequence processed by self- or joint attention.

FullDiT Ju et al., 2025 maps heterogeneous conditions into one sequence and models them with full attention, avoiding separately trained side branches. In-context generation places reference examples or source–target pairs in the sequence so that the model infers the requested transformation, as Emu2 Sun et al., 2024 showed for multimodal image generation and UNIC Ye et al., 2026a applies to unified video editing. Retrieval can supply further condition tokens: PhysRAG Cheng et al., 2026a retrieves videos of relevant physical phenomena, extracts physical information, and injects it into a video diffusion model through learnable queries.


##### 6.4.5 Guidance-Based Control

Guidance changes the sampling direction instead of adding a persistent condition branch. Classifier-free guidance (CFG) Ho & Salimans, 2022 combines conditional and unconditional predictions at each denoising step,

where $z_{k}$ is the noisy latent at denoising step $k$ , $\varnothing$ denotes the null condition, and $\gamma$ is the guidance scale; $\gamma=1$ recovers the conditional prediction. CFG needs a model trained with condition dropout, and raising $\gamma$ usually strengthens alignment but reduces diversity.

More general guidance uses the gradient of an energy, attention, correspondence, or physical objective to modify each update and can impose constraints absent from the training data. Universal guidance Bansal et al., 2024 and training-free energy guidance Yu et al., 2023 use external objectives, whereas diffusion self-guidance Epstein et al., 2023 defines them on the generator’s internal attention and feature maps.


##### 6.4.6 Tuning-Based Control

Tuning stores a new subject, style, or source-video prior in learned embeddings or weights. For images, Textual Inversion Gal et al., 2023 learns new text embeddings for a user concept, DreamBooth Ruiz et al., 2023 fine-tunes the model on a few subject images with a prior-preservation loss, and LoRA Hu et al., 2022 learns low-rank weight updates while keeping the base weights fixed. Video adds the requirement that the learned identity stay stable under motion and viewpoint change.

Tune-A-Video Wu et al., 2023 and LoRA-Edit Gao et al., 2026 tune on each source video. Tune-A-Video adapts an image diffusion model from one text–video pair with spatiotemporal attention and DDIM inversion, and LoRA-Edit applies mask-aware LoRA tuning to an image-to-video model, taking structure and motion from the source and edited appearance from reference images. Video2LoRA Wu et al., 2026d avoids per-reference optimization: a hypernetwork predicts reference-specific LoRA weights in one forward pass.


#### 6.5 Applications

Generated video serves as content, as an avatar, as a visual plan, or as training data, and each use places different demands on conditioning, latency, consistency, and evaluation. Systems that also model action-conditioned transitions are treated in Section 8 .


##### 6.5.1 Creative Content Production

Video generation enters production at several stages, from concept shots and storyboards to narrative footage, advertising and educational variants, and visual-effects edits. The agentic systems of Section 6.3.5 handle planning and tool orchestration, while foundation generators increasingly bundle creation and revision operations. Following Sora Brooks et al., 2024 , Movie Gen Polyak et al., 2024 combines text-to-video generation with personalization, instruction-based editing, and synchronized audio. Closed systems such as Kling Kuaishou Technology, 2024 and Sora 2 OpenAI, 2025 offer text- and image-to-video generation as products, and Sora 2 adds synchronized audio. Open models such as VideoCrafter2 Chen et al., 2024a , Open-Sora Zheng et al., 2024 , Wan Wan et al., 2025 , HunyuanVideo Kong et al., 2024 , and Step-Video-T2V Ma et al., 2025 make text-to-video generation available through released weights. Seedance 1.0 Gao et al., 2025a adds native multi-shot generation with consistent subjects, and Seedance 2.0 Team Seedance et al., 2026 accepts text, image, audio, and video inputs for joint audio-video generation, reference-based generation, and editing.

Production also needs what per-clip realism does not measure: keeping a character, location, or style across shots, revising one element without disturbing the rest, and controlling camera and motion. These needs tie personalization and control (Section 6.4 ) to cross-shot and preference evaluation (Section 6.1.2 ).


##### 6.5.2 Avatars and Character Animation

Avatar generation maps speech, motion, or other control signals to the visible behavior of a person or character. Its lineage runs through the audio-driven systems of Section 6.1 : Wav2Lip Prajwal et al., 2020 edits the mouth region with a lip-sync expert, and SadTalker Zhang et al., 2023b predicts expression and head-pose coefficients of a 3D face model. Diffusion models such as EMO Tian et al., 2024 and Hallo Xu et al., 2024 generate expressive portrait motion directly from audio, and VLOGGER Corona et al., 2025 extends the output to the upper body with hand and gesture motion from a single reference image. Pose-driven animation, as in Animate Anyone Hu, 2024 and MagicAnimate Xu et al., 2024a , replaces audio with a motion sequence and is judged against that motion instead of speech. In both settings, identity, clothing, background, and non-speech motion must stay stable over long or varied inputs.

Real-time avatars must also produce frames at a sustained rate with bounded delay while staying synchronized with incoming audio or motion. MuseTalk Zhang et al., 2024a dubs video in latent space and reports 30 frames per second at $256\times 256$ resolution on a V100 GPU. Vidu S1 Zhang et al., 2026f lets voice instructions redirect an ongoing character stream, and Vidu S2 Zhang et al., 2026e adds references that can be updated during streaming and frame-aligned editing of an incoming video stream. Mid-stream control adds action-to-output latency to throughput as a criterion and links avatars to the real-time interactive world models of Section 8.2.3 .


##### 6.5.3 Video Plans for Embodied Agents

Generated video can serve as a plan between a task specification and physical execution, and methods differ mainly in how they recover actions from it. UniPi Du et al., 2023 generates a future video from a text task and extracts controls with an inverse dynamics model, using images as a planning space shared by embodiments with different action spaces. AVDC Ko et al., 2024 infers actions from dense correspondences between generated frames, so policies can be learned from videos without action labels. RoboDreamer Zhou et al., 2024 composes video predictions of reusable instruction primitives to generalize to new object–action combinations, and Dreamitate Liang et al., 2025 fine-tunes a video model on human demonstrations and tracks the generated tool motion to control a robot. A generated plan stays fixed, whereas an interactive simulator such as UniSim Yang et al., 2024 updates its predictions as actions arrive (Section 8 ).


##### 6.5.4 Synthetic Data for Embodied AI and Driving

Embodied and driving systems need observations of rare tasks, environments, and failures that are costly or unsafe to collect. Video generation can supply them offline by varying appearance, layout, traffic, camera pose, or actions while keeping the annotations carried by the condition. In driving, MagicDrive Gao et al., 2024 conditions multi-camera street views on camera poses, road maps, 3D bounding boxes, and text with cross-view attention, and Panacea Wen et al., 2024 generates annotated multi-view videos from bird’s-eye-view layouts. DriveDreamer Wang et al., 2024 and GAIA-1 Hu et al., 2023 go further and predict future driving observations under scene or action conditions (Section 8 ). In robotics, the data must also carry actions: DreamGen Jang et al., 2025 adapts an image-to-video model to a target embodiment and labels its generated trajectories with a latent-action or inverse-dynamics model. GigaWorld-0 Team et al., 2025 pairs a controllable video generator with 3D generation and system identification to train vision-language-action policies (Section 8 ). Generated data are judged by downstream perception and policy results on real observations, not by visual fidelity.


### 7 Unified Video Models: Sharing the World

Video understanding and generation have traditionally relied on separate encoders and generators. Unified video models implement the sharing relation $V\leftrightarrow W$ of Section 3 : inferred content can condition generation, and generative structure can support understanding and reasoning.

Definition evaluates sharing at three levels: capability coverage (one system performs both directions), architectural sharing (the directions use common components), and functional synergy (one direction improves the other under a controlled comparison). We cover systems with video input or output that link understanding and generation, and cite image-only work where it supplies a design or evaluation protocol missing for video.

##### 7.1.1 Discrete-Token Unification

Discrete-token models convert text, images, and video into symbols that are interleaved in one sequence. A single Transformer then serves both directions with next-token prediction: answering predicts text tokens after video context, and generation predicts visual tokens after a text or visual condition. Emu3 Wang et al., 2024b follows this design without diffusion heads, covering image and video understanding and generation, and Emu3.5 Cui et al., 2025 scales the same objective to video-heavy data and reports world-modeling behavior such as spatiotemporally consistent exploration. Video-LaVIT Jin et al., 2024a cuts the token cost of video by tokenizing keyframes and motion separately, and VILA-U Wu et al., 2025e trains its vision tower to align discrete tokens with language while keeping the detail needed for generation. SEED-LLaMA Ge et al., 2024a pairs discrete visual tokens with a diffusion image decoder; it answers video questions but generates only images. Two variants change the role of the tokens or the objective: Vega Wang et al., 2026e predicts semantic keyframe tokens autoregressively and leaves appearance to a diffusion renderer, and NExT-OMNI Luo et al., 2026d replaces autoregression with discrete flow matching over text, image, video, and audio tokens.

This route gives many input–output combinations one vocabulary, one sequence format, and one likelihood objective. However, the tokenizer becomes the bottleneck: codes must preserve appearance and motion without making sequences unmanageably long, and quantization discards information before the Transformer sees it. Long autoregressive outputs also accumulate errors through their generated prefix.


##### 7.1.2 Continuous Latent-Space Unification

Continuous latent-space models avoid quantization: an encoder maps images or video into a continuous feature space that an understanding head reads and a decoder, diffusion model, or flow head turns back into pixels. The latent space is shared, but the losses and output heads can differ, because language prediction and pixel generation have different numerical requirements. TUNA Liu et al., 2026k cascades a VAE encoder with a representation encoder, and Show-o2 Xie et al., 2025a replaces the discrete vocabulary of Show-o Xie et al., 2025 with a 3D causal VAE; both predict text autoregressively and generate images and video with flow matching. Uni-ViGU Qin et al., 2026 starts from the other end, extending a video diffusion generator with discrete flow matching for text and lightweight understanding layers. AToken Lu et al., 2026 and HYDRA-X Zhang et al., 2026a make the tokenizer itself the shared component, training it with reconstruction and semantic objectives over images, video, and, for AToken, 3D. PixelUMM Wei et al., 2026a drops the pretrained encoder and VAE: pixel patches and video tubelets enter a mixture-of-transformers in which the two directions keep separate projections and feed-forward layers but share attention, supporting video question answering, generation, and editing.

Continuous spaces fit diffusion and flow generators naturally, but they expose a tension that recurs across unified models. Understanding benefits from invariance to texture and small appearance changes, whereas generation must retain enough detail to reconstruct them.


##### 7.1.3 Decoupled and Modular Unification

Decoupled systems sidestep this tension by keeping separate visual pathways for the two directions, linked through an LLM, a projection layer, or a learned adapter. The understanding encoder can drop nuisance detail and emphasize objects, relations, actions, or text, while a separate tokenizer or decoder keeps the fine spatial and temporal detail needed to generate video. NExT-GPT Wu et al., 2024c and GPT4Video Wang et al., 2024h set the modular baseline around an instruction-following LLM and frozen pretrained encoders and generators, so only a small part of the system is trained: NExT-GPT links the LLM to diffusion decoders through lightweight projections, whereas GPT4Video has the LLM write textual prompts for an off-the-shelf text-to-video model. Later systems train the bridge. Omni-Video Tan et al., 2025 , UniVid Luo et al., 2025a , and UniVideo Wei et al., 2026 let an MLLM interpret the instruction and pass continuous features through an adapter or bridge to a diffusion decoder; UniVid adds a reflection mechanism for temporal reasoning. Omni-Video 2 Yang et al., 2026 keeps the MLLM frozen and conditions the decoder on a predicted target caption and source-video latents for editing, and ReViSE Liu et al., 2025d trains an Omni-Video model with feedback from its own VLM (Section 7.2.4 ).

Other systems redesign the bridge itself. Lumos-Nexus Xing et al., 2026 aligns a small generator with the understanding model and mixes its velocity predictions with those of a larger pretrained generator at inference. MoRoute Gao et al., 2026a learns which layer of a frozen VLM conditions each diffusion block, and Mamoda2.5 Shi et al., 2026c feeds semantic features to a sparse-expert diffusion backbone through learned queries. Lance Fu et al., 2026a replaces the bridge with shared attention: separate semantic and VAE encoders feed dual experts that attend over one interleaved sequence. JavisGPT Liu et al., 2025c extends the encoder–LLM–decoder pattern to sounding video, passing synchrony-aware queries to a joint audio–video generator.

Decoupling keeps strong pretrained components and makes each one replaceable. However, the bridge can become an information bottleneck: a semantic clue sufficient for following an instruction may omit the appearance, geometry, or motion that the decoder needs.


##### 7.1.4 Agentic and Tool-Augmented Unification

Agentic systems leave perception, generation, editing, and evaluation to specialized models. A controller reads the request and media, plans operations, calls tools, inspects their outputs, and revises the plan; what is shared is a task state such as a script, shot plan, prompt, edit graph, or critique. VideoAgent (editing) Zhou et al., 2026a plans shots and retrieves source clips before calling a library of editing agents, and VISTA Long et al., 2026 wraps a video generator in a loop of temporal planning, candidate selection, multimodal critique, and prompt rewriting. UniVA Liang et al., 2025a plans and calls understanding, generation, editing, and segmentation tools that share global, task, and user memories.

Agents can combine strong tools without forcing their internal representations to agree, and each tool can be swapped as better models appear. However, they depend on tool selection, intermediate formats, and evaluator reliability: one wrong caption, retrieval result, or critique can redirect the whole workflow.

#### 7.2 Learning Paradigms

Architecture fixes where understanding and generation meet; training determines what signal flows between them (Figure 9 ). Isolated learning connects independently trained experts, dual learning relates the two directions through inverse or cycle objectives, co-learning optimizes both over shared parameters or representations, and synergistic learning uses one direction to improve the other. The paradigms can coexist; for example, a cycle objective can be part of joint training.


##### 7.2.1 Isolated Learning

Isolated learning trains understanding and generation as independent experts and connects them after pretraining through a small trained module, while most expert parameters stay frozen. NExT-GPT Wu et al., 2024c and GPT4Video Wang et al., 2024h follow this recipe (Section 7.1 ), as GILL Koh et al., 2023 did for a frozen language model and a frozen image generator. Isolated learning is cheap and modular, and the source of each capability stays easy to identify. However, generation losses never update the video encoder and understanding losses never update the decoder, so no cross-direction transfer can occur, which makes isolated systems the natural baseline for measuring what tighter coupling adds.


##### 7.2.2 Dual Learning

Dual learning treats understanding and generation as approximately inverse problems: a model reconstructs a condition from generated content, regenerates an observation from an inferred representation, or enforces agreement over a round trip. Such objectives let one direction supervise the other when paired labels are scarce. As many videos fit one description and one condition allows many valid futures, a video cycle must preserve task-relevant events, identities, viewpoints, and motion while tolerating appearance variation.

Uni-ViGU Qin et al., 2026 is the clearest video-native example. Starting from a video generator, it recovers understanding as the inverse task: it first reconstructs prompts to recall learned text–video associations and then trains on detailed captions, keeping the original generation path. Dedicated cycle objectives remain image-based: CyCLeGen Shan et al., 2026 couples image–layout cycles, UniCorn Fang et al., 2026a distills text–image–text self-play among proposer, solver, and judge roles, and Reconstruction Alignment Xie et al., 2026 trains a model to reconstruct an image from its own understanding embeddings.


##### 7.2.3 Co-learning

Co-learning optimizes understanding and generation objectives in one shared model or a jointly trained bridge, and each of the first three families of Section 7.1 supports it. Discrete-token models such as Emu3 Wang et al., 2024b , Video-LaVIT Jin et al., 2024a , and VILA-U Wu et al., 2025e apply one next-token objective to both directions, while Vega Wang et al., 2026e shapes a shared semantic token space and keeps a separate diffusion renderer. Continuous and hybrid models share a visual space or Transformer but keep distinct losses, as in TUNA Liu et al., 2026k , Show-o2 Xie et al., 2025a , HYDRA-X Zhang et al., 2026a , PixelUMM Wei et al., 2026a , and the single-Transformer HaploOmni Xiao et al., 2025 . BAGEL Deng et al., 2025 co-trains a mixture of transformer experts that share attention but keep direction-specific projections and feed-forward layers, a design Lance applies to video (Section 7.1 ). Bridge models such as Omni-Video Tan et al., 2025 , UniVid Luo et al., 2025a , and UniVideo Wei et al., 2026 co-train the adapter between an MLLM and a video decoder.

Joint training creates an optimization imbalance between the two objectives. Understanding data rewards semantic invariance and concise answers, whereas generation data rewards retaining appearance and motion detail. Differences in dataset scale, loss magnitude, and convergence speed can let one direction dominate the shared parameters, so the data mixture and schedule decide how strongly each capability updates the model. Lance Fu et al., 2026a counters this interference with staged multitask training and adaptive data scheduling. Tong et al. Tong et al., 2026a study related choices when pretraining a Transfusion-style backbone from scratch, comparing representation autoencoders, data mixtures, and dense or sparse-expert architectures with compute-matched image–text analyses.


##### 7.2.4 Synergistic Learning

Synergistic learning uses one direction to improve the other; its evidence is a measured gain in the receiving direction. From understanding to generation, OmniWeaving Pan et al., 2026 conditions a video generator on an MLLM that can first write a reasoning trace about interleaved text, image, and video inputs; after end-to-end training, enabling this thinking mode raises its scores on intent-driven and interpolative tasks of IntelligentVBench. ReViSE Liu et al., 2025d turns understanding into a training signal for editing: the frozen VLM inside an Omni-Video model scores a one-step estimate of the edited video, and its answer-token probabilities serve as a differentiable reward alongside flow matching. Ablations against supervised fine-tuning on RVE-Bench support this understanding-to-editing transfer.
