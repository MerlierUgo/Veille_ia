## Lazy Agents Answer Against Their Own Beliefs: Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Beliefs Teaches Them to Act on What They Believe

AI agents working on long tasks often stop too soon and commit to an answer before they have enough evidence, a behavior often called laziness. In our evaluations, they frequently commit even while their stated belief still names several possible answers. At every turn, the agent decides whether to take a further step or to commit to an answer. An outcome reward at the end of the episode scores only the final answer. It does not show which turns helped, and it cannot teach the agent when to commit, because a lucky guess receives the same reward as an answer backed by evidence. During training, however, the simulator knows the rules linking the hidden answer to the evidence, so it can compute the exact posterior over the answer at every step and tell whether the evidence is already enough. We propose Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Beliefs ( RLVB ) , which takes this posterior as the belief supported by the evidence. RLVB rewards the agent for taking further steps while this belief still allows several answers and for committing once the evidence is sufficient. The reward depends only on the evidence the agent has gathered and needs no judge, critic or reward model. It never reads the belief line the agent writes before each action, although the update trains this line together with the action. We also introduce Pro-Worlds, a benchmark of visual puzzles where the agent views a large world through a small window and must decide when it has enough visual evidence to commit. We evaluate three vision-language models on three benchmarks, two of which are never seen in training. RLVB improves the base model in all nine settings, by 17.1% on average and by 10.4% with expectation maximization (EM) as the learner. Under the outcome reward, GRPO and PPO fall below the base model on average. RLVB also outperforms PPO with a process reward from an LLM judge by 9.3% on average while using 1.6 times fewer GPU-hours. To find where this gain comes from, we group the answers by the evidence the agent has when it answers. The trained agent answers with sufficient evidence in 51%, 12% and 74% of the episodes on the three benchmarks, against 8%, 0.4% and 36% for the base model. For every method, 66 to 92% of the answers given with sufficient evidence are correct. The accuracy gain of the trained agent therefore comes from answering more often at high levels of evidence, while at the same level of evidence its answers are no more accurate than those of the base model. The belief line shows how the trained agent reaches this evidence: it acts on its own belief. It commits at only 0.1 to 2.2% of the turns at which the evidence is insufficient and its line still names several answers, against 3 to 68% for the base model with the same line. Over all turns, its line is not consistently more exact than that of the base model, and each of its steps adds about as much evidence as a step of the base model. Because the trained agent acts on its belief, we expect better reading of the evidence to translate into better answers.


### 1 Introduction

Agents that work over long horizons are now used in games, embodied settings, and other interactive environments ( Yao et al., 2023 ; Yang et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2025a ; Wang et al., 2025b ) . Such an agent must keep working until it has gathered sufficient evidence to act ( Bajcsy, 1988 ; Das et al., 2018 ) . However, current agents often stop too early ( Laban et al., 2026 ; Liu et al., 2026 ; Cuadron et al., 2025 ) and answer with a guess. This behavior is called laziness ( Ma and Liu, 2025 ) . In the cups task in figure 1 , the agent lifts one cup at a time to find a hidden object. After two lifts, the evidence is not yet sufficient because the object could still be under either of two cups. The next action should therefore lift another cup instead of guessing. In our experiments, the base models often guess even after they write that several cups remain possible.

At every turn, an evidence-seeking agent should keep a belief that matches the evidence. It should take steps aimed at what this belief leaves open. It should commit only when the belief identifies the answer. Knowing how much one knows and acting on it is called metacognition ( Flavell, 1979 ) . Metacognition has two parts: monitoring what one knows and controlling what one does with it ( Nelson and Narens, 1990 ) . Language models can often recognize what they know ( Kadavath et al., 2022 ) . However, the base models in our evaluations commit too early, even though our prompts tell them to commit only once the evidence identifies the answer. When they write a belief line before each action, they commit at 3 to 68% of the turns at which the evidence is insufficient and this belief line still names several answers. They often state that the answer remains open and commit anyway. These models do not act on what their own belief line states. A check in the harness that rejects the first answer does not solve this problem, because the harness cannot read the observations, as section 4.6 shows. Whether the evidence determines the answer depends on what the agent has observed. Only the agent reads these observations. The agent’s own belief can therefore guide each decision once the agent learns to act on it. We call an agent reliable when its answers are both correct and backed by sufficient evidence, because a user who receives an answer usually cannot check how the agent reached it. Training an agent to make this decision reliably over a long horizon is a central problem in agentic reinforcement learning (RL).

RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR) is a common way to train large language models on math ( Shao et al., 2024 ; Zeng et al., 2025 ; Liu et al., 2025 ; Yu et al., 2025 ) and code ( Le et al., 2022 ; Gehring et al., 2025 ; Wei et al., 2025 ) tasks. A verifier checks the final answer and rewards the model when the answer is correct ( Lambert et al., 2025 ; Guo et al., 2025 ; Kimi Team, 2025 ) . Recent work trains multi-turn agents with the same kind of framework ( Zhou et al., 2024 ; Bai et al., 2024 ; Jin et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2025b ; Wang et al., 2025a ; Feng et al., 2025 ) . Over a long horizon, however, a reward on the final answer cannot tell which steps helped and which steps caused a failure ( Minsky, 1961 ; Sutton and Barto, 2018 ) . A lucky guess is reinforced as fully as an answer supported by the evidence. A careful search is penalized whenever the episode is lost. Agents also tend to answer before the evidence supports the final answer ( Laban et al., 2026 ; Liu et al., 2026 ) or fall back on fixed patterns that ignore the evidence ( Wang et al., 2025b ; Wang et al., 2026b ) . As table 2 shows, the base models on HiddenGames give 64 to 97% of their answers before their evidence is sufficient, whether or not they write a belief line. RL with the outcome reward even lowers the accuracy of these models on average.

To credit single steps, recent work returns to the PPO framework ( Schulman et al., 2017 ) and rewards every step with a process reward ( Uesato et al., 2022 ; Lightman et al., 2024 ) . This reward is often given by a process reward model or an LLM judge ( Cui et al., 2026 ; Khalifa et al., 2026 ) . Yet the value of a step is hard to estimate, because the critic of PPO is fitted to outcomes that depend on the hidden target. As figure 2 (b) shows, an LLM judge that sees the target may reward a lucky guess and penalize a correct but still uncertain belief. The critic and the judge also make training more expensive, because the critic is a second model trained next to the policy and the judge is called at every step.

Agentic RL usually trains in a sandbox simulator ( Wang et al., 2025b ; Wang et al., 2025a ; Feng et al., 2025 ) . This simulator knows much more than whether the final answer is correct. It knows the hidden target and what each action will reveal about it. Prior work passes this privileged information to a teacher or a critic ( Pinto et al., 2018 ; Chen et al., 2019 ; Choudhury and Sodhi, 2025 ) . In tasks where the agent gathers evidence, this information lets the simulator compute the exact posterior over the hidden answer after every step. This posterior is the belief state ( Åström, 1965 ; Kaelbling et al., 1998 ) . The final answer is then no longer the only thing the simulator can verify. The posterior is the belief supported by the evidence. The simulator can verify it at every step, just as RLVR verifies the answer at the end. This belief shows whether the agent should take further steps or answer. This moves us from reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards to Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Beliefs ( RLVB ) .

At each turn, we give every method its full history of actions and observations. As a result, no method has to remember what it has explored. From the exact posterior, the agent learns when to commit and when to keep working. The reward also scores which step the agent chooses next, but the trained agent does not choose more informative steps than the base model, as section 4.6 shows. Before each action, the agent states the answers it still sees as possible, such as Possible: cups 2, 4 . This belief line shows whether the agent thinks it has sufficient evidence. The reward never reads this line. Because the update raises the likelihood of the whole turn, it still trains the line along with the action. As section 4.6 shows, the trained agent then learns to act on the line. The turn’s reward compares the agent’s next action to the action a teacher chooses by looking ahead on the exact posterior ( Howard, 1966 ) . The reward can also score the stated belief against the posterior, but scoring the action is enough, as section 4.4 shows. The reward never pays for a guess. It depends on the evidence the agent has gathered. It does not depend on which answer happens to be correct in the episode. In figure 1 , cups 2 and 4 are still possible. Lifting another cup gets a reward close to zero. Answering cup 4 gets about $-7$ , whether or not the object is under cup 4. At each history, the agent samples several of its own turns and makes the higher-reward turns more likely. The learner uses either a policy gradient that compares turns within a group, as in GRPO ( Shao et al., 2024 ) , or expectation maximization (EM) ( Dempster et al., 1977 ) . RLVB needs no judge, critic or reward model because the posterior is exact.

Our contributions are:

- • RLVB . We use the simulator’s privileged information to compute the exact posterior after every step. We turn this posterior into a process reward. This reward scores each action against a teacher acting on the exact posterior. The reward never reads the agent’s belief line, but the update still trains the line along with the action. After training, the agent rarely commits while it has insufficient evidence and its belief line still names several answers. The policy learns from this reward using policy gradient or EM. It needs no judge, critic or reward model.

- • Experimental results. On three vision-language models and three benchmarks, RLVB improves the base model in all nine settings, by 17.1% on average and by 10.4% with EM as the learner. It outperforms SFT and RL with the outcome reward in every setting. It also outperforms PPO with a process reward from an LLM judge on average and uses 1.6 times fewer GPU-hours, or 4 times fewer with EM. Along with this gain, the share of answers given too early falls from 76–87% to 44–46%.

- • Why the training helps. RLVB answers with sufficient evidence in 51%, 12% and 74% of episodes on the three benchmarks. The base model does so in 8%, 0.4% and 36% of episodes. Using a belief line and the exact posterior, we find that the trained agent acts on its own belief. It commits at only 0.1 to 2.2% of the turns with insufficient evidence at which its belief line still names several answers. The base model with the same line commits on 3 to 68% of those turns. When it commits, the trained agent gives the answer its line names, just like the base model. Across all turns, its belief line is not consistently more exact than the base model’s. The single answers it names are often wrong. Its steps add roughly as much evidence as the base model’s. In metacognitive terms, training mainly links decision control to the agent’s own monitoring. Monitoring improves only at the states that both agents reach, where the line names a single answer too early less often. We expect a better reading of the evidence to lead to better answers.

- • Pro-Worlds Benchmark. We introduce Pro-Worlds, a new benchmark of visual puzzles. The agent sees a large world only through a small window. It moves this window and must decide when it has seen enough to answer. Pro-Worlds tests this decision in visual worlds never seen in training.


### 2 Related Work


##### RL for language and vision-language agents.

RL with outcome rewards has improved the reasoning of language models ( Guo et al., 2025 ; Kimi Team, 2025 ; Shao et al., 2024 ; Yu et al., 2025 ) . Self-training methods follow a similar idea, as they sample the model’s own rationales and keep those that reach a correct answer ( Zelikman et al., 2022 ; Singh et al., 2024 ) . For multi-turn agents, RAGEN ( Wang et al., 2025b ) finds that sparse and delayed rewards fail to reinforce the reasoning of each step. GiGPO ( Feng et al., 2025 ) obtains a finer credit by grouping the steps that share a state. VAGEN ( Wang et al., 2025a ) adds a turn-level reward for the agent’s estimate of the world state. These rewards still come from the outcome or from a learned estimate, while RLVB computes its rewards from the exact posterior.


##### Process rewards.

Process reward models score each step of a solution instead of only the final answer ( Lightman et al., 2024 ) . Most are trained on human or Monte Carlo labels, and their quality depends on how these labels are made ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) . Others learn from outcomes alone ( Cui et al., 2026 ) , reason before they judge ( Khalifa et al., 2026 ) , or derive the step reward from a regularized RL objective ( Yao et al., 2026 ) . Closer to our setting, PRInTS ( Lee et al., 2026 ) learns a reward model for long-horizon information seeking. Our process reward is computed exactly by the environment, and it needs no reward model, no judge and no step labels.


##### Belief states and information gathering.

The belief state is the posterior over the hidden state. It is a sufficient statistic for acting under partial observability ( Åström, 1965 ; Kaelbling et al., 1998 ) . The value of an evidence-gathering action is the information gained from it ( Howard, 1966 ; Lindley, 1956 ) . BED-LLM ( Choudhury et al., 2026 ) asks the questions with the highest expected information gain at test time. Bayesian teaching ( Qiu et al., 2026 ) fine-tunes a model to imitate a Bayesian assistant. CURIO ( Wan et al., 2025 ) and ECHO ( Nath et al., 2026 ) reward each turn in a multi-turn interaction by how much it improves a belief over a hidden variable. Unlike these methods, RLVB uses a lookahead on the exact posterior to value the decision to stop. It also compares several turns at the same history. Agent-BRACE ( Singh et al., 2026 ) is closest to our work. It trains an agent in text environments to state its belief as claims about the environment, each with a certainty word. Its policy acts on this belief instead of the history. An LLM judge grades these claims against the true hidden state rather than the posterior. When two answers are equally likely, the judge rewards a claim that names only the true answer, just as an outcome reward rewards a lucky guess. In RLVB , the agent is visual and states the set of answers it still sees as possible. The exact posterior scores its action and can also score this belief. As a result, the reward is verifiable and needs no judge.

#### 3.1 Problem formulation


##### Exact posterior.

A simulator knows more than the agent trained in it. Prior work passes this privileged information to a teacher or a critic as the true state ( Pinto et al., 2018 ; Chen et al., 2019 ; Choudhury and Sodhi, 2025 ) , while we use it to compute the exact posterior over what the agent cannot see. Each task has a hidden target $\theta\in\Theta$ drawn from a prior $p_{0}$ , a correct answer $y^{*}(\theta)\in\mathcal{Y}$ , and evidence actions $a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}$ with observations $o_{t}\sim p(\cdot\mid\theta,a_{1:t})$ . In the cups task of figure 2 , $\theta$ is the cup hiding the red cylinder, an evidence action lifts a cup, and an answer names a cup. The simulators of our tasks in section 4.1 enumerate $\Theta$ , and before every step $t$ they compute the exact posterior

[Tableau] $$b_{t}(\theta)\propto p_{0}(\theta)\textstyle\prod_{s<t}p(o_{s}\mid\theta,a_{1:s}).$$ (1)

Since all our tasks have deterministic observations, $b_{t}$ is $p_{0}$ restricted to the targets consistent with every observation. We write $b_{t}(y)$ for the posterior probability that $y$ is correct, $b_{t}(Y)$ for the mass of a set $Y\subseteq\mathcal{Y}$ , and $B^{*}_{t}=\{y\in\mathcal{Y}:b_{t}(y)>0\}$ for the answers still allowed by the evidence. We drop the index $t$ when the history is clear. In figure 2 (c), lifting cups 1 and 3 leaves $B^{*}_{3}=\{2,4\}$ .


##### Agent.

The agent is a vision-language model $\pi_{\phi}$ that reasons before it acts ( Wei et al., 2022 ; Guo et al., 2025 ) . At step $t$ , it reads the task instruction and its history $h_{t}$ and writes a turn $x_{t}=(z_{t},\beta_{t},u_{t})\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid h_{t})$ . The history holds the past actions and observations and the last belief line of the agent. The turn consists of a reasoning $z_{t}$ in the agent’s thinking block, a belief line $\beta_{t}$ and an action $u_{t}\in\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{Y}$ . The belief line names the set $B_{t}$ of answers still possible in the agent’s view. It is exact if it names exactly the answers allowed by the evidence, too narrow if it rules out one of them, and too wide if it keeps an answer ruled out by the evidence. At $h_{3}$ in figure 2 , the line Possible: cups 2, 4 is exact, Possible: cup 4 is too narrow and the open line Possible: all is too wide. Because the history keeps no earlier reasoning, the belief line carries the agent’s belief from one turn to the next. We call $d_{t}=(\beta_{t},u_{t})$ the decision of the turn and treat $z_{t}$ as a latent variable.


##### Objective.

The agent and a task form a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP) ( Kaelbling et al., 1998 ) whose hidden variable $\theta$ stays fixed during an episode. An answer $y$ at step $T_{\text{stop}}$ ends the episode with the outcome reward

[Tableau] $$G=c(T_{\text{stop}})\,\mathbf{1}\big[y=y^{*}(\theta)\big],\qquad c(t)=e^{-\lambda t/T},$$ (2)

where $\lambda$ is a time discount and $T$ is the step limit. An episode without an answer earns $0$ . An optimal agent decides using only $b_{t}$ and $t$ , because $(b_{t},t)$ is a sufficient statistic of $h_{t}$ for $\mathbb{E}[G]$ ( Åström, 1965 ) . Choosing when to answer is an optimal stopping problem ( Wald, 1947 ; Chow et al., 1971 ) . The exact posterior lets us value every action with a lookahead on $b_{t}$ . It also lets us verify every belief line against $B^{*}_{t}$ . We want the agent to learn this stopping decision. It should keep going while it has insufficient evidence and answer once it has sufficient evidence. The action term also scores which evidence action the agent picks, but improving that choice is not the goal of this work. Sections 3.2 and 3.3 turn this action value and belief line check into a reward for each step.


#### 3.2 The posterior teacher

The exact posterior gives two quantities at every history, and both depend only on the evidence. One is the value of every next action, and these values define the teacher policy $\pi^{*}$ that scores the action. The other is the set $B^{*}_{t}$ of answers still allowed by the evidence, and a variant also checks the belief line against this set.

The teacher values an answer $y$ by the log of its discounted chance of being right, and an evidence action $a$ by the expected value of the posterior $b^{a,o}$ after its observation $o$ ( Howard, 1966 ) :

[Tableau] $\displaystyle Q(b,y)$ $\displaystyle=\log\big(c(t)\,\hat{\rho}(b)\,b(y)/b_{\max}\big),$ (3) $\displaystyle Q^{(n)}(b,a)$ $\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{o}p(o\mid b,a)\,V^{(n-1)}(b^{a,o}),$ $\displaystyle V^{(n)}(b)$ $\displaystyle=\max_{u}Q^{(n)}(b,u),$

where $b_{\max}=\max_{y}b(y)$ , $\hat{\rho}(b)$ is the measured answer accuracy of the agent, $u$ ranges over the answers and the evidence actions with $Q^{(n)}(b,y)=Q(b,y)$ , and $n$ is the depth of the lookahead (supplementary material). The teacher answers with probability $p_{\text{ans}}=\sigma(\Delta/\tau)$ , where $\sigma$ is the logistic function and $\Delta$ the value of the best answer minus that of the best evidence action. It chooses among the answers or among the evidence actions by a Boltzmann distribution with temperature $\tau$ ( Haarnoja et al., 2017 ) :

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\pi^{*}(y\mid b)$ $\displaystyle=p_{\text{ans}}\,\operatorname{softmax}_{y}\big(Q(b,\cdot)/\tau\big),$ (4) $\displaystyle\pi^{*}(a\mid b)$ $\displaystyle=(1-p_{\text{ans}})\,\operatorname{softmax}_{a}\big(Q^{(n)}(b,\cdot)/\tau\big).$

The teacher depends on the agent only through $\hat{\rho}$ , which is measured from the agent’s own answers. Where these answers are unreliable, answering has a low value and the teacher asks for more evidence. The stopping rule of the teacher thus fits the agent (supplementary material).

In figure 2 (c), $b_{3}$ splits evenly between cups 2 and 4. Answering now is right with probability at most $1/2$ , while lifting either cup reveals where the cylinder is and lets the agent answer correctly one step later. The teacher therefore puts almost all of $\pi^{*}(u\mid b_{3})$ on the two lifts.


#### 3.3 Posterior process rewards

The teacher gives each turn a reward at the history where the agent wrote it. The reward uses the exact belief $b$ , which the simulator can verify. The main term checks the action against $b$ . A second term, the belief term, directly checks the belief stated in the agent’s line:

[Tableau] $$R(h,x)=\underbrace{\log\pi^{*}(u\mid b)}_{\text{action}}+\underbrace{S_{\text{dec}}+\eta\,(S+W)}_{\text{stated belief}},$$ (5)

where $\eta$ is a small weight. The action term is the action’s log probability under the teacher. The teacher acts on the exact belief $b$ . As a result, the action term rewards taking more steps while $b$ still allows several answers. Once $b$ no longer allows several answers, it rewards giving an answer. In this way, the action term is a belief-based reward. It uses the belief that the simulator can verify. It does not use the line written by the agent. The agent commits when the action $u$ is an answer. It keeps working when $u$ is an evidence action. Therefore, the action term rewards both the decision to commit or keep working and the choice of evidence action.

The action term penalizes the two possible mistakes very differently: answering before the evidence is sufficient receives a very low reward ( $-7.42$ in figure 1 ), while taking one additional step after the evidence is sufficient incurs only the small time discount of a single step. The reward therefore primarily teaches the agent not to answer too early.

RLVB learns from the verifiable belief $b$ through the action term. In every turn, the agent writes its belief line right before its action. Because the update increases the likelihood of the whole turn, it trains the line together with the action. As section 4.6 shows, the agent then learns to commit or keep working as its own belief line says. This is the main idea of RLVB .

The belief term is a variant that also rewards the line itself. We train RLVB (EM) with both terms, and we train RLVB with the action term alone. We report the belief term as an ablation in section 4.4 . It does not raise accuracy. The belief term reads the line as the uniform distribution $b_{\beta}$ over $B$ , with $\log b_{\beta}(y)=-m$ for an answer outside $B$ . It combines three scores of $\beta$ against $b$ :

[Tableau] $\displaystyle S(\beta;b)$ $\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{y}b(y)\log b_{\beta}(y),$ (6) $\displaystyle W(\beta;b)$ $\displaystyle=-m\,|B\setminus B^{*}|/|B|,$ $\displaystyle S_{\text{dec}}(\beta;b)$ $\displaystyle=\log\pi^{*}(a_{\beta}\mid b),$

where $a_{\beta}=\arg\max_{u}\pi^{*}(u\mid b_{\beta})$ is the teacher’s most probable action if the line were the posterior.

$S$ is the log score of the line ( Good, 1952 ) , but it punishes a line that is too narrow far more than one that is too wide. On its own, $S$ would teach the agent to hedge. We therefore score the line mainly by its implied decision. $S_{\text{dec}}$ is a proper scoring rule of the teacher’s decision problem ( Grünwald and Dawid, 2004 ; Gneiting and Raftery, 2007 ) , and $W$ charges a line that is too wide for the answers ruled out by the evidence. The small weight $\eta$ on $S+W$ breaks ties between lines that imply the same action. Since $R$ depends only on the posterior and the decision, no later observation enters it. Figure 1 gives both terms for the four turns at $h_{3}$ in figure 2 (c). The action term gives both lifts $0$ and both answers $-7.42$ , whatever their belief line. The belief term then ranks the exact line above the open line and the guess Possible: cup 4 .


#### 3.4 Policy update

> Algorithm 1 Training with posterior process rewards

RLVB updates the policy one step at a time ( figure 2 (c) and algorithm 1 ), and we train it in phases. In phase $k$ , a fixed copy $\pi_{k}$ of the model plays episodes, and at every history $h$ of these episodes we sample $N$ turns $x_{i}\sim\pi_{k}(\cdot\mid h)$ and score each by equation 5 . The teacher’s action replaces the agent’s in these episodes with a probability that starts at 0.5 and decays from phase to phase, as in DAgger ( Ross et al., 2011 ) . On half of the training scenes, one more episode starts with random evidence actions, and the histories then also cover states rarely reached by the agent’s own actions. The update compares these $N$ decisions at the same history, and a decision is thus credited only against alternatives under the same evidence. Trajectory RL such as GRPO ( Shao et al., 2024 ) instead credits every step of an episode with the advantage of the whole episode, and the two gradient estimates differ in exactly this respect:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\text{trajectory:}$ $\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle\sum_{e}\sum_{t}A_{e}\,\nabla_{\phi}\log\pi_{\phi}(x_{e,t}\mid h_{e,t}),$ (7) $\displaystyle\text{step:}$ $\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle\sum_{h}\sum_{i}w_{i}\,\nabla_{\phi}\log\pi_{\phi}(x_{i}\mid h),$

where $A_{e}$ is shared by all the steps $t$ of episode $e$ , and $w_{i}$ weighs turn $i$ against the other turns at $h$ .

Each phase moves the model toward the improved policy ( Peters et al., 2010 ; Levine, 2018 ; Donsker and Varadhan, 1975 )

[Tableau] $$q_{k}(x\mid h)\propto\pi_{k}(x\mid h)\,e^{R(h,x)/\alpha},$$ (8)

which maximizes $\mathbb{E}_{\pi}R-\alpha\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\,\|\,\pi_{k})$ at every history. The model moves toward $q_{k}$ by gradient steps on the weighted log likelihood

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}(\phi)=-\textstyle\sum_{h}\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}\log\pi_{\phi}(x_{i}\mid h).$$ (9)

We use two weightings, the first for RLVB and the second for its variant RLVB (EM). RLVB uses the policy gradient ( Williams, 1992 ) . It is group relative as in GRPO, but its group is the $N$ turns at one history. Each turn is weighted by its reward minus the mean reward of the other turns ( Ahmadian et al., 2024 ) :

[Tableau] $$w_{i}\propto R(h,x_{i})-\tfrac{1}{N-1}\textstyle\sum_{j\neq i}R(h,x_{j}).$$ (10)

This weight is proportional to $R(h,x_{i})-\bar{R}(h)$ in figure 2 (c). RLVB (EM) instead uses expectation maximization (EM) ( Dempster et al., 1977 ; Abdolmaleki et al., 2018 ) and weights the turns by $w_{i}\propto e^{R(h,x_{i})/\alpha}$ , normalized over the history. These normalized weights estimate $q_{k}$ itself. The two weightings are two limits of one family (supplementary material). To keep each step small, a phase ends when the KL divergence of $\pi_{\phi}$ from $\pi_{k}$ exceeds $\varepsilon$ . Each phase is then one step of policy mirror descent on $R$ ( Lan, 2023 ; Tomar et al., 2022 ) . RLVB gives a turn its own reward only, because the lookahead of the teacher already values what follows. GRPO with process supervision ( Shao et al., 2024 ) instead adds the rewards of all later steps to a step. With both weightings, a turn gets no positive weight if the teacher rejects its action, that is, if $\pi^{*}(u\mid b)<\pi^{*}(u_{\text{best}}\mid b)/20$ for the teacher’s most probable action $u_{\text{best}}$ . A small length reward favors the shorter reasoning among correct turns, as in Kimi k1.5 ( Kimi Team, 2025 ) . The supplementary material gives the rules for sampling, acceptance and fitting.


### 4 Experiments


#### 4.1 Benchmarks

We evaluate on three benchmarks: HiddenGames, Pro-Worlds and RoboQuest ( Liu et al., 2026 ) . In all benchmarks, the answer is hidden. The agent must take actions to collect evidence and decide when it has seen enough to answer.


##### HiddenGames.

It consists of seven tasks from existing agent benchmarks: MineSweeper ( Wang et al., 2026a ) , MiniGrid KeyCorridor ( Chevalier-Boisvert et al., 2023 ) , FrozenLake and Sokoban ( Wang et al., 2025a ) , and three Tabletop tasks rendered in ManiSkill3 ( Tao et al., 2025 ) . We train on these tasks and test on 756 of their games with seeds, maps and colors never seen in training.


##### Pro-Worlds.

To assess transfer to out-of-domain visual tasks, we introduce Pro-Worlds, a benchmark that tests how visual agents gather, retain and combine evidence before they commit to an answer. In each game, the agent sees a larger world only through a small window, moves the window to collect evidence, and must decide when it has seen enough to answer. The window shows one site of the world at a time. The agent can make at most 35 moves before it names one of eight candidates (ten in Jigsaw) as its answer. We compute the exact posterior of every game from its game engine. A teacher acting on this posterior solves every game in 14.7 moves on average. We test on 15 games (Face, Jigsaw, Kaleido, Bird, Fish, Recipe, Turtle, Balloon, Rocket, Bell, Bee, Candle, Sailboat, Barn and Lantern), with 20 plays of each.


##### RoboQuest.

To test transfer to tasks without an exact posterior, we use the ten mobile manipulation tasks of RoboQuest ( Liu et al., 2026 ) . They take place in simulated kitchens and require search, inspection and interactive testing, such as finding the odd parcel by weighing. We test on 50 games of each task. The supplementary material gives further details of all three benchmarks.


#### 4.2 Experiment Setup


##### Models.

We train three open-source vision-language models: Qwen3-VL-4B-Thinking and Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking ( Bai et al., 2025 ) , and Qwen3.5-4B ( Qwen Team, 2026 ) .


##### Baselines.

Base is the untrained model and outputs an action only. Base (w/ belief) is the same model with a belief line before the action. We consider two groups of trained baselines. The first group acts without a belief and learns from whole episodes. SFT fine-tunes the base model on the teacher’s actions in winning games. GRPO ( Shao et al., 2024 ) and PPO ( Schulman et al., 2017 ) train on the outcome reward of each episode. PPO also learns a critic. The second group learns from a process reward. In PPO (belief + action) , the model writes its belief as short claims with certainty words and then an action. PPO trains it with the outcome reward of the episode. A learned value function carries this reward back to every turn. At each turn, an LLM judge (DeepSeek V4.1 Flash ( DeepSeek-AI, 2026 ) ) rewards the belief by checking its claims against the newest observation and the true hidden state. Only the belief gets a per-turn reward. The decision to continue or to answer is rewarded only by the outcome of the episode. Both rewards of this baseline are based on the truth. The reward of RLVB is instead based on the evidence. The outcome reward pays a correct answer even when it was a guess. The judge rewards a true claim even when the evidence does not support it. PPO (belief + action) also samples whole episodes and learns from one outcome per episode. RLVB instead samples multiple turns at each history and rewards each of them against the exact posterior. All methods train LoRA adapters ( Hu et al., 2022 ) for up to 50 updates on the same tasks. At every turn, every method receives the full history of its actions and observations but not its earlier reasoning.


##### Metrics.

We test the adapter after the last update of each run. Each test runs three times with different sampling seeds. When trained three times on Qwen3.5-4B with small changes to the settings, the three runs reached 48.5 to 50.0% on HiddenGames, 28.3 to 32.1% on Pro-Worlds, and 54.0 to 56.9% on RoboQuest. . Accuracy is the share of episodes ending with the correct answer. The supplementary material lists the settings of RLVB .

##### The belief line and the belief reward.

For Base (w/ belief), the belief line is already exact on many turns. It is exact on 46.8 to 48.4% of turns on HiddenGames and on 75.7 to 82.4% of turns on Pro-Worlds. Yet the model often does not act on this belief. It commits on 3 to 68% of the turns where the evidence is insufficient and its own belief line still names several answers ( section 4.6 ). We test whether rewarding the belief line fixes this problem. For this test, table 5 compares training with and without the belief term in equation 5 . We replay every test episode using the exact posterior. At each turn, we count how many answers the belief line names and how many answers the evidence still allows. On HiddenGames, we look at Qwen3-VL-4B trained with the belief term and select the turns where its belief line names a single answer. The agent answers on 98% of these turns when the evidence allows only that answer, and on 95% of them when the evidence still allows several. With the belief term, the agent commits on almost every single-answer line, whether or not the evidence supports it. This matters because the evidence still allows several answers on about half of the turns where the belief line names one answer. RLVB is trained without the belief term, and it treats these two kinds of turns differently. It answers on 66% of the turns where the evidence allows only one answer, and on 32% of the turns where the evidence still allows several. The other two models show the same change. On HiddenGames, the belief term makes the belief line exact more often for the two Qwen3-VL models (57 against 41% and 70 against 46% of turns). It does not do so for Qwen3.5-4B (53 against 60%). With the belief term, the agent also answers earlier. On HiddenGames, 43.0 to 52.9% of its answers are premature, compared to 34.5 to 43.1% for RLVB , as table 2 shows. The belief term does not raise accuracy on any model. Removing the belief term helps most on RoboQuest. Without the belief term, 20.0 to 28.9% of the agent’s RoboQuest answers come before it has seen what the task requires, compared to 25.3 to 41.9% with the belief term.

With the belief term, the agent commits whenever its belief line names a single answer. With the action term alone, the agent rarely commits while the evidence is insufficient and its belief line names several answers. Removing the belief term does not remove the belief from the reward. The action term is still computed from the exact belief verified by the simulator. Therefore, RLVB ’s main effect is on the decision to commit. As table 2 shows, RLVB changes the share of exact belief lines over all turns by $-5.8$ to $+12.5$ % on HiddenGames and by $-7.8$ to $-4.9$ % on Pro-Worlds. The rise on Qwen3.5-4B comes from fewer belief lines that cannot be read, at 1.3% of turns compared to 29.5%.


#### 4.6 Why RLVB works

There are three possible explanations for RLVB ’s gain. The trained agent could read its evidence better (RQ1), take steps that bring more evidence (RQ2), or act on what its belief line says (RQ3). In terms of metacognition, RQ1 and RQ2 look at monitoring the evidence. RQ3 looks at whether controlling the decision follows this monitoring. At every turn, the exact posterior measures how strongly the evidence gathered so far settles the answer. This lets us test each explanation.

We call each evidence-gathering action a step . We call the evidence sufficient when the exact posterior of the top answer is at least 0.95. RoboQuest has no posterior, so there the evidence is sufficient once all evidence requirements of the task are met. We define the evidence at the answer as the top answer’s posterior on the turn when the agent commits. On RoboQuest, it is the share of requirements met on that turn. A belief line is exact when it names exactly the answers that are still possible. Base is the base model without a belief line. Base (w/ belief) is the same model with a belief line. In this section, HiddenGames means its five tasks with an answer, and RoboQuest means its eight tasks with countable requirements. The accuracies therefore differ from those in table 1 . Appendix K gives details of these measures and further tests.


##### The evidence at the answer.

##### Where the gain comes from.

A method can improve its accuracy in two ways. First, it can answer at higher levels of evidence by taking more steps before it commits. Second, it can answer more accurately at the same level of evidence, for example by reading its observations better. To measure both effects, we write a method’s accuracy as a sum over evidence levels $e$ . Each term multiplies the share of episodes answered at that level, $f(e)$ , by the accuracy of the answers at that level, $a(e)$ . We count episodes with no answer as wrong. The gain of a method $M$ over Base $B$ then splits exactly into two parts,

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{Acc}_{M}-\mathrm{Acc}_{B}=\underbrace{\sum_{e}\bigl(f_{M}(e)-f_{B}(e)\bigr)\,\bar{a}(e)}_{\text{more evidence}}+\underbrace{\sum_{e}\bigl(a_{M}(e)-a_{B}(e)\bigr)\,\bar{f}(e)}_{\text{same evidence}},$$ (11)

where $\bar{a}(e)$ and $\bar{f}(e)$ are the two methods’ means at level $e$ . Figure 5 (d) to figure 5 (f) show these two parts for RLVB . RLVB is more accurate because a larger share of its answers come at high levels of evidence, where every method’s answers are often correct. At the same level of evidence, RLVB ’s answers are no more accurate than Base’s. In HiddenGames, the 18.1% gain splits into $+26.7$ % from more evidence and $-8.5$ % at the same evidence. On Pro-Worlds, the two parts are $+15.8$ and $-2.8$ %. On RoboQuest, they are $+31.8$ and $+0.3$ %. In HiddenGames, the second part is negative because RLVB misreads more of its sufficient evidence, as RQ1 shows. Table 21 reports both parts for each model and for PPO (belief + action). Answers with sufficient evidence are also correct much more often than other answers in the same test game. RLVB gives a correct answer with sufficient evidence in 33.3% of HiddenGames episodes, 8.7% of Pro-Worlds episodes, and 66.6% of RoboQuest episodes. Base does so in 7.5%, 0.3%, and 31.7% of episodes, as table 22 shows.

##### RQ2: Does RLVB judge what further steps can bring?

If RLVB had learned to anticipate upcoming evidence, it would choose steps that add more evidence than those of Base and recognize when its own steps stop adding evidence. We measure the value of a step by how much it raises the exact posterior of the correct answer, or by the share of requirements met on RoboQuest. By this measure, steps from RLVB add about as much evidence as those from Base. From the states reached by Base, PPO (belief + action), and RLVB , the first step raises the posterior by 15.6, 14.9, and 14.8% in HiddenGames and by 1.5, 1.9, and 1.5% on Pro-Worlds, while meeting 19.1, 19.3, and 19.8% of the requirements on RoboQuest, as appendix K shows. Later steps have similar value across every window of steps from the start of the game, as figure 7 shows. Across states reached by both Base and RLVB , about 20% of each agent’s actions meet one more requirement on RoboQuest, and 4–6% of their moves add evidence on Pro-Worlds, as table 9 shows. Only in MiniGrid and MineSweeper does the first step of RLVB add less evidence, raising the posterior by 0.14%, against 1.24% for Base.

RLVB also does not choose better actions. Measured against the actions ranked best by the posterior teacher, RLVB and Base choose a best action about equally often, in 40 versus 37% of HiddenGames states and 53 versus 52% of Pro-Worlds states. A better belief line does not lead to better steps either. From the same state and with an exact belief line, a step from RLVB raises the posterior of the correct answer by 0.9% more than a step from Base (w/ belief) on the Tabletop tasks, where a step adds about 30%, and by 0.02% less on Pro-Worlds, where a step adds about 0.9%. The 95% intervals for both differences include zero. The same holds when the posterior teacher continues playing to the end after each step. With an exact belief line, a step from RLVB leaves the teacher with as many remaining steps as one from Base (w/ belief) on Pro-Worlds and in the Tabletop tasks, and 0.53 more in MiniGrid and MineSweeper, as appendix K shows.

RLVB also does not recognize when its own steps stop adding evidence. In MineSweeper, the posterior teacher reaches sufficient evidence in 83% of scenes, as table 32 shows. RLVB ’s steps rarely reach sufficient evidence, and the agent answers in only 3 to 16% of episodes. On Qwen3-VL-4B, 44 of its 81 MineSweeper episodes without an answer add no evidence across their last ten turns, as table 37 shows.


##### Why the steps do not improve.

Three things explain this result. First, RLVB gains the most on the Tabletop tasks. On those tasks, every step that tests a new object splits the possible answers in the same way, as appendix D shows. The choice of step does not matter on these tasks. Second, on MiniGrid and MineSweeper, the reward hardly separates a step without evidence from the best step. That kind of step only delays the teacher’s best plan by one turn. As a result, its score is lower than the best step’s score by about one time discount. This discount is 0.2 nats, or 0.5 nats in MineSweeper. An early answer scores several nats lower, for example $-7.42$ in figure 1 . Because of this, the reward mostly teaches when to answer. Third, Pro-Worlds and RoboQuest never appear in training. Better steps on those tasks would have to carry over from the training tasks. A test on Qwen3.5-4B supports these points. With the three rules in section C.4 , the teacher rejects steps without evidence. On the HiddenGames tasks with an answer, the share of steps that add evidence then rises from 21.2 to 28.0%. On Pro-Worlds, this share changes from 5.1 to 5.0%. On RoboQuest, it changes from 21.3 to 21.9%. Accuracy changes by $-0.2$ to $+2.9$ %.

##### RQ3: Does RLVB act on its belief line?

The base model often acts against its own belief line. Over all turns where the belief line names several answers, Base (w/ belief) commits at 3.2 to 69.1% of these turns, while RLVB commits at 0.1 to 10.0%. On RoboQuest, Base (w/ belief) commits at 3.8 to 8.7% of these turns, while RLVB commits at 1.9 to 2.6%, as figure 8 (a) shows. As a result, many of the base model’s answers go against its own belief line. When the base model answers, its belief line still names several answers in 23 to 59% of its Tabletop answers, 22 to 40% in MiniGrid and MineSweeper, 57 to 91% on Pro-Worlds, and 27 to 46% on RoboQuest. For RLVB , these shares are 6 to 30%, 1 to 29%, 0.3 to 3.3%, and 20 to 24%, as figure 8 (b) shows. On Pro-Worlds, RLVB gives most of its other answers with such a line after all 35 moves are used. When the evidence is also insufficient, RLVB commits at only 0.1 to 2.2% of these turns, against 3.2 to 67.9% for Base (w/ belief). In all nine settings, the 95% intervals do not overlap, as table 25 shows. From the same states with insufficient evidence, Base commits in 30–49% of cases on RoboQuest, against 7–13% for RLVB . On the Tabletop tasks, Base commits in 45–87% of cases, against 5–12% for RLVB , as table 9 shows. Instead of committing, RLVB keeps working. RLVB acts on its belief line almost entirely through its first single-answer line. Figure 9 compares its accuracy with the accuracy it would reach by committing to that line’s answer. The two accuracies differ by at most 2.3% in all nine settings. When RLVB keeps working after such a line, it most often gives the same answer later. Changes from a wrong answer to a right answer happen about as often as changes from a right answer to a wrong answer. Its extra step most often tests the named answer. On Qwen3-VL-4B, it lifts the named cup in 46 of 48 such steps on cups. On lamps, it presses the last button in 177 of 198 such steps, and the belief line has already inferred that button’s lamp. After this step, its belief line names the same answer in 74% of cases, and it commits on the next turn in 88%. This check rarely corrects a wrong belief line because the next line names the same wrong answer in 66% of cases. The check also depends little on the evidence. Pooled over the three models on the Tabletop tasks, RLVB commits at 78.5% of single-answer turns with insufficient evidence and at 78.6% with sufficient evidence. Because RLVB gives the answer that its belief line names, errors in the belief line become wrong answers. This produces the negative second part of equation 11 in HiddenGames.

A likely reason for this behavior lies in the reward. The reward never reads the belief line, and it scores every action against the exact posterior. A commit gets a low reward when the evidence leaves several answers open, even if the guess happens to be correct. In contrast, an outcome reward gives a lucky guess the full reward. At these states, the belief line usually names several answers. The update raises the likelihood of the whole turn, including the line. As a result, the agent learns to keep working when its own line names several answers. Taking one more step after the evidence is sufficient costs only that step’s small time discount in the action term, as section 3.3 describes. This makes the extra check cheap. Adding a reward on the belief line itself does not raise accuracy, as table 5 shows.

The on-policy variant of RLVB doubts its belief line more and keeps exploring. On Qwen3-VL-4B, after it takes a step while its belief line names a single answer, its next line keeps that answer in 61% of cases, against 74% for RLVB . It commits on the next turn in 71%, against 88%. Of these steps, 20% are rejected as invalid, against 5%. On the Tabletop tasks, its belief line still names several answers on 62% of turns with sufficient evidence, against 46% for RLVB . It commits at only 9% of these turns, against 37% for RLVB . It ends half of its HiddenGames episodes without an answer, against 18% for RLVB . It is less accurate than RLVB on all three benchmarks, as appendix L shows.


##### A check in the harness.

Such a check cannot replace an agent that acts on its own belief. Our harness does not accept an episode’s first answer. It asks the agent once to check whether its evidence identifies the answer. The agent can then repeat its answer, change it, or keep working. This check raises Base’s accuracy by 6.1 to 11.3% on RoboQuest. On HiddenGames and Pro-Worlds, it changes accuracy by at most 1.9%, as table 36 shows. On the Tabletop tasks, Base keeps working after the check in only 2 to 17% of the checked episodes, as appendix K shows. Otherwise, it repeats its answer. In these tasks, Base (w/ belief) has a belief line that already names a single answer in 38 to 72% of its early commits. Because the harness does not read the observations, it cannot tell the agent when its evidence is sufficient. RLVB writes its belief line from its own observations, and that line guides most of its decisions to commit.


##### Limits.

An agent that keeps working gains only where its steps bring evidence. Among the HiddenGames tasks, RLVB gains most on the Tabletop tasks, where every valid step adds evidence, with 51% on lamps and 35% on cups, as appendix F shows. On RoboQuest, where about one action in five meets a requirement, the gain on the three models is 14 to 33%, and on Pro-Worlds, where about one move in twenty adds evidence, the gain is 12 to 15%. In MiniGrid and MineSweeper, a step raises the posterior of the correct answer by about 1% or less, and RLVB shows no gain on these tasks. As RQ2 shows, RLVB has not learned to commit to its best answer in MineSweeper when its steps stop adding evidence or when no step remains. Across the three models, RLVB ends 19% of its HiddenGames episodes without an answer, compared with 7% for Base, almost all of them in MineSweeper.


### 5 Limitations

RLVB needs a task with known rules. The environment can compute the exact posterior only when it knows the prior over the hidden answer and the likelihood of each observation. Many puzzles, games, and simulated household tasks meet this condition. For tasks with unknown or open-ended rules, the posterior must be estimated. Very large action spaces need a shallower or sampled lookahead. The cost of the teacher’s lookahead grows with the number of actions and observations. Finally, a belief line names a set of possible answers. This set states the posterior exactly only when every possible answer is equally likely. The belief line cannot express finer degrees of certainty. With training, wrong answers given with enough evidence also rise from 2–4% to 10–15% of the episodes, mostly in the Tabletop tasks ( tables 6 and 30 ). On these tasks, the agent misreads observations that already settle the answer. It then gives the wrong answer that its belief line names ( section 4.6 ). The action term also scores which step the agent takes, but the trained agent does not choose more informative steps. On Pro-Worlds, a step by the posterior teacher raises the posterior by 5.9%, compared to 0.7 to 1.3% for a step by any method. The trained agents also stay far below the posterior teacher (for example 32.4% against 100% on Pro-Worlds). The agents have not yet learned much of what the exact posterior allows.


### 6 Conclusion

We showed that a simulator can compute the exact posterior of an evidence-seeking task from its privileged information. This posterior gives a process reward at every turn. The posterior scores the agent’s choice between taking more steps and answering. It can also score the agent’s stated belief. RLVB learns from this reward with a policy gradient, and RLVB (EM) learns from it with EM. Across three vision-language models and three benchmarks, both RLVB learners improve the base model in every setting. With the same learner, the posterior process reward beats the outcome reward by 18.1% on average. On each of the three benchmarks, RLVB also beats a process reward from an LLM judge on average. The trained agent answers with sufficient evidence in 51%, 12%, and 74% of episodes, compared to 8%, 0.4%, and 36% for the base model. It does this by acting on its own belief. With the same belief line, it commits on only 0.1 to 2.2% of turns with insufficient evidence where this line still names several answers, compared to 3 to 68% for the base model. Across all turns, its belief line is not consistently more exact than the base model’s. Each of its steps adds about as much evidence as a step from the base model. A check in the harness does not raise the base model’s accuracy on HiddenGames and Pro-Worlds because the harness cannot read the observations. We expect a better reading of the evidence to carry into the answers when an agent acts on its belief. RLVB ’s steps are not yet more informative than the base model’s. An agent with sufficient evidence can still misread its observations. We want to extend RLVB to tasks where the posterior must be learned, to improve the choice of the next step, and to train the agent to read its observations correctly once its evidence is sufficient.

### Appendix A The posterior teacher in detail

This section describes the posterior teacher of section 3.2 in detail.


##### Boundary values.

In equation 3 , $V^{(0)}(b)=\log c(t)+\log\hat{\rho}^{+}(b)$ . Here $\hat{\rho}^{+}$ is an upper confidence bound on $\hat{\rho}$ that adds one standard deviation, as given in equation 13 . This bound is high for belief classes with few measured answers. The lookahead thus gives a high value to evidence that leads to these classes. The time of $b^{a,o}$ is $t+1$ . A belief at the step limit $t=T$ and a belief that identifies the answer both have the value $\log c(t)+\log\hat{\rho}(b)$ . For an answer with $b(y)=0$ , we floor the accuracy at $10^{-9}$ . Such an answer has the value $\log c(t)+\log 10^{-9}$ . An outcome that ends the episode without an answer, such as a revealed mine, has the value of knowing nothing, $\log c(t)+\log(1/|\Theta|)$ . A lookahead of depth 2 evaluates every pair of evidence actions with their observations. Its cost thus grows as $(|\mathcal{A}|\,|\mathcal{O}|)^{2}$ Bayes updates per history.


##### Task lookaheads.

We use equation 3 with depth 2 for the Tabletop tasks. The other tasks have many evidence actions. Each of them computes the same kind of value, $\log c(t)+\log\hat{\rho}$ , with its own lookahead. MiniGrid, FrozenLake and Sokoban value each move by the best view reached on a shortest plan starting with this move. MineSweeper values each reveal by the posterior after it. The cost above holds for the depth-2 recursion only.


##### Why the logarithm.

The average of $\log\hat{\rho}$ counts progress at every step. Each halving of the possible answers adds $\log 2$ , whether it comes early or late. For a uniform posterior, this average is the expected information gain on the answer. When the answers are many, the average of $\hat{\rho}$ itself stays close to 0 until the answer is almost decided. For example, on a $6\times 6$ MineSweeper board with 6 mines and 1,947,792 mine sets, the average of $\hat{\rho}$ cannot rank the actions. It also gives almost no cost to a risk that ends the episode.


##### Answer accuracy.

$\hat{\rho}(b)$ is the measured answer accuracy of the agent:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(b)$ $\displaystyle=b_{\max}\,\hat{\varphi}_{\kappa(b)},\qquad r_{y}=\frac{b(y)}{b_{\max}},$ (12) $\displaystyle\hat{\varphi}_{\kappa}$ $\displaystyle=\frac{4+\sum_{\omega\in\Omega_{\kappa}}\frac{1}{\bar{n}}\sum_{y\in\omega}r_{y}}{4+\sum_{\omega\in\Omega_{\kappa}}\frac{n_{\omega}}{\bar{n}}}.$

$\kappa(b)$ is the belief class. It is the number of answers still possible, capped at 4. $\hat{\varphi}_{\kappa}$ is the mean of $r$ over the agent’s answers in class $\kappa$ , with a prior mean of 1 and 4 pseudo-counts. $\hat{\varphi}=1$ when the agent always names the most probable answer. Each update gives one measurement for each cell $\omega=(\text{scene},\text{belief class})$ . This measurement is the mean of $r$ over all of the agent’s answers in $\omega$ . This mean replaces a single sample by its conditional mean given the cell. As in the Rao–Blackwell theorem ( Rao, 1945 ; Blackwell, 1947 ) , it thus has a smaller variance than one sample. $n_{\omega}$ is the number of answers in $\omega$ . $\bar{n}$ is the mean of $n_{\omega}$ over all cells measured in the same update as $\omega$ . $\Omega_{\kappa}$ is the set of cells of class $\kappa$ measured so far over all updates. Each $r_{y}$ is taken at the belief where the agent gave the answer $y$ . Episodes starting with random evidence actions keep each belief class measured.

The estimate $\hat{\varphi}_{\kappa}$ is the mean of a Beta posterior with parameters $A_{\kappa}=4+\sum_{\omega\in\Omega_{\kappa}}\frac{1}{\bar{n}}\sum_{y\in\omega}r_{y}$ and $A_{\kappa}+D_{\kappa}=4+\sum_{\omega\in\Omega_{\kappa}}\frac{n_{\omega}}{\bar{n}}$ . The upper confidence bound adds one standard deviation of this posterior:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\hat{\rho}^{+}(b)$ $\displaystyle=\min\big\{1,\ b_{\max}\,(\hat{\varphi}_{\kappa}+\mathrm{sd}_{\kappa})\big\},\quad\kappa=\kappa(b),$ (13) $\displaystyle\mathrm{sd}_{\kappa}$ $\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{A_{\kappa}\,D_{\kappa}}{(A_{\kappa}+D_{\kappa})^{2}\,(A_{\kappa}+D_{\kappa}+1)}}.$


##### Teacher policy.

In equation 4 , we normalize the step level over the groups $\mathcal{G}(b)$ of evidence actions with equal values $Q^{(2)}(b,\cdot)$ . Each action in a group gets the full probability of its group. The step level of $\pi^{*}$ is thus a distribution over groups. If $b$ identifies the answer, $p_{\text{ans}}=1$ . An answer outside the support of $b$ has accuracy 0. It thus gets the floor value $\log c(t)+\log 10^{-9}$ . An action that cannot be executed in the environment uses up the turn. It has the value of an answer one step later, $\log c(t+1)+\log\hat{\rho}(b)$ . The log-probability has the floor $-200$ . $\pi^{*}$ depends on the agent only through $\hat{\rho}$ . Where the agent’s answers are not reliable, $Q(b,y)$ is low and $\pi^{*}$ asks for more evidence. The stopping rule of the teacher thus fits the agent.

Suppose the lookahead reaches the step limit and $\hat{\rho}$ is the true answer accuracy of the agent. Then, as $\tau\to 0$ , the best action of $\pi^{*}$ maximizes $\mathbb{E}\big[\log\big(c(T_{\text{stop}})\,\hat{\rho}(b_{T_{\text{stop}}})\big)\big]$ .

We have $\max_{y}Q(b,y)=\log c(t)+\log\hat{\rho}(b)$ . With depth $n\geq T-t$ , equation 3 is therefore the Bellman recursion of the stopping problem with the reward $\log c(t)+\log\hat{\rho}(b)$ at the stop. By backward induction on the steps left, $V^{(n)}$ is its optimal value. As $\tau\to 0$ , $\pi^{*}$ puts all its mass on the maximizers of the values. ∎

The best action of $\pi^{*}$ therefore maximizes the expected log of the discounted answer accuracy. The proposition holds for the recursion of equation 3 . The task lookaheads above approximate this recursion. With depth 2, $Q^{(2)}$ approximates this optimum. This criterion differs from the expected return. It prefers steady progress to a gamble that can lose all the evidence. If $\hat{\rho}$ is the true accuracy, $\mathbb{E}[G]=\mathbb{E}\big[c(T_{\text{stop}})\,\hat{\rho}(b_{T_{\text{stop}}})\big]$ . By Jensen’s inequality, the teacher’s criterion is then a lower bound on $\log\mathbb{E}[G]$ . The gap between the two makes the teacher averse to risk.


### Appendix B The process reward in detail

This section describes the process reward in equation 5 in detail. The action term is the reward for RLVB . It scores the action against the teacher. The teacher acts on the exact belief $b$ , and the simulator can verify this belief. The belief term is a variant that also scores the stated belief line against $b$ . We use it in RLVB (EM) and in the ablation in section 4.4 .


##### Action term.

The action term scores the agent’s action against the teacher policy $\pi^{*}$ . Inside one level of equation 4 ,

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\log\pi^{*}(u\mid b)$ $\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{Q}(b,u)}{\tau}+\nu_{\mathcal{L}}(b),$ (14) $\displaystyle\nu_{\mathcal{L}}(b)$ $\displaystyle=\log P_{\mathcal{L}}(b)-\log\textstyle\sum_{u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}}e^{\bar{Q}(b,u^{\prime})/\tau},$

where $\bar{Q}(b,u)=Q(b,y)$ for an answer $u=y$ , and $\bar{Q}(b,u)=Q^{(2)}(b,a)$ for an evidence action $u=a$ . The level $\mathcal{L}$ of $u$ is “answer” or “step”. We set $P_{\text{answer}}=p_{\text{ans}}$ and $P_{\text{step}}=1-p_{\text{ans}}$ . For the step level, the sum runs over the groups $\mathcal{G}(b)$ . Because $\nu_{\mathcal{L}}(b)$ depends only on $b$ and on $\mathcal{L}$ , the action term is a soft advantage of the lookahead values in units of $\tau$ . An action whose value is one nat below the best costs $1/\tau$ nats. In the belief term, a line that leaves out all of the posterior mass costs $m$ nats in $S$ .


##### Belief language.

The belief language is the set of lines Possible: $v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}$ with distinct values $v_{i}\in\Theta$ , plus the open line Possible: all , which rules out nothing. We read a line in the language exactly as its set $B\subseteq\Theta$ , and the open line as $B=\Theta$ . Any other text is outside the language and receives the floor of every term. The open line is thus the safe statement. A set states only the support of $b_{t}$ . It states $b_{t}$ itself only when $b_{t}$ is flat on its support, as when each observation is either possible or impossible under each target. The last belief line in the prompt is the agent’s own earlier estimate. In the variant with the belief term, the teacher scores each new line against $b_{t}$ , which comes only from the evidence. A line that copies an earlier mistake thus scores low.


##### Properties of the log score $S$ .

The stated distribution $\hat{b}_{\beta}$ is uniform on $B$ with the log-probability floor $-m$ outside $B$ . Then $S(\beta;b)=\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim b}[\log\hat{b}_{\beta}(\theta)]=-b(B)\log|B|-m\,(1-b(B))$ .

- • The log score is proper ( Gneiting and Raftery, 2007 ) . Among normalized distributions, none gets a higher expected score under $b$ than $b$ itself. Because $\hat{b}_{\beta}$ is not normalized and names only a set, the property holds in the two forms below.

- • When $b$ is flat on its support and $m>1+\log|\Theta|$ , the exact line $\beta^{*}$ gets the highest score.

- • When $b$ is not flat, the best set can leave out targets of small mass. Adding a target of mass $\delta$ to $B$ raises $S$ only when $\delta\,\big(m-\log(|B|+1)\big)>b(B)\,\log\big((|B|+1)/|B|\big)$ . This holds when $\delta$ is above about $1/(m|B|)$ .


##### The decision score $S_{\text{dec}}$ .

Let $\tilde{b}_{\beta}$ be the belief stated by the line. It holds the targets that agree with the line. Outside MineSweeper, it also holds the targets already excluded by the evidence. Let $a_{\beta}$ be the teacher’s action when $\tilde{b}_{\beta}$ is its posterior. Then $S_{\text{dec}}(\beta;b)=\log\pi^{*}(a_{\beta}\mid b)$ , averaged over $a_{\beta}$ when the teacher’s best actions tie. Every decision problem has its own proper scoring rule, the value of the act implied by a belief ( Grünwald and Dawid, 2004 ; Gneiting and Raftery, 2007 ) . $S_{\text{dec}}$ is this rule for the teacher’s problem. The exact line implies the teacher’s own action and receives the highest score. $S_{\text{dec}}$ is proper but not strictly proper, because lines that imply the same act tie. The sum of a strictly proper and a proper score is strictly proper. Adding $\eta\,S$ with any $\eta>0$ thus keeps the exact line the unique best line. If the task cannot read a line as a belief, the line receives the floor $-200$ .


##### The mirror term $W$ .

$W(\beta;b)=-m\,|B\setminus B^{*}|/|B|$ is $-m$ times the line’s probability outside the support $B^{*}$ of $b$ , with the line read as uniform on its options. Each task maps the line to $B$ and the posterior to $B^{*}$ with its own parser. $W$ mirrors the miss term $-m\,(1-b(B))$ of $S$ . A line two times too wide pays about $m/2$ , as does a line that leaves out half of the posterior. Because $W=0$ for the exact line and for every line inside it, the exact line keeps its score and the term needs no new constant. Under $S+W$ , the best line under the agent’s own uncertainty keeps an option when the option is more likely possible than not.

##### Likelihood view.

Every term of $R$ is a log-probability or a non-positive score, and hence $R\leq 0$ . With $\alpha=1$ , $e^{R}$ lies in $(0,1]$ and acts as the likelihood of the decision under the teacher. This view holds for $R$ without the length reward, because that reward adds $C\ell_{i}\leq C/2$ to the weights. $R$ does not depend on the reasoning $z$ . The reasoning gets credit only through its effect on the probability of the decisions.

#### C.1 Shared by both learners

For each history $h$ ,

##### Accepted actions.

The self-normalized weights show only the order of the samples and do not change when the same constant is added to every $R$ . We therefore accept a turn only when $\pi^{*}(u\mid b)\geq\pi^{*}(u_{\text{best}}\mid b)/20$ , where $u_{\text{best}}$ is the action of highest probability under $\pi^{*}$ . Only accepted turns get weight. A turn with a rejected action gets weight 0 whatever its belief line. In the Bayes view, its likelihood is 0. With the belief term, the floor of the line score could otherwise give the weight to a rejected action. This could happen when every line leaves out part of the posterior. Formally, the E-step uses the score $\tilde{R}(h,x)=R(h,x)$ for an accepted turn and $\tilde{R}(h,x)=-\infty$ otherwise. Proposition 2 , the Bayes view and equation 19 then hold with $\tilde{R}$ in place of $R$ at every history with an accepted turn.


##### Covered histories.

With the action term alone, a history is covered when one of its turns has an accepted action. With the belief term, that turn must also have a belief line within 2 nats of the exact line. If no turn of the first round covers the history, the trainer samples one more round of $N$ turns, with $2K$ reasonings in all. In either case, it then fits the history with its accepted turns. With the belief term, the two thresholds have two roles. The loose threshold of 2 nats decides whether to sample more. The tight threshold of 0.1 nats in the M-step decides whether a line is a target.


##### Histories without a target.

A history has no positive target when it has no accepted turn. With the belief term, it also has no positive target when none of its accepted turns names any of the posterior mass. Such a history gets no weight in the M-step.


##### Length reward.

Within one history, let $|z_{i}|$ be the number of reasoning tokens of turn $i$ and $s_{i}=\tfrac{1}{2}-(|z_{i}|-\min_{j}|z_{j}|)/(\max_{j}|z_{j}|-\min_{j}|z_{j}|)$ , as in Kimi k1.5 ( Kimi Team, 2025 ) . A turn is correct when its action is accepted. With the belief term, its line must also be within 2 nats of the exact line. A correct turn gets $\ell_{i}=s_{i}$ . Shorter correct reasoning thus gets more weight. An incorrect turn gets $\ell_{i}=\min(0,s_{i})$ . A short incorrect turn thus gets no bonus, and a long one gets a penalty. All $\ell_{i}=0$ when every reasoning has the same length. From the first phase, the E-step weights add $C\,\ell_{i}$ to $R$ with $C=4$ .


##### Fitting the turns.

For both learners, three rules decide how the trainer fits a turn. For the policy gradient, the first rule applies only to turns with positive weights. A turn with a negative weight keeps all of its tokens. First, with the belief term, a fitted turn trains its whole text only when its belief line is verified. The belief line must satisfy $S(\beta;b)\geq S(\beta^{*};b)-0.1$ . Otherwise, its belief-line tokens are not counted and its reasoning gets weight 0. The turn then trains its accepted action alone, with the reasoning and the line kept as context. With the action term alone, every fitted turn trains its whole text. Second, a turn has two blocks of tokens, the reasoning and the decision. Each block takes its own gradient step with its own clip of the gradient norm. The objective and its minimizer stay the same. The split keeps the long and noisy gradient of the reasoning from setting the pace of the short decision block. Third, the likelihood $\pi_{\phi}$ uses the sampler of the policy. For the reasoning, it uses the sampling temperature, the top- $p$ and top- $k$ cuts and the presence penalty. For the decision, it uses the same temperature and presence penalty with no cut. Under a cut, the likelihood of a token is its tempered probability renormalized over the kept tokens. The sampled token always counts as kept. The fixed point is thus the policy used by the agent.


##### Histories and sample sets.

In phase $k$ , the teacher action replaces the agent action with probability $\xi_{k}=\xi_{0}\,\gamma^{k}$ . As in DAgger ( Ross et al., 2011 ) , the share of teacher actions in $\mu_{k}$ thus decreases with $k$ . In our runs of two phases, the probability $\xi_{k}$ falls from 0.5 to 0.465, and in the five phases on Qwen3.5-4B to 0.374. On half of the training scenes, one more episode starts with random evidence actions. Because $q_{k}$ does not change inside a phase, the samples of $\pi_{k}$ stay valid for all of its updates. One sample set of $H$ histories thus serves the whole phase. Following the dynamic sampling of DAPO ( Yu et al., 2025 ) , we draw the batches in random passes and only from histories that give a gradient. These are the histories in which one of the agent’s own turns has an accepted action.


##### Step limit.

The M-step fits only a few samples of $q_{k}$ at each history. It can thus also fit their noise. To limit this, the trainer computes $\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{k}\,\|\,\pi_{\phi})$ after each update. It computes this KL exactly at every position of the probe turns, which are turns of $\pi_{k}$ from the first batch of the phase:

[Tableau] $$\Delta_{k}(\phi)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{v}\pi_{k}(v\mid x_{<p})\log\frac{\pi_{k}(v\mid x_{<p})}{\pi_{\phi}(v\mid x_{<p})}\leq\varepsilon,$$ (16)

where $\mathcal{P}$ is the set of token positions of the probe turns, $x_{<p}$ is the prefix before position $p$ , and $v$ runs over the vocabulary at the sampling temperature. When $\Delta_{k}>\varepsilon$ , the phase stops. The model after that update is $\pi_{k+1}$ . This rule follows the early stop of PPO implementations at 1.5 times a target KL ( Schulman et al., 2017 ; Achiam, 2018 ) and the KL limit in the M-step of MPO ( Abdolmaleki et al., 2018 ) .

##### Bayes view.

Let $O$ be the event “the teacher accepts the turn”, with $p(O\mid h,x)=e^{R(h,x)/\alpha}$ . This is a probability because $R\leq 0$ . Then $q_{k}(x\mid h)=p(x\mid h,O)$ under the prior $\pi_{k}$ . The E-step is Bayes’ rule, and the M-step is the M-step of EM ( Dempster et al., 1977 ) .


##### Factors of $q$ .

$R$ depends on the decision $d$ only. Thus

[Tableau] $\displaystyle q_{k}(z,\beta,u\mid h)\;\propto$ $\displaystyle\underbrace{\pi_{k}(z\mid h)}_{\text{reasoning}}\ \cdot\ \underbrace{\pi_{k}(\beta\mid h,z)\,e^{R_{\beta}(\beta;b)/\alpha}}_{\text{belief line}}$ (17) $\displaystyle\cdot\ \underbrace{\pi_{k}(u\mid h,z,\beta)\,\pi^{*}(u\mid b)^{1/\alpha}}_{\text{action}},$

with $R_{\beta}=S_{\text{dec}}+\eta\,(S+W)$ the belief term of equation 5 . For the action, with $\alpha=1$ , $q_{k}$ is the normalized product of the policy and the teacher policy. With the action term alone, $R_{\beta}=0$ . A line then gains weight only through the actions that follow it. With the belief term, $q_{k}$ also moves the policy toward lines with a high belief score. For the reasoning, $q_{k}(z\mid h)\propto\pi_{k}(z\mid h)\,\mathbb{E}_{d\sim\pi_{k}(\cdot\mid h,z)}[e^{R(h,d)/\alpha}]$ . Thus no teacher text is a target for $z$ .


##### The M-step.

The M-step projects $q_{k}$ onto the model class by a weighted maximum likelihood on the samples of the agent:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\pi_{k+1}$ $\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\phi}\ \mathbb{E}_{h}\,\mathrm{KL}\big(q_{k}(\cdot\mid h)\,\|\,\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid h)\big)$ (18) $\displaystyle\approx\arg\max_{\phi}\ \textstyle\sum_{h}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{w}_{i}\,\log\pi_{\phi}(x_{i}\mid h).$

The trainer draws $J$ turns from the weights by systematic resampling ( Kitagawa, 1996 ) and fits each draw with the weight $1/J$ . The expected gradient is then that of the weighted loss. The weights are not negative and sum to 1 at each history. The probability of a turn with a small weight goes down only because the probabilities sum to 1. The update thus needs no critic and no group baseline.


##### Mirror descent.

The prior $\pi_{k}$ stays fixed for the updates of phase $k$ . Then $\pi_{k+1}=\pi_{\phi}$ . Phase 0 uses the base model $\pi_{0}$ . If each projection is exact and $R$ is fixed,

[Tableau] $$\begin{gathered}\pi_{k}\;\propto\;\pi_{k-1}\,e^{R/\alpha}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\pi_{k}\;\propto\;\pi_{0}\,e^{kR/\alpha},\\ \pi_{k}(u\mid h,z,\beta)\;\propto\;\pi_{0}(u\mid h,z,\beta)\;\pi^{*}(u\mid b)^{\,k/\alpha}.\end{gathered}$$ (19)

Phase $k$ thus has the effective temperature $\alpha/k$ . As $k$ increases, the policy moves to the maximizers of $R$ . These are the teacher’s best action, and also the exact line $\beta^{*}$ with the belief term. In practice, $R$ is not fixed, because $\hat{\rho}$ is measured again at every update. In addition, $\mu_{k}$ changes from phase to phase. Equation 19 thus describes the ideal case. Each phase is a mirror-descent step on $R$ , as in policy mirror descent ( Lan, 2023 ; Tomar et al., 2022 ) . Unlike standard policy mirror descent, the step uses the teacher score $R$ in place of the Q-function of the current policy.

#### C.3 The policy-gradient learner

The policy gradient uses the histories, turns and rewards above. It replaces the weights of the E-step by those of equation 10 . Let $r_{i}=R(h,x_{i})+C\ell_{i}$ be the reward of turn $i$ with its length term. At each history, we compute the advantages $A_{i}=\tilde{r}_{i}-\tfrac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\tilde{r}_{j}$ over all of its sampled turns, the extra round included. Here $\tilde{r}_{i}=\max(r_{i},\max_{j}r_{j}-c)$ raises every reward to at least the best one minus $c=20$ nats. A turn with a very low reward thus cannot raise the advantages of all the others. The advantages also stay in $[-c,c]$ . The weight of a turn is $w_{i}=sA_{i}/N$ , with one factor $s$ per mini-batch. We do not divide by the standard deviation of the group, because the rewards are already in nats. Dividing would also give large weights to a group of nearly equal rewards. A turn with a rejected action gets a weight of at most zero. Its probability can thus be lowered but never raised. As with the weights of EM, the factor $s$ makes the absolute weights of a mini-batch sum to the number of its histories with a target. This factor only balances the mini-batches, because we use Adam ( Kingma and Ba, 2015 ) and clip the gradient norm of each block. Unlike EM, the policy gradient does not draw turns. It fits every turn with a nonzero weight. A phase uses its turns for several updates. A block with a positive weight therefore stops contributing once its mean log-ratio to $\pi_{k}$ per counted token rises above $\log(1+\delta)$ . A block with a negative weight stops once this log-ratio falls below $\log(1-\delta)$ . We use $\delta=0.2$ and compute the log-ratio once per phase. This rule follows the clipped probability ratio of PPO ( Schulman et al., 2017 ) .

In our runs, the policy gradient fitted about 1,300 turns per update and EM about 300. This is because the policy gradient gives a weight to every sampled turn, and the draws of EM fall on the few turns of high reward. The steps of the policy gradient were also larger.

RLVB in table 1 is trained with the action term in equation 5 alone. As a result, the rules above for the belief term do not apply to it. They apply to RLVB (EM) and to the ablation runs with the belief term in table 5 . For the two Qwen3-VL models, a phase has 25 updates and a sample set of 1,200 histories, of which 800 are used. The training takes 50 updates. Qwen3.5-4B learns faster. With phases of 25 updates, its reasoning became very short. We therefore give it a new sample set of 240 histories every 5 updates, of which 160 are used. We train it for 25 updates with no length reward. Section C.4 describes three more rules in the reward that we tested on Qwen3.5-4B.


#### C.4 Rules against steps without evidence


##### Why we tested the rules.

With phases of 5 updates, the agent of Qwen3.5-4B learned a second way to delay its answer. It took steps that bring no evidence, such as turning on the spot in MiniGrid. The action term lets such a step pass. In the teacher’s score, a step that brings no evidence costs only its time discount, which is 0.2 nats in most tasks and 0.5 nats in MineSweeper. The acceptance rule accepts every turn whose score is within $\log 20=3.0$ nats of the teacher’s best action. A step without evidence is therefore still accepted. The measured answer accuracy $\hat{\rho}$ of equation 12 can add to this. Where the agent’s answers are often wrong, $\hat{\rho}$ is low. Answering then has a low value, and the teacher itself asks for more steps.


##### The three rules.

All three rules change the reward of the action term, and all three are computed from the exact posterior.

- 1. Exact answer accuracy. The estimate $\hat{\varphi}$ of equation 12 stays at its prior mean of 1, so $\hat{\rho}(b)=b_{\max}$ . This is the accuracy of an agent that always reads its evidence correctly. The teacher’s choice between a step and an answer then depends on the evidence alone and not on the agent’s own errors.

- 2. A cap on steps without evidence. An illegal action gets a teacher score of at most the best action’s score minus 3.5 nats. So does a step that brings no expected evidence and is not one of the teacher’s best steps. The acceptance rule therefore rejects both. The teacher’s own moves in the training games follow the same capped scores.

- 3. The teacher’s turn as a positive example. With the cap, more histories have no accepted turn of the agent. At such a history, the update can only lower the probability of the agent’s turns. We then add the teacher’s own belief line and action to the turns of this history as a positive example.


##### Results.

We trained Qwen3.5-4B with the three rules in the same way as the reported run, with phases of 5 updates and 25 updates, and we tested it on the same three test seeds. Table 12 compares the two runs. With the rules, the accuracy is 1.6% higher on HiddenGames, 0.2% lower on Pro-Worlds and 2.9% higher on RoboQuest. In a paired test over the test games, only the gain on RoboQuest is significant ( $p=0.014$ ). On the HiddenGames tasks with an answer, a larger share of the steps adds evidence, 28.0% compared to 21.2%, and the agent takes fewer steps per episode. Fewer episodes end without an answer on all three benchmarks, for example 6.0% compared to 11.6% on Pro-Worlds. On HiddenGames and Pro-Worlds, the agent reaches sufficient evidence about as often as without the rules. On RoboQuest, it answers with sufficient evidence in 71.3% of the episodes, compared to 68.8%. #### C.5 One family of weights

The two weightings are two limits of one family. Let $r_{1},\ldots,r_{N}$ be the rewards of the turns at a history, $\bar{r}$ their mean, and $\omega_{i}^{\alpha}=e^{r_{i}/\alpha}/\sum_{j}e^{r_{j}/\alpha}$ the EM weights at temperature $\alpha$ , without the acceptance rule.

(i) As $\alpha\to 0$ , $\omega^{\alpha}$ puts all of its weight on the turns of highest reward. (ii) As $\alpha\to\infty$ , $\alpha\,(\omega_{i}^{\alpha}-1/N)\to(r_{i}-\bar{r})/N$ . (iii) The leave-one-out advantage is $r_{i}-\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j\neq i}r_{j}=\frac{N}{N-1}(r_{i}-\bar{r})$ .

For (i), $\omega_{i}^{\alpha}/\omega_{j}^{\alpha}=e^{(r_{i}-r_{j})/\alpha}$ tends to $0$ whenever $r_{i}<r_{j}$ . For (ii), $e^{r/\alpha}=1+r/\alpha+O(\alpha^{-2})$ , which gives $\omega_{i}^{\alpha}=\big(1+(r_{i}-\bar{r})/\alpha\big)/N+O(\alpha^{-2})$ . For (iii), $\sum_{j\neq i}r_{j}=N\bar{r}-r_{i}$ . ∎

By (ii) and (iii), the weights of the policy gradient at each history are $\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\alpha\,(\omega_{i}^{\alpha}-1/N)$ times the positive factor $sN/(N-1)$ . This holds without the floor, the limit $c$ and the acceptance rule. The policy gradient thus uses the centered EM weights $\omega_{i}^{\alpha}-1/N$ at high temperature. EM uses the uncentered weights. At the first update of a phase, $\pi_{\phi}=\pi_{k}$ and $\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi_{k}}\big[\nabla_{\phi}\log\pi_{\phi}(x\mid h)\big]=0$ . The centered and the uncentered weights thus give the same expected gradient of equation 9 . Their steps differ by a term of mean zero. By (i), EM at a low temperature fits only the best turn of each history, as rejection-sampling fine-tuning does ( Zelikman et al., 2022 ) . Our EM uses $\alpha=1$ . At this temperature, $e^{R}$ is the teacher’s probability of approving the turn.

The two learners differ in how far they move from $\pi_{k}$ on the same turns. The EM weights are non-negative and sum to one at each history. The loss of equation 9 is thus smallest when $\pi_{\phi}$ matches them on the sampled turns. The fit then settles near $q_{k}$ . The policy gradient also has negative weights, which keep lowering the probability of poor turns. Only the ratio clip and the step limit of equation 16 bound its steps.

### Appendix E Benchmarks in detail


##### HiddenGames.

HiddenGames is a mixture of seven tasks from different agent benchmarks. In each task, the agent must find a hidden target from what it observes. MineSweeper ( Wang et al., 2026a ) hides the mines of a small board. In MiniGrid KeyCorridor ( Chevalier-Boisvert et al., 2023 ) , the agent must find the room that holds the key. In FrozenLake ( Wang et al., 2025a ) , we hide the goal and the distant holes. In Sokoban ( Wang et al., 2025a ) , we hide the target square. The agent must thus explore before it can plan. The three Tabletop tasks are rendered scenes of a table ( Tao et al., 2025 ) . The agent must find which button lights which lamp, which cup hides a named object as in figure 1 , or which block slides farther. It sees only two camera images after each action. The environment of every task knows the prior and the likelihood of each observation. The teacher of section 3.2 can thus compute the exact posterior. We train and test on all seven tasks. The test split consists of 756 games whose seeds, maps and Tabletop colors are never seen in training.


##### Pro-Worlds.

We introduce Pro-Worlds, a benchmark of how visual agents gather, retain and combine evidence before they commit to an answer. Each game hides one of a few answers, for example which emotion a face shows or which fish a picture shows. The game draws its world as a grid of sites, each a tile of $16\times 16$ color cells. The agent sees one site at a time through a window, rendered as a $256\times 256$ picture. At each turn, the agent either moves the window by one site with ACTION: MOVE UP , DOWN , LEFT or RIGHT , or submits an answer with ACTION: SUBMIT . A move at the edge keeps the window in place but still counts. A play allows at most 35 moves. It ends with a submission, with a move when no moves are left, or after 43 turns. The prompt gives the rules of the game, the list of answers and every picture seen so far with its position. We place the start where the first view never decides the answer.

The engine knows every possible world of a game and can thus compute the exact posterior. This posterior holds the worlds consistent with every site seen so far, with the same prior weight for each answer. As in the other benchmarks, a submission is premature when the posterior of the top answer is below 0.95. Table 14 lists the 15 test games. We test on 20 plays of each game, 300 plays in all. Eight of the games are the training games of the benchmark, drawn at random from the 15. However, no model in this paper trains on Pro-Worlds.


##### RoboQuest.

RoboQuest ( Liu et al., 2026 ) consists of ten mobile manipulation tasks built around search, inspection and interactive testing. In each task, such as finding the odd parcel by weighing, the agent must gather information through physical interaction before it decides to finish. Liu et al . report that agents often stop exploring too early ( Liu et al., 2026 ) . Unlike the other two benchmarks, RoboQuest runs in a physics simulator of real kitchens. Its possible states cannot be listed, and there is no exact posterior. Many of its answers are also actions in the scene rather than a choice among listed options. The belief lines of all agents are therefore free text, such as Possible: blue water bottle may be in drawer 2 or another compartment . We use RoboQuest to test whether what an agent learns from exact posteriors transfers to a task without one. We use the text version of its decision-level setting, where the agent chooses high-level actions and scripted helpers carry them out. We test on 50 games of each task, 500 games in all.

### Appendix I The PPO (belief + action) baseline

PPO (belief + action) states a belief before each action. An LLM judge rewards this belief at every turn. A turn consists of the reasoning, a belief state and an action. The belief state is a list of bullet points inside <belief_state> tags, each with one word of certainty such as confirmed , probable or ruled out . The action is inside <action> tags. The turn receives the same input as the other methods, with the rules of the task, the actions and observations so far and the current observation. It also receives the belief state of the previous turn.

The belief reward of a turn is $R_{b}=f\,(s_{t}+s_{c}+d+o)/4$ . Here $f$ scores the format of the belief state by the number of bullets, a certainty word in each bullet, no repeats and the length. The judge score $s_{t}$ measures whether the new belief state takes in the newest observation. The judge score $s_{c}$ measures whether its claims are true. $d$ is the entropy of the certainty words, and $o$ is the outcome reward. To score $s_{c}$ , the judge checks the claims against a text description of the true hidden state. Only the judge sees this description. The judge is DeepSeek V4.1 Flash at temperature 0, without thinking. A failed judge call receives the batch mean of its term.

The tokens of the belief state are trained on $R_{b}$ , normalized over the belief states of the batch. The reasoning and action tokens are trained with PPO on the outcome reward $0.999^{t}\cdot\mathbb{1}[\text{correct}]$ . PPO uses a learned value function and GAE ( Schulman et al., 2016 ) with $\gamma=\lambda=1$ . Training uses LoRA with rank 16 and alpha 32, a learning rate of $10^{-5}$ and 50 updates of 16 episodes each, with no warm start. At test time, the prompt keeps the rules of the belief state but drops the list of task-specific fields and the format example used in training.

### Appendix K Why RLVB works: details

This section provides the details and additional tests underlying section 4.6 . Unless a table or figure states otherwise, all numbers are for Qwen3-VL-4B.


##### Measures.

A step refers to a move on Pro-Worlds, a lift or press in the Tabletop tasks, and an action such as opening a drawer or weighing two parcels on RoboQuest. A step adds evidence when it changes the exact posterior or, on RoboQuest, when it meets a new evidence requirement of the task, such as the agent now seeing a target object. On HiddenGames, we use the five tasks with an answer: the three Tabletop tasks, MiniGrid, and MineSweeper. On RoboQuest, requirements can be counted in eight of the ten tasks, so we omit Stamps and Puzzle Box from these measures. The chance of a correct answer is the exact posterior of the top answer, representing the accuracy of an agent that reads its evidence without error.


##### Reading the belief lines on RoboQuest.

On RoboQuest, belief lines are free text. The task has no posterior. We read every belief line in the test episodes of Base (w/ belief) and RLVB on the 361 of 400 instances that at least one method solves. These are 56,556 lines from the three models and three test seeds. Language-model readers judge each line against the trace of an episode that solved the same instance. The readers do not see which agent wrote the line. They record whether the line names a single answer or leaves part of the answer open. They also record whether it keeps the true answer. When the agent submits at that turn, they record whether the submission gives the answer that the line names. A second reader labels 2,595 of these lines. It agrees on whether the line names a single answer for 2,539 of them, on whether it keeps the true answer for 2,514, and on whether the submission gives the named answer for 2,562.
