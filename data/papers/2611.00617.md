## Words Caught in the Heart: Simulating Person-Specific Expression for Self-Opaque Clients in Psychotherapy

Psychotherapy clients may experience distress before they can coherently describe it, and their expression changes with emotion, perceived safety, and trust. Large language model-based client simulators often rely on clinically organized case descriptions, which can encourage responses explaining the client’s difficulties more clearly than their current understanding warrants. We propose Pandora , a psychotherapy client simulation framework that models how psychological experience becomes situated, individual expression. Pandora updates psychological and relational states in response to therapist interventions, constructs subjective appraisals and attributions, and plans response content and strategy. A separate expression planner combines these decisions with language patterns distilled from the client’s utterances to guide linguistic realization. We evaluate Pandora against five baselines on 115 cases from four counseling and motivational interviewing datasets. It achieves the highest expression similarity and overall disclosure-alignment score among the compared systems. In a four-evaluator study of 45 matched disclosure items, Pandora receives AI-like judgments in 7.2% of ratings, compared with 43.3–67.2% for the baselines and 35.6% without expression planning. The ablation also reduces expression similarity. These results support explicit expression planning in client simulation to improve alignment with individual language patterns and reduce perceived AI-like expression in the evaluated setting.


### 1 Introduction

> Figure 1 : An example of representative psychotherapy client simulators and our proposed framework, Pandora, responding to the same therapist utterance. In this case, the therapist misses emotional reflection, lowering the client’s perceived safety and increasing defensiveness. Only Pandora explicitly captures this omission and its psychological consequences, producing a brief, tentative response.

Realistic user simulation depends on capturing how people turn their experiences and intentions into language ( Levelt et al., 1999 ) . This requirement is particularly important in psychotherapy, where what a client says can shape the therapist’s understanding of the problem and the direction of the conversation ( Stiles et al., 1990 ; Pos et al., 2003 ; Blanchard and Farber, 2016 ; Safran and Muran, 1996 ) . Clients may seek help before they can give a coherent account of their difficulties, and their ability to express themselves can vary with emotional distress, perceived safety, and trust in the therapist ( Beukeboom and Semin, 2006 ; Farber et al., 2004 ; Blanchard and Farber, 2016 ) . A concern may initially emerge through a concrete incident or a tentative remark, then become more explicit as the conversation develops ( Gendlin, 1961 ; Stiles et al., 1990 ; Pos et al., 2003 ) . For simulators used in therapist training and evaluation, these differences matter: an unusually articulate client may make a difficult concern easier to identify and explore than it would be in practice ( da Silva et al., 2018 ; Kahn et al., 2008 ) . Credible simulation therefore requires attention to the relationship between a client’s psychological experience and its expression at a particular moment.

Existing client simulators model cognitive case formulations and conversational styles (Patient- $\Psi$ ), metacognition and openness (MindVoyager), and relational patterns and emotional reactions (PatientAct) ( Wang et al., 2024 ; Kim et al., 2025 ; Sabour et al., 2026 ) . These representations provide useful structure, but their organization often reflects the clinician’s explanatory account of the client. A case formulation connects experiences, beliefs, and coping patterns into an interpretation that the client may only partially recognize or articulate ( Eells et al., 1998 ; Persons, 2008 ) . Using this formulation to guide generation can consequently encourage responses with greater explanatory coherence than the client’s current understanding warrants ( Kim et al., 2025 ) . For example, when a client begins to recognize a recurring tendency to devalue personal achievements, that awareness need not immediately become a coherent explanation ( Gendlin, 1961 ) . It may instead appear through a specific experience: “Whenever someone praises me, I feel uncomfortable. It doesn’t really feel like something I accomplished.” The underlying pattern is present, but its expression remains tied to the immediate situation and incomplete. A conversational style label may alter the tone of such a response while leaving its psychological explanation unusually explicit ( Wang et al., 2024 ) .

To bridge this gap, we propose Pandora , a psychotherapy client simulation framework that models how psychological experience becomes individual expression. Drawing on the distinction between conceptual preparation and linguistic formulation in speech production ( Levelt et al., 1999 ) , Pandora explicitly plans both what a client communicates and how that client says it. As illustrated in Figure 1 , the framework first interprets the therapist’s intervention and updates the client’s psychological and relational states. It then constructs a subjective appraisal and attribution frame, capturing how the client experiences and understands the situation, and plans the response’s content, stance, and conversational purpose. A separate expression planner combines these decisions with language patterns distilled from the client’s own utterances to guide wording, hedging, emotional explicitness, and response length. In the illustrated exchange, an unanswered message becomes a brief conjecture, “Maybe he’s upset with me, or I said something wrong”, that conveys the client’s concern while preserving uncertainty about its meaning. This design connects evolving psychological states with a recognizable individual voice, making the transformation from subjective experience to situated speech an explicit part of client simulation.

We evaluate Pandora on 115 cases drawn from four counseling and motivational interviewing datasets, comparing it with five baselines and an ablation without expression planning. Automatic evaluation measures both disclosure alignment and expression fidelity against the corresponding source dialogues. A complementary human study assesses perceived AI-like expression on 45 matched disclosure items, with judgments from two counselors and two synthetic-data researchers. Pandora achieves the highest expression similarity and overall disclosure-alignment score among the compared systems. In human evaluation, its AI-like judgment rate is 7.2%, compared with 43.3–67.2% for the baselines. Removing expression planning raises this rate to 35.6% and reduces expression similarity, highlighting the contribution of linguistic realization beyond content planning. Together, these findings support explicit expression planning as a central component of client simulation, improving fidelity to individual language patterns while reducing perceived AI-like expression.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

- • We identify the gap between clinically organized client representations and clients’ own expression, and formulate psychotherapy client simulation around how psychological experience becomes situated speech (Section 3).

- • We propose Pandora , which connects psychological state updates with subjective interpretation, content planning, and a separate linguistic realization plan. The framework combines the demands of the current interaction with habitual language patterns to produce responses that reflect both the client’s psychological state and individual voice (Section 3).

- • We develop an evaluation combining disclosure alignment, expression fidelity, and human judgments of AI-like expression. Comparisons with five baselines on 115 cases, complemented by human assessment of 45 matched disclosure items, show stronger expression fidelity and less perceived AI-like expression; the ablation further supports the contribution of expression planning (Section 4).


### 2 Related Work

Existing user simulators model expression through linguistic controls, user profiles, and internal states. Personality-oriented generation controls hedging, verbosity, and lexical and syntactic choices ( Mairesse and Walker, 2011 ) ; mTAD models utterance traits such as emotion, fluency, and repetition, while USP infers personality and communication styles from conversations ( Ferreira et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2025a ) . More recent approaches reconstruct unexpressed thoughts or align cognitive, affective, and communication states before generating responses ( Wu et al., 2026b ; Wu et al., 2026a ) . Psychotherapy adds a specific modeling demand: how a client’s evolving understanding and experience of the interaction shape the expression of a concern. A concern may be felt before it can be coherently explained ( Gendlin, 1961 ) , and willingness to disclose it can be shaped by perceived safety and trust ( Farber et al., 2004 ) . Style controls and internal-state descriptions do not by themselves specify how these conditions jointly shape momentary expression. Pandora takes the client’s subjective interpretation as the starting point for expression planning. Subjective appraisal and attribution guide content planning, while a separate linguistic realization plan expresses that perspective through the client’s habitual language patterns under the current psychological and interactional conditions.

Existing client simulators seek realism through psychological profiles and mechanisms that regulate counseling behavior. Patient- $\Psi$ combines CBT case formulations with conversational styles, while Interactive Agents grounds simulated clients in expert-written role cards ( Wang et al., 2024 ; Qiu and Lan, 2026 ) . MindVoyager models cognitive recognition and disclosure through metacognition, openness, and rapport, whereas ConsistentMI separates readiness for behavioral change, action selection, and response generation ( Kim et al., 2025 ; Yang et al., 2025 ) . AnnaAgent models evolving emotions and complaints, and Eeyore learns depression-related client expression through expert-guided supervised and preference optimization ( Wang et al., 2025b ; Liu et al., 2025 ) . PatientAct connects clinical and relational formulations to emotional reactions and behaviors, expressing resistance through quantity, content, and style; PsyCLIENT guides responses through behavioral trajectories and reference content ( Sabour et al., 2026 ; Qiu et al., 2026 ) . These methods provide clinically grounded content and mechanisms for shaping interactional behavior. Our focus is how this material becomes the client’s own account: an explanatory pattern available to the simulator may remain only partly understood by the client. Pandora constructs a situated subjective interpretation through appraisal and attribution, then plans its content and linguistic realization using the client’s habitual language patterns. This explicitly targets the relationship between psychological understanding and individual expression in client simulation.


### 3 Method

Pandora organizes client simulation around the transformation of psychological experience into situated speech. Following the distinction between conceptual preparation and linguistic formulation in speech production ( Levelt et al., 1999 ) , the framework connects three modeling tasks: representing the client’s evolving experience of the interaction, planning a response from the client’s subjective perspective, and realizing that response in the client’s individual voice. Figure 1 shows the modular process: intervention evaluation and state update provide the context for subjective appraisal and attribution; content and response strategy planning organize the reply; and individual expression planning guides its linguistic realization.

At turn $t$ , the inputs are the therapist utterance $x_{t}$ , preceding dialogue $H_{t-1}$ , previous psychological state $s_{t-1}$ , case material $M_{t}$ , and an individual language profile $\rho$ . The case material $M_{t}$ contains memories and events available at the current dialogue point, with future events excluded according to their occurrence time. All psychological depths in this material are available to planning. This separates the case information available to the simulator from the client’s evolving understanding and manner of expressing it.


#### 3.1 Therapist Intervention Evaluation and State Update

This module provides a persistent representation of the client’s experience of the interaction ( Hill et al., 1993 ; Safran and Muran, 1996 ) . A rule-based evaluator identifies opportunities for clarification, questioning, and emotional reflection, together with their use in the therapist’s utterance. The resulting intervention signal represents both the techniques used and how they address the conversational context, including opportunities left unaddressed.

The state distinguishes affective, relational, defensive, and reflective dimensions, allowing willingness to disclose and capacity to articulate an experience to vary separately ( Farber et al., 2004 ; Grant et al., 2002 ) . The full representation is specified in Appendix A . Bounded rule-based transitions combine intervention-related and memory-related increments with the preceding state. These dimensions jointly guide subsequent planning: a client can gain clarity about a concern while remaining hesitant to discuss it. Figure 1 illustrates this combination of cognitive and relational responses. Disclosure readiness shapes the client’s stance, while the supplied case material remains available. Update rules and numerical settings are provided in Appendix A .


#### 3.2 Subjective Appraisal and Attribution

This module turns the updated state and case material into the perspective from which the client speaks. Subjective appraisal $a_{t}$ represents what an experience means to the client, including its emotional significance, perceived threat, implications for the self and relationships, and confidence in that interpretation. Attribution $b_{t}$ represents how the client currently explains the experience. Both are constructed using the updated state, case material, and intervention signal.

Appraisal and attribution distinguish the explanatory information in a case from the account the client can currently formulate. State-conditioned attribution ranges from immediate behavior and circumstances to tentative relational interpretations and connections with underlying beliefs. The intervention signal further conditions how clearly this account is formulated. Thus, the same case material can support different subjective accounts as the interaction develops, preserving uncertainty even when the client begins to understand a concern more clearly.


#### 3.3 Content Target, Client Stance, and Response Strategy

This module organizes the subjective account into a response to the current therapist turn. A content plan $d_{t}$ represents available case content and client stance, while a response-act plan $r_{t}$ specifies the immediate conversational purpose:

[Tableau] $$C_{t}=(a_{t},b_{t},d_{t},r_{t}).$$ (1)

The content plan carries the supplied memory identifiers together with a stance such as hesitant, defensive, confused, emotionally overwhelmed, or open. The updated state and intervention signal determine this stance, linking case content to the client’s current engagement with the conversation.

The response strategy makes the plan responsive to the therapist’s utterance. Factual questions favor an answer first with limited introspection; practical requests favor brevity; reflections invite confirmation or refinement; and challenges or invalidation allow resistance or clarification. Separating response purpose from stance allows a hesitant client to answer a question directly while expressing uncertainty about its psychological meaning. These decisions form the semantic input to expression planning.


#### 3.4 Individual Expression Planning

Expression planning translates the semantic plan into realization guidance for the particular client. It combines a stable individual language profile distilled offline from the client’s utterances with the psychological and conversational conditions of the current turn:

[Tableau] $$L_{t}=F_{\mathrm{realize}}(s_{t},C_{t},H_{t-1},\rho).$$ (2)

This interface makes individual voice and the current interaction explicit inputs to realization.

The language profile summarizes habitual discourse markers, hedging, emotional explicitness, utterance length distributions, and sentence counts. Length statistics describe central tendencies and distributional ranges, while marker statistics capture expressions recurring in the client’s own language. Reusing this profile across turns provides concrete guidance for maintaining individual voice.

The planner specifies speech style, pronoun focus, certainty, emotional explicitness, hedging, and length and sentence-count targets. These variables draw on research on psychologically informative word use ( Pennebaker et al., 2003 ) , controllable stylistic generation ( Mairesse and Walker, 2011 ) , hedging under uncertainty ( Smith and Clark, 1993 ) , and the communicative functions of filled pauses ( Clark and Fox Tree, 2002 ) . Client stance establishes a base speech style, attribution guides focus on the self, others, or the situation, and response strategy adapts these choices to the current exchange. An answer-first strategy, for example, favors direct wording and low emotional explicitness, while resistance or uncertain interpretation favors tentative expression. The individual profile separately conditions habitual hedging, allowing a direct answer to retain a familiar marker such as “maybe.” This combination preserves the client’s voice while adapting expression to the current exchange.


#### 3.5 Response Generation and Execution

The client LLM realizes the intermediate plans as a natural-language utterance. Its prompt combines the psychological context, subjective appraisal and attribution, content and response strategy plans, and linguistic realization plan with the client profile, interaction instructions, current therapist utterance, and dialogue history. The model is instructed to answer the current request from the client’s perspective and follow the individual realization guidance, with internal identifiers and numerical state fields kept out of the utterance. Explicit rules organize the intermediate plans, and the LLM supplies their linguistic realization.

Content targeting and claim verification track the expression of case material across turns, as specified in Appendix A . The resulting dialogue context supports the next planning cycle. A stage-by-stage running example is provided in Appendix B .


### 4 Experiment

We evaluate Pandora’s central design choice: explicitly planning how a client’s subjective perspective takes linguistic form in the current exchange. The experiments address three questions: RQ1 , does Pandora better reproduce individual expression patterns and reduce perceived AI-like expression? RQ2 , what does the expression planner contribute beyond the preceding psychological and content planning stages? RQ3 , are these expression gains accompanied by faithful disclosure timing and structure? We use automatic comparisons on 115 cases and human judgments of matched disclosures from 45 cases. The full-system comparisons address expression fidelity, the expression-planning ablation assesses the contribution of the realization stage, and disclosure metrics examine how case material unfolds in the generated conversation.


#### 4.1 Data Collection and Case Selection

Evaluating client expression requires source material that reveals both what a client discloses and how that disclosure unfolds through interaction. The retrieval and assessment procedure is detailed in Appendix C .

After semantic screening and removal of exact and near duplicates, the final collection contains 115 cases: 97 Chinese and 18 English, comprising 97 PsyDial-D4, 11 AnnoMI, five Eeyore Full, and two ConsistentMI cases. The selected dialogues provide reference trajectories for evaluating disclosure timing, content organization, and individual expression across turns.

#### 4.2 Evaluation Framework

We evaluate two complementary aspects of client simulation: disclosure content (RQ3) and expression style (RQ1 and RQ2). Content evaluation examines how case material unfolds across turns, while style evaluation combines textual similarity with direct human judgments of AI-like expression.


##### 4.2.1 Disclosure Content

We use a shared memory-card codebook with levels L1–L3 and compare the first disclosure of each card in the reference and generated dialogues. For controller-trace inputs, disclosure is determined by evidence-grounded claim verification: a card is completed when all its required claims have been supported cumulatively by client utterances. A generator’s self-reported disclosure depth is not used as the card-disclosure observation. LDDA measures disclosure timing, DS measures prerequisite satisfaction, BFE and BSE measure bundling errors, and GDS aggregates timing, dependency, and bundling fidelity. The complete definitions and conventions are provided in Appendix D .


##### 4.2.2 Expression Style

Following the stylometric approach used in Eval4Sim ( Bao et al., 2026 ) , we represent client utterances using character 4-gram TF-IDF with up to 4,000 features and cosine similarity. We adapt this representation to paired comparisons between generated client speech and its corresponding source dialogue, rather than reproducing Eval4Sim’s trained authorship verifier or aggregate PAN consistency score. To distinguish expression patterns from shared subject matter, we compute both raw and content-reduced similarities. Full weighting and matched-block definitions are provided in Appendix D . We report case-averaged raw and content-reduced similarities separately, with larger values indicating closer textual expression. The current 115-case evaluation fits a separate reference instrument for each case and aggregates matched similarities; it does not compute cross-client retrieval scores. The masking lexicon is Chinese-oriented and compresses Latin-script words into generic masks, so the content-reduced view provides coarser evidence for English cases. Neither view establishes psychological authenticity or clinical equivalence, and their absolute difference is not interpreted as a measured content effect.

We sampled 45 disclosure items from distinct cases, with 15 items at each memory level (L1–L3). Each item presents eight candidates in randomized order: the reference, five baselines, Pandora, and its expression-planning ablation. Four evaluators (two counselors and two synthetic-data researchers) independently judged every candidate using a binary response: $y_{isr}=1$ for AI-like expression and $0$ otherwise, for item $i$ , source $s$ , and evaluator $r$ . We report the AI-like judgment rate (AIR) as the mean of these binary judgments, yielding 180 judgments per source. Across all 360 candidates, mean pairwise agreement is 62.9%, and positive agreement for AI-like judgments is 53.7% (95% CI: 49.4–58.1%). Full presentation, annotation guidelines, aggregation, and uncertainty details are provided in Appendix E .

State-based baselines differ substantially in expression fidelity. Consistent MI, which conditions response actions on its state, is the strongest baseline on both similarity measures. MindVoyager’s cognitive and relational conditioning yields a content-reduced similarity of 0.408, below the role-card-based Interactive Agents at 0.501. This ordering suggests that psychological context alone does not ensure fidelity to a client’s manner of speaking. Pandora explicitly carries the client’s subjective interpretation and response purpose into individual realization, achieving content-reduced/raw similarities of 0.566/0.385, exceeding Consistent MI by 0.053/0.032. The same ordering holds on the 97 Chinese cases: 0.489/0.422 for Pandora, 0.427/0.385 for Consistent MI, and 0.362/0.337 for the expression ablation. The advantage therefore persists where the content-reduction procedure is best matched to the language. The design implication is to specify how psychological context shapes wording, certainty, and utterance form through an explicit realization interface.

The ablation provides the clearest evidence for this separation. Without expression planning, Pandora retains a GDS of 0.828, above the strongest baseline’s 0.664, but its content-reduced similarity falls to 0.458, below Consistent MI. Thus, the upstream state, subjective interpretation, and response plans can organize disclosure effectively while leaving individual expression insufficiently specified. Restoring the expression module raises content-reduced similarity from 0.458 to 0.566 and raw similarity from 0.311 to 0.385, gains of 0.108 and 0.074. LDDA, DS, and GDS also improve by 0.042, 0.079, and 0.043. These descriptive comparisons support a modular design in which semantic planning organizes the client’s account and a separate realization stage preserves individual voice. The ablation evaluates the expression module as a whole, combining the individual language profile with its conditioning on the current interaction.

The content-side results complement the expression gains. Pandora achieves a GDS of 0.871, compared with 0.664 for Consistent MI and 0.828 for the ablation, with timing alignment of 0.852 and dependency satisfaction of 0.875. Its timing advantage spans L1–L3 (0.843, 0.854, and 0.866), exceeding every baseline and the ablation at each level (Figure 2 ). This pattern supports organizing available case material through a situated subjective account and response plan, alongside planning its expression. The strongest gains concern when material is disclosed and how supporting disclosures precede deeper ones. Bundling reflects a different tradeoff: Interactive Agents has the lowest frequency error (0.099 versus Pandora’s 0.102), while Pandora has the lowest size error (0.127 versus Consistent MI’s 0.128). Effective simulation therefore calls for coordinated planning of disclosure timing, dependencies, and individual expression; brevity or low bundling error alone captures only part of that goal.

Human judgments show that the realization difference is perceptible (Table 3 ). Among the baselines, Interactive Agents has the lowest AIR, whereas Consistent MI leads the automatic expression metrics: textual similarity and perceived AI-like expression capture complementary properties. Pandora improves both, receiving 13 AI-like votes out of 180 (7.2%), compared with 43.3–67.2% for the baselines, a 36.1-point reduction relative to Interactive Agents. Removing expression planning raises AIR to 35.6%, a paired difference of 28.3 points (exploratory case-bootstrap 95% CI: 20.6–36.1 points). Together with the similarity results, this contrast supports explicitly realizing the planned account through the client’s habitual language patterns and current interactional stance. A well-organized psychological account benefits from a dedicated mechanism for expressing it in a recognizable client voice.

Pandora also has the lowest generated-system AIR in both evaluator groups: 10.0% among counselors and 4.4% among synthetic-data researchers, compared with 40.0% and 31.1% for the ablation. With two evaluators per group, these are descriptive comparisons. ### 5 Conclusion

We introduced Pandora to model how a psychotherapy client’s psychological experience becomes situated, individual expression. The framework separates subjective appraisal and attribution, content and response strategy planning, and linguistic realization, allowing the client’s current perspective and habitual language patterns to guide the final utterance. Comparisons with five baselines on 115 cases show stronger expression fidelity alongside closer alignment in disclosure timing and dependencies. Human assessment of 45 matched disclosures further finds less perceived AI-like expression. Removing expression planning reduces textual similarity and increases perceived AI-like expression, supporting the contribution of a separate realization stage beyond psychological and content planning. These findings make the relationship between subjective understanding and individual speech a concrete target for client simulation: what a client understands and intends to communicate must be considered together with how that understanding is expressed in each exchange.


### Limitations

Our evaluation focuses on expression fidelity and perceived AI-like language in selected counseling dialogues. The case collection is predominantly Chinese and includes reconstructed or generated material; broader coverage of languages, cultures, and independently collected client speech remains to be evaluated. Psychological state variables and their updates provide an operational model of the client’s perspective. Their numerical settings are design choices, and the current ablation evaluates expression planning as a whole. Finer-grained comparisons could clarify the contribution of individual conditioning signals.

Human evaluation provides direct evidence about perceived expression, but the present study uses a small panel and isolated utterances, with variation across raters. Source-reveal usage was not logged, so complete blinding cannot be confirmed. Extending evaluation to complete conversations and larger panels would test whether the expression benefits persist across interaction contexts. Whether these benefits improve therapist training or downstream therapist evaluation remains an important question for future application studies.


### Ethical Statement

Pandora is intended for research on psychotherapy client simulation. Our evaluation examines expression fidelity, disclosure alignment, and perceived AI-like expression. These measures do not establish clinical safety or treatment efficacy, and the simulator should not substitute for professional clinical assessment or care. Its internal psychological states are modeling constructs, not clinical findings about real individuals.

We obtained the counseling and motivational interviewing datasets through their official distribution channels under the applicable licenses and required access approvals. The reference collection includes reconstructed or generated dialogues and is not uniformly verified speech from real clients. Source provenance, upstream privacy practices, and our source-specific release policy are documented in Appendix I .

Human evaluation involved four co-authors who had not viewed the original datasets before the assessment. Participation was voluntary, informed consent was obtained, and evaluators could withdraw at any time. The secondary-data study and author-provided evaluation are described in Appendix I .

Client simulation may oversimplify psychological experiences or reproduce biases in the source material. Future applications in therapist training or evaluation should involve professional oversight and independent validation, with clear disclosure that the interactions are simulated.

### Appendix A Implementation Details


#### A.1 Preprocessing and Role Distillation

The preprocessing routines were written by the authors and are available in the code repository listed on the first page. For each selected dialogue, we normalize speaker roles and message indices, then construct case information from the source profile and reviewed client evidence, preserving the original utterances and any available counselor system prompt. Client-grounded memory extraction identifies concise, first-person autobiographical facts supported by client speech; the structured outputs are checked for source language, first-person form, and valid supporting client-turn indices before duplicate facts are removed. Separate client and therapist packages are then distilled from the corresponding speaker turns. The client package combines case content with observed utterance-length distributions and habitual discourse markers to guide individual expression. The therapist package preserves the source role instructions and summarizes observable questioning, response length, representative utterances, and conversational progression; keyword-based intent categories organize retrieval of source examples. These offline packages supply stable case material and role-specific behavior, while Pandora models the client’s changing psychological state and situated expression online.


#### A.2 Psychological State Representation

The psychological state contains twelve numerical dimensions: distress, emotional arousal ( Russell, 2003 ; Shenk and Fruzzetti, 2011 ) , defensiveness, avoidance tendency ( Cramer, 2000 ; Hayes et al., 1996 ) , trust, perceived safety, disclosure readiness ( Bordin, 1979 ; Farber et al., 2004 ) , self-awareness, insight ( Grant et al., 2002 ) , mentalization ( Bateman and Fonagy, 2010 ) , core-belief activation ( Beck and Haigh, 2014 ) , and problem clarity. Textual fields describe dominant emotions and needs, presenting concerns, interpersonal patterns, beliefs, and the current self-explanation.


#### A.3 Psychological State Updates

Numerical updates are postprocessed with field-specific step limits, intervention-type scaling, directional adjustments, and final value bounds. The scaling factors for practical exchanges, factual questions, reflective statements, challenges, and invalidation are 0.10, 0.40, 0.50, 0.80, and 1.00, respectively. Trust and perceived safety additionally use a weighted update with 0.75 on the preceding value and 0.25 on the proposed bounded value before directional adjustments. The resulting state $s_{t}$ provides a shared context for the downstream planners. This update is rule-based and does not require a separate LLM to infer a new psychological state.


#### A.4 Individual Length Planning

Length planning uses the client’s median utterance length, or the mean when the median is unavailable, together with the observed distributional range. The central target is scaled by 0.78 for factual answers and 0.55 for practical replies; sentence-count statistics provide an additional target when available. These targets provide prompt-level guidance during generation. Recent utterances inform constraints against repeated explanations and nearly identical sentences, while familiar fillers and hedges may recur naturally. When individual statistics are unavailable, length planning uses the psychological and conversational inputs.


#### A.5 Content Targeting and Claim Verification

Content planning and coverage control operate at complementary levels. Pandora’s private context provides up to three memory cues and encourages one main cue, linking the available material to the current response plan. All evaluated methods also receive the full neutral fact catalog, with shuffled identifiers and no L1–L3 metadata. A shared coverage controller tracks progress across turns and supplies a deadline target when needed, prioritizing required core facts over brevity. After generation, a separate claim-verification step checks the candidate utterance and accumulates supported facts; missing required claims can trigger bounded regeneration. A memory card is completed when all its required claims have been supported across turns. The accepted utterance and coverage record are then committed for the next turn. The case memory bank remains fixed, while psychological state and accepted dialogue history carry forward.


#### A.6 Computational Resources and Budget

We used GPT-5.4 mini (OpenAI; API identifier gpt-5.4-mini ) for all LLM-based client simulation, case screening, and model-assisted evaluation. All model inference ran through APIs on provider-managed infrastructure, with an estimated total API cost of $600.

### Appendix C Case Retrieval and Semantic Review

A static profile provides limited evidence of individual expression patterns, while a brief exchange may not expose the connections between current difficulties and underlying experiences or beliefs. We therefore constructed a collection of extended counseling and motivational interviewing dialogues in which psychologically meaningful content develops across turns and is grounded in client speech.

We assembled 5,754 standardized records from PsyDial-D4 ( Qiu and Lan, 2025 ) , AnnoMI ( Wu et al., 2022 ) , ConsistentMI ( Yang et al., 2025 ) , and Eeyore ( Liu et al., 2025 ) . Speaker roles and message indices were unified while preserving source provenance and available profiles. To ensure sufficient conversational context, we required at least 24 counselor–client exchanges, 500 client characters, and a speaker-alternation ratio of 0.70, retaining 2,084 dialogues. Because lexical signals can miss relevant disclosures, we broadened retrieval beyond cases matching depth, causal, and elicitation cues: the candidate pool also included all structurally eligible Eeyore Full, ConsistentMI, and AnnoMI cases, followed by the 100 highest-ranked remaining retrieval candidates. Their union yielded 509 cases for semantic assessment.

Dialogue length alone does not establish whether a case supports expression evaluation. We therefore assessed candidates using a common, evidence-constrained rubric covering surface information, psychological patterns, deeper drivers, causal links, counselor elicitation, progressive disclosure, cross-turn dependency, and the recoverability of client profiles and expression styles. Depth judgments required indexed evidence from client utterances; counselor interpretations and symptom-severity vocabulary alone were insufficient. The documented assessment inputs comprised the source dialogue and scoring requirements, without simulator outputs or comparative performance scores. A second model-assisted assessment, blinded to the first assessment’s scores, evidence, ranking, and selection status, checked these judgments and examined leading interpretations, premature profile disclosure, repetition, unsupported depth, profile inconsistencies, and role or coherence errors. Base-score disagreements exceeding 12 points triggered a third blind assessment.

To prevent a high aggregate score from compensating for missing evidence, inclusion required minimum consensus scores for psychological-pattern depth (8/15), deeper-driver depth (10/20), causal links (7/15), counselor elicitation (7/15), and progressive disclosure (7/15), together with valid supporting evidence and no disqualifying validity flags. These criteria applied to every candidate without source, language, topic, or diagnosis quotas or manual inclusion overrides. The collection retains source provenance, indexed selection evidence, scores, exclusion and duplicate summaries, and the assessment rubric and prompts, allowing the selection decisions to be inspected.


### Appendix D Evaluation Metric Definitions


#### D.1 Expression Fidelity

Following the topic-masking principle of Stamatatos (2017) , our deterministic transformation preserves a Chinese function/stance/filler lexicon, punctuation, symbols, and turn boundaries while replacing other character runs with generic masks. This masking and the reference-block sampling below are adaptations for our evaluation. We use TF-IDF weighting ( Salton and Buckley, 1988 ) , with the smoothing and normalization below:

[Tableau] $$w_{dk}=c_{dk}\left[\log\frac{1+D}{1+\mathrm{df}_{k}}+1\right],\quad\mathbf{v}_{d}=\frac{\mathbf{w}_{d}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{d}\|_{2}},$$ (3)

where $c_{dk}$ is the occurrence count of 4-gram $k$ , $D$ the number of reference blocks, and $\mathrm{df}_{k}$ its reference-block document frequency. Zero vectors receive zero similarity. Vocabulary and IDF are fitted only on reference blocks and shared by all methods within the same case comparison.

To limit text-length effects, we sample $B=200$ contiguous blocks with replacement from each client transcript using seed 42. Within each case, every method and the reference use the same raw-character block length, capped at 500 and reduced to accommodate the shortest transcript. With $\phi_{v}$ denoting the raw or content-reduced transformation, the matched expression score is

[Tableau] $$S_{i,v}^{(a)}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\cos\left(\mathbf{v}_{\phi_{v}(A_{i,b}^{(a)})},\mathbf{v}_{\phi_{v}(R_{i,b})}\right),$$ (4)

where $A_{i,b}^{(a)}$ and $R_{i,b}$ are sampled generated and reference blocks for case $i$ .


#### D.2 Memory Disclosure

Let $\tau_{m}^{r}$ and $\tau_{m}^{g}$ denote the first client turn disclosing card $m$ , and $T_{r},T_{g}$ the dialogue lengths in client turns. We normalize positions as

[Tableau] $$p_{m}^{x}=\frac{\tau_{m}^{x}-1}{T_{x}-1},\qquad x\in\{r,g\},$$ (5)

with position zero for a single-turn dialogue. A reference-observed card absent from the generated dialogue is assigned $p_{m}^{g}=1$ . For the set $\mathcal{M}_{\ell}^{r}$ of reference-observed cards at level $\ell$ , timing alignment is

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{LDDA}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{L}|}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}}\left(1-\frac{\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}_{\ell}^{r}}|p_{m}^{g}-p_{m}^{r}|}{|\mathcal{M}_{\ell}^{r}|}\right),$$ (6)

where $\mathcal{L}$ contains levels with at least one reference-observed card. Matching card identities prevents differently timed memories from compensating for one another; averaging across levels limits domination by numerous surface memories. The missing-card convention is a timing surrogate, rather than a separate coverage penalty, and cards unobserved in the reference do not enter LDDA.

Timing alone can overlook whether a deeper disclosure is preceded by its supporting material. We therefore measure prerequisite satisfaction using explicit reference constraints when available. Otherwise, for each reference-observed L2/L3 card, the implementation selects the nearest earlier reference card from each shallower level, retaining ties. These reference-derived prerequisites encode the disclosure order observed in the source dialogue. Let $\mathcal{P}(b)$ be the prerequisites of target card $b$ , $\mathcal{B}_{\ell}$ the targets at level $\ell$ , and $\mathcal{H}$ the nonempty target levels among L2 and L3. We compute

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{DS}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}|}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_{\ell}|}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{\ell}}\frac{\sum_{a\in\mathcal{P}(b)}I_{g}(a,b)}{|\mathcal{P}(b)|},$$ (7)

where $I_{g}(a,b)=1$ only if both cards are disclosed and $\tau_{a}^{g}<\tau_{b}^{g}$ . Missing cards and same-turn disclosure do not satisfy a prerequisite.

To capture disclosure pacing, let $n_{t}^{x}$ be the number of cards first disclosed at turn $t$ , and $\mathcal{D}_{x}=\{t:n_{t}^{x}>0\}$ . Reference turns are first mapped to the generated dialogue length by $1+\operatorname{round}[(t-1)(T_{g}-1)/(T_{r}-1)]$ , with turn one used when either dialogue has a single turn. Bundle frequency and excess bundle size are

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathrm{BF}_{x}$ $\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{D}_{x}}\mathbf{1}[n_{t}^{x}\geq 2]}{|\mathcal{D}_{x}|},$ (8) $\displaystyle\mathrm{BS}_{x}$ $\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{D}_{x}}(n_{t}^{x}-1)}{|\mathcal{D}_{x}|}.$

Their errors are $\mathrm{BFE}=|\mathrm{BF}_{g}-\mathrm{BF}_{r}|$ and $\mathrm{BSE}=|\mathrm{BS}_{g}-\mathrm{BS}_{r}|$ . The overall gradual-disclosure score is

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\mathrm{BFid}$ $\displaystyle=\max\{0,1-(\mathrm{BFE}+\mathrm{BSE})/2\},$ (9) $\displaystyle\mathrm{GDS}$ $\displaystyle=(\mathrm{LDDA}+\mathrm{DS}+\mathrm{BFid})/3.$

Together, these metrics characterize disclosure timing, prerequisite satisfaction, and bundling. Higher LDDA, DS, and GDS indicate closer reference alignment; lower BFE and BSE indicate closer pacing. Undefined components remain unavailable, and GDS is computed only when all three components exist.


### Appendix E Human Evaluation Protocol

Human evaluation tests whether the expression benefits in RQ1 and RQ2 are perceptually meaningful. A response can reproduce a memory and resemble the source text while still sounding like polished AI-generated self-analysis. We therefore ask evaluators to judge the client’s expression directly. X-TURING reports divergent human and LLM judgments, including different model preferences on the same conversations ( Wu et al., 2025 ) ; MirrorBench reports moderate human–LLM correlation for pairwise indistinguishability and sensitivity to the choice of judge ( Hathidara et al., 2026 ) . These findings motivate human assessment to complement stylometric similarity.


#### E.1 Matched Disclosure Presentation

Each item contains eight candidates: the source reference, Pandora, five baselines, and Pandora without expression planning. Candidates are aligned to the same case and disclosure item using its required claim evidence. Each excerpt is one complete client utterance; counselor text is removed to avoid attributing the counselor’s style to the client. Extraction balances comparable lengths with preserving complete utterances. Source provenance is retained for every reference excerpt.


#### E.2 Evaluator Qualifications and Interface

All four evaluators are co-authors of this paper. Both counselors had received formal training in psychological counseling, held relevant professional qualifications, and had over 1,000 hours of counseling experience each. Each researcher had published at least one first-author paper on synthetic data at a top-tier AI or machine learning conference. A mobile-compatible interface presents one case per page with eight candidates, labeled A–H in a randomized order, and requests a judgment for every candidate independently. Evaluators assign a binary label to each candidate independently. Each reference excerpt appears anonymously once per case.


#### E.3 Endpoint Aggregation and Uncertainty

Evaluators directly assigned binary judgments using the rubric in Appendix G : AI-like expression receives label 1 and otherwise label 0. We report

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{AIR}_{s}=\frac{1}{45R}\sum_{i=1}^{45}\sum_{r=1}^{R}y_{isr},$$ (10)

where $R=4$ , yielding 180 judgments per source and 1,440 judgments in total. Lower AIR indicates less perceived AI-like expression. We retain individual binary judgments and report raw pairwise agreement and positive agreement, which excludes jointly negative judgments.


### Appendix F Supplementary Evaluation Results

Pandora achieves the highest timing alignment at every memory level, and the full model exceeds the expression-planning ablation at L1, L2, and L3. The consistency across levels complements the aggregate results: closer disclosure timing accompanies the gains in individual expression across material of different psychological depths.


### Appendix G Binary Annotation Guidelines

The following rubric guided the binary judgments in our human evaluation. An online demonstration lets readers inspect all 45 cases and the binary response options without recording responses. The instructions below summarize annotation in the study questionnaire.

Based only on the client’s language and expression, does this utterance sound AI-generated? Select one response for each candidate:

- • AI-like (1): the expression offers sufficient stylistic evidence for an AI-like judgment.

- • Not identified as AI-like (0): it sounds plausibly human, or the excerpt provides insufficient evidence for an AI-like judgment. This category does not certify human authorship.

Assess the overall manner of expression: register, phrasing, organization, certainty, self-explanation, and whether the voice plausibly belongs to a client describing their own experience. Potential cues include formulaic summaries, excessive completeness, generic psychological explanations, or a mismatch between intimate disclosure and an unusually authoritative explanatory voice. Judge these cues together in the context of the utterance. Length, grammatical fluency, emotional explicitness, and filler words should be considered alongside the overall voice and organization. Judge whether the wording sounds like AI-generated text.

Read the complete client utterance and select a label immediately below it. The eight candidates concern the same underlying disclosure, with wording varying across candidates. Vertical comparison is permitted; assign each label independently, and allow any number of candidates to receive either label. Base judgments on the presented language, keeping source names hidden and completing the questionnaire independently of other evaluators. Judge each candidate without guessing system identity or using external detectors. If evidence is insufficient for an AI-like judgment, select label 0. In the study questionnaire, evaluators completed all 45 pages and submitted their judgments; cloud saving allowed evaluators to pause and resume.

#### I.2 Source Privacy and Case Fidelity

PsyDial constructs its released conversations using Retrieve, Mask, Reconstruct, and Refine (RMRR), including masking client utterances and reconstructing them with an LLM to reduce exposure of source information ( Qiu and Lan, 2025 ) . AnnoMI uses permissioned motivational-interviewing teaching demonstrations from video-sharing platforms. ConsistentMI derives profiles and interaction structures from AnnoMI and generates simulated conversations ( Yang et al., 2025 ) . Eeyore curates depression-related conversations from existing forum, counseling-demonstration, and emotional-support datasets, with profile extraction and expert review ( Liu et al., 2025 ) .

Our case construction preserves the released dialogue content and grounds profiles and memory cards in indexed client utterances. Speaker and index normalization and duplicate removal support traceability without rewriting the underlying case. Source-specific privacy safeguards and access conditions are retained throughout case construction and release. Counseling cases may discuss distress and other sensitive experiences; these topics are retained as part of the research material. The release documentation will describe this content and its intended research use, with access controls applied to restricted source material.


#### I.3 Public and Controlled Release

We will maintain a GitHub repository for code, selected source dialogues, and derived profiles and memory cards. Any public release of data-bearing artifacts will comply with the licenses of the original datasets. The repository listed on the first page currently provides the preprocessing and modeling code. Data-bearing artifacts will be organized by source, with provenance and applicable terms. Dialogues and derived cases whose source permissions allow public redistribution will be released openly with the required attribution and license information.

For sources using approval-based access, requesters first obtain access from the original provider and send evidence of approval, such as an approval screenshot, through the contact channel listed with the release. After verification, the permitted processed material will be provided by email under the original access terms. Source identifiers and processing instructions also support reconstruction from users’ authorized copies. Derived profiles and memory cards retain the access conditions of their source material.
