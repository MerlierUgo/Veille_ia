## Résumé

Video diffusion models are able to generate samples with high-quality appearance and textural qualities. However, content produced by these methods is known to exhibit significant physical errors. This is because, in a classical video generation setting, all physical reasoning must take place simultaneously and exclusively within the diffusion process.We propose to resolve these issues by redistributing the task of physical reasoning onto a language model-backed keyframe generation module, which allows for explicit reasoning in language, and fine-grained control over the appearance of the video at important moments in the video. We then condition a video generation model with these keyframes, reducing the role of the diffusion model to the simpler task of interpolating between physically-correct intermediate states, rather than inferring an entire sequence with diffusion. Results demonstrate that our approach significantly outperforms existing approaches, achieving state-of-the-art performance on the Physics-IQ Verified benchmark and PhyGenBench.

## K2V: Generative Keyframing For Physically Plausible Video Generation

> Figure 1. Our approach generates keyframes to serve as stronger conditioning for a video model, leading to an approach which outperforms existing methods both qualitatively and quantitatively.

Video diffusion models are able to generate samples with high-quality appearance and textural qualities. However, content produced by these methods is known to exhibit significant physical errors ( Motamed et al., 2026 ) . We propose to resolve these issues by redistributing the task of physical reasoning onto a language model-backed keyframe generation module, which allows for explicit reasoning in language, and fine-grained control over the appearance of the video at important moments in the video. We then condition a video generation model with these keyframes, reducing the role of the diffusion model to the simpler task of interpolating between physically-correct intermediate states, rather than inferring an entire sequence with diffusion. Results demonstrate that our approach significantly outperforms existing approaches, achieving state-of-the-art performance on the Physics-IQ Verified benchmark ( Rädsch et al., 2026 ) and PhyGenBench ( Meng et al., 2025 ) .


### 1. Introduction

Video generation models are increasingly positioned as ‘world simulators’ ( OpenAI, 2024 ) : models which, given a starting observation or a text prompt alone, can evolve a scene forward in time to give plausible future states. The framing originates with Sora ( OpenAI, 2024 ) , which points to properties such as 3D consistency and object permanence as evidence that a generator trained only on pixels acquires a model of the world it depicts. Other works build on this premise. Genie ( Bruce et al., 2024 ) uses a video model directly as an interactive environment which a user acts in, and the model renders the consequences. Cosmos 3 ( NVIDIA et al., 2026 ) positions its ‘omnimodal world model’ as a potential backbone for autonomous physical agents. However, video diffusion models are known to perform poorly when tasked with generating physically plausible videos. Physics-IQ ( Motamed et al., 2026 ) defines a world model as a video model which has internalised physical principles, and shows that existing models are severely limited in the satisfaction of this definition despite the high visual fidelity of their outputs. The same conclusion is reached by VideoPhy-2 ( Bansal et al., 2025 ) , PhyGenBench ( Meng et al., 2025 ) and PhyWorldBench ( Gu et al., 2026 ) , which together demonstrate failures across a broad spectrum of physical phenomena.

In this paper we introduce Keyframes to Video (K2V), a novel approach to video generation which simplifies the task required of a video model to interpolation between keyframes, offloading physical inference to a separate keyframe generation module. Our first insight is that video models are more successful at reconstructing the physical motion connecting two explicit keyframes than at inferring plausible physical motion from a starting state entirely in diffusion space. We identify a simple training-free approach to condition an existing video generation model, Wan2.2 ( Wan et al., 2025 ) , to perform the task of keyframe interpolation. Our second insight is that the physical reasoning required to plan those keyframes is already present in language models. We therefore introduce a novel keyframe generation module, in which a language model plans the physical evolution of the scene. This approach redistributes the load of physical planning away from a video diffusion model and onto a language model, which can then finely control an image edit model to produce the intended physical effect.

We argue that this division between planning keyframes and interpolating between them is the right allocation of effort for physically plausible generation, since the keyframe module can concentrate compute, both in tokens and in keyframe density, on the scenes and tasks which are most complex. A per-scene analysis of our results on the Physics-IQ Verified benchmark confirms that our approach does scale its effort with scene complexity in this way.

K2V contributes to an existing line of research which enables physically plausible generation by delegating the physics to a separate model. Existing works, such as PhysGen ( Liu et al., 2024 ) and VDAWorld ( O’Mahony et al., 2026 ) , leverage explicit physics simulators to propagate the physical properties of scenes forward in time, before rendering these scenes or using the output of these simulations to condition a video model. NEWTON ( Feng et al., 2026 ) instead equips an agent with a sandboxed interpreter in which the relevant physical quantities are computed explicitly. However, reliance on simulators carries significant limitations. Using simulators restricts the operating domain of these methods to settings where simulators are accurate and available, and the methods must first create a mapping from image to code, which can introduce errors. We dispense with simulators entirely, relying on the physical reasoning of language models in their place. In contrast to these works, we also reformulate the task posed to the video generator into one of interpolation, rigidly imposing the conditions created by the keyframe module.

Through comprehensive experiments we show that K2V is highly effective at producing physically plausible generations, reaching state-of-the-art performance by a significant margin on Physics-IQ Verified ( Rädsch et al., 2026 ) , a benchmark designed specifically to evaluate physical plausibility. Keyframing raises Wan2.2-14B from 32.2 to 55.0 on the Physics-IQ Verified image-to-video track. The gain also extends to the efficiency of generation: the much lighter Wan2.2-5B reaches 50.0 when supported by our method, substantially outperforming the unmodified 14B backbone at roughly a third of the parameters. We also report state-of-the-art results on PhyGenBench ( Meng et al., 2025 ) , raising the state of the art from 0.56 to 0.63. The two benchmarks test complementary abilities. Physics-IQ Verified is image-to-video and scores how a scene evolves from a given observation, so it chiefly measures the motion between frames. PhyGenBench is text-to-video and scores whether the frames themselves carry the correct physical appearance of a phenomenon, such as the flame colour of a burning metal, so it chiefly measures accuracy within a frame.

K2V carries two further advantages over end-to-end pixel generation. Firstly, a user can inspect and correct the keyframes of a scene before a full video is generated, and so revise an output without incurring the cost of producing a complete sample, which can be very high for modern state-of-the-art video generators. Secondly, in contrast to pure diffusion, K2V incorporates explicit reasoning into video generation. The thinking tokens produced by the language model in our keyframe generation module illustrate a consideration of how various objects would interact and move over the course of a video, and lead to correct results in scenes which require reasoning about complex effects such as air pressure or chemical combustion.

In summary, we make the following contributions:

- • K2V, a training-free approach to physically plausible video generation which reduces the task posed to a video model to interpolation between keyframes, and which delegates physical planning to a separate module.

- • A keyframe module in which a vision-language model directs an image edit model, producing a stack of keyframes with timestamps and an expanded caption. Physical reasoning is performed in language, and no physics simulator is used at any stage.

- • A training-free mechanism for conditioning Wan2.2 on keyframes pinned at arbitrary interior latent frames, which the model’s stock conditioning interface cannot express.

- • State-of-the-art results on Physics-IQ Verified and PhyGenBench, together with an analysis showing that planning effort is spent in proportion to the physical complexity of a scene.


### 2. Related Work


#### 2.1. Boundary- and Keyframe-Conditioned Video Generation

Conditioning a video model on frames other than the first is an established setting. Wan2.1 ( Wan et al., 2025 ) was later released with a first-last-frame variant, which imposes boundary conditions at the two ends of the clip, requiring that the model interpolate between these two frames, and VACE ( Jiang et al., 2025 ) , built on the same backbone, is trained to accept reference frames at other positions, including randomly chosen ones. LTX-Video ( HaCohen et al., 2024 ) goes further still, accepting conditioning images at arbitrary frame positions in its standard inference pipeline. Wang et al. (2025) adapt an image-to-video model to keyframe interpolation by fine-tuning a backwards-in-time variant and combining it with the forward direction. Meanwhile, Zhu et al. (2025) drive the same task with explicit frame-wise conditions. Zhang et al. (2026) go further, distilling a model that generates in arbitrary temporal order rather than from a fixed anchor.

These works share our observation that video models can interpolate effectively. However, they differ from our work in two respects. First, they are given their keyframes; these are supplied by an artist or sampled from a real video, while the contribution is the interpolator. Our contribution is a system that predicts keyframes. We argue that this is where physical reasoning has to happen if the video is to be generated. Second, these methods train a dedicated model or adapter for frame conditioning. We instead condition the stock image-to-video checkpoints without further training, writing keyframes into interior latent frames through the existing first-frame conditioning channel, at an arbitrary number of positions chosen in the planning phase according to where the physical motion has specific boundaries.


#### 2.2. Serial and Parallel Computation

Liu et al. (2026) draw a distinction between problems which admit parallel solution and those which are inherently serial, requiring a chain of dependent steps that cannot be collapsed into simultaneous work. They show that diffusion models remain within $\mathrm{TC}^{0}$ however many denoising steps are taken, so a bidirectional video diffusion model supplies no more serial depth than its network has layers, irrespective of the length of the clip it generates. Physical evolution is serial in precisely this sense, since where a scene arrives depends on the states it passed through, which suggests a reason for the failures measured by the benchmarks above that is independent of data and parameter count.

One response is to make the generator itself serial, producing a clip in temporal order rather than all at once. Diffusion Forcing ( Chen et al., 2024 ) gives each token an independent noise level, so a causal model can roll a sequence forward beyond its training horizon. CausVid ( Yin et al., 2025 ) distils a bidirectional teacher into a causal student which emits frames as a stream, and Self Forcing ( Huang et al., 2025 ) trains such a model on its own rollouts to close the train-test gap this introduces. S2PD ( Hu et al., 2026 ) splits the schedule rather than the architecture, running autoregressive diffusion at high noise, where the serial computation coordinates interdependent events, before reverting to parallel diffusion at low noise.

These methods buy serial depth with the generator itself: each trains, distils or otherwise modifies the video model, and generating in temporal order gives up the bidirectional context which full-sequence diffusion exploits. We obtain the serial computation elsewhere. The keyframe module advances through a scene one keyframe at a time, each conditioned on the last, so the chain of physical dependence is resolved serially before any video is generated. What remains for the video model is a parallel problem, interpolation between states which are already fixed, for which bidirectional attention over the whole clip is an advantage rather than something to be given up. The generator is used unmodified and untrained.


#### 2.3. Agentic and Language-Model-Planned Generation

A recent line of work combines language models with video generators, and uses the language model to supply the physics which the caption omits. Wang et al. (2026b) use a language model to reason about a chain of causally connected physical events, and subsequently condition a generator on that chain. Cudlenco et al. (2026) constrain an agent’s plan against the capabilities of a 3D engine and executes it deterministically, thus obtaining physical validity by construction. Li et al. (2026) treat each physical program as an executable hypothesis, closing a loop of simulation, verification and targeted program repair.

Closest to our own system is NEWTON ( Feng et al., 2026 ) , which likewise uses an agentic planner to unload physical reasoning from the diffusion process, conditioning the generator on guidance frames and an expanded caption. The two differ in what they claim. NEWTON is principally concerned with training its planner, optimised on-policy with Flow-GRPO, and is deliberately generator-agnostic: its keyframes pass through whatever conditioning interface a frozen generator already exposes. Our claim concerns where the conditioning enters the generator: Wan2.2’s stock interfaces cannot express a keyframe pinned at an interior latent frame, so we write keyframes into its existing first-frame conditioning channel, and nothing in our pipeline is trained. Two further differences follow. NEWTON verifies by scoring generated videos, so each correction cycle costs a full generation, where our agent inspects images alone; and it writes an independent prompt for each keyframe, where ours are produced by editing the previous one, which preserves scene consistency.


#### 2.4. Simulator-Grounded Video Generation

An alternative is to obtain dynamics from an explicit simulator rather than from a generative prior. PhysGen ( Liu et al., 2024 ) recovers geometry and material properties from a single image, simulate rigid-body dynamics in image space, and use a diffusion model to render and refine the result. VDAWorld ( O’Mahony et al., 2026 ) takes the abstraction furthest, having a vision-language model assemble a grounded 2D or 3D scene representation from an image-caption pair, select a simulator appropriate to the scene, and infer latent dynamics to roll the state forward. However, it is not a pixel generator: its output is a simulated abstract scene rather than photorealistic video.

These approaches abstract the scene into a representation a simulator can integrate, leading to a queryable state and physical guarantees. However, this approach degrades wherever the abstraction is unable to reproduce accurate dynamics; for example deformation, fluids, fracture and contact-rich interaction. We maintain pixels throughout, construct no scene model, and ask a language model for the states the scene passes through rather than for equations defining them. A recent survey situates both lines of work within a common taxonomy of physical cognition in video generation ( Lin et al., 2025 ) .

These are then used as the conditioning signal for Wan2.2, resulting in videos with more accurate physical plausibility.


#### 2.5. Measuring Physical Plausibility

Physics-IQ Verified ( Rädsch et al., 2026 ) is our primary benchmark, and reports the finding that motivates this work: visual quality and physical understanding are largely uncorrelated across current generators. VideoPhy-2 ( Bansal et al., 2025 ) evaluates action-centric physical commonsense, and PhyGenBench ( Meng et al., 2025 ) scores 160 prompts across 27 physical laws in four domains. Both these are used by the agentic methods discussed above, and we report on PhyGenBench for direct comparison. Wang et al. (2026a) separate what a video model represents about scene state from what it propagates as dynamics, a distinction that our division of labour enforces in its architecture: the planner is responsible for state at chosen instants, while the video model is a stateless interpolant of the motion which happens between those keyframes.

Several image-to-video entries were added to the Physics-IQ Verified leaderboard ( Anates Labs, 2026 ) very recently, the strongest being FLUX 3 [large] (51.1), Odyssey 3 Pro (50.0), Odyssey 3 with prompt enhancement (48.8), and Physis-Lang applied to Cosmos3-Super and Cosmos3-Nano (48.2 and 43.3). We treat these as concurrent work and do not include them in Table 1 , but K2V with Wan2.2-14B (55.0) nonetheless outperforms all of them with a single generation per scene. It also scores above the best-of-eight submissions of FLUX 3 [large] and Odyssey 3 Pro (54.7 each), which generate eight candidates per scene and select one with a reward model or a ranking.


### 3. Method

Our approach leverages a generative keyframing module to produce keyframes along with timestamps, marking out distinct physical phases which occur in the generated video. This keyframe set is used as conditioning for a video generation model. In addition, the keyframe generation module returns an expanded caption, which describes precisely how the scene evolves, further reducing the physical inference which the diffusion model must perform itself. An overview of our method is shown in Figure 2 .


#### 3.1. Keyframe Module

The keyframe module is an agentic pipeline. A central planning agent, Gemini 3.8 Flash ( DeepMind, 2026b ) , orchestrates the keyframe generation. The planning agent is given the original caption for the video, a task-agnostic prompt, and, optionally, the image intended for the video generation model. Where no image is supplied, as in the text-conditioned setting, the agent generates the first keyframe from the caption alone, and generation proceeds identically thereafter. The prompt informs the agent that it has a set of tools which it should use to generate a stack of keyframes which represent the motion in the next five seconds of video. The general pipeline by which the planning agent generates the keyframes is fixed, although it is given flexibility through its use of tooling. A diagram of the keyframe module is shown in Figure 3 .

First, the planning agent is directed to imagine how the scene will evolve in the next five seconds, and consider how these could be divided by keyframes into distinct phases of motion. The agent generates each keyframe in sequence, enforcing the causal nature of physically-plausible generation.

For each keyframe, the agent writes an editing instruction describing how the scene should change to reach the next keyframe. This instruction is passed to an image edit model, Gemini 3.1 Flash Image ( DeepMind, 2026a ) , along with the most recently-generated keyframe. If no keyframes have been generated yet, the input image is passed to the image edit model. Once the keyframe is generated, it is added to the stack of keyframes along with a timestamp for the keyframe provided by the planning agent.


##### 3.1.1. Agentic Tooling

Tooling gives flexibility to this generation loop. At each stage, the agent is free to select which tool it uses, so the pace of the progression through the keyframe generation pipeline is controlled by the agent, and many loops may be undertaken until the keyframes are asserted to be complete. The tools let the agent render an edit, view and evaluate the result, commit it to the keyframe stack with a timestamp, and close the loop with an expanded caption. Their full specifications are given in Appendix C .

> Figure 3. Keyframe module detail. A VLM planning agent orchestrates a recursive loop producing keyframes. The first frame is passed to an image editor with an instruction from the VLM to produce the appearance of the frame later in time. The VLM reviews this frame and, if deemed correct, the frame is added to a stack of keyframes. The VLM repeats this process, now passing the top image to the image editor to add to the keyframe stack. Once all keyframes are produced, the model finishes, returning an expanded caption describing the motion of objects in the video with the keyframe stack.


##### 3.1.2. Repeat Keyframes

It is often advantageous for the model to repeat a keyframe where no motion occurs in a given time interval, which it can do trivially by calling add_keyframe(time) twice with no render_edit(instruction) between them. However, often, when two identical keyframes are placed far apart in time, for example at $0.0s$ and at $5.0s$ , the video model invents spurious motion between these timestamps. For this reason, where repeat keyframes are used, we also automatically insert additional keyframes every second between the two identical keyframes. We additionally hold the video still once the plan says the scene has come to rest. Where the plan ends in repeated keyframes, or where its final two keyframes are near-identical (a mean absolute difference below 4 grey levels), we repeat the frame at the start of that interval for the remainder of the video. We ablate this treatment of repeated keyframes in Section 4.5 .


#### 3.2. Conditioning a Video Generation Model With Keyframes

For our video generation model, we use Wan2.2 I2V-A14B and Wan2.2 TI2V-5B ( Wan et al., 2025 ) . By default, Wan2.2 accepts only an initial conditioning frame and caption pair. However, we observe that it is trivially possible to condition Wan2.2 for multiple frames at several points in a given video.


##### 3.2.1. First Frame Conditioning Wan2.2

Conventionally, Wan2.2 is conditioned with a single image $I\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3}$ . To perform the conditioning, this image is concatenated with $N$ additional frames, which are all set to be mid-grey (N.B. mid-grey eventually maps to $\mathbf{0}$ since the tensor is normalised to $[-1,1]$ in the VAE), producing a padded image tensor of $N+1$ frames:

[Tableau] (1) $$I^{\prime}[n]=\begin{cases}I&n=0\\ \mathbf{0.5}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times H\times W\times 3}$$

The padded image tensor $I^{\prime}$ is passed through the VAE of Wan2.2. This VAE changes the dimension of the tensor to $(N/L_{T}+1)\times H/L_{H}\times W/L_{W}\times C$ where $L_{T},L_{H},L_{W}$ are all hyperparameters of the VAE relating to spatial and temporal compression, and $C$ is the feature dimension of the VAE.

A mask vector is constructed which is simply $1$ repeated $L_{T}$ times followed by $N$ zeros to indicate that only the first frame is filled. The mask vector is reshaped to $(N/L_{T}+1)\times L_{T}$ . This is then concatenated in the channel dimension with the VAE output, resulting in a final conditioning tensor with shape $(N/L_{T}+1)\times H/L_{H}\times W/L_{W}\times(F+L_{T})$ .

The full conditioning tensor is concatenated with the noise tensor along the channel dimension before being passed to the denoising transformer. At each subsequent denoising step, the conditioning tensor is re-concatenated to the noise tensor before passing to the transformer.


##### 3.2.2. Keyframe Conditioning Wan2.2

Our approach is a trivial extension of this. We simply replace the grey frame at each keyframe timestamp with the corresponding generated keyframe. Given a set of $M$ keyframes $K_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3},0\leq i<M$ with associated timestamps $t_{i}$ we construct the new padded image-keyframe mixture:

[Tableau] (2) $$I^{\prime}[n]=\begin{cases}I&n=0\\ K_{i}&n=a_{i},\ 0\leq i<M\\ \mathbf{0.5}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

Where the indices of the placed keyframes are set to be the nearest frame which corresponds to the provided timestamp at the end of one latent timestep (i.e. every $L_{T}$ frames), and clamped to be between frame number $L_{T}$ and the final frame.

[Tableau] (3) $$a_{i}=L_{T}\left\lfloor\frac{f}{L_{T}}t_{i}\right\rceil,\qquad a_{i}\in\{L_{T},2L_{T},\dots,N\}$$

Where $f$ is the frame rate of the generator in frames per second.

The reason that frames are only inserted every $L_{T}$ frames is because we note in our experiments that intermediate conditioning only works if the conditioning is applied at the end of a temporal block of $L_{T}$ frames. A single temporal block represents the set of frames Wan2.2’s VAE treats as a single latent frame, and our hypothesis is that by placing a conditioning frame at the end of such a block, Wan2.2 is able to treat it most similarly to an initial conditioning frame.

Finally, we update the mask vector to indicate that the specific keyframe slots are now also filled.

Surprisingly, this approach works with no training or fine tuning of Wan2.2, and works with the default weights. This appears to be a surviving behaviour of the Wan2.2 network from its pre-training, which did explicitly include frame interpolation as one task ( Wan et al., 2025 ) . In all our experiments we use $N=80$ , while the Wan2.2 VAE has temporal compression factor $L_{T}=4$ and frame rate $f=16$ . This approach is illustrated in Figure 4

#### 3.3. Image and Video Post-Processing

While the method works remarkably well out of the box, we notice improvements to our results with the use of image and video post-processing. We attribute this to the fact that the image edit model, while very successful at maintaining physical consistency, occasionally introduces subtle color variations relative to the edit image. For this reason, before passing the keyframes to the video model, the images are colour matched using a simple affine transformation to the input image. Once the video is generated, we also match each frame to the first frame in the same manner. This mitigates any artefacts induced by the subtle colour variations resulting from the image edit model.


### 4. Results

We select our experiments to show the broad applicability of our approach in first frame and text conditioned settings. Our results highlight that, both qualitatively and quantitatively, our approach outperforms existing methods and produces videos which correctly follow physical laws.


#### 4.1. First Frame-Conditioned Generation

To examine our approach on first frame-conditioned video generation, we use the Physics-IQ Verified benchmark. This benchmark is designed to evaluate video models’ ability to adhere to physical laws across a range of categories. Categories include solid mechanics, optics, thermodynamics, magnetism and fluid mechanics. In each example, two ground truth videos are provided within Physics-IQ Verified from a single identical setting, and generative models are scored on whether the variation between their generation and one of the ground truth videos is greater than the variance between the two ground truth samples. Thus, all results are scored as an error beyond physical variance. We evaluate our method on the image to video setting, wherein the first frame of a video and a caption describing the setting is passed to the video generator.

We show a selection of results in Figure 5 . Our approach produces videos which strictly adhere to physical laws such as gravity, collisions, and which preserve the number and size of objects throughout the video.

> Figure 5. Qualitative results from our approach. The top row shows an example from Physics-IQ Verified and the bottom row an example from PhyGenBench. These results highlight the diverse placement of keyframes in different videos, and the physical quality of the resulting generated videos.

Quantitatively, our approach outperforms all baselines by a significant margin. Compared to a vanilla implementation of Wan2.2-14B, our approach leads to an improvement of 22.8 points. Compared to the prior state of the art, our approach leads to an improvement of 12.3 points. We attribute the significant improvement in this metric to the ability of our method to reason about and plan the physical evolution of a scene, and to its ability to impose that plan on the video generation phase.

#### 4.2. Text-Conditioned Generation

We evaluate text-conditioned generation on the PhyGenBench ( Meng et al., 2025 ) benchmark. In PhyGenBench, text-to-video models are required to generate videos of different physical phenomena across settings which span a wide range of domains, such as mechanics, optics, thermodynamics and materials.

Models are evaluated on their ability to accurately visualise the features which uniquely determine the physical phenomena, such as showing the correct flame color of copper burning, or correctly reasoning about the buoyancy of objects of different densities.

Qualitatively, we show in Figures 5 and 8 that the language reasoning enforces correct physics throughout our generation.

#### 4.3. Controllability

The right panel of Figure 9 shows that the keyframes are also trivially controllable via user-specified edits. In this example, two keyframes are generated of a flower bud, and the flower once blossomed using Gemini 3.1 Flash Image ( DeepMind, 2026a ) . These are used to produce a video showing the full timelapse of the bud opening. We then use the same image model to simply update the final image, changing the color of the inner petals to be white and orange. Regenerating the video with these keyframes correctly updates the video dynamics.


#### 4.4. Keyframe Conditioning

We next verify that conditioning on keyframes is what drives the improvement, independently of how well the keyframes are chosen. In this experiment, we take $N$ evenly-spaced keyframes from ground-truth videos in the Physics-IQ Verified dataset. We then condition Wan2.2-5B with one trial on these ground-truth keyframes in the central perspective and score the resulting videos. Our results show that this conditioning structure consistently improves Physics-IQ Verified score as the number of keyframes increases. Results are shown in the left panel of Figure 9 .

While increasing the number of keyframes initially linearly improves the Physics-IQ Verified score, it plateaus where a high number of keyframes are used, indicating that once the motion is specified by the supplied keyframes, adding information in the form of more keyframes does not improve generation. We also note that in this experiment the keyframes are selected to be evenly-spaced, and are therefore naive in their placement. We propose to use a small number of keyframes at carefully placed locations within a given video.

#### 4.5. Ablations

We conduct a thorough ablation of all components of our method on the central perspective of Physics-IQ Verified. We ablate the agentic loop, instead prompting the language model once to provide a chain of image edit instructions which are passed directly to the image model. This reveals that our agentic pipeline is essential due to its ability to review and correct errors made by the image edit model, or to refine its image edits. We also replace the language and image editing models with open-weight variants, using Qwen3-VL Instruct ( Bai et al., 2025 ) for planning and the recently released Qwen-Image-2.1 ( Qwen Team, 2026 ) for image editing. This variant scores 40.2, 15.8 points below the full method, which shows that closed-source models are presently still needed for their ability to edit an image with the precision required to construct an accurate video.

We additionally ablate several conditioning features of our method. We ablate any keyframes provided which do not sit at 0s or 5s, and show that intermediate keyframes are essential for producing accurate generations. We ablate the conditioning to be sparse between identical keyframes, so that when two keyframes are provided which are identical, we do not automatically repeat this identical keyframe every second between the two keyframes, nor hold the video still once the plan declares the scene at rest, both as described in Section 3.1.2 . This demonstrates our earlier note, that the video model often invents spurious motion betwen two identical keyframes. We ablate the benchmark caption, instead providing the original caption which was provided with the input frame, which shows that densely prompting the motion in a scene also supports more accurate video generation.

Finally, we ablate color correction, showing that the use of keyframes does lead to color varying throughout a video, but that this can be rectified with a simple affine transformation of the frame colors.

### 5. Limitations

Our proposed approach incurs a minor additional cost to the process of generation. On average for Physics-IQ Verified, the median time taken to plan one scene was 62 seconds. However, we highlight here that this is a minor cost compared to the time required to generate a video, which is around 32 minutes for Wan2.2-14B. We additionally note that scaffolding Wan2.2-5B, which requires just 4 minutes per generation, with our method outperforms the 14B model, leading to an effective acceleration of accurate generation.

Although our method does resolve almost all physical plausibility problems in our test sets, there remain some instances where the language model fails to reason correctly about the physics of a scene, leading to an incorrect generation. For example, in one scene a stack of blocks was reasoned incorrectly to be stable, whereas in fact the blocks were visibly unbalanced. This led to the generation of incorrect keyframes, and ultimately an implausible video. We expect that the use of a stronger language model would further improve results.

Finally, while our method can set moments in a video, it cannot currently determine intermediate motion. This can lead to remaining physical implausibilities where the model inserts incorrect motion. We observe this most strongly in scenes with balls rolling, where a ball follows a trajectory which is not straight between keyframes, violating Newton’s First Law of motion.


### 6. Conclusions and Discussion

In conclusion, we proposed K2V, a novel approach for video generation which enforces adherence to set keyframes which are generated with a custom language model-backed keyframe module. We proposed our method to redistribute the physical generation task required for accurate video generation from within video diffusion onto a language model, which has the ability to reason in text and to plan how a scene will evolve physically.

Through our experiments we show that our approach is significantly more successful at generating videos which are physically plausible than any existing video generators. On the Physics-IQ Verified benchmark we demonstrate state-of-the-art results by a significant margin. Our approach scaffolding Wan2.2-14B outperforms Wan in its vanilla state by 22.8 points; and outperforms the existing state of the art by 12.3 points. On PhyGenBench our method outperforms all comparable approaches in all physical categories tested.

### Ethics and Privacy Statement

The rapid proliferation of generative video models has had widespread effects. These include significant beneficial progress in fields such as computer graphics, robotics, and physical intelligence. However, some effects have been negative, such as the distribution of content intended to deceive viewers. Research which intends to improve the physical plausibility of generated content could make detection of generated content more difficult; and developments in this field should be balanced with work to facilitate detection and watermarking of generated content. However, presently we feel that the benefits of this research mean that improving physical plausibility is a justified objective.

### Appendix C Agent Tools

The planning agent of Section 3.1 acts entirely through the following tools. At each step it chooses which to call, so the number of iterations spent on a scene is set by the agent rather than fixed in advance.

- (1) render_edit(instruction) calls the image edit model, passing it both a textual edit instruction and the latest keyframe.

- (2) view_image() passes the render back to the agent, allowing it to view the image.

- (3) evaluate_render(evaluation) requires the image model to evaluate the render before deciding whether to add it to the keyframe stack. This forces the model to consider whether the render adheres to its instructions. add_keyframe(time) cannot be called until this method has first been called on a given render.

- (4) add_keyframe(time) adds the rendering to the list of accepted keyframes with a timestamp provided by the model. This keyframe then becomes the latest keyframe, which is edited by subsequent calls of render_edit(instruction) .

- (5) finish(expanded_caption) ends the keyframe generation loop, passing the complete stack of keyframes and the expanded caption to the video model.


### Appendix D Planner prompt

The complete brief given to the planning agent for image-to-video generation. The benchmark’s scene description is appended in place of <scene description> ; all other text is sent verbatim.

You are given one frame from a video, and a short description of the scene.

The frame is the **handover frame**: the video continues from this moment for 5.00 more seconds. It is not necessarily the start of the action. Footage precedes it, and motion may already be under way in the frame you are looking at. The description may be written as though nothing has happened yet.

Your job is to build a **stack of keyframes**: pictures of this scene at fixed times, which together say what happens over those 5.00 seconds. The stack already holds the handover frame at 0.00s.

You work through tools, and you can see what you make. Use that: look at the photograph before you commit it.

## What you are working with

The **stack** is the keyframes you have committed. Every photograph you render is made by editing the picture on top of the stack, so the arrangement one moment settles into is the arrangement the next moment starts from.

## Working out what happens

- Reason physically, from what the frame actually shows: the objects present, their sizes and likely masses, what is in contact with what, what is already in motion and in which direction.

- Reason about where things end up.

- If an interaction seems likely but will not in fact occur, do not depict it.

- Do not invent objects, cuts or camera movement unless the description says so.

## Choosing the keyframes to add to the stack

A keyframe is a moment worth pinning: a boundary between distinct phases of motion. Something arrives somewhere and the situation changes qualitatively — an object lands, stops, breaks contact, reverses, tips past balance, or reaches a configuration it then holds.

Choose them sparsely. Most scenes need one or two. Use three or four only when the span genuinely contains that many distinct phases. Do not narrate the mechanism: where several things happen in quick succession as part of one continuous event, that is one keyframe, the state the event leaves behind.

**Always include a keyframe at 5.00s**, even if this moment does not represent the boundary of a distinct phase.

## Rendering and committing

`render_edit` takes a text instruction and the keyframe at the top of the stack, and asks an image model to edit the keyframe according to the instruction. You should specify the edit in terms of what should and should not change to accomplish the edit. You should consider that your instruction should direct the edit relative to the top keyframe, not the handover frame unless the top keyframe is not the handover frame and no renders have already been added to the stack.

Renders are the one expensive thing you do, and the budget is small: **8 of them for this scene**, and 24 turns in total. Every tool result ends with what is left of both.

**Every render must be evaluated before you can do anything else.** As soon as one comes back, `evaluate_render` is the only tool available to you: say what the picture actually shows, item by item. Count what is countable. Only once you have answered can you render again, or add it to the stack.

**Look at what came back, and anticipate that it might be wrong.** It may quietly change things it was not asked about — the most common failures are objects appearing or disappearing, object counts changing, an object ending up in a different place or a different orientation from the one you drew. Judge it item by item:

- Is everything you expect present in the photograph, and nothing else?

- Is each thing that moved where you expected it, at the orientation described?

- Has anything changed that you did not expect?

Nothing checks this after you. Whatever you push is what is accepted as a keyframe, and a keyframe that says something untrue about the scene is worse than one you spent another render getting right.

If it does not match, you have three options to correct it. You can adjust the edit instruction given to `render_edit` to more strictly enforce the edit, or you can simply run the render again, since the rendering model is stochastic.

You have a numbered list of **images**. Image 0 is the handover frame; every render you make is added to it as image 1, 2, and so on, and they all stay available for the rest of the run.

`add_keyframe` puts one of those images on the stack at a time. It uses the most recent image unless you name another, and it uses nothing up:

- If a second attempt comes back worse than the first, add the earlier one by its number rather than paying for a third. `view_image` shows you one again if it has scrolled out of sight.

- If nothing changes between one moment and a later one, add the _same_ image at both times rather than rendering the same picture twice. It costs no render and cannot introduce a difference nobody asked for.

- If nothing changes at all, add image 0 — the handover frame — at the end of the clip.

Choosing between pictures you already have costs nothing. Making another costs a render.

## Finishing

Call `finish` with one paragraph of plain description of the action in the whole 5.00 seconds. The description you give should expand on the content contained in the scene description. It should contain all of the information in the original description, with a detailed account of what happens in the 5.00 seconds after the handover frame, and no more. Content should only be removed if it refers to something that occurs before the handover frame, and would therefore not be relevant for the 5.00 seconds after it.
