## PyRUA-Dex: Learning Programmatic Policies for Dexterous Bimanual Manipulation with Coding Agents

Learning for dexterous manipulation is limited by the scarcity of high-quality demonstrations: retargeting from human video is imprecise, teleoperation gives no force feedback to the operator, and exoskeleton-based collection is hard to scale up. We present PyRUA-Dex , a framework that enables coding agents to develop dexterous manipulation through programmatic policy , without the need of demonstrations. The programmatic policy is developed in two stages: during exploration , multiple agents explore in parallel for task skills and ways to compose them, and a distilling agent condenses the successful attempts into a task library and one program. During refinement , this program is improved through randomized layouts for enhanced robustness and execution efficiency. In simulated environments, PyRUA-Dex achieves SOTA on Bench2Dex with a score of 72.9 and reaches 81.0% success on DexJoCo under full randomization, ahead of every trained policy on both benchmarks. In real world experiments on a bimanual platform with two 22-DoF hands, PyRUA-Dex learns to play piano, write calligraphy, and other tasks efficiently: learning a new piece from sheet music takes as little as 30 minutes. These results suggest that PyRUA-Dex is an effective framework for developing dexterous manipulation policies with coding agents in the absence of high quality dexterous manipulation demonstrations.

### 1 Introduction

Dexterous manipulation is learned today mainly from demonstrations. Imitation learning [ 51 , 10 ] , vision-language-action models [ 5 , 18 , 3 , 4 , 25 ] and world-action models [ 13 ] are all trained on teleoperated episodes, and data generators [ 24 , 17 ] expand a few such episodes into many. For multi-fingered hands, however, high-quality demonstrations are scarce. Retargeting human video to a robot hand is imprecise, teleoperation gives the operator no force feedback during contact, and exoskeleton-based collection is hard to scale. The data are also tied to the hand they were collected on, so a new hand requires new data and retraining. Policies trained on such data remain fragile. On Bench2Dex, which evaluates bimanual tasks on 12 arm–hand embodiments [ 49 ] , the strongest trained policies succeed in little more than half of the episodes [ 19 , 2 ] , and all of them degrade when a layout’s appearance or object placement is perturbed.

Coding agents offer an alternative to collecting demonstrations. A programmatic policy represents behavior as a program rather than as network weights, so it can be read, tested and verified [ 39 , 40 ] . Language models already write programs that control robots [ 20 , 34 , 16 ] and improve them from environment feedback [ 41 , 23 , 32 , 45 ] , and general coding agents run, test and debug software for hours [ 48 , 26 ] . Robot programs, however, are typically written once from a prompt [ 20 ] or found by search in a small domain-specific language [ 38 , 21 ] , and are not refined for robustness across layouts. For dexterous hands, the agent has so far stayed in the loop instead: it observes and acts in every test episode [ 31 ] , consumes millions of tokens per episode, and still fails most episodes within the benchmark’s step budget. We ask whether a coding agent can learn a dexterous task once, during development, and produce a program that then runs on its own reliably without per-episode cost.

Learning such a program poses two distinct problems. The first is discovering a solution: the grasps, the ordering of the two hands and the motions that complete each stage of the task. This is a search problem that requires many attempts on a few layouts, and it proceeds faster when parallel agents share their progress. The second is making the solution robust. A dexterous task consists of consecutive stages, and its success rate is the product of the stage success rates , the probability of completing each stage given that the preceding stages were completed. A program that succeeds on one layout can therefore fail on most others: five stages at 80% each give an overall success rate of only 33%. In our experiments, most failed runs end with steps to spare, at a stage that was not completed (Section 4.3 ), so failures come from unreliable stages rather than from the step budget. Because the overall rate cannot exceed the lowest stage rate, robustness requires measuring every stage over many layouts and improving the least reliable one.

PyRUA-Dex addresses these two problems in two stages (Figure 2 ). In the exploration stage (Stage 1), multiple coding agents work on one task in parallel in simulation. They write and run code, rewind the simulator to retry a stage, use the number of completed stages as feedback, and share their progress with one another. A distilling agent then condenses the successful programs into a task library and one program. In the refinement stage (Stage 2), an agent refines this program over many randomized layouts. It measures the stage success rates, improves the least reliable stage, and keeps a revision only if it performs no worse on layouts that no agent has seen. Agents and programs control every robot through the same codebox , a layer of hand, arm and sensing calls built from the robot’s description, so the procedure applies to any hand the codebox can model. The resulting policy is general Python and runs frozen. It takes the inputs of an ordinary policy (calibrated cameras, joint positions and fingertip touch), calls no language model, and runs at the robot’s control rate. Because it measures the scene in every episode, it remains reliable when a layout is perturbed.

On Bench2Dex, the frozen programs reach a score of 72.9, a new SOTA ahead of every trained policy, and at the benchmark’s step budget they succeed 2.4 times as often as the same model, GPT-6 Astra, acting online (Sections 4.2 and 4.4 ). On DexJoCo, a second benchmark with Allegro hands [ 42 ] , they succeed in 81.0% of episodes under full randomization, against 40.3% for the strongest trained policy, and are above each trained policy on every task (Section 4.5 ). On a real bimanual platform with two 22-DoF hands, the same two stages run in a calibrated simulation that is updated with the robot’s feedback after each round, and the frozen programs then run on the robot (Section 5 ). They play piano, write calligraphy and unpack objects, and learning a new piano piece from sheet music takes about 30 minutes.

In summary, our contributions are as follows:

- • An agent-driven framework for dexterous manipulation. We propose PyRUA-Dex, which enables coding agents to develop programmatic policies for dexterous manipulation without demonstrations. The resulting policy runs as frozen code at test time, with no trained model and no online agent.

- • Two-stage programmatic robot policy development using tactile feedback. Parallel agents first explore in simulation for task skills and their composition. The skills are then refined over randomized layouts for robustness and efficiency, with fingertip tactile feedback used throughout.

- • Comprehensive simulated and real-world experiments across diverse robot hands and tasks. PyRUA-Dex achieves SOTA on Bench2Dex. On a real bimanual platform with two 22-DoF hands, with system identification of the simulated hands, PyRUA-Dex develops code programs to perform piano playing, calligraphy and other tasks efficiently.


### 2 Related Work

Our work lies at the intersection of dexterous manipulation, programmatic policies and coding for robots.


##### Learning dexterous manipulation.

Reinforcement learning has produced dexterous skills in simulation and on hardware [ 28 , 8 ] , at the price of task-specific rewards and large-scale training per skill. Imitation learning from teleoperated demonstrations [ 51 , 10 ] and vision-language-action models [ 5 , 18 , 3 , 4 , 25 ] are now the dominant recipe, with data generators reducing the number of human episodes [ 24 , 17 ] , and recent benchmarks evaluate them across hands and settings [ 49 , 42 , 22 , 43 , 7 ] . To reach hardware, a common route is to train in a simulation built from the deployment scene, with randomization covering what the model gets wrong [ 37 , 9 , 11 , 35 , 29 , 46 ] . PyRUA-Dex takes the same route to the real robot but trains no network: agents write and test a program in simulation, and the program is what transfers.


##### Programmatic policies.

Programmatic reinforcement learning represents a policy as a program in a symbolic language, which is easier to interpret and verify than a neural policy [ 39 , 40 ] ; to keep the search over programs tractable, the languages are small and the programs short [ 38 , 21 ] . Language models write such programs directly: they compose perception and motion primitives from a task description [ 20 , 34 , 16 ] , keep libraries of programs that worked [ 41 ] , refine reward code from training outcomes [ 23 ] and generate tasks and simulations [ 44 , 47 ] . These systems write a program once or improve it over a handful of trials, mostly for parallel-jaw grippers. PyRUA-Dex writes general Python programs for multi-fingered hands and develops them against success rates measured over hundreds of randomized layouts.


##### Coding agents for robots.

A coding agent can control a robot directly, observing and issuing commands throughout every episode [ 31 , 12 , 30 ] ; it keeps its full reasoning at test time but pays for it in tokens, time and step budget in every episode. The alternative is to let agents develop programs that then run without them. RAX scales exploration by coding agents to as many as 256 episodes per task and compiles what it finds into modular programs [ 14 ] , and the PyRUA family studies token-efficient execution [ 33 ] , data generation [ 50 ] , self-improvement on real robots [ 45 ] and what agents learn from a benchmark’s success criteria [ 15 ] . PyRUA-Dex belongs to this second line and targets dexterous hands: parallel agents explore a task in simulation, a second stage refines the resulting program stage by stage, and the program that is handed over calls no language model.


### 3 Method

> Figure 2 : Overview of PyRUA-Dex. All learning takes place in simulation. Stage 1 (exploration): eight agents (one is drawn) explore the task, each on a training layout of its own with state resets, and share a memory; their programs are evaluated without the agents and distilled into a task library and one program. Stage 2 (refinement): one agent finds the program’s bottleneck stage by a stage-wise failure analysis, refines it on randomized layouts and selects the version with the best held-out record. The resulting program runs frozen, without a language model. For the real world, the simulation is built from the real scene (real-to-sim), the frozen program is transferred (sim-to-real), and real-world feedback updates the simulation for the next round of refinement.

PyRUA-Dex learns one programmatic policy per task in simulation and deploys it as a frozen program (Figure 2 ). Learning proceeds in two stages, both carried out by general coding agents: Codex CLI sessions [ 26 ] of GPT-6 Astra [ 27 ] , each equipped with a shell, its own simulator instance and a library of perception and control primitives shared by all robots (the codebox ). Exploration (Stage 1) searches for a program that completes the task at all; refinement (Stage 2) improves how reliably that program completes the task on unseen layouts.


#### 3.1 Problem Formulation

For each task $\tau$ , stated as a goal in natural language for two arms with multi-fingered hands, we seek a policy in the form of a program $\pi_{\tau}$ . At step $t$ it maps the observation history to an action,

[Tableau] $$a_{t}=\pi_{\tau}(o_{\leq t}),$$ (1)

where $o_{t}$ contains what a learned policy receives on the benchmark (the RGB images of the cameras with their calibration, the joint positions of arms and hands, and the fingertip tactile maps) and $a_{t}$ is a joint-position target. The program may call the robot’s forward kinematics and a segmentation model [ 6 ] , but it receives no simulator state, object pose, stage count or success signal, and it calls no language model.

A task is evaluated over layouts $\xi\sim\mathcal{D}_{\tau}$ , initial states of the scene that differ in object poses, physical parameters and appearance, and its progress is measured by $K$ ordered stages , sub-goals that the simulator counts the first time they are reached. Let $\rho_{k}(\xi)\in\{0,1\}$ indicate that $\pi_{\tau}$ reaches stages $1,\dots,k$ on layout $\xi$ , with $\rho_{0}\equiv 1$ , and let $\sigma(\xi)\in\{0,1\}$ indicate success, which requires all stages and the task’s final conditions within the episode’s step budget, so that $\sigma\leq\rho_{K}$ . We define the stage success rate of stage $k$ as the fraction of layouts that reach it among those that reached the stage before,

[Tableau] $$r_{k}(\pi_{\tau})=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\rho_{k}(\xi)\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\rho_{k-1}(\xi)\right]},\qquad k=1,\dots,K,$$ (2)

with the convention $0/0=0$ , so that the success rate factorizes along the chain of stages:

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{SR}(\pi_{\tau})=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\sigma(\xi)\right]=s(\pi_{\tau})\prod_{k=1}^{K}r_{k}(\pi_{\tau}),\qquad s(\pi_{\tau})=\Pr\left[\sigma=1\mid\rho_{K}=1\right].$$ (3)

The factorization in equation 3 motivates the two stages. A program is useful only once every stage success rate is positive, which requires a search over grasps, hand orders and motions on a few layouts: the task of exploration. Once every rate is positive, the product dominates, and because $\partial\log\mathrm{SR}/\partial r_{k}=1/r_{k}$ , the largest gain comes from the least reliable stage, the bottleneck , and finding it requires stage success rates estimated over many layouts: the task of refinement.

Learning a task is therefore a search over programs under a development budget:

[Tableau] $$\max_{\pi_{\tau}\in\Pi}\ \mathrm{SR}(\pi_{\tau})\qquad\text{subject to}\qquad c_{\mathrm{LLM}}(\pi_{\tau})=0,\quad n_{\mathrm{dev}}(\pi_{\tau})\leq B,$$ (4)

where $\Pi$ is the set of programs of the form given in equation 1 , $\mathrm{SR}$ the success rate on the benchmark’s test layouts, $c_{\mathrm{LLM}}$ the number of language-model calls in a test episode, $n_{\mathrm{dev}}$ the number of simulated layouts spent on development and $B$ their budget (Section 4.1 ). The test layouts are never used during development. Agents interact only with training layouts , on which they also see the simulator’s stage count, and a fixed set of 32 validation layouts per task, never shown to any agent, decides whether a new program replaces the current one.


#### 3.2 Policy Representation


##### Program structure.

Every program has the same staged form: a setup function that measures the scene, and one function $f_{k}$ per stage, executed in order by a fixed runner within the episode’s step budget (Appendix B , Figure 7 ). Each $f_{k}$ starts from where $f_{k-1}$ left the robot and the scene, works toward stage $k$ and returns whether, by the program’s own measurement, the stage holds. A failed function does not abort the episode: later functions check on entry what they need, and the runner calls the failed functions again while steps remain. This form lets measurements be reported per stage, functions written by different agents be combined, and a single stage be executed in isolation.


##### Perception and control primitives.

Hands are what makes these tasks hard to program: the twelve hands of Bench2Dex have 6 to 22 driven joints, named and arranged differently on each robot, and whether a grasp holds is revealed only by touch. Agents and programs therefore control every robot through one library of primitives, which is generated from the robot’s description file. It models each hand as fingers and a thumb whose joints are grouped by function (bend, curl, spread), derives which object widths each set of fingers can hold, and exposes hand primitives at that level: shape the hand, grasp an object of a measured size, close until it is held, check that it is still held. Contact is read from the fingertip tactile maps and from the lag between commanded and measured joint positions. Arm primitives move a frame of the hand to a target and report an unreachable or colliding pose before moving, and perception primitives locate objects from the calibrated cameras with SAM 3 [ 6 ] . Every primitive returns an outcome with its reason, so a program can check each step and retry. The same primitives drive all hands in our experiments (Appendix B ).


#### 3.3 Stage 1: Exploration

Exploration runs eight agents on a task in parallel. Each receives the task library, initialized from the task’s definition, a training layout of its own, and one of the stages not yet solved as its assignment.


##### Exploration with state resets.

An agent controls the robot interactively through the primitives and queries the simulator’s stage count after each attempt. It can checkpoint the simulator’s state during an episode and reset to a checkpoint, eight times per layout, so that it retries one stage many times without repeating the stages before it.


##### Shared memory.

Every attempt an agent finishes is written to a memory shared by all agents of the task, with the stages it reached and the code that reached them. A dedicated agent curates the memory, and each agent sees what is new in its next tool reply, so a stage solved by one agent becomes the starting point of the others.


##### Program evaluation and distillation.

Each agent ends by writing a program that reproduces what it achieved, and every program that reached a stage is evaluated without the agent on eight fresh training layouts. A distilling agent then reads all evaluation records and writes the task library: what is established about the task, how each stage was solved and how often, with code, and what remains unsolved. It also selects the task’s program: the evaluated program with the best record (completions, then mean stage rate, then fewer steps), or a composition that takes each stage function from the agent whose program reaches that stage most often, itself evaluated before it can be selected. The selected program replaces the current one only if it is no worse on the validation layouts.


#### 3.4 Stage 2: Refinement

Refinement starts from the program and task library of exploration, and a single agent edits the program.


##### Stage-wise failure analysis.

Before the agent starts, the program runs without the agent on 32 fresh training layouts, and the result is broken down by stage: for each stage, how many layouts entered it, how many reached it, how its function ended on the others and how much of the step budget was left. Layouts are grouped by the first stage they missed, which gives the stage success rates of equation 2 and shows the bottleneck before any change is made.


##### Bottleneck refinement.

The agent works on one stage at a time, the bottleneck first. It opens failing layouts interactively, repairs the stage function with the help of state resets, and then runs the program without itself in the loop on tens of fresh layouts. To keep these measurements cheap, a partial rollout executes the program only up to the stage under work, so thirty layouts return in about the time those stages take on one. During refinement an episode has twice the benchmark’s step budget, so that a stage is made reliable before it is made fast.


##### Program selection.

After every 160 training layouts, the current version is evaluated without the agent, at the benchmark’s step budget, on 32 fresh layouts and on a fixed held-out set of 32 layouts of which the agent sees only the counts. The agent stops when the task completes on most held-out layouts, when the stage success rates stop improving, or when its 1,500 layouts are used up. A distilling agent selects the version with the best held-out record, and, as in exploration, this version replaces the current program only if it is no worse on the validation layouts; otherwise the program from exploration is kept.


#### 3.5 Sim-to-Real Transfer

For a real robot, both stages run in a simulation of the robot’s scene, and the robot itself only executes frozen programs (Figure 2 , right). We build the simulation from the platform’s calibration and from objects reconstructed from photographs (real-to-sim), randomize in every layout what the calibration leaves uncertain, and run exploration once. Refinement then alternates with the robot: after each round the program is executed on the robot, the discrepancies it reveals are written back into the simulation as updated physical parameters or task rules, and the next round refines the same program in the updated simulation (Section 5 ).


### 4 Experiments

We evaluate PyRUA-Dex on two simulated benchmarks for dexterous manipulation and, in Section 5 , on a real bimanual platform. The simulation experiments answer four questions, each about a separate comparison:

- • Q1: Do programs written and iteratively refined by coding agents outperform policies trained on demonstrations? (Section 4.2 )

- • Q2: What does refinement contribute beyond exploration? (Section 4.3 )

- • Q3: Is a frozen program better than the same model acting online? (Section 4.4 )

- • Q4: Does the method generalize to another benchmark, with a different hand and simulator? (Section 4.5 )


#### 4.1 Experimental Setup


##### Benchmarks and metrics.

We use Bench2Dex [ 49 ] , with 26 bimanual tasks, each paired with one of 12 arm–hand embodiments, and DexJoCo [ 42 ] , with 11 tasks for Allegro hands; together they cover twelve dexterous hands with 6 to 22 driven joints (Figure 1 e,f). On Bench2Dex we follow the leaderboard protocol [ 2 ] : every task is evaluated on four channels of 50 episodes, the unperturbed layouts (None) and layouts perturbed along the invariance axis, which leaves the correct action unchanged (Inv.), along the equivariance axis, which changes it (Equi.), or both (Full). We report the success rate (SR), the latched stage completion rate (LSCR, the mean fraction of stages reached), the efficiency (Eff, the expert’s steps divided by the steps of successful episodes) and the leaderboard’s $\mathrm{Score}=0.45\,\mathrm{SR}+0.45\,\mathrm{LSCR}+10\,\mathrm{Eff}$ , with SR and LSCR in percent. On DexJoCo we report the success rate over three seeds of 50 episodes per task, with the objects randomized (rand-obj) and with cameras, lighting and textures randomized as well (rand-full). Appendix A describes both benchmarks.


##### Implementation.

All agents are Codex CLI sessions of GPT-6 Astra at the highest reasoning effort, with no memory across sessions. Exploration uses eight agents per task, each with eight training layouts and eight state resets per layout. Refinement uses one agent per task and at most 1,500 training layouts, with an evaluation on 32 fresh and 32 held-out layouts after every 160 (the budget $B$ of equation 4 ). A program replaces the current one only if it is no worse on the 32 validation layouts. No budget limits time or tokens. The final program of each task is evaluated once on the benchmark’s test layouts, with access to the program’s own inputs only.


##### Baselines.

We compare with two groups of methods (Appendix A lists them individually). Trained policies learn from the benchmarks’ teleoperated demonstrations: on Bench2Dex, every leaderboard entry that reports all four channels, covering imitation learning (IL), vision-language-action (VLA), world-action (WAM) and visuo-tactile action (VTA) models [ 2 , 13 , 1 , 19 ] ; on DexJoCo, the benchmark’s five trained policies [ 42 ] . The online agent is the GPT-6 Astra agent of SenseNova [ 31 ] , which uses the same model as PyRUA-Dex but controls the robot in every test episode after preparing in context on the task’s training data.

#### 4.2 PyRUA-Dex Sets a New SOTA on Bench2Dex

> Figure 3 : Bench2Dex results. (a) Score of every four-channel entry; the dashed line marks the best trained policy. (b) Success rate against latched stage completion of the same entries. (c) Full-channel success rate against the execution budget; the frozen programs spend no tokens at test time; arrow: the benchmark’s own budget; dotted line: where the online agent matches them.

PyRUA-Dex scores 72.9 on Bench2Dex, a new SOTA (Table 1 , Figure 3 a). Its success rate of 60.6% and stage completion of 82.3% exceed those of every trained policy, including ME-Dex 1.5, which fine-tunes one checkpoint per task. The lead is largest in stage completion (Figure 3 b): when a program fails, it has usually completed part of the task, because each stage function checks its own result and the next one starts from what is there.


##### The programs are robust to perturbations.

The success rate of PyRUA-Dex varies by only 4.1 points across the four channels, and the equivariance channel, in which the perturbation changes the correct action, is its best. Every trained policy loses most on the perturbed channels; GR00T N1.7 drops from 39.4% to 13.2%. A program measures the scene in every episode and computes its motions from that measurement, whereas a trained policy reproduces the mapping it saw in the demonstrations.


##### The margin is widest where precision and order decide the task.

PyRUA-Dex exceeds the best trained policy on 15 of the 26 tasks (Appendix C , Figure 8 ). The widest margins are on the bimanual piano melody (99.5% against 32.0%), ball sorting and baking-tray preparation. A trained policy leads on the other 11 tasks. On three of them our programs almost never succeed, each with a stage that no agent solved during development: placing the pan with the bread on its stand, returning the bottle after pouring from hand to hand, and completing the jigsaw within the step budget. In answer to Q1, programs written and refined by coding agents outperform policies trained on demonstrations on the benchmark as a whole, but not on every task.


#### 4.3 Refinement Makes Explored Programs Reliable

(b) Held-out completions (of 32) of the versions evaluated during refinement; version 0 is the program after exploration, and the large dot is the version that was selected. Refinement raises the success rate from 44.6% to 60.6% and the score from 59.1 to 72.9, with gains on 20 of the 26 tasks (Appendix C ). The largest gains are on stationery sorting (6.5% $\rightarrow$ 49.0%), baking-tray preparation (26.0% $\rightarrow$ 68.0%) and the hammer task, which exploration never completed (0% $\rightarrow$ 37.0%).


##### Explored programs fail at a single stage, with budget to spare.

We classify the 341 failed validation runs of the programs after exploration on the 22 tasks they completed at least once (Figure 4 a). In 68% of them the program had returned with at least 30% of the step budget unused, and in 59% one stage function had failed once and every later function had returned at once; only 2% ran out of steps. Calling failed stage functions again puts the unused budget to work, and refining the bottleneck targets the stage that fails. On task 12 (Figure 4 c), the program after exploration places both screwdrivers on almost every held-out layout but strikes the wooden block on 4 of 32 and returns the hammer on none; after refinement, on 17 and 15.


##### The gain carries over to unseen layouts.

Held-out completions rise over successive versions on most tasks (Figure 4 b). After exploration and after refinement the programs complete 43.6% and 61.7% of the 832 validation layouts, close to their test success rates of 44.6% and 60.6%. In answer to Q2, refinement adds 16.0 points by repairing the stage at which an explored program fails, and the gain holds on unseen layouts.


#### 4.4 Frozen Programs Outperform the Same Model Acting Online

The online agent and PyRUA-Dex use the same model: the agent queries it in every test episode, PyRUA-Dex only during development. At the benchmark’s step budget, the frozen programs complete 61.5% of the Full-channel episodes, 2.4 times the online agent’s 25.6% on the 78 episodes that its report runs at every budget [ 31 ] (Figure 3 c). The online agent reaches 61.5% only with ten times the step budget, at 33.7M tokens per episode.


##### Development is paid once.

Learning a task takes a median of 427M tokens and 21.6 agent hours in exploration and 214M tokens and 14.8 agent hours in refinement (Appendix C ). Developing all 26 tasks took less than a third of the tokens that the online agent spends on the benchmark’s 5,200 test episodes at the step budget, and every further episode of a frozen program costs no tokens and runs at the control rate. In answer to Q3, a frozen program is better: moving the model’s reasoning from test time to development yields both higher success and lower cost.

### 5 Real-World Experiments

We apply the same two stages to a real bimanual platform: two Franka FR3 arms with two Sharpa Wave SE hands (22 joints each), a ZED-M stereo camera above the table and an Intel RealSense D405 camera on each wrist (Figure 5 ).


##### Real-to-sim: a calibrated simulation of the platform.

The simulation is a MuJoCo [ 36 ] model built from the platform’s calibration: the cameras’ intrinsics and poses, the hand-eye transforms of the wrist cameras, the table, the transform between the arms and each hand’s mount. Objects are reconstructed from photographs or a phone video and a few measured sizes. The hands start from the manufacturer’s model, and their drives are identified on the real hands under the controller the robot uses (system identification, Appendix D.1 ): on motions held out from the fit, the simulated joints follow the real ones to 0.12–0.18 ∘ , against 0.80–3.1 ∘ for the manufacturer’s model. What the calibration leaves uncertain, from the hands’ gains and friction to camera poses, lighting and object masses, is randomized in every layout (Appendix D.2 ). A program receives the three RGB images with their calibration, the head camera’s depth, the joint positions and each fingertip’s force and contact point, exactly as on the robot.


##### Sim-to-real: refinement with real-robot feedback.

Exploration runs once, as on the benchmarks, and refinement is repeated in rounds. After each round the program is executed on the robot, where a safety guard rejects any motion that comes within a few millimeters of a joint limit, the table or the other arm. What the robot reveals is written back into the simulation, in its physics (the measured table height, the thumb’s coupled swing, softer contacts) or in the task’s rules (a wrist camera kept clear of the table), together with the instructions the user gives at the robot, and the next round starts in the updated simulation. Over these rounds the six tasks rise from 119 to 168 of their 192 validation layouts (Table 3 , Figure 5 d); stacking three cubes improves from 6 to 20 and then to 30. When an update of the simulation breaks a program, the next round restores it: Ode to Joy goes from 32 to 0 and back to 31. On the robot, the frozen programs put both cubes into the basket, build the tower of three cubes, open the box by its fabric loop and hold it open, take the brush and write “Dex”, and play Night Cruise on the piano with both hands (Figure 6 ).

### 6 Limitations and Future Work


##### Limitations.

Coding agents write motion well and sustained contact poorly. The programs are strongest where geometry, order and timing decide a task, and weakest where continuous control of force and slip is required: trained policies lead on the frying pan, the pour from hand to hand and the jigsaw puzzle, and assembly is the lowest of the DexJoCo tasks (34.0% under object randomization). A program acts through explicit targets and thresholds, and the simulated fingertip sensors render contact coarsely (the tactile maps of Bench2Dex respond only on flat faces square to the pad), so the programs infer contact mainly from joint lag. Refinement strengthens stages that already hold on some layouts; a stage that never holds remains a problem of exploration. Finally, each task is learned separately, and on the real robot we show the frozen programs running but measure their reliability in the calibrated simulation rather than over repeated hardware trials.


##### Future work.

The most direct extension is to let a program hand its contact phases to learned controllers and to develop it against a more faithful tactile simulation. A second direction is transfer across tasks: a task library stores what worked, with code and measured rates, and the piano pieces show that a skill distilled from it carries over within a family; seeding the exploration of a new task with the libraries of related tasks would extend this across families. A third direction is a systematic evaluation on hardware, with success rates over repeated trials on the real robot.

### Appendix A Benchmarks, Baselines and Evaluation


##### Bench2Dex.

Bench2Dex [ 49 ] runs in Isaac Lab and pairs each of its 26 bimanual tasks with one of 12 arm–hand embodiments: UR5, xArm7, Franka Panda, KUKA IIWA7, RM65 and JAKA ZU7 arms with Shadow, Allegro, LEAP, Ability, Orca, Schunk, Wuji, Sharpa, Revo2, DexHand021, RH56DFX and RH5DG2 hands, with 6 to 22 driven joints per hand. The tasks include tool use (hammer and screwdrivers, a ladle for soup), articulated objects (microwave, fridge, toilet lid, faucet), pouring, sorting and multi-stage packing, and a bimanual piano melody. Each scene has five cameras, among them a head camera and one camera on each wrist, and tactile maps on the fingertip pads. We evaluate under the leaderboard’s protocol at the benchmark commit used for submission, with the benchmark’s own result files checked by its validator (5,200 episodes, no errors). Efficiency is averaged over the tasks with at least one success, and SR, LSCR and Eff are averaged equally over channels and tasks.


##### DexJoCo.

DexJoCo [ 42 ] runs in MuJoCo and has 11 tasks for Franka arms with 16-DoF Allegro hands, six for one arm (hammering a nail, clicking a mouse, picking up a bucket, pinching tongs, folding glasses, watering a plant) and five for two (unlocking an iPad, the Tower of Hanoi, assembly, cooking with a microwave, taking a photograph). Its rand-obj setting randomizes the placement of the objects and the height of the table; rand-full also randomizes the camera poses, the lighting and the tabletop texture. A program receives the benchmark’s policy observation, the RGB images of its cameras without calibration, the flange poses and the hand joints, and commands flange poses and hand joint targets at 30 Hz. Both stages run in rand-full. Refinement ran on ten tasks, and the photograph task keeps its program from exploration. One frozen program per task is evaluated in both settings under the official protocol, three seeds of 50 episodes per task and setting (1,650 episodes per setting).


##### Baselines.

On Bench2Dex the trained policies are imitation learning (ACT [ 51 ] , Diffusion Policy [ 10 ] ), vision-language-action models ( $\pi_{0.5}$ [ 4 ] , GR00T N1.5 [ 25 ] and N1.7 with and without tactile input, Mora V0, MXI), world-action models (Phoenix-MWA [ 1 ] , GigaWorld-Policy-0.6 [ 13 ] ) and ME-Dex 1.5, a visuo-tactile action model with task-specific checkpoints [ 19 ] ; all of them train on the benchmark’s 1.3K teleoperated demonstrations. The online GPT-6 Astra agent prepares in context on each task’s training data and then controls the robot in every test episode; it reports the Full channel only. Its report runs 78 episodes (three per task) at budgets from 0.55 $\times$ to 10 $\times$ the step budget and gives its token costs [ 31 ] ; we compare at equal budget and cost on those episodes, and Table 1 lists its leaderboard entries (260 episodes at 1 $\times$ , 78 at 10 $\times$ ). On DexJoCo the trained policies are ACT, Diffusion Policy with transformer and CNN backbones (DP-T, DP-C), $\pi_{0.5}$ and GR00T N1.5, each trained per task and setting on 100 teleoperated demonstrations [ 42 ] , and the online agent runs at 0.5–10 $\times$ the step budget in rand-full [ 31 ] . RAX also reports DexJoCo results [ 14 ] , with a different model (Claude Opus 5.5 in Claude Code); as the model differs, they are not comparable, and we leave them out.


##### Agents and compute.

Exploration ran eight agents per task (208 sessions), one agent that curates the shared memory and one distilling agent; refinement ran one agent per task and one distilling agent. Each agent is one conversation. Simulators run on NVIDIA B200 nodes with eight GPUs each, one to four simulators per GPU, and every simulator shares its GPU with a SAM 3 worker; three refinement runs used an RTX PRO 6000 cluster with the same code.


##### Test evaluation.

The test evaluation runs the final program under the benchmark’s protocol with access to the program’s own calls only (observation, stepping, calibration, tactile maps, forward kinematics and segmentation), without stage count and without a language model. The test layouts decide nothing: a task whose refinement produced no better program keeps the test record of its program from exploration.

##### Program and runner.

A programmatic policy for a task with stages $1,\dots,K$ consists of a setup function and one stage function $f_{k}$ per stage (Figure 7 ). setup measures the scene into a context; $f_{k}$ starts where $f_{k-1}$ left robot and scene and returns an outcome that says whether, by the program’s own measurement, the stage holds. With AGAIN = True the runner makes further passes, in which every function that did not end ok is called again, in order, while steps remain and a pass still uses steps; each function is told how it last ended. A program reads its remaining step budget from the codebox and never assumes a number.


##### A model of every hand.

The codebox builds a model of each hand once, from the robot’s public description alone: the kinematic tree, the commanded and coupled joints with their ranges, the links’ collision shapes and the links that carry the fingertip pads. It reads no link or joint name. Each fingertip link makes a finger, and the thumb is the finger whose base stands out of the row of the others. A finger’s joints are grouped by what they do: the joints that bend the finger at its base, those that curl the rest of it, and those that spread it sideways, each group on one scale from open (0) to closed (1); the thumb can also oppose and flex. Which way a joint closes is read from its range.


##### Grasps instead of joint angles.

From the model the codebox works out, for every set of fingers, which object widths their pads can hold squarely (the pads within $30^{\circ}$ of facing each other) or at an angle (up to $45^{\circ}$ ), and which bars the palm can wrap. A program asks to grasp an object it has measured, and the codebox picks the fingers and how far to open them. The same calls ( shape , close with a squeeze, grasp , release and single-finger motions) drive the hands of all 12 Bench2Dex robots and the Sharpa hands of our real platform.


##### Touch.

A program does not assume that a grasp held; it checks. holding reads the fingertips’ touch and compares how far the hand closed with how far it closes when empty, and feel moves a hand until something is touched. Touch comes from the fingertip maps and from joint lag, the gap between a joint’s commanded and measured position: a finger that meets an object falls behind its command. Each robot’s drive responses (lag, ramps, settling) were measured once in the simulator, so this lag is told apart from the lag of a free motion.


##### Arms.

Arm calls put a frame of the hand (the grasp point, the palm, a fingertip or a pad) at a point, with an approach direction, via points and a hand shape formed on the way. arc moves the hand on a circle about an axis, for doors, lids and knobs, and together runs one call per arm at the same time. Every call plans whole before it moves: it solves all its poses first and, without having moved, returns ik_unreachable with the nearest pose it can take, table when a link would go below the measured table, or needs_reconfigure with the cost of reconfiguring the arm, which it takes only when allowed. All motions run at the joints’ speed limits, so a program saves steps by needing fewer motions, not by moving faster.


##### Perception.

Sensing uses only the episode’s camera calibration and the robot’s own sensors. segment returns SAM 3 [ 6 ] masks for a ladder of prompts, from an object’s name to its appearance; locate combines an object’s masks in the calibrated fixed cameras into its position, footprint, height and yaw, at no cost in steps; relocate and look measure it again with a wrist camera, from where the hand is or from close above; table and probe measure the table’s height, from the cameras and by a light touch-down. Every call returns an outcome with its reason, data and the steps it took, and never raises for a failure it names.


##### From an episode to a program.

An agent explores interactively, and what it found must become a program that runs alone. When an explored layout that reached a stage is closed, the harness compiles the path the agent kept into a recipe : the palm’s waypoints relative to the objects the agent located, split into phases at every closing and opening of a hand, with the grasps it made. It also writes a draft program in the staged form whose stage functions play the phases that led to each stage. A recipe is open loop: it carries free motion and targets relative to objects, and contact does not carry over by itself. The agent turns the draft into a program by closing the loop: measuring again before contact, checking that a hand holds, and stating how each stage function fails.


##### Exploration.

A state reset returns the episode to a checkpoint by running it again from its start, and no simulated time passes while an agent thinks. An agent’s session has eight fresh training layouts, one to explore and seven on which its program then runs whole without the agent. The shared memory holds single episodes with state resets: its entries say that a method exists, never how often it works, and a program copies what it uses rather than depending on them. Only what earned a stage enters the task library, and every rate in it comes from evaluations without the agent. The library has fixed sections: the task and what is established about it; what scored; how each stage scored and where it failed, with method, code and rate as $n$ of $N$ fresh layouts; the checks these runs depend on; the step budget; and what is not solved yet, from which the next assignments are drawn.


##### Refinement.

A partial rollout runs the program up to and including the function of a chosen stage, holds the robot for 3 s of simulated time so that a released object comes to rest, and ends the episode there; its record is the simulator’s own count. Every reply to the agent reports the stages: for each, the layouts that entered and reached it, how the function ended on the others, the steps of a call, how often the function was called again and with what result, and whether the program’s own verdict agreed with the simulator’s count. Every episode the agent starts and every layout of a rollout counts toward its 1,500 training layouts; the periodic evaluations draw their own layouts, and further work is refused until the evaluation that is due has been run. Between versions that differ by a layout or two on the held-out set, the agent keeps the one whose stages hold more often. The submitted program and the current one also run on 32 fresh layouts that nobody has used, which gives an unbiased rate. On the benchmarks refinement runs once per task; for the real robot it is repeated in rounds (Section 5 ).


##### What an agent is given.

Every agent of either stage receives the same material and nothing learned elsewhere: the primitives’ manual (the calls of the codebox, their arguments and how each reports its outcome); the robot’s structure as the hand model derives it (fingers, joints, limits, grasp frames) and the measured joint responses; the benchmark’s rules (what an episode is, what a program may read, the step limit); and the task’s goal in words, written by hand from the task’s definition, with every stage stated in terms an agent can see and measure. The goal texts never describe the benchmark’s checking code and give no way to satisfy a stage other than doing it. While developing, an agent reads the simulator’s stage count after each episode; a program never does. Exploration starts from an empty task library; refinement starts from the library and program that exploration left, and from nothing that the refinement of any other task produced.

### Appendix D Real-Robot Details


#### D.1 System Identification of the Hands

System identification estimates the parameters of a model from measurements of the real system, so that the model responds to a command as the real system does. We apply it to the drives of the hands, because the programs depend on them: the codebox reads contact from the lag between a joint’s commanded and measured position (Appendix B ), so a simulated finger that follows its commands differently from the real one would report contact where there is none, or miss it, once the program runs on the robot. The manufacturer’s model describes the hand’s geometry, but its drive parameters do not match the hand as we run it: on the test motions below, its joints deviate from the real ones by 0.80–3.1 ∘ (Table 7 ).


##### What is identified.

We keep the links, joints and meshes of the manufacturer’s MJCF model of the Sharpa Wave SE and set, for each of the 22 joints of a hand, three groups of drive parameters. The first is the controller. On the robot, a joint with angle $q$ and reference $q^{\mathrm{ref}}$ is driven with the torque

[Tableau] $$\tau=K_{p}\left(q^{\mathrm{ref}}-q\right)+K_{d}\left(\dot{q}^{\mathrm{ref}}-\dot{q}\right),$$ (5)

where $K_{p}$ is the stiffness and $K_{d}$ the damping. Our controller raises the factory gains (1.5 to 2 times the stiffness, up to 1.7 times the damping), adds an 80 Hz dither scheduled by each joint’s reference speed, and corrects the reference in advance. In simulation, each joint has a position actuator with the same gains, whose torque is $K_{p}(u-q)-K_{d}\,\dot{q}$ for a command $u$ ; commanding $u=q^{\mathrm{ref}}+(K_{d}/K_{p})\,\dot{q}^{\mathrm{ref}}$ reproduces the torque of the robot’s controller exactly. The second group is the joint’s range and its speed and torque limits, which we read from the real hand (the model’s 0–15 ∘ for the little finger’s CMC joint is 0–25 ∘ on the hand). The third group is the joint’s friction loss $f$ (its dry friction) and its armature $a$ (the inertia of the rotor as seen at the joint), which we fit to the real joint’s response.


##### Fitting.

For joint $j$ we record the real joint while it follows single-joint reference motions at 10, 50 and 150 ∘ /s, and replay the same references in simulation. With $\mathcal{M}_{j}$ the set of these recordings and $T_{m}$ the number of samples of recording $m$ , the parameters $\theta_{j}=(f_{j},a_{j})$ minimize the mean squared difference between the simulated and the measured joint angle,

[Tableau] $$\theta_{j}^{\ast}=\arg\min_{\theta_{j}}\;\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}_{j}|}\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\frac{1}{T_{m}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{m}}\left(q^{\mathrm{sim}}_{j,m}(t;\theta_{j})-q^{\mathrm{real}}_{j,m}(t)\right)^{2},$$ (6)

where $q^{\mathrm{real}}_{j,m}(t)$ is the joint’s measured angle at sample $t$ of recording $m$ and $q^{\mathrm{sim}}_{j,m}(t;\theta_{j})$ its simulated angle under the parameters $\theta_{j}$ . We solve this problem with the Nelder–Mead method, one joint at a time, for the 44 joints of the two hands.


##### Validation.

We test the identified model on motions that the fit did not use: one joint moving slowly, five-finger grasps at three speeds, a finger wave and random smooth motions of all joints (Table 7 ). The simulated joints follow the real ones to 0.12–0.18 ∘ , against 0.80–3.1 ∘ for the manufacturer’s model. The real hand follows its own references to 0.11–0.22 ∘ on the same motions, so the remaining difference between simulation and robot is as small as the hand’s own tracking error.

##### What is not identified.

The dither is not simulated. Contact stiffness is not identified either: the simulation reports each fingertip’s force and contact point as the robot does, but their scale is not calibrated against the robot’s sensors. For the arms, the simulation uses the FR3’s own limits and the same trajectory generator as the robot. What identification leaves uncertain is randomized, as described next.


#### D.2 Randomization and Objects


##### Randomization.

Every layout of the simulation draws what the calibration leaves uncertain: the hands’ gains, friction and armature, the pads’ friction, the cameras’ poses within their calibration accuracy, the arms’ bases, the cameras’ noise and color, the lighting, distractor objects, and the objects’ masses and frictions.


##### Cube to basket.

Cube to basket is the running example of Figure 5 : its objects were reconstructed from a phone video, and every layout draws the cubes’ edge, the basket’s size, all masses and frictions, and the placement of basket and cubes.
