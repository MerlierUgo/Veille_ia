## Look Only When Needed: Certificate-Guided Visual Routing for Efficient Diffusion VLMs

Diffusion vision-language models (DVLMs) process their entire input at every denoising step. Since visual tokens make up 89–97% of this input on the benchmarks we study, most of the decoding computation is spent repeatedly processing the same image. Visual token pruning methods partially relieve this burden by removing part of the visual tokens, but they lose the removed information and still process the remaining image at every step. To measure how often the image is actually needed, we compare LLaDA-V’s predictions with and without the image at every decoding step and find that removing the image changes a token being unmasked at only 22.8% of steps on average and at as few as 7.9%. We therefore propose LOWN, a decoding method that decides at every step whether to process the image and how many tokens to unmask. LOWN predicts the masked tokens without the image and processes the image only when this prediction disagrees with the last image-conditioned prediction. Agreement certifies the earlier prediction for reuse, and the number of certified tokens sets how many are unmasked in that step. We further observe that the need for the image differs across benchmarks, so LOWN’s adaptive decision is needed rather than a fixed schedule. Without training or a tuned threshold, LOWN retains 99.6% of LLaDA-V’s score on ten benchmarks at 35.3% of the decoding FLOPs on average. LOWN also generalizes across LLaDA-based DVLMs, composes orthogonally with visual token pruning, and runs under key-value caching for further efficiency gains. ### 1 Introduction

Recent diffusion language models match similarly sized autoregressive models while refining several masked positions in parallel over denoising steps ( Nie et al., 2025 ; Ye et al., 2025a ) . In diffusion vision-language models (DVLMs) such as LLaDA-V ( You et al., 2026 ) and others ( Yang et al., 2025a ; Yu et al., 2025 ) , bidirectional attention prevents direct use of the exact key-value caching found in autoregressive models, so standard inference processes the full multimodal sequence at every step. Visual tokens dominate this sequence, accounting for 89–97% of LLaDA-V’s input across our benchmarks (Figure 1(b) ), while a forward without them costs only 3–10% as much. DVLM decoding therefore spends most of its compute repeatedly processing an unchanged visual input.

Existing methods reduce this cost through visual token pruning ( Chang et al., 2026 ; Xu et al., 2026 ; Kim et al., 2026 ) (Figure 1(a) ), intermediate-state caching ( Wu et al., 2026a ; Liu et al., 2026 ; Nguyen-Tri et al., 2026 ) , or unmasking more tokens per step ( Kim et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2026b ; Wu et al., 2026b ; Wang et al., 2026 ; Lee et al., 2026 ) . These approaches make image-conditioned forwards cheaper, reuse their computation, or run fewer decoding steps, but none asks whether the current step needs the image. We ask whether removing the image would change the model’s decision at the current decoding state.

Comparing LLaDA-V’s predictions with and without the image along the same decoding trajectory reveals substantial redundancy (Section 3.2 ). The two give the same top-1 token at 83.4% of the positions selected for unmasking, and 85.0% of final tokens had already appeared as top-1 predictions before unmasking. Within a trajectory, image-dependent steps tend to cluster early rather than recur periodically, while their overall share varies from 7.9% on MMBench to 43.8% on DocVQA. A fixed schedule therefore finds it hard to capture when the image is needed. These observations motivate deciding image access per input and step from predictions the model has already computed.

We propose LOWN (Look Only When Needed), a decoding method built on this redundancy. LOWN stores the top-1 predictions and confidences from the latest image-conditioned forward, which we call the FULL forward, as an anchor and tests it with a cheap TEXT-ONLY forward at each step. Agreement certifies the anchor prediction for reuse, while disagreement triggers a new FULL forward. The confidence-ordered run of agreements also determines how many tokens to unmask. LOWN requires neither training nor a task-specific threshold and can be added to visual token pruning or key-value caching because it reduces how often a FULL forward is needed (Figure 1(b) ).

We evaluate LOWN on ten benchmarks spanning multiple-choice, document and chart understanding, mathematical reasoning, and open-ended captioning. On LLaDA-V, it retains 99.6% of the baseline score at 0.35 $\times$ the decoding FLOPs (Figures 1(c) and 1(d) ). Against accelerated methods given at least its FLOPs budget, it scores highest on eight of the ten benchmarks. Combined with key-value caching or visual token pruning, it further cuts the partner’s FLOPs by 1.8–2.5 $\times$ , and with the key-value cache the pair decodes 5.6–7.0 $\times$ faster than the model alone. The same rule transfers to LaViDa and VidLaDA.

Our contributions are as follows.

- • We show through paired FULL and TEXT-ONLY forward comparisons that most DVLM decoding steps can skip image access, while the necessary steps vary across trajectories and are not well captured by fixed schedules.

- • We introduce LOWN, a training-free and threshold-free method that uses prediction agreement to jointly decide when to process the image and how many tokens to unmask.

- • We validate LOWN across ten benchmarks and three DVLM backbones, retaining 99.6% of LLaDA-V’s score at 0.35 $\times$ its decoding FLOPs, composing orthogonally with visual token pruning, and running under key-value caching.


### 2 Related Work


##### Diffusion language and vision-language models.

Discrete diffusion and masked diffusion models generate sequences through iterative token recovery ( Austin et al., 2021 ; Sahoo et al., 2024 ; Shi et al., 2024 ) . This paradigm has scaled to large language models ( Nie et al., 2025 ; Ye et al., 2025a ) and extended to image and video understanding ( DiffusionGemma Team, 2026 ; You et al., 2026 ; Li et al., 2025 ; Yu et al., 2025 ; He et al., 2026 ) . These models establish the DVLM setting studied here. LOWN addresses the complementary inference question of how often denoising must reprocess the visual input.


##### Efficient inference for diffusion LMs and VLMs.

Visual-token reduction shortens the visual sequence in vision transformers and autoregressive VLMs ( Bolya et al., 2023 ; Chen et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2025 ; Xing et al., 2025 ; Shang et al., 2025 ; Yang et al., 2025b ) . ZOO-Prune ( Kim et al., 2026 ) combines projector-level sensitivity estimates with diversity-aware selection.

For diffusion VLMs, RedVTP prunes using attention from still-masked response tokens, while D 3 ToM uses previously decoded tokens to guide visual-token selection and merging ( Xu et al., 2026 ; Chang et al., 2026 ) . These methods determine which visual tokens a forward processes, but they lose the removed information ( Endo et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2026a ) .

Other work keeps the visual input and reduces the number of steps or the cost of a forward. Decoding schedules unmask multiple tokens per step when confidence, cross-step stability, or attention to the image supports it ( Wei et al., 2026 ; Wu et al., 2026a ; Yu et al., 2025 ; Wu et al., 2026b ; Wang et al., 2026 ; Lee et al., 2026 ) . Prophet ( Li et al., 2026b ) unmasks all remaining tokens once the average top-two logit gap in the answer region is large enough. KLASS ( Kim et al., 2025 ) combines confidence with cross-step distribution stability and is our schedule baseline. Caching methods reuse intermediate states across denoising steps ( Ma et al., 2025 ; Liu et al., 2026 ; Nguyen-Tri et al., 2026 ; Li et al., 2025 ) , and we compose LOWN with Fast-dLLM ( Wu et al., 2026a ) , a blockwise approximate key-value cache. Pruning and caching act inside a forward, on the tokens it processes or the states it reuses, and schedules on how many tokens a step unmasks. LOWN decides both whether a step processes the image and how many tokens it unmasks from the agreement between a TEXT-ONLY and the last image-conditioned prediction, and, keeping only predictions, it runs on top of pruning and caching.


### 3 Background and Motivation


#### 3.1 Preliminaries

Starting from a fully masked response, a DVLM repeatedly predicts response tokens over $T$ denoising steps while conditioning on fixed visual and prompt tokens ( Sahoo et al., 2024 ; Nie et al., 2025 ; You et al., 2026 ) . Let $v$ denote the visual tokens, $c$ the prompt tokens, $y$ the response, $x_{t}$ the partially masked response at step $t$ , and $M_{t}$ its masked positions. A FULL forward over $(v,c,x_{t})$ gives the top-1 prediction and confidence at each $i\in M_{t}$ ,

[Tableau] $$\hat{y}^{\mathrm{full}}_{t,i}=\arg\max_{w}\,p_{\theta}(y^{i}{=}w\mid v,c,x_{t}),\qquad\hat{s}^{\mathrm{full}}_{t,i}=\max_{w}\,p_{\theta}(y^{i}{=}w\mid v,c,x_{t}).$$ (1)

LLaDA-V runs a FULL forward at every step and unmasks the $k$ most confident positions ( Nie et al., 2025 ; You et al., 2026 ) , where $k$ is the response length divided by $T$ .


#### 3.2 When Does a DVLM Need the Image?

To measure how often the DVLM uses the image, we compare two forward passes along LLaDA-V’s standard decoding trajectory on all ten benchmarks of Section 5 . The FULL forward evaluates $p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid v,c,x_{t})$ , whereas the TEXT-ONLY forward removes the visual tokens while preserving the position IDs and evaluates $p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c,x_{t})$ . At every $i\in M_{t}$ , we record the top-1 predictions from both forwards, $\hat{y}^{\mathrm{full}}_{t,i}$ and $\hat{y}^{\mathrm{text}}_{t,i}$ , together with the FULL confidence $\hat{s}^{\mathrm{full}}_{t,i}$ . The FULL forward alone determines the decoding trajectory, and the TEXT-ONLY outputs are recorded without affecting its decisions. Appendix A gives the full protocol and per-benchmark values. The image rarely changes a decision. Of the positions the model unmasks, 83.4% receive the same top-1 token from the FULL and the TEXT-ONLY forward, averaged over the ten benchmarks, so at most steps the prediction follows from the text alone and the presence of the visual tokens rarely changes the token being unmasked. Moreover, 85.0% of the final tokens had already appeared as the top-1 prediction at the same position before the step at which they were unmasked. The baseline nevertheless continued to recompute those positions with the image until unmasking them.

The need for image access varies along the decoding trajectory. On eight of the ten benchmarks, disagreement between the two forwards is largest in the early denoising steps and decreases as the response takes shape. This pattern is not universal, as illustrated by DeCapBench in Figure 2(a) . The affected token positions also differ across tasks, and the share of image-dependent steps ranges from 7.9% on MMBench to 43.8% on DocVQA, 22.8% on average (Figure 2(b) ). Variation across steps, positions, and benchmarks therefore makes a fixed image-access schedule poorly matched to the observed trajectories and motivates input- and step-adaptive image access.

A method that skips the image therefore needs a test that runs at every step, and the TEXT-ONLY forward that exposed the redundancy is a natural candidate, since it costs 3–10% of a FULL forward. The next section turns the agreement between the two forwards into that test.

### 4 Method

LOWN separates two decisions that standard DVLM decoding makes with a single FULL forward, whether the current response state needs the image and how far decoding can advance (Figure 3 ). It retains the last image-conditioned prediction as an anchor, tests that anchor with a TEXT-ONLY forward, and runs a FULL forward only when the test cannot certify enough tokens. The same test controls how many tokens are unmasked, allowing a successful TEXT-ONLY step to advance farther than the baseline. LOWN changes neither the model parameters nor the attention pattern.


#### 4.1 Anchor and Certificate

Whenever LOWN runs a FULL forward, it retains its predictions $\bar{y}$ and confidences $\bar{s}$ on the positions that remain masked, and we call this pair the anchor . At step $t$ , LOWN runs a TEXT-ONLY forward and orders the masked positions by decreasing anchor confidence $\bar{s}$ . A candidate token $i$ is certified when its current TEXT-ONLY prediction agrees with the anchor, $\hat{y}^{\mathrm{text}}_{t,i}=\bar{y}_{i}$ . The two predictions differ in whether they see the image and in the response state they were computed from, so their agreement approximates two conditions at once, that the image is not needed for this token and that the anchor remains valid after the state has changed, without an absolute confidence threshold. In the measurement of Section 3.2 , a fresh FULL forward overturns 4.1–11.0% of the tokens certified against a one-step-old anchor, and in LOWN’s own runs it overturns 2.2–4.7% of the tokens unmasked on TEXT-ONLY steps (Section 5.3 ).

The longest confidence-ordered prefix of certified candidates forms $\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{text}}$ . The first disagreement ends the prefix, so the acceptance length $|\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{text}}|$ is set by the current input and step rather than by a constant. The scan also applies a repetition guard, detailed in Appendix B.1 . The certificate is checked against a prediction that ages as the response changes, and its error grows with that age (Section 5.3 ). LOWN therefore replaces the entire anchor whenever it runs a FULL forward, so that every certificate is checked against the most recent FULL forward.

#### 4.2 Certificate-Guided Decoding

Each step runs a TEXT-ONLY forward and forms the certified prefix $\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{text}}$ of Section 4.1 . The length of this prefix decides whether the step also needs a FULL forward. When the prefix contains at least $k$ tokens, where $k$ is the number of tokens the baseline unmasks per step, the step skips the FULL forward and accepts the whole prefix, unmasking every certified position to its anchor prediction rather than only $k$ of them. This whole-prefix acceptance is what lets one step unmask more than $k$ tokens. When the prefix is shorter than $k$ , the anchor no longer certifies enough tokens for a step, so the step runs a FULL forward, replaces the anchor with its predictions and confidences, and unmasks $k$ positions to their FULL predictions in disagreement-first order. Positions whose TEXT-ONLY and FULL predictions differ, $\hat{y}^{\mathrm{text}}_{t,i}\neq\hat{y}^{\mathrm{full}}_{t,i}$ , come first, and the remaining positions follow FULL confidence. These $k$ positions form $\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{full}}$ .

The resulting state update is

[Tableau] $$x_{i}\leftarrow\begin{cases}\bar{y}_{i},&i\in\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{text}},\quad|\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{text}}|\geq k,\\ \hat{y}^{\mathrm{full}}_{t,i},&i\in\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{full}},\quad|\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{text}}|<k.\end{cases}$$ (2)

Both branches unmask at least $k$ tokens, so LOWN never takes more than the baseline’s $T$ steps, while the TEXT-ONLY branch can take fewer. Appendix B.1 provides the detailed algorithm.


#### 4.3 Cost Analysis

Let $\mathcal{C}(n)$ denote the FLOPs of one forward over $n$ tokens. The FULL and TEXT-ONLY input lengths are $N_{F}=|v|+|c|+|x_{t}|$ and $N_{T}=|c|+|x_{t}|$ . If the baseline runs $T$ FULL forwards, its cost is $F_{\mathrm{base}}=T\cdot\mathcal{C}(N_{F})$ . LOWN runs $n_{T}$ TEXT-ONLY forwards and adds a FULL forward on $n_{F}$ of those steps, which gives $F_{\mathrm{LOWN}}=n_{F}\cdot\mathcal{C}(N_{F})+n_{T}\cdot\mathcal{C}(N_{T})$ . Relative FLOPs are therefore

[Tableau] $$R=\frac{F_{\mathrm{LOWN}}}{F_{\mathrm{base}}}=\frac{n_{F}}{T}+\frac{n_{T}}{T}\frac{\mathcal{C}(N_{T})}{\mathcal{C}(N_{F})}=f+\sigma\rho.$$ (3)

Here $f=n_{F}/T$ is the frequency of FULL forwards, $\sigma=n_{T}/T\leq 1$ is the relative number of steps, and $\rho=\mathcal{C}(N_{T})/\mathcal{C}(N_{F})$ is the unit cost of a TEXT-ONLY forward. The certificate lowers $f$ , and accepting more than $k$ tokens can lower $\sigma$ . The input determines $\rho$ , which decreases as visual tokens occupy more of the FULL input. Appendix B gives $\mathcal{C}$ and the per-benchmark $\rho$ .

Visual token pruning changes only the FULL term. Let $\pi=\mathcal{C}(N_{F}^{\mathrm{pruned}})/\mathcal{C}(N_{F})$ be the FULL-forward cost with the pruned visual input. Pruning alone costs $\pi$ relative to the baseline, while its composition with LOWN costs $\pi f+\sigma\rho$ , and both are charged the pruning overhead once. Pruning reduces the cost of each FULL forward, whereas LOWN reduces how often one is run. Section 5 evaluates this composition.

### 5 Experiments

Our experiments test whether LOWN preserves quality while reducing decoding cost. Section 5.1 defines the models, benchmarks, baselines, and evaluation protocol. Section 5.2 evaluates standalone LOWN, its composition with visual token pruning and key-value caching, and its transfer to other image and video DVLMs. Section 5.3 compares image-access schedules, isolates visual routing and adaptive acceptance, and analyzes visual information preservation and certificate reliability.


#### 5.1 Implementation Details


##### Benchmarks and evaluation.

We evaluate LOWN on LLaDA-V ( You et al., 2026 ) and LaViDa ( Li et al., 2025 ) across ten benchmarks, namely MathVista ( Lu et al., 2024 ) , ScienceQA ( Lu et al., 2022 ) , ChartQA ( Masry et al., 2022 ) , MME ( Fu et al., 2025 ) , MMBench ( Liu et al., 2024 ) , DocVQA ( Mathew et al., 2021 ) , GQA ( Hudson and Manning, 2019 ) , LLaVA-Bench ( Liu et al., 2023 ) , CapArena ( Cheng et al., 2025 ) , and DeCapBench ( Ye et al., 2025b ) . We additionally evaluate VidLaDA-8B ( He et al., 2026 ) on EgoSchema ( Mangalam et al., 2023 ) and MLVU ( Zhou et al., 2025 ) . We evaluate quality using each benchmark’s task-specific metric and apply the same scorer to all methods within a comparison. Analytical decoding FLOPs are reported relative to each backbone’s baseline.


##### Baselines.

We compare RedVTP ( Xu et al., 2026 ) , ZOO-Prune ( Kim et al., 2026 ) , KLASS ( Kim et al., 2025 ) , and D 3 ToM ( Chang et al., 2026 ) . All methods share prompts and response budgets within each comparison. Where multiple operating points are available, we select settings by FLOPs rather than task scores and assign each comparator at least the corresponding LOWN budget. We apply the same principle to LLaDA-V and LaViDa and evaluate Fast-dLLM ( Wu et al., 2026a ) separately in the caching comparison.


##### LOWN implementation.

For standalone LOWN on LLaDA-V, we use batch size 1 and greedy decoding with temperature and classifier-free guidance scale set to zero, without key-value caching. The image is encoded once. FULL forwards reuse its visual embeddings, whereas TEXT-ONLY forwards remove those embeddings while preserving the original position IDs of the retained prompt and response tokens. Appendix C provides the complete evaluation protocol, baseline settings, and decoding details.

##### Results on LLaDA-V.

The upper block of Table 1 reports LOWN’s standalone results. LOWN retains 99.6% of the baseline score on average at 0.353 $\times$ the baseline FLOPs. It scores highest among the accelerated methods on eight of ten benchmarks. LOWN uses one adaptive rule without additional tuning, whereas KLASS reports benchmark-specific thresholds. On a FULL step, this order first unmasks the positions where the image changed the prediction, so image-dependent tokens are not deferred.


##### Composition with visual token pruning.

We combine LOWN with RedVTP and ZOO-Prune without retraining or changing their retention ratios. With RedVTP at 50% retention, LOWN reaches a normalized average of 98.3 at 0.223 $\times$ the baseline FLOPs and scores highest on all ten benchmarks (Table 1 ). It further reduces FLOPs by 57.6% for RedVTP at 50% retention and 60.6% for ZOO-Prune at 33% retention (Table 11 ). For a visual-token pruner, further cost reductions require discarding more visual information. LOWN instead reduces how often the retained visual tokens are processed, allowing less aggressive pruning to outperform a more aggressive setting in both quality and compute. On MathVista, RedVTP at 50% retention with LOWN scores 54.9 at 0.195 $\times$ FLOPs, against 51.7 at 0.354 $\times$ for RedVTP at 30% retention. Figure 4 shows the broader trade-off.

##### Composition with key-value caching.

LOWN also provides plug-and-play acceleration on top of key-value caching, since the cache lowers the cost of each forward and LOWN’s acceptance lowers the number of forwards. We retain Fast-dLLM’s cache block and refresh settings and combine its token selection with LOWN’s acceptance rule. Across MathVista, LLaVA-Bench, and DeCapBench, adding LOWN reduces mean generation time by 38.1–50.1% (1.61–2.01 $\times$ further speedup) and FLOPs by 44.1–58.9% relative to Fast-dLLM (Table 2 , Appendix D ). The combined scores are within 2.0 points of LLaDA-V, but lower than Fast-dLLM on all three tasks, with a decrease of 5.0 points on LLaVA-Bench.

##### Visual information preservation.

Visual token pruning permanently removes part of the visual representation before decoding, whereas LOWN skips image access only at selected steps and can consult the complete image later. The distinction is most apparent on tasks that require reading or composing fine-grained visual evidence, where pruning incurs its largest quality losses (Figure 5 and Table 13 ). In Figure 6 , LLaDA-V and LOWN use the relevant visual evidence and answer correctly, whereas RedVTP prunes that evidence and answers fluently but incorrectly. Here LOWN concentrates FULL-forward steps on visually grounded content, while surrounding language is handled by TEXT-ONLY forwards.


##### Certificate reliability.

On the baseline decoding trajectories of Section 3.2 , we compare each certified token with a fresh FULL prediction. With a one-step-old anchor, the disagreement rate is 4.1–11.0%, and it generally increases with anchor age, reaching 25.3% on DocVQA when the anchor is eight steps old. On LOWN’s own trajectories, the same rate is 2.2–4.7% for the tokens it unmasks on TEXT-ONLY steps. In practice the anchor rarely gets old, since every FULL forward replaces it and 92–97% of these tokens use an anchor from the preceding step, none older than five (Table 16 ). An oracle schedule that uses the image only on the steps where it changes an unmasked decision costs 0.33 $\times$ the baseline against LOWN’s measured 0.35 $\times$ , and, since it still runs one forward per baseline step, it is not a lower bound. Appendix F.2 gives per-benchmark results.


### 6 Conclusion

DVLMs need not process visual tokens at every denoising step. Along LLaDA-V’s decoding trajectories, most unmasked decisions agree between FULL and TEXT-ONLY forwards, but the steps requiring visual input vary across tasks, motivating adaptive rather than fixed image access. LOWN turns this agreement into a training- and threshold-free rule for deciding when to run a FULL forward and how many tokens to unmask. Across ten benchmarks, it retains 99.6% of LLaDA-V’s score at 0.35 $\times$ the decoding FLOPs. The same rule transfers unchanged to LaViDa and extends to VidLaDA under a controlled video protocol, while also composing orthogonally with visual token pruning and running under key-value caching. These results establish image-access scheduling as a broadly applicable axis for efficient DVLM decoding.


##### Limitations.

LOWN reuses anchor predictions that agree with the TEXT-ONLY forward, so a reused token can differ from a fresh FULL prediction, more often as the anchor ages (Appendix F.2 ). It also reduces cost less on chart and document reading, where visual dependence persists and more steps need a FULL forward (Appendix E.5 ). The same agreement decides how many FULL forwards a response needs, so its cost cannot be set before decoding. Combining LOWN with visual token pruning, however, partly addresses this, as its retention ratio gives some control over cost.

### Appendix B Method Details and Cost Accounting

This appendix gives the complete decoding procedure, restates the cost model of Section 4.3 and derives its forward cost $\mathcal{C}(n)$ , and writes visual token pruning in the same form as Equation 3 .


#### B.1 Detailed Decoding Algorithm

Each response starts fully masked, with its length and baseline unmasking schedule set by the benchmark protocol. The first iteration runs TEXT-ONLY followed by FULL on the same response, establishing the anchor and unmasking $k$ positions. Confidence is the softmax probability of the top-1 token. The repetition guard ends the certified prefix before a candidate whose token ID matches either immediate neighbor that is already unmasked or was accepted earlier in the same scan. The guard is needed because the positions accepted on one TEXT-ONLY step are predicted independently of one another, so two adjacent positions can receive the same token, and without the guard such pairs would be unmasked together and appear as repeated words in the response. Algorithm 1 gives the full procedure.

#### B.2 Cost of One Forward

Section 4.3 writes the cost of LOWN relative to the baseline as $R=f+\sigma\rho$ (Equation 3 ). The baseline runs $T$ FULL forwards over $N_{F}=|v|+|c|+|x_{t}|$ tokens. LOWN runs $n_{T}$ TEXT-ONLY forwards over $N_{T}=|c|+|x_{t}|$ tokens and adds a FULL forward on $n_{F}$ of those steps, so $f=n_{F}/T$ is the frequency of FULL forwards, $\sigma=n_{T}/T$ the relative number of steps, and $\rho=\mathcal{C}(N_{T})/\mathcal{C}(N_{F})$ the cost of a TEXT-ONLY forward relative to a FULL one, where $\mathcal{C}(n)$ is the FLOPs of one forward over $n$ tokens. A FULL step is charged both forwards. This section gives $\mathcal{C}(n)$ .

We count matrix multiplications only, with one multiply-add as two FLOPs. For a model with $L$ layers, hidden width $d$ , FFN width $m$ , and vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ (for LLaDA-V, $L{=}32$ , $d{=}4096$ , $m{=}12288$ , $|\mathcal{V}|{=}126{,}349$ ), one layer over $n$ tokens costs

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{layer}}(n)\;=\;\underbrace{8nd^{2}+4n^{2}d}_{\text{attention}}\;+\;\underbrace{6ndm}_{\text{FFN}},\qquad\mathcal{C}(n)\;=\;L\cdot\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{layer}}(n)+2nd|\mathcal{V}|,$$ (4)

where $8nd^{2}$ is the Q/K/V/O projections, $4n^{2}d$ is $QK^{\top}$ and $AV$ and is quadratic in length, $6ndm$ is the three matrices of the gated FFN, and the last term of $\mathcal{C}(n)$ is the LM head. The quadratic term is kept because it is what makes $\rho$ smaller than the length ratio $N_{T}/N_{F}$ , since removing $v$ shrinks $n$ by roughly 9–33 $\times$ and the quadratic term by roughly the square of that. #### B.3 Other Methods in the Same Form

Relative to the unpruned baseline, visual token pruning costs $\pi$ , LOWN costs $f+\sigma\rho$ , and the two together cost $\pi f+\sigma\rho$ . Pruning fixes its budget $\pi$ before decoding starts, whereas LOWN decides $f$ and $\sigma$ during decoding, per input and per step. Key-value caching instead lowers the cost of every forward that reuses the cache, and LOWN’s acceptance lowers the number of forwards, which is the saving measured in Table 2 .

#### C.4 Video Evaluation

We evaluate the 500-question EgoSchema subset and the 502-question MLVU test set. We use VidLaDA-8B with 32 frames per video, sampled at one frame per second and uniformly subsampled when needed. Each frame yields 210 visual tokens after spatial pooling. We adopt a controlled decoding protocol with a 128-token response, 128 baseline diffusion steps, and block length 32, following the efficiency-ablation setup of He et al. (2026) . Decoding is greedy, with zero classifier-free guidance and low-confidence remasking. The baseline’s parallel decoding is disabled, and LOWN uses adaptive acceptance within each block. We ask the model to reason inside <think> and </think> before answering with an option letter. This protocol differs from VidLaDA’s released 64-frame, two-token evaluation.

The two methods are evaluated on identical questions and frames. We score only the answer span after the closing tag, using the released task parsers. EgoSchema reports accuracy. MLVU reports the mean accuracy over its nine task types (M-Avg). For each benchmark, the FLOPs ratio divides LOWN’s summed decoding FLOPs by the baseline’s sum over the same questions. It includes the decoder and LM head and excludes the vision encoder.


### Appendix D Wall-Clock Measurements


#### D.1 Generation Time

All LLaDA-V timing experiments were run on the same server with NVIDIA H200 GPUs, with one experiment per GPU at a time. Each task mean averages all per-example times from one recorded execution of the full evaluation set.
