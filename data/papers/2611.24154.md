Games are a rich testbed for visual agents, demanding perception, long-horizon planning, and precise control. However, collecting human gameplay is costly, and learning from gameplay videos requires inferring missing actions. Existing datasets also cover a limited range of games, often without explicit tasks or verifiable outcomes. To address these gaps, we introduce Game 2 Player , a framework that co-develops game environments and players to collect diverse and verifiable gameplay experience at scale. In the Game 2 Player loop, a Builder agent adapts existing web games, exposes their internal state, and defines tasks with state-based progress measures and success checks. A Coding Player and a VLM Player then attempt the tasks and report problems to the Builder for repair, so that play serves as both environment validation and training data collection. With Game 2 Player , we build 568 verified web games with 3,436 tasks, collect 15.7K trajectories (307 hours of gameplay), and hold out 50 games as the benchmark Player Bench . To evaluate the collected experience as training data, we train VLM agents with progress-weighted SFT , assigning greater weight to steps that advance task progress. Game2Player-9B improves progress from 14.3% to 31.2% on Player Bench and from 30.0% to 53.1% on the external GameWorld benchmark. More broadly, our work suggests a path for visual agents to scale their gameplay experience by building, verifying, and learning from existing game worlds.


## Game 2 Player : Scaling Gameplay Experience for Visual Agents through Environment–Player Co-Development


### 1 Introduction

Visual agents powered by vision-language models (VLMs) have made rapid progress on computer-use and embodied tasks [ 37 , 67 , 25 , 53 , 43 ] . Video games pose a broader challenge for visual intelligence [ 46 , 52 , 39 , 58 , 47 , 28 ] : each game brings its own visuals, rules and controls, which an agent must infer from the screen while reasoning over a changing scene and acting with precise timing. This diversity makes gameplay experience a valuable source of training data for general-purpose visual agents. We therefore aim to collect gameplay data at scale across diverse games for visual agent training.

A central bottleneck is obtaining such gameplay experience at scale: learning requires trajectories that pair actions with explicit goals and reliable signals of success. Human gameplay recordings [ 16 , 80 , 23 , 34 , 19 ] are expensive to collect, and online gameplay videos lack actions, which have to be recovered with inverse-dynamics or latent-action models [ 2 , 41 , 9 , 73 ] ; in both cases, goals and outcomes are annotated after the fact [ 3 , 56 , 74 , 39 ] . Interactive game environments provide all three natively [ 32 , 77 , 20 , 33 , 30 , 79 , 1 , 27 ] , but each new game demands game-specific engineering, which limits their coverage.

Coding agents could automate both the environment building and gameplay needed for data collection. They can synthesize verifiable environments from code repositories and websites [ 66 , 5 , 51 , 60 ] , write games from scratch [ 72 , 36 ] , and develop executable policies for playing games [ 52 ] . Adapting existing web games for such data collection requires locating their internal state in engine-specific code and using it to define task initialization, progress measures and success checks. However, the resulting environment may pass the coding agent’s own tests even when a task is impossible to complete, its progress measure fails to update, or its success check triggers too early. Such defects may only become apparent when an agent actually plays the task. This motivates environment–player co-development : environments and the players that test them are developed together, each improving the other through play.

Building on this principle, we present Game 2 Player , a framework in which a Builder agent and two players, a VLM Player and a Coding Player , co-develop verifiable environments from in-the-wild web games ( Fig. 2 ). The Builder sets up each game in a sandboxed, reproducible runtime, exposes its internal state, and defines tasks whose progress and success are read from that state, guided by engine-specific skills. The players then attempt every task: the VLM Player plays from screenshots, pausing the game while it reasons, whereas the Coding Player writes a Python policy over player-visible state, runs it in real time and refines the code from the recorded outcomes. When a failure traces back to the environment, the players report it, either as a defect, such as a key with no effect or progress that never moves, or as a request for on-screen information that the policy cannot read. The Builder checks each report against the recorded episode before repairing the environment, and accepts an environment once the players’ episodes confirm its own evaluation ( Sec. 3.2 ).

Once an environment is accepted, the players turn from testing it to collecting experience in it, and every recorded episode is paired with its task instruction and a dense, state-based progress signal. The two players are complementary teachers, offering efficient demonstration collection and visually grounded action explanations, respectively. A code policy can be rerun without any model calls, supplying high-quality demonstrations quickly and at scale. The VLM Player acts on the same screenshots that the trained agent will see and explains each action in a short note, so its episodes show how to approach a game even when they fall short of the goal. We distill both sources into one pixel-based agent with progress-weighted SFT , which up-weights actions that advance task progress.

Using Game 2 Player , we have built 568 verified environments with 3,436 tasks across more than 25 genres and seven engines. As verifiable game benchmarks remain limited in both number and diversity, we also use it to construct Player Bench , 50 held-out games with 287 tasks for evaluating game agents on unseen games. In a controlled rebuild of 20 games, player reports raise the share of correctly implemented tasks from 65% to 72% and the tasks solved by code policies from 34 to 46. The code policies written in the loop play in real time without model calls and record 22 times as many steps per hour as a VLM Player. Training on 15.7K trajectories more than doubles the Player Bench progress of Qwen3.5-9B, from 14.3% to 31.2%, and the gains transfer to the external benchmark GameWorld [ 30 ] , where Game2Player-9B reaches 53.1%, higher than the published results of several proprietary models. Beyond these results, our ablations examine how progress-weighted SFT, the data from each player, and thinking at inference time contribute to the gains.

In summary, our contributions are:

- • Environment–player co-development : Game 2 Player , a scalable framework where a Builder and two players use gameplay feedback to build and validate verifiable environments;

- • Player Bench : 50 held-out games and 287 tasks, more challenging than GameWorld, where agents of Tab. 3 score on average 12.8 and 7.6 points lower progress and success;

- • Progress-weighted distillation : training on 15.7K trajectories from both players more than doubles Qwen3.5-9B ’s progress on Player Bench , with gains transferring to GameWorld.


### 2 Related Work

Interactive game benchmarks. Existing game benchmarks span text and grid games [ 61 , 32 , 14 ] , puzzles and adventures [ 11 , 1 ] , browser games [ 48 , 30 , 20 ] , and commercial or 3D environments [ 77 , 46 , 27 ] . GameWorld [ 30 ] provides state-based verification for 34 browser games; AI GameStore [ 72 ] generates 100 games with human involvement; and PlaySuite [ 50 ] evaluates thousands of open-source games with a video-language judge. Game 2 Player automates the adaptation of existing games into state-verified environments for both training and evaluation.

Offline game benchmarks and gameplay data. Question-answering benchmarks on gameplay video [ 29 , 44 , 4 , 45 , 57 , 56 ] test perception without play, and strong offline scores do not imply strong play [ 64 , 56 ] . Gameplay corpora with recorded or inferred actions train generalist game agents [ 2 , 28 , 9 , 73 , 26 , 58 ] , and some add language annotations afterwards [ 39 , 3 , 56 ] . Synthetic games with verifiable rewards improve VLM reasoning [ 49 , 62 , 7 ] . Our trajectories are collected against a task, so every step carries its instruction, its state-grounded progress and its outcome. Table 1 compares Game 2 Player with game benchmarks and gameplay datasets.

Environment synthesis for agents. Automated pipelines construct verifiable environments from code repositories [ 66 , 22 ] , terminal tasks [ 15 , 42 ] , websites and applications [ 5 , 51 , 60 ] , or recreate games from observation [ 36 ] . Open-ended learning further co-evolves environments and agents [ 54 , 12 , 13 , 75 ] . In Game 2 Player , the feedback that drives repairs comes from two players with different views who play every task of an existing game, and the Builder checks their failures against the recordings before repairing the environment. We apply co-development to refine the environments while developing two complementary players for scalable data collection.

Training visual agents. Visual agents learn from trajectories through supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning across embodied, computer-use and game environments [ 6 , 63 , 55 , 69 , 8 , 68 , 28 ] . Concurrent work scales gameplay training: PLAYER-1 [ 31 ] uses demonstrations from 172 games followed by RL in 16 synthetic environments, while GameScaling [ 78 ] continues pre-training on up to 1,600 hours of gameplay. We instead collect complementary demonstrations from code policies and a visual player in verified environments and distill them through progress-weighted SFT, achieving higher progress and success rates on both Player Bench and GameWorld.

Code policies, privileged teachers and reasoning. Code can serve directly as a game agent’s policy [ 52 , 65 ] . Distilling a teacher with privileged information into a student that sees less is a long-standing recipe in embodied learning [ 59 , 10 , 35 ] . We use state-reading code policies as teachers for pixel-based students and weight their supervision by measured task progress, related in spirit to step-level filtering of agent trajectories [ 18 ] . Our weights come from progress events measured in the game state, and they set how often each step is sampled. Reasoning in agent trajectories is either produced by the agent itself [ 71 , 37 , 58 ] , written afterwards [ 70 , 63 , 55 ] , or grounded in privileged state [ 21 , 76 , 10 ] . Game 2 Player follows the first route, retaining the VLM Player’s own notes and native thinking during play as supervision, whereas code policy demonstrations carry neither kind of text and supervise actions only.

### 3 Game 2 Player

Game 2 Player turns games that are open to download from the web into verifiable environments and records gameplay in them for training ( Fig. 2 ). The Scout finds games that match a request, either a description or a game name, and that one person can play offline in a browser ( Sec. 6 ). The Builder, a coding agent, turns each game into an environment, and a VLM Player and a Coding Player play every task. Section 3.1 defines environments, tasks and the two players. Section 3.2 describes how the Builder and the players co-develop each environment, Sec. 3.3 how both players record training data in accepted environments, and Sec. 3.4 how progress-weighted SFT trains a visual agent on them.

> Figure 2 : Overview of Game 2 Player . The Scout selects games from web portals ( top ). In the co-development loop ( middle ), the Builder turns each game into an environment with state-verified tasks, the VLM Player and the Coding Player play every task and report faults of the game, and the Builder checks each report against the recording before repairing it. Both players then record gameplay in the accepted environments to train Game 2 Player with progress-weighted SFT ( bottom ).


#### 3.1 Environments, tasks and players

We model a game as a partially observed process with hidden state $s_{t}$ , keyboard and mouse actions $a_{t}$ and screenshots $o_{t}$ . A verifiable environment wraps the game so that it runs offline from a fixed seed and pauses while a model chooses an action. Through one interface, the state API , it exposes two views of $s_{t}$ . The state probe $\phi(s_{t})$ is a small set of named variables read from the running game, such as the player’s health, and only scoring reads it. The player observation $\psi(s_{t})$ , which code policies read, holds named fields, such as the positions of visible objects, limited to what a person could see or infer from the screen, with no hidden information such as face-down cards.

A task is a tuple

[Tableau] $$\begin{gathered}\tau=(\iota,\,c,\,p,\,g),\\ p_{t}=p\bigl(\phi(s_{t})\bigr)\in[0,1],\qquad g_{t}=g\bigl(\phi(s_{t})\bigr)\in\{0,1\},\end{gathered}$$ (1)

where $\iota$ is a natural-language instruction, $c$ a start routine that puts the game into the situation where the task begins, $p$ a progress function and $g$ a success predicate, with $g_{t}=1\Rightarrow p_{t}=1$ . Both $p$ and $g$ read only the state probe, so scoring needs no human or model judge. An episode is one attempt at a task. It begins with the start routine, lasts at most $T$ steps, each one observation followed by one action, and scores the best progress $\bar{p}=\max_{t}p_{t}$ that it reaches. Progress rises with the milestones of ordinary play, so an episode that falls short of success still earns partial credit. In the task of Fig. 2 , for example, the state probe reads the delivered iron ( resources.iron ), progress grows with each delivery, and success requires buying Drill 1.

The two players act on different views of the same game ( Fig. 3 ). A VLM Player is a vision-language model that sees only the screenshots $o_{t}$ . Every VLM Player plays through one harness . At each step the harness pauses the game and gives the model the task instruction, the current screenshot, its recent steps and the control guide , the Builder’s account of what each key and mouse action does. The model returns one action with a note , one sentence on why it chose the action. The Coding Player is a coding agent that writes one code policy per game, a Python program that reads only the player observation $\psi(s_{t})$ and plays each of the game’s tasks in real time without calling a model. Either player’s episode is recorded as a trajectory

[Tableau] $$\xi=\bigl(\tau,\;\{(o_{t},\,a_{t},\,z_{t},\,p_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}\bigr),$$ (2)

where $z_{t}=(n_{t},r_{t})$ holds a VLM Player’s note $n_{t}$ and, if any, its thinking $r_{t}$ .

> Figure 3 : Two players on the same task and one training format. The code policy reads the player observation $\psi$ and sends keys at every frame without calling a model ( top ). The VLM Player sees only the screenshot and pauses the game to choose each action ( bottom ). The code policy’s keys are cut into steps in the VLM Player’s format, so both players yield the same kind of training example ( middle ).


#### 3.2 Environment–player co-development

When the Builder tests its own environment, it uses the inputs it designed and reads the variables it exposed, so it can miss problems that only a player would meet. A player who knows only the control guide and the screen may find a control that the guide does not explain or a quantity that a policy needs but cannot read. Co-development therefore lets both players test every environment through the interfaces that later produce training data and evaluation scores , and report what they find to the Builder. The loop repeats the four stages of Fig. 2 until the environment is accepted.

Build. The Builder works in one persistent session with the game, its source and a library of engine skills , instructions for hooking into engines such as Unity WebGL, Flash, Godot and PICO-8. It first proposes a task plan backed by evidence from the source or from play. The plan is then frozen: a task may be removed only if the Builder cannot implement it, and a task that proves hard can be neither dropped nor made easier . The Builder packages the game for offline play and writes the state probe, the control guide and the start routine, progress function and success predicate of every task. Finally, it evaluates the environment without editing it.

Play. The VLM Player plays every task through the harness with a fixed model. The Coding Player runs the game’s code policy on every task and revises it until the episodes succeed or their failures point to the environment.

Report. A player reports each failure that it traces to the environment and cites the recorded episodes. Either player can file a defect report , which describes something that does not work, such as a menu that hides part of the game, a key with no effect, progress that never moves or success that fires too early. Only the Coding Player files observation requests , which name a quantity that a code policy needs but cannot read from the player observation, with its meaning and where it appears on the screen; a VLM Player reads the screen directly and needs none. No report may ask for hidden information, a shortcut to success or an easier task.

Verify, then repair. Each report reopens the Builder. A player can mistake its own error for a defect, so the Builder first replays the recorded episode and checks the claim against the video and the state trace . If the checks contradict the claim, the Builder marks it disproved and cites the evidence. Otherwise it repairs the environment: a defect report leads to changes in the game code, the state probe or the control guide, and an observation request adds fields to the player observation. The players then play again.

Acceptance. The environment and the code policy are accepted separately. An environment is accepted when every player report has been fixed or disproved and the environment passes a scripted gate that re-checks it for obvious errors and shortcuts. A code policy is accepted when it completes a sufficient share of the game’s tasks, with at least two successful episodes for every task it claims ( Sec. 6 ).

The collection. Running the pipeline on games from itch.io, Newgrounds and other portals produced 568 accepted environments with 3,436 tasks, after the Scout skipped games that cannot be played offline by one player and the Builder removed tasks that it could not implement. They span more than 25 genres and seven major engines ( Fig. 4 ). We hold out 50 of these games as Player Bench , our test set, and use the other 518 for training.


#### 3.3 Gameplay data from two players

Both players record training gameplay in the accepted environments, and their data are complementary. A code policy calls no model while it plays, so it can be rerun from new seeds at little cost, whereas VLM Player episodes keep the model’s notes and thinking and also cover tasks that no code policy completes. To train on both, we bring them into one format. VLM Player episodes already follow the harness, one action per step. A code policy acts continuously, so we cut its recording into harness steps ( Fig. 3 , middle): the recorder logs every key press and release with its time, and we convert this log into harness actions. In total, the two players recorded 15.7K trajectories with 307 hours of gameplay, with more details in Tab. 8 . Of the resulting 1.56M training examples, code policies provide 87.7% and VLM Players 12.3%.


#### 3.4 Progress-weighted SFT

A trajectory spends long stretches waiting, wandering or recovering from mistakes, while only a few actions, such as a well-timed jump or the turn into a portal, move the task forward. The progress $p_{t}$ recorded in every trajectory ( Eq. 2 ) tells these actions apart, and progress-weighted SFT uses it to weight the training examples. Each example pairs the harness context $x_{t}$ at step $t$ , the same input that the trained model receives when it plays, with a target $y_{t}$ : the action $a_{t}$ for a code policy step, and the action together with the note and thinking, when present, for a VLM Player step. Its weight is

[Tableau] $$w_{t}\;\propto\;\rho_{t}\cdot\max(1,\,c_{t}),$$ (3)

where the credit $c_{t}$ raises the weight of the last few actions before each rise in progress or success, since these actions most likely caused it, and the penalty $\rho_{t}$ removes or lowers the weight of the final actions of failed and timed-out episodes and of repeated actions that do not improve progress. The weights of games with many examples are also scaled down, so that a few long games do not dominate. Figure 5 illustrates these rules, and Tab. 11 gives their exact values and time windows. Instead of weighting the loss, we round each weight stochastically to an integer multiplicity $m_{t}$ with $\mathbb{E}[m_{t}]=w_{t}$ and resample the examples accordingly, so training minimizes

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\sum_{t}m_{t}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x_{t}).$$ (4)

From the 2.05M eligible steps left after segmentation and filtering, this resampling draws 1.56M training examples.

### 4 Experiments


#### 4.1 Benchmarks and setup

Benchmarks. We split the Game 2 Player collection by game and hold out 50 games with all 287 of their tasks as Player Bench , so no trajectory from a Player Bench game is used for training. Player Bench covers seven engines and four difficulty tiers ( Sec. 9 ). We also evaluate on GameWorld [ 30 ] , an independently built benchmark of 34 browser games and 170 tasks with state-based scoring and no overlap with our collection. We evaluate GameWorld with our harness rather than its generalist one; neither is consistently better ( Tab. 14 ). On both, we report Progress , the mean episode score $\bar{p}$ over equally weighted games, and Success , the share of tasks completed.

Protocol. A VLM Player attempts each task once from the same seed, playing through the harness at temperature 1 for at most 100 steps while the game pauses for each decision. Our trained models and the open-weight baselines see 50 steps of text history and six earlier screenshots besides the current one, while API models receive their full text history. Each step may use at most 512 output tokens with thinking off and 32,768 with thinking on.

Training. We fine-tune Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-9B [ 38 ] with progress-weighted SFT on our gameplay data, keeping the vision encoder frozen, which gives Game2Player-4B and Game2Player-9B . Both sizes share one recipe: one epoch of 9,237 steps at learning rate $10^{-5}$ with cosine decay. More details are given in Sec. 8 .

Setup. We measure what the co-development loop adds by building the same 20 games with 145 frozen tasks in three settings ( Sec. 10 ). The full loop runs Game 2 Player with a GPT-6.1 Terra Builder and GPT-6.1 Sol players, and reports return to the Builder for up to two rounds of repair. The no-reports setting shares the build and play of the full loop up to the first report, after which the environment stays as delivered and the Coding Player continues with the same budget. The generic-agent setting gives the same games and task requests to a generic coding agent with the Builder’s model but without our skills and tools, and a second generic agent writes the code policy. An auditor model checks every task against the original game and the frozen task definition, and we verify its verdicts by hand. A task’s environment is correct when it runs the original game and implements the task’s start, progress and success faithfully, and a task is solved when its environment is correct and the final code policy completes it from two seeds.

Results. Figure 7 splits the 145 tasks of each setting into solved, correct but unsolved, and incorrect. The generic agent leaves 120 environments incorrect and solves one task, so the same model without our skills, tools and players cannot build faithful environments. With our Builder, player reports cut the incorrect environments from 51 to 40 and raise the solved tasks from 34 to 46. They work in two ways: defect reports expose bugs that the Builder’s own tests missed, and observation requests give the Coding Player state that it cannot read itself ( Tab. 2 ). In There Is No Game , for example, the Coding Player reported that progress stopped at 0.9, and after the repair all nine tasks are correct and solved, while without reports all nine remain wrong. With GPT-6.1 Sol as the Builder, the full loop and the no-reports setting both give 136 correct environments, and reports still raise the solved tasks from 62 to 82.

Cost. Reports add 20% to the model cost per game, from $17.7 to $21.3. Recording gameplay then costs the two players differently in kind ( Fig. 7 ). A VLM Player pays for every step: GPT-6.1 Sol costs $54.7 per hour of gameplay. A code policy pays mostly once: writing it with the same model costs $10.8, after which it plays without model calls at $0.13 per hour of gameplay. It therefore becomes cheaper than the VLM Player after 12 minutes of gameplay, and our training data, with 40 minutes of code policy gameplay per game on average, lies well beyond this point. A code policy also records data much faster, because the game runs in real time for it but pauses while the VLM Player decides. In an hour of wall-clock time, the code policy records 7,775 harness steps, whereas the VLM Player records only 354.

Player Bench is more challenging than GameWorld. Every model evaluated on both benchmarks except Qwen3.8-Flash scores lower on Player Bench , by 12.8 progress and 7.6 success points on average. Even human players, 15 first-time participants who play in real time with the same instructions as the VLM Players ( Sec. 11 ), reach only 69.4% progress and 51.8% success, against 82.6% and 77.1% on GameWorld. A likely reason is that GameWorld uses games with available source code, whereas we admit any game that can be downloaded.

Difficulty tiers match human play. The Builder labels every task as easy, medium, hard or very hard ( Sec. 9 ), and human play follows this ordering ( Fig. 9 ): success falls from 76.5% on easy tasks to 11.8% on very hard ones, and the median attempt grows from about one minute to six.

Training roughly doubles Player Bench progress and transfers to GameWorld. With thinking on, Game2Player-9B reaches 31.2% progress and 15.0% success on Player Bench and 53.1% and 29.4% on GameWorld, the best among open-source models under 10B parameters and above the game-specific PLAYER-1 [ 31 ] and GameScaling [ 78 ] models. Compared with the Qwen3.5 base models, both trained models roughly double their Player Bench progress and gain more than 20 points on GameWorld, although GameWorld shares no games, tasks or success checks with our collection. Across four evaluation seeds with thinking off, the standard deviation of progress stays within 2.5 points ( Tab. 12 ), far below these gains. On GameWorld, Game2Player-9B even surpasses general-purpose models such as Qwen3.8-Flash, GLM-5.3-Flash, GPT-6 Luna and Claude Haiku 5.5, and comes close to Claude Sonnet 5.5 . On Player Bench , however, the strongest closed-source models reach about 60% progress, leaving much room for open models to improve.

Training makes thinking useful. Figure 8 evaluates each model with thinking on and off, using the same parameters. Before training, thinking has small and mixed effects: it changes progress by $+3.2$ and $-1.9$ points on Player Bench for the 4B and 9B models, and by $-0.4$ and $+1.3$ on GameWorld. After training, it adds 7.7 and 2.6 points on Player Bench and 14.6 and 19.2 on GameWorld. Thinking thus consistently improves performance once the models are trained on our gameplay, most of all on the unseen games of GameWorld.

Tables 4 and 10 vary the data source, the sampling weights, the amount of data and the number of training steps for both model sizes. We run all ablations with thinking off, which substantially reduces inference cost and allows a broader set of comparisons.

Data source. Each kind of player improves the base models on its own, and the two help in different ways. On Player Bench , VLM Player data is far more sample-efficient: with 0.19M examples it raises the 4B model from 12.7% to 25.1% progress, against 17.3% for 1.37M code policy examples, since it matches the evaluation format, with paused steps from screenshots, and keeps the model’s notes and any native thinking. On GameWorld, code policy data alone gives the best model at both sizes, with 36.8% and 38.0% progress, likely because its precisely timed key presses and holds transfer to games outside our collection. Combining both sources gives the best 9B model on Player Bench , with 28.6% against 25.6% and 20.1% for single sources, while the 4B combination falls between them.

Progress weighting. At an equal number of examples, progress-weighted SFT outperforms uniform sampling from the same recordings for both model sizes and on both benchmarks. It raises Player Bench progress from 20.9% to 23.4% for the 4B model and from 23.9% to 28.6% for the 9B model, and GameWorld progress from 33.5% to 34.9% and from 31.1% to 33.9%. Its credits, penalties and game balancing ( Sec. 3.4 ) focus training on the few actions that move a task forward.

Data volume and training steps. More training data keeps helping ( Fig. 10 , left): even 100K examples improve on the base models, and growing to all 1.56M raises Player Bench progress from 21.2% to 23.4% for 4B and from 21.2% to 28.6% for 9B. Over training steps, Player Bench progress keeps rising until the final checkpoint, while GameWorld progress plateaus after about 3K steps ( Fig. 10 , right).


#### 4.5 Case studies on held-out tasks

> Figure 11 : Qwen3.5-4B and Game2Player-4B on four held-out Player Bench tasks , with thinking on and at most 100 steps. Each panel shows the final frame of each model with its final progress, and the best progress reached after each step.

An important behavior that training teaches is precise timing control. In the held-out platformer Give Up ( Fig. 11 , top left), how long the direction keys are held largely decides how far the character runs and jumps. Within 100 steps, Qwen3.5-4B reaches 12.3% progress, while Game2Player-4B reaches 92.5%. Game2Player-4B sets an explicit hold duration on 47% of its key presses, against 1% for its base model. With identical harness instructions for both models, the model learns timing control from our data, in which code policy recordings are cut into timed holds and VLM Players use holds routinely.

The other three tasks in Fig. 11 come from different genres. In Alfonzo’s Bowling Challenge , the task is to destroy forty bricks with a ball steered by the arrow keys. Qwen3.5-4B sets a hold duration on 2 of its 98 key presses, alternates between left and right, and destroys two bricks. Game2Player-4B sets a duration on 69 of its 71 key presses, waits on 29 steps to follow the ball and destroys 39 bricks within 100 steps. In Kingdom Rush , the task is to build four towers. Qwen3.5-4B clicks and presses keys for 100 steps without building a tower, and 11 of its steps are invalid. Game2Player-4B opens the build menu on grass tiles beside the road, waits for gold when it cannot pay for the next tower, and builds the fourth tower at step 56. In the card game Forward , the knight moves by being dragged onto an adjacent card. Qwen3.5-4B mixes 34 clicks with 58 drags, and its progress stops at 20% after step 23. Game2Player-4B drags the knight from card to card and clears the area in 78 steps. Before some of its moves it hovers over a card to read it. Figure 23 in Sec. 12 adds the task instructions and the initial frames.


### 5 Conclusion

Game 2 Player is a scalable pipeline that co-develops verifiable game environments with the players that use them, whose reports catch defects the Builder’s own tests miss. The same loop scales up gameplay experience through cheap code policy reruns and VLM Player episodes that keep notes and thinking. Distilled with progress-weighted SFT, this experience roughly doubles the Player Bench progress of Qwen3.5 models and transfers to GameWorld.


##### Limitations.

Accepted environments are not guaranteed to be correct. An environment is accepted once it passes the Builder’s evaluation, the scripted gate and the players’ reports, without a full human audit. The audit of the co-development study finds that 72% of the tasks built by the GPT-6.1 Terra Builder and 94% of those built by the GPT-6.1 Sol Builder are implemented correctly, and human review of all 287 Player Bench tasks found no environment or scoring errors ( Sec. 11 ). The pipeline covers single-player web games that run offline in a browser, and online, multiplayer and large commercial games add licensing and runtime constraints that we do not address. A VLM Player plays with the game paused at every decision, so our scores do not measure play under real-time latency. The Builder, the players and many baselines are proprietary models whose versions change over time, so rebuilding the collection with other models may give different environments. We discuss the broader impact in Sec. 13 .

### 6 Environment construction


##### Scout.

The Scout searches game portals such as itch.io and other public source repositories. For each candidate it checks whether the game can be played alone and offline, whether its goals split into short tasks, whether it can be restarted reliably, and how its state could be read, for example from named variables in the source. It records evidence of play and the game’s license, and recommends to build, reserve or skip the game.


##### Builder phases.

The Builder runs four phases. Discover inspects feasibility and proposes tasks grounded in the source or in play. Implement packages the offline game and implements the state API, a JavaScript module loaded with the game, the control guide, and the start routine, progress and success of every task, editing only a fixed set of paths. Evaluate plays every task start from the player’s view, then tests real controls, state changes and task definitions without editing files. It labels each task’s start, progress and success as verified, pending or unsupported. A Builder pass requires that every task still matches the frozen plan, every start is verified, no progress or success definition is unsupported, and every verified progress definition cites a recorded change of its state variable. It also requires at least one state variable read from the game rather than estimated from the screen, and a check from the player’s view that the screen shows no defect and that every control does what the control guide says. Revise diagnoses the recorded evidence of the last evaluation before applying a bounded fix. The evaluation instructions state that the presence of an interface or a zero exit code is not evidence and that tasks should not be solved merely to pass evaluation.


##### Retired tasks and unsuitable games.

A frozen task that the game itself cannot complete, or whose start, progress or success still cannot be measured faithfully after a bounded attempt, is retired with its reason and a record that shows it. A task that is long or hard stays in the plan. At most a third of the plan may be retired. A game that would need more retired tasks, or whose progress could only be estimated from the screen, is unsuitable.


##### Engine skills.

The pipeline uses 13 skills, namely the Builder skill, eight engine skills for HTML5 capture, Unity WebGL, Flash, PICO-8, GameMaker, Godot, Construct and Defold, and four policy skills for general policies, canvas user interfaces, PICO-8 and Unity. The Builder selects an engine skill from the detected engine, and the Coding Player selects the relevant policy guidance. Each skill is a short instruction file with references to engine tools, and Fig. 12 shows the skill for Godot.

> Figure 12 : An engine skill. The Builder receives this skill once it has identified a Godot game. The linked workflow and tools are part of the skill.


##### State contract and scripted gate.

The state returned by the state API contains a schema version, the game identifier, wall-clock and game time, a status, whether the game is actionable, a terminal outcome, a game-specific state block and numeric metrics. The scripted gate checks these types, an actionable start, numeric score paths for every task, the terminal outcome domain, task definitions against the frozen plan, and finite targets above their start values. For each task start it loads the game with the external network blocked and a fixed seed, and then tests two resets.


##### Coding Player procedure and acceptance.

The Coding Player runs Discover , Implement , Evaluate , Diagnose and Revise . It inspects the visible information and the player observation, writes one code policy per game, records bounded headless episodes, separates policy failures from environment defects and retests after each revision. The Coding Player collects the player observation with a probe that it writes for each game. Every field of the probe comes from a named source, such as a game object or a page element, and the probe may read anything a person could see on the screen but no hidden information. When a visible quantity cannot be read without changing the game, the Coding Player files an observation request, and the Builder adds the quantity to the player observation. Every task needs at least two recorded attempts, and every task reported as completed needs at least two successful episodes of the current code policy. For a game with $n>1$ tasks, a code policy is accepted if it completes at least $\max(2,\lfloor n/4\rfloor+1)$ tasks, and a single-task game requires that task.


##### Reports and repair.

Table 5 lists what each player may report. The reports of a game start a new Builder run, which goes through all four phases with the frozen task plan. In Evaluate , the Builder gives each report one of three verdicts. A report is fixed when the Builder found the defect, repaired it and checked the affected behavior through player inputs. It is disproved when a check from the player’s side explains or contradicts the reported failure. It is unresolved when neither holds, and the Coding Player then examines it while writing its policy. An environment is accepted only when every report is fixed or disproved. Until then it stays pending, and no gameplay is recorded in it. After an observation request, the Coding Player checks each added field against the screen before its policy relies on it. Failures that the harness detects by itself, such as a game that does not start or reset, return to the Builder in the same way, and the affected tasks are played again after the repair.

##### Trajectory diversity.

We compare two runs $a$ and $b$ of the same task by their progress and by the keys they press. Let $p_{a}(t)$ be the best progress that run $a$ has reached by time $t$ and $T$ the duration of the longest run of the task. The progress distance is

[Tableau] $$D_{\mathrm{prog}}(a,b)=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\bigl|p_{a}(t)-p_{b}(t)\bigr|\,dt.$$ (5)

A progress curve rises from 0 to at most 1, so if the progress that a run never reaches is placed at time $T$ , the curve is a cumulative distribution of when progress arrives, and $D_{\mathrm{prog}}$ is the earth mover’s distance [ 40 ] between these distributions divided by $T$ . Let $k_{a}$ be the sequence of keys that run $a$ presses, in order. The key distance is

[Tableau] $$D_{\mathrm{key}}(a,b)=\frac{\mathrm{Lev}(k_{a},k_{b})}{\max(|k_{a}|,|k_{b}|)},$$ (6)

where $\mathrm{Lev}$ is the Levenshtein distance [ 24 ] , the smallest number of insertions, deletions and substitutions of keys that turn one sequence into the other. It is 0 for identical sequences and 1 for sequences without any common key. Both distances are averaged over all pairs of runs of a task, then over the tasks of a game and then over games. $D_{\mathrm{prog}}$ covers all 1,348 tasks in 330 games with repeated code policy runs, and $D_{\mathrm{key}}$ the 962 tasks in 248 games whose runs press keys, since clicks alone carry no key sequence. Two runs of the same code policy differ by $D_{\mathrm{prog}}=7.3\%$ and $D_{\mathrm{key}}=0.28$ .

As a baseline we use the repeated runs of MiniMax-M3.1-Flash, a VLM Player with low thinking and temperature 1, that were recorded with six runs per task over three seeds. We compare its runs with different seeds, as every code policy rerun uses a new seed. Over 346 tasks in 73 games it gives $D_{\mathrm{prog}}=10.2\%$ , and over the 263 of these tasks that use keys it gives $D_{\mathrm{key}}=0.48$ . Reruns of a code policy thus differ in progress and in keys on a scale comparable to a VLM Player that samples every action. Figures 15 and 16 show progress curves and key presses of repeated code policy runs on randomly chosen tasks, together with the density of both distances over tasks.

##### Recording.

Both players run in the same browser runtime and are recorded by the same recorder. The recorder logs each input when it is sent, so input that a game ignores or generates by itself is neither lost nor invented. It samples the state probe and task progress on the same clock as the video, and for VLM Players it also stores every request, response and reasoning trace.


##### Segmentation and filtering.

Held inputs are snapped to the nearest of 26 durations between 0.05 and 7.8 seconds. Presses shorter than 0.075 seconds become taps. Up to five consecutive key-state segments, each at most 0.25 seconds long, separated by at most 0.2 seconds and spanning at most 0.8 seconds, become one chained action, and longer holds are split at 7.8 seconds. An idle gap between actions becomes a run of waits of at most 1 second each, and a remainder of 0.2 seconds or less is dropped. Each action is paired with the frame at its start, and the preceding steps form its history as in the harness. Episodes are kept when they have a valid outcome, a probed video and at least 90% state coverage, and duplicates of the same source, game, task and seed are removed. All Coding Player episodes of a task are excluded if no policy ever completed that task.


##### Effect of preprocessing.

The recordings produce 2,088.5K candidate steps after action segmentation and wait insertion. Basic validity filtering removes 13.6K invalid action targets. Weighting assigns zero weight to 1.2K actions at failed endings and 19.6K waits. Of the remaining positive-weight steps, weighted sampling leaves out 379.1K actions and 243.5K waits, then adds 132.2K repeated uses of actions. This yields 1,563.6K training examples, comprising 1,164.9K actions and 398.8K waits ( Tab. 10 ).

##### Sampling weights.

A material progress event is a positive increment at least as large as both 0.02 and the 75th percentile of positive increments, or a crossing into a new progress decile, with events at least 0.35 seconds apart. Table 11 lists the weighting stages behind Eq. 3 , and Fig. 5 illustrates them. Repeated-action penalties affect 80.9K action targets, and timeout penalties affect 7.5K. Game balancing assigns an aggregate action budget of 1,165.0K uses to 1,413.0K valid action targets. These weights determine the subsequent resampling rather than removing one step for every penalty.

Each weight $w_{t}$ becomes an integer multiplicity

[Tableau] $$m_{t}=\lfloor w_{t}\rfloor+\mathbf{1}\bigl[u_{t}<w_{t}-\lfloor w_{t}\rfloor\bigr],$$ (7)

where $u_{t}\in[0,1)$ is a reproducible pseudo-random number, so that an example appears $w_{t}$ times in expectation. Let $n_{j}$ be the number of eligible action targets of game $j$ , excluding waits, and $M$ the median of these counts across games. The budget of game $j$ is

[Tableau] $$B_{j}=\begin{cases}n_{j},&n_{j}\leq M,\\ \min\!\bigl(n_{j},\operatorname{round}(M^{1-\alpha}n_{j}^{\alpha})\bigr),&n_{j}>M,\end{cases}$$ (8)

with $\alpha=0.8445$ , which reduces the contribution of large games while keeping the full budget of smaller ones. We set $\alpha$ by hand for this balance and did not tune it on evaluation results.

##### Notes, thinking and supervision.

A short note explains the chosen action in the note field of the action response, while native thinking is the longer text between <think> and </think> before that response. These are the two components $n_{t}$ and $r_{t}$ of $z_{t}$ in Eq. 2 . Only notes accompany earlier actions in the input history, and each target contains the thinking generated for its own step, when available.

Code policy recordings contain neither kind of text. Their targets have an empty thinking prefix with zero loss weight, followed by the action response with normal loss weight. For VLM Player recordings, the action response and the recorded note receive supervision, together with the native thinking when it was recorded. Missing notes are left empty rather than generated after the episode.

Both inference modes use the same trained parameters. With thinking on, the chat template opens the thinking span and the model generates it before the action response. With thinking off, the template supplies an empty thinking span and generation begins with the action response. The difference is an inference setting rather than a separate fine-tuning stage.


##### Training recipe.

Game2Player-4B and Game2Player-9B use the same recipe and differ only in the base model. Full fine-tuning keeps the vision encoder and aligner frozen and uses BF16, learning rate $10^{-5}$ with cosine decay and 3% warmup, one epoch, and packed sequences of at most 25,000 tokens. Training runs on eight 80 GB GPUs with two packed sequences per device and three gradient-accumulation steps, for 9,237 steps. The bottom row of Fig. 8 compares the success rates of the base and trained models with thinking on and off.

##### Action space.

Each step returns one action, which may contain a timed key sequence. Keyboard actions press one key, press several keys together, set held keys or play an ordered key sequence. Pointer actions click, hold, move, drag and scroll, and the vocabulary also includes typing and waiting. A key press or click without a duration is a 0.05-second tap, and durations are capped at 10 seconds. Qwen models emit a JSON tool call with pointer coordinates normalized to 0–1000.


##### Budgets and failures.

The step budget counts every model call, including calls without a valid action. Reaching the budget without completing the task is a timeout that keeps the best progress reached. Depending on the task, a terminal failure ends the episode or triggers a reset within the same budget. A case counts as evaluated only with a valid recording, a usable outcome and at least 90% state coverage, and infrastructure failures are rescheduled.

##### Setup.

The study uses 20 games from the collection, spread over seven engines, with their 145 task definitions frozen, so all settings implement the same goals. In the full loop and the no-reports setting, the Builder builds each game once with its engine skills and tools. The VLM Player then plays every task and the Coding Player writes a code policy until the first player report, and the run forks at that report. In the full loop, the report returns to the Builder, which checks it against the recording and repairs the environment, and the players continue on the repaired environment, for up to two rounds of repair. Each Builder run may revise its environment up to three times, the Coding Player may revise its policy up to 50 times, and the VLM Player plays each task for up to 100 steps from seed 67. In the no-reports setting, no report reaches the Builder, and the Coding Player continues on the delivered environment with the same budget. The two settings thus start from the same environment and differ only in the reports, and a game without any report ends identical in both. In the generic-agent setting, a generic coding agent receives the original game, the frozen task requests and the basic interface to run the game, without project skills or engine-analysis tools. A second generic agent then writes a code policy for the delivered environment, which it may not edit, and both agents use the Builder’s model. Each generic agent works in a single session of up to 90 minutes and may test its own work. The final code policies of the full loop and the no-reports setting play every task from seeds 67 and 68 for up to 600 seconds, and the policy writer of the generic-agent setting records its own episodes. A task is solved when its environment is correct and two of these episodes complete it. We run the study with a GPT-6.1 Terra Builder and GPT-6.1 Sol players, as in Sec. 4.2 , and repeat it with GPT-6.1 Sol for every agent, all at medium reasoning effort.


##### Judging environments.

One auditor session per game, GPT-6.1 Sol at high reasoning effort, compares the environments of all settings with the original game files and the frozen task requests. It traces each metric to native game state, inspects the recorded episodes and runs its own checks, and for each task it decides whether the environment runs the original game and whether its start, progress and success match the task. Humans additionally check its verdicts.

##### Results.

Table 15 gives the outcome of every game in both experiments, and Fig. 21 their totals. With the Terra Builder, the reports make 11 more tasks correct and add 12 solved tasks, at 20% more model cost. Defect repairs make the environments of three games correct, and observation requests let the code policies solve tasks in three more games ( Tab. 16 ). The generic agent builds 25 correct environments and solves one task. It replaced the original game in three games and left it unplayable in one, and in most other tasks its progress or success checks do not follow the game.

With GPT-6.1 Sol as both the Builder and the players, the full loop and the no-reports setting each produce 136 correct task environments, while the number solved by the code policy rises from 62 to 82. The additional solutions come from six games where the reports lead to richer observations and clearer control guidance, allowing the code policies to solve more tasks without changing which task environments are correct.

##### Reports and repairs.

Table 16 lists every report of the Terra experiment and what the full loop changed. Eleven games received player reports, and two more had start failures reported by the harness. The full loop changed eleven environments, with three repairs of defects, four sets of new observations and four clarifications of the control guide, the start or the reported state. In There Is No Game , the Coding Player found that progress stopped at 0.9 after the letter O had fallen, because the Builder had hooked the game’s events before some of their variables existed; after the repair all nine tasks are correct and solved, while without reports all nine remain wrong. This is typical of the defects that reports catch: the tasks of a game share its instrumentation, so one defect breaks all of them and one repair fixes them all. Reports of the VLM Player led to two more repairs, of a crash in Dungeons & Degenerate Gamblers and of an upgrade menu that ran off the screen in Dome Romantik . In Where the Water Flows and Strike Force Heroes , observation requests made the Builder expose the terrain, the player and the visible enemies, and the code policies solved four tasks in each game, against none without reports. Of the 568 games in the collection, 298 have complete construction records. The Builder marked VLM Player reports as fixed in 23 games and revisited the environment after Coding Player feedback in 125 games, covering 134 games in total.

### 11 Human play


##### Protocol.

Participants play Player Bench tasks in their own desktop browser, on a web page that serves the same environments as the evaluation with the same seed. They read the task instruction and the control guide, as the VLM Players do, and play in real time with keyboard and mouse, for at most 10 minutes per attempt. An attempt ends when the task’s success check fires, when the participant ends it or at the time limit, and a game that allows another try after a loss restarts within the attempt. The page records the video, the inputs and the state trace in the same format as the other players, and the same evaluator scores every attempt.


##### Participants and coverage.

The study had 15 participants, recruited through our advertisements in various channels. Each participant played between 3 and 67 tasks, 19.8 on average, with 302 attempts over all 287 tasks. Alongside the performance evaluation, human review checked all 287 tasks for playability and scoring consistency. We checked whether progress reflected advancement toward the stated goal and whether the success conditions matched completion of that goal in the game. No environment or scoring errors were found. Figure 24 summarizes the participants’ age and gender and the attempts of each participant.


##### Consent and privacy.

Before playing, participants were told on the study page that their gameplay video and their keyboard and mouse input would be recorded for research, and they took part voluntarily and could stop at any time. The recording covers only the game area of the shared browser tab, without audio, microphone or webcam. Participants enter a name or nickname, which is stored separately from the recordings, and the age group and gender in Fig. 24 are self-reported and shown only in aggregate.

> Figure 24 : Participants of the human study. (a) Age group and gender. (b) Attempts of each participant, split into successful and unsuccessful ones.


##### Scoring.

We score the first attempt of each participant at a task, so that practice on the same task does not count. A task’s progress and success are the means over its scored attempts, and they are then averaged over tasks and games. Different tasks are played by different participants, so the human row of Tab. 3 describes a population of players who meet these games for the first time, not a single expert. Humans play in real time, while models pause the game at every step, so the human scores are a reference for first-time play rather than an upper bound. The human scores on GameWorld in Tab. 3 are those of the expert player reported by GameWorld [ 30 ] , who had studied the game rules and practiced the controls and played each task with at most 100 primitive actions.


##### Results.

Human players reach 69.4% progress and 51.8% success on Player Bench . Their play by difficulty tier is analyzed in Sec. 4.3 ( Fig. 9 ).

### 13 Broader impact

The games in our collection belong to their creators. Only a small share of them come with open-source licenses, and the others are freely playable on public web portals but are not licensed for redistribution. We use them only for research and do not distribute the games, their assets or the instrumented environments, and all third-party games and assets remain subject to their owners’ rights and licenses. We release the source code of Game 2 Player , the trained models and the task specifications. Other materials remain under review pending copyright and redistribution considerations. Agents that learn to play games from screenshots could also be used to automate play in online games whose terms of service forbid bots, and our collection contains only single-player games that run offline.
