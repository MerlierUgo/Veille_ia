## VibePrevisBench: Evaluating Agentic Coding for 4D Scene Generation in Blender

Controllable real-world video generation models can condition on a rendered graybox proxy video, which fixes layout, motion and camera. Coding agents can build such videos by reconstructing a 4D scene in Blender, yet no benchmark checks both these videos and the long, multi-shot scenes behind them. We introduce VibePrevisBench , a benchmark for 4D scene reconstruction: an agent turns a brief into an executable 4D scene and renders its proxy video. Its 242 tasks span four input kinds, including scripts, photos, continuations, and reference videos. Each delivery is scored along two evaluation dimensions. Rule-based Scene Check re-runs the code and compares the exported 4D scene with hidden ground truth facts in nine dimensions. VLM-based Screen Check asks a VLM judge whether the facts that pass can be recognized in the proxy video. We analyze the evaluation results and trajectories of 11 frontier coding agents and identify three key observations. First, multimodal understanding separates the agents, especially in understanding space and camera motion. Second, all agents seem to already master basic camera work and staging, and fail at challenging requests. Third, agents inspect their own renders but fail to correct their errors. Agents thus need better visual understanding of space and camera, explicit checks for challenging requests, and item-by-item verification of renders against the brief.

### 1 Introduction

Controllable real-world video generation models now take previsualization (previs), a rough 3D mock-up of a shot, as input and paint the real-world video on top [ 60 , 42 , 31 , 3 ] . This input is a graybox proxy video, the untextured render of a 4D scene whose layout, people, actions, cameras and cuts unfold over time. Conditioning on proxy video makes all of these more controllable [ 30 , 20 , 44 , 4 , 36 ] , but building it by hand takes skilled artists and time [ 23 , 67 ] . Coding agents can build it instead: from a script, photos, a continuation or real footage, they write Blender code, render previews, inspect their frames and revise [ 34 , 7 , 8 , 36 , 32 , 10 ] . We call this task 4D scene generation: the agent writes the executable 4D scene that a brief describes or shows, and renders its proxy video. Figure 1 (a) shows this pipeline. The agent becomes the director; the real-world video generation model, the renderer.

The proxy video shapes the final real-world video: the real-world video generation model keeps the camera and layout of proxy video, so an agent’s errors carry into the resulted real-world video, as Fig. 2 shows. Therefore, it is important to generate the high-quality proxy video. To evaluate the quality of proxy video, we need to check two things. First, is the 4D scene right? In Fig. 2 (a), the task asks for riders turning at eye level, but the weak agent films them from high above a flat lot. The proxy video looks fine, and the VLM judge even gives it 95.5. Thus, it is necessary to find this error by rule-based scene check in 4D scene. Second, can a viewer see it is right? In Fig. 2 (b), the car is in frame and turning, so the rules pass it. However, the car is a tiny dot on screen, which can be found by the VLM-based screen check. This happens often: 31% of annotated actions pass the rules but are hard or impossible for a viewer to see in the proxy video, as Sec. C.3 reports.

Existing benchmarks support such an evaluation only in part. Table 1 compares them along four dimensions and shows three gaps. (i) Narrow inputs. Benchmarks of dynamic scenes mostly start from text prompts [ 70 , 56 ] or videos to reconstruct [ 47 , 52 , 54 ] . A director, in contrast, may start from a script, photos of a real location or footage to restage, where each source tests a different skill. (ii) Static content. Scene-building benchmarks mostly target single objects, static scenes or short clips without people [ 19 , 18 , 13 , 65 , 47 ] . None tests a long, multi-shot scene in which several people’s routes, event timing, camera moves and cuts must hold together. (iii) Screen-only evaluation. Dynamic-scene benchmarks score only proxy or real-world video frames [ 56 , 54 ] , while checks of scene state against ground truth cover only static scenes or short clips [ 18 , 65 , 47 ] .

To address these gaps, we introduce VibePrevisBench , a benchmark for 4D scene reconstruction: an agent turns a brief into an executable 4D scene and renders its proxy video. Figure 1 b shows what it scores: the 4D scene and its proxy video, both checked against hidden GT facts. VibePrevisBench has three key features that match the three gaps mentioned above. (i) Diverse inputs. Briefs come in four kinds, scripts, photos, continuations and reference videos, across seven scene domains, as Fig. 3 shows. (ii) Staged and filmed scenes. Most tasks stage several people in multi-shot scenes of about 40 s, so routes, event timing, camera moves and cuts are tested together. (iii) Two evaluation dimensions. Rule-based Scene Check re-runs the delivered code, exports the 4D scene frame by frame and checks it against hidden GT facts in nine dimensions. VLM-based Screen Check asks a VLM judge whether a viewer can recognize each entity, read each action and follow the story in the proxy video.

We evaluate 11 frontier coding agents, and we make three observations. First, multimodal understanding separates the agents, especially in understanding space and camera motion. The gap between the best and the worst agent grows from 12.0 Rule-based Scene Check points on scripts to 19.9 on photos, while weak agents require more rounds facing failed API calls. Second, all agents master only basic camera work and staging, and fail at challenging requests. When the reference films from a low angle, 66% of shots come out at eye level, whereas eye-level shots go wrong only 9% of the time. Third, agents inspect their own renders but fail to correct their errors. Even when agents say their renders match the reference, camera-angle errors are just as frequent as in runs without such claims. These observations call for better visual understanding of space and camera, explicit checks for challenging requests, and item-by-item verification of renders against the brief.

In summary, our contributions are:

- • A benchmark for 4D scene reconstruction. VibePrevisBench has 242 tasks over four input kinds: scripts, photos, continuations and reference videos, in seven scene domains. Most tasks stage several people in long, multi-shot scenes, where we can test the moving trajectory of people, the event happening time, the camera moves and cuts together.

- • Two evaluation dimensions. Rule-based Scene Check tests whether the 4D scene is right by checking it against hidden GT facts, and VLM-based Screen Check tests whether what is right can be seen in the proxy video.

- • A diagnostic study of 11 coding agents. From their scores, trajectories and ablations, three observations show what separates the agents, where they all fail and why they do not correct themselves.


### 2 Related Work

Proxy-conditioned real-world video generation. Real-world video generation models follow ever richer conditions, from camera paths [ 21 , 58 , 1 ] and object trajectories [ 17 , 55 , 72 ] to a proxy video [ 20 , 46 , 63 , 71 , 30 , 44 , 4 ] . A proxy video is the render of a coarse 4D scene, which previs tools and agents build [ 23 , 11 , 67 , 32 , 43 ] . It fixes layout, motion and camera at once, so the real-world video inherits the scene’s mistakes. Real-world video generation benchmarks evaluate the real-world video: most score it with learned or VLM judges, including benchmarks that test real-world video generation models as world models [ 27 , 70 , 50 , 12 , 40 , 2 , 33 , 59 ] , and some check it against ground truth for short, single-shot physics or motion [ 29 , 6 , 14 ] . Multi-shot benchmarks rely on judges or detected cuts [ 56 , 48 , 57 ] , and others check whether a real-world video generation model renders what a program specifies [ 28 , 45 ] . All of them evaluate the real-world video, not the 4D scene behind it or the coding agent that builds this scene.

Understanding a video by rebuilding it. Video understanding benchmarks test perception through answers, groundings or captions [ 16 , 62 , 41 , 39 , 5 , 53 , 64 ] , and camera benchmarks show that VLMs still misread camera motion [ 37 , 9 ] . An answer can be right even when the model’s picture of the scene is not, and writing the scene out explicitly exposes such gaps [ 38 , 25 ] . Rebuilding a video in code takes this furthest, since every object, motion and camera must be written out; existing benchmarks do so for physics clips, indoor scans, embodied demonstrations and short cinematic clips [ 47 , 52 , 51 , 54 ] . None of them both rebuilds multi-shot videos of several people and checks the rebuilt scene against ground truth, as the last columns of Tab. 1 show.

Building scenes with code. LLM agents write Blender code for objects, scenes, animation and films [ 49 , 24 , 26 , 66 , 22 , 61 ] , and recent code world models already write the 4D scene behind a proxy video as code and leave rendering to a real-world video generation model [ 34 , 7 , 8 , 36 , 15 ] . Yet benchmarks for such agents mostly target edits, objects or static scenes [ 19 , 18 , 13 , 65 , 69 ] , and camera benchmarks only add cameras to a finished scene [ 68 , 35 ] . None tests multi-shot scenes in which several people act and the camera moves and cuts.

As Tab. 1 shows, VibePrevisBench fills these gaps: an agent reads a script, photos, a continuation or footage and writes the 4D scene behind a proxy video, which Rule-based Scene Check and VLM-based Screen Check verify against hidden GT facts and on screen. VibePrevisBench thus evaluates the agent that builds the scene, not only its rendered video.

#### 3.1 Task formulation

Briefs and deliveries. A task $\tau=(b,m,E,o)$ gives an English brief $b$ , optional media $m$ , a list $E$ of places and subjects with ids, and output requirements $o$ : the frame count $T$ , frame rate and resolution of the proxy video. The input kinds differ only in what specifies the scene. A script describes it in prose alone, without distances or angles. Photo and reference video tasks show it in $m$ , as one to five photos or a clip, while $b$ only names the entries of $E$ . A continuation shows the opening frame of a clip and describes the next seconds. The agent answers with a Blender program $p$ . To score it, we run $p$ and read out the 4D scene $S=(\{x_{e}(t)\}_{e\in E},\,c(t),\,\mathcal{K})$ : for every place and subject listed in $E$ , its oriented box $x_{e}(t)$ at each frame $t=1,\dots,T$ , together with the camera $c(t)$ and the cut times $\mathcal{K}$ . Rendering $S$ gives the proxy video $V$ , with the same $T$ frames.

Rubric. The hidden GT facts form a rubric $F=\{f_{j}\}$ , where each $f_{j}$ is one checkable statement about the scene. The facts state which subjects exist, where they stand and face, how the places connect, who moves where and when, and how each camera frames, moves and cuts. For example, a fact may require that P01 stops between 3.9 and 4.9 s, within a 1.5 s tolerance. Its score $s_{j}(S)\in[0,1]$ measures how well the agent’s scene $S$ meets $f_{j}$ . A fact met within its tolerance scores 1, and a metric fact loses credit linearly once its error exceeds the tolerance. All tolerances are fixed before any agent is run. Each fact belongs to one of nine dimensions: entities, layout, spaces, picture structure, routes, events, camera, cuts and physical validity. Picture structure compares the depth, normals and segmentation of each shot with the reference frames, so only photo, continuation and video tasks have it; Appendix Tab. 3 defines all nine. Appearance is never checked, since the real-world video generation model supplies it. Whatever the input kind, the agent delivers the same kind of program $p$ , which is scored through the same $S$ and $V$ against facts of the same form. Scores thus compare directly across input kinds.


#### 3.2 Rule-based Scene Check and VLM-based Screen Check

> Figure 4 : One task through the evaluation pipeline (video task M50_049, solved by GPT-6 Astra). The agent writes Blender Python in a sandbox and inspects its own renders. We re-run the delivered program from scratch. The replay exports the 4D scene $S$ , the tagged boxes, cameras and cuts of every frame, and renders the proxy video $V$ from it. Rule-based Scene Check compares $S$ with the hidden GT facts, two of 805 checks shown; VLM-based Screen Check asks the VLM judge about $V$ , only for items the rules passed, two verdicts shown.

Rule-based Scene Check asks whether the scene is right, and VLM-based Screen Check whether what is right can be seen. Figure 4 follows one task through both, and Sec. B.4 validates them with injected defects, re-scoring, three VLM judges and a human study.

Delivery and replay. Each delivery must pass three gates: its program runs on its own, renders the full video and reopens in Blender. A delivery that fails a gate scores 0 on both checks. Such failures stay in the average, so an agent gains nothing by failing hard tasks. Every other program is re-run from scratch in a fresh sandbox, which exports $S$ frame by frame and renders $V$ . Scores come only from this replay, never from videos the agent rendered in its own sandbox, so a video cannot show a scene the program does not build.

Rule-based Scene Check. It binds subjects and places by their tagged ids and matches untagged objects by class. Script facts are relations, since scripts give no coordinates, whereas media facts are metric and are compared after each place is aligned to the ground truth by a similarity transform. The score is then built in three steps. First, the facts of one kind form a block $b$ , whose raw score $P_{b}$ is the mean of $s_{j}$ over its facts. Second, each block is corrected for chance and for missing entities: $Q_{b}=\mathrm{clip}\big((P_{b}-R_{b})/(1-R_{b})\big)\,\kappa_{b}$ , where clip bounds the value to $[0,1]$ . The chance rate $R_{b}$ is the $P_{b}$ that the agent’s own objects earn when shuffled at random within each place, and $\kappa_{b}$ is the share of the block’s facts whose entities exist. A random layout thus scores 0, extra clutter raises $R_{b}$ along with $P_{b}$ , and every missing entity lowers $\kappa_{b}$ . Third, blocks combine into dimension scores $Q_{d}$ with weights fixed in advance, and $\mathrm{Scene}=100\cdot\frac{1}{|D_{\tau}|}\sum_{d\in D_{\tau}}Q_{d}$ averages them over the dimensions $D_{\tau}$ that the task exercises. The scorer is deterministic, and Sec. B.2 details the scoring.

VLM-based Screen Check. A VLM judge, Gemini 4, watches the proxy video at 2 fps, plus an orbit render of the delivered scene on photo tasks, blind to the agent’s name and the object names. Without the brief, it first retells the clip. It then receives the brief, the reference video on video tasks, and checklists from the hidden GT facts, limited to the items the rules passed, and rates each item as no, partial or yes, $v_{k,i}\in\{0,\frac{1}{2},1\}$ . The items fall into four dimensions: recognition, action, visibility and story. Recognition counts as yes only if the blind retelling already named the subject or place, and story asks whether the retelling recounts the story beats. With $\bar{v}_{k}$ the mean verdict of dimension $k$ , $\mathrm{Screen}=100\cdot\frac{1}{|K_{\tau}|}\sum_{k\in K_{\tau}}\bar{v}_{k}$ , where photo tasks use recognition only, and the overall score is $(\mathrm{Scene}+\mathrm{Screen})/2$ . Section B.3 gives the judge’s rubric.

#### 3.3 Construction

VibePrevisBench is built in the three stages of Fig. 5 . Section A.1 details the taxonomies, the screening rules and the pruning.

Stage 1: Collection and curation. Two frozen taxonomies, of what a scene holds and of how a task is asked, set quotas that new tasks fill. Clips for video and continuation tasks come from over 27k source videos. Keeping a few clips per source with several moving people, camera motion or cuts leaves 1.8k clips, of which 388 pass VLM checks for live action, visible events and distinct subjects. Human reviewers choose among these, and Sec. A.1 describes the steps from these clips to the final video and continuation tasks. Of the 39 photo tasks, 38 draw on scan datasets with measured 3D boxes and camera poses.

Stage 2: Task construction. Every brief is derived from a fixed scene. For a script, a model builds the scene from a card and then writes the script, each scored claim citing its sentence. Two blind readers from the Claude and DeepSeek families see only the script and write down where everything is and when it happens; a claim that neither recovers is reworded or dropped. A media brief gives each place and subject an id and a description. Human reviewers check and correct every brief and its hidden GT facts.

Stage 3: Ground truth and checks. Scripts take their hidden GT facts from the built scene, in which every claim is checked to hold, and photo tasks from their scans. For videos, most continuations and one photo task, a pipeline estimates it: it recovers shots, subjects, cameras, depth and bodies and lifts them to 3D, as Appendix Fig. 10 shows. Facts the pipeline cannot verify are marked unscorable and never count. All 11 agents then ran the candidates, and tasks that did not separate them were dropped, judged by item-rest correlation and spread rather than by any single agent’s score. Tasks, media, hidden GT facts and scorer are frozen with checksums, so every score can be reproduced from the release.

#### 3.4 Statistics

Coverage and scale. VibePrevisBench contains 242 tasks: 94 scripts, 86 reference videos, 39 photo tasks and 23 continuations. As Fig. 3 shows, each of the seven domains holds 19 to 46 tasks and each of the 33 places 3 to 13. In Fig. 6 a, briefs run 153 to 1,200 words with a median of 368: scripts spell out the scene in about 800 words, while media tasks mainly list ids. Outputs run 6 to 60 s with a median of 40 s, as Fig. 6 b shows.

Staging demands and checks. Figure 6 c shows that 88% of tasks have two or more shots, 80% a moving camera, 79% two or more people, 69% hand–object interaction and 61% two or more places, and 71% combine several shots, a moving camera and two or more people. A task carries 20 to 2,175 checks, 408 at the median and 99.0k in all, as Fig. 6 d shows, and exercises a median of eight of the nine dimensions. Events hold 57% of the checks, since event order is checked pair by pair, but Rule-based Scene Check averages dimensions rather than checks, so they do not dominate the score. Section A.3 splits them by input kind.

#### 4.3 Observations and implications

Three observations show what separates the agents, where they all fail and why they do not correct themselves, and we analyze each and give its implications.

Observation 1: Multimodal understanding separates the agents, especially in understanding space and camera motion. The more an input relies on vision, the wider the gap between agents. In Tab. 2 , the gap between the best and worst agent grows from 12.0 Rule-based Scene Check points on text-only scripts to 19.9 on photos. This gap comes mainly from errors in space and camera. On photos, picture structure and camera account for 63% of the gap between the top four and the other seven, entities and layout for only 9%. The top four score 66–71 on picture structure, the other seven 24–38, as Fig. 8 b shows. The bottleneck is reading images and videos, not building the scene. Given a text description of the hidden GT facts on five video tasks, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash gains 14.0 points, as Appendix Tab. 5 reports, nearly the full 16.7-point gap between the best and the worst agent on videos. Model updates gain here too: in Fig. 8 a, a newer model of the same family gains 11–14 points on photos but only 3–4 on scripts. A larger tier of the same generation, GPT-6 Astra over GPT-6.1 Sol, gains at most 1.7 points on any input kind. Writing code is not the bottleneck. The three weakest agents often call an API removed in Blender 5, as Fig. 9 c shows, yet later rounds repair these calls. Within each agent, runs with a failed API call score within 0.1 points of runs without one. Progress on this step thus depends on visual understanding of space and camera, whereas more knowledge of the Blender API saves rounds, not points.

Observation 2: All agents master only basic camera work and staging, and fail at challenging requests. Appendix Fig. 14 a shows that the errors of all 11 agents concentrate on challenging requests, which they turn into the common choice. When the reference films from a low angle, 66% of shots come out at eye level rather than high. Only 4% of orbits around a subject stay orbits, and 47% become sideways moves. Half of the video and continuation runs have more shots than the reference, while only 4% have fewer. Objects mentioned only in prose go missing 35% of the time. The common version of each request, by contrast, mostly succeeds: eye-level shots go wrong 9% of the time, follow and push-in moves 5–8%, and listed objects go missing 11% of the time. Challenging requests are likely scarce in the data agents learn from, so agents fall back on what they know. Two observations fit this reading. The errors lean one way, toward the common choice, whereas a misreading would err both ways. They also barely vary across agents: in Fig. 8 c, errors in reading the picture fall with the overall score, whereas every agent films 32–41% of shots at the wrong camera height. Agents therefore need explicit checks for challenging requests, such as camera height and move, shot count and objects named only in prose.

Observation 3: Agents inspect their own renders but fail to correct their errors. Every agent inspects its renders, viewing 13–40 images per run after its first Blender call, yet more looking does not help. In Fig. 9 a, the third of runs that looks most views 3.3 times as many images as the third that looks least, yet scores only 0.8 points higher at the median once task difficulty is removed. Removing every image from DeepSeek V4.1 Flash’s context on eight script tasks changes its score by only $-$ 0.8 points, as Appendix Tab. 5 reports. The reason is that agents cannot tell a wrong render from a right one. In 1,377 of 2,582 runs with comments after a render, the agent states that its render matches the reference or looks right. With agent and task held fixed, these runs get camera angles, camera moves and events wrong as often as the other runs, as Fig. 9 b and Sec. C.2 show. Only framing and shot count, which show at a glance, are 2–3 points lower. More renders and looking thus do not correct errors; agents need item-by-item verification of renders against the brief.


### 5 Conclusion

We introduced VibePrevisBench to evaluate coding agents that build executable 4D scenes and render proxy videos from four kinds of input. Rule-based Scene Check evaluates the scene against hidden GT facts, while VLM-based Screen Check evaluates how clearly those facts read on screen. Analysis of the evaluation results and trajectories of 11 agents yields three observations. Visual understanding of space and camera separates agents. Challenging staging and filming requests remain difficult across capability levels. Agents inspect their renders, but their judgments do not reliably correspond to fewer errors. These observations point to better visual understanding, explicit checks for challenging requests, and verification of renders against the brief. VibePrevisBench makes progress on these capabilities measurable.

### Appendix A Benchmark details


#### A.1 Task construction


##### Coverage first.

Two frozen taxonomies tag every task. One covers what a scene holds: places, spaces, layout relations, subjects, actions, camera and editing, and genre, in 67 categories. The other covers how a task is asked: input kind, writing style, information level, organization and length. Each tag and each kind of place has a minimum number of tasks, and the gaps became slots for new tasks, routed to three lanes.


##### Scripts.

Each slot becomes a card fixing genre, writing style, length, places, people, actions and a story seed. Scripts come in seven writing styles, from screenplays and shot lists to ad briefs and novel-like prose, at about 600 to 1,200 words. The hidden GT facts are written before the script: a model (GPT-6.1 Sol) first codes the scene, then writes the script, then the claims, each citing the sentence it comes from and checked to hold in the coded scene. Automatic gates check every task’s scoring and output specification. Two blind readers from the Claude and DeepSeek families, who see only the script, try to recover every claim; a claim both misread is reworded and re-read up to twice, then dropped if still misread.


##### Videos and continuations.

We collected candidate clips ourselves. The screening keeps clips of at least 5 s and 2 fps, with a short edge of at least 480 pixels for local clips and 720 for web videos, and drops clips whose adjacent frames are more than 85% identical. Local clips are cut into windows of 25 to 50 s, a seeded eighth also into 15 s windows, and web videos into 25–45 s video windows and 12 s continuation windows; each source keeps at most three or four windows that overlap by at most 60%. Windows are ranked by subject presence, the number of moving people or vehicles and its changes, motion beyond the camera’s own, camera motion, cuts, person size and duration, and flagged for letterboxing, burned-in text, repeated frames and darkness. We then pre-screened the windows by model ratings of contact sheets (Gemini 4, Claude Sonnet 5.5), and tagged the survivors by place, genre, people, shots and moving vehicles. Human reviewers decided whether a clip becomes a video task, a continuation or neither. Quotas then capped over-represented content such as vehicles, and borders and logos were cropped. A video brief gives an id and a description for each place and subject, and the output must start where the clip starts and cut where it cuts. A continuation brief gives the clip’s opening frame and an account of the next 6 to 20 s, mostly following the clip. Figure 10 shows how one clip’s hidden GT facts are built.


##### Photos.

We drew on scan datasets with measured 3D boxes and camera poses, indoors and in street scenes, and used each scene once. Boxes from the hidden GT facts were drawn back onto every photo and checked first by a model and then by a human; candidates whose boxes did not match the photo were rejected. The output must show each photo’s view for 12 frames and then sweep the place for 8 s. One photo task instead uses five keyframes of a real clip, and its hidden GT facts are estimated as for a video.


##### Pruning.

All 11 agents ran the pool, and we dropped tasks that did not discriminate between agents: low item-rest correlation or little spread across agents, never a single agent’s score. Finally we removed tasks with source or licensing problems or an unscorable run.


##### Model assistance and fairness.

Several of the evaluated models help build tasks. GPT-6.1 Sol writes the script specs and scripts; the blind readers come from other families, Claude and DeepSeek; Claude Sonnet 5.5, of the same family as Opus 5.5, pre-screens clips; and Gemini 3.8 and Gemini 4 register and observe the subjects behind the hidden GT facts of videos and continuations, while Gemini 4 also rates web clips. Three safeguards limit their influence. Agents are scored by rule checks on facts, never by similarity to a model’s own output; the hidden GT facts are never edited to make a reader pass; and every brief and its facts are reviewed and corrected by hand. Ranked by Rule-based Scene Check, the agents show no strong self-preference. GPT-6.1 Sol is second on scripts and over all tasks; DeepSeek V4.1 Flash is seventh on scripts and over all tasks; and Opus 5.5 is third over all tasks and fourth on videos. The Gemini models rank somewhat higher on the media tasks whose hidden GT facts they helped estimate: Gemini 4 Argon is fourth over all tasks but third on videos and second on continuations, and Gemini 3.8 Flash is eighth over all tasks but sixth on videos. A small preference cannot be ruled out; the judge’s own-family bias is measured separately in Sec. B.4 .

#### A.3 Task statistics

Scripts come in seven writing styles with 9 to 24 scripts each, and photo tasks give a single photo in 14 cases and three to five photos in the other 25. Outputs run about 40 s for scripts and videos, 12 s for continuations and 10 s for photo tasks. A script stages a median of four people in three places over seven shots, and a video four people over 12 shots, with up to 15 people and 38 shots. Scripts and videos carry about 500 and 420 checks at the median, while photo tasks and continuations carry fewer than 100. Beyond events, camera holds 11% of the checks, routes 8% and cuts 7%, layout, picture structure and entities 4 to 5% each, and spaces and physical validity 2% each.

#### B.2 Rule-based Scene Check scoring


##### Gates and replay.

The three gates run 21 automatic checks: the code runs standalone; the video is rendered in full at the specified resolution, frame rate, duration and codec; and the scene reopens in Blender with the task’s settings. Fourteen of the 2,662 runs fail a gate and score 0, two of them by Gemini 4 Argon. The replay dumps the tagged entities and places, their oriented boxes and parts, the cameras and the cuts.


##### Binding and alignment.

Script facts are read in the agent’s own frame, whose compass the brief fixes. For media tasks, each place is aligned by a similarity $T(x)=s\,R_{\theta}x+t$ fitted by weighted least squares to the anchored bodies, with $s\in[0.67,1.5]$ . A place with fewer than three anchors is scored by pairwise relations only.


##### Fact scores.

The 84.4k scorable facts expand into 99.0k checks. A yes-or-no check scores 0 or 1, and most metric checks with error $e$ score $\mathrm{clip}\big((z-e)/(z-f)\big)$ , which is full within the registered tolerance $f$ and zero beyond $z$ .


##### Chance rates and physical validity.

The chance rate of a block is $R_{b}=\frac{1}{200}\sum_{k=1}^{200}P_{b}(\pi_{k}S)$ , where $\pi_{k}$ randomly permutes the agent’s static objects within each place. Camera checks, which no permutation moves, take $R_{b}=0$ . Physical validity is scaled by $\min(1,E/0.25)$ , where $E$ is the mean of layout, routes and events, so that a near-empty scene earns no physical-validity points.


#### B.3 VLM judge rubric

The judge (Gemini 4) watches the proxy video at 2 fps. Its prompt says that the video comes from a simplified previs, with simple shapes and mannequin-like figures in at most flat colors, so this look is not penalized. It never sees the agent’s name or the object names.


##### Turn 1: blind recap.

Without the brief, the judge describes only what it sees: in time order, who is where and what each person does, with people referred to by their look and objects by their shape (“unidentified shape” or “unclear movement” otherwise); what kind of place it is; and every object it can identify from its shape. For photo tasks it also watches an evaluation-side orbit of the delivered scene (a 360 ∘ look from the room center, then a top-down orbit with the ceiling hidden) and lists places and objects only.


##### Turn 2: verdicts.

The judge then receives the brief (and, for video tasks, the reference video) and checklists generated from the hidden GT facts; items the rules already failed are not asked. It is told that the automatic checks verify presence, positions, layout, camera placement, timing, shot count and physical validity, and that materials, lighting, realism, aesthetics and composition are not judged. Every entry gets yes , partial , no or n/a (entity or shot absent, already penalized) with timestamps, and the lower verdict when in doubt.

yes only if the blind recap already named the category or kind of place; partial if it was not named blind but a shape at a plausible spot fits once the brief is known; no otherwise.

Would a viewer who has not read the brief describe the footage as that action? yes : action and contact are clear; partial : the pose changes but the action or contact is unclear; no : miming, standing still or another action. Events whose moment is not visible in V3 are left out of V2.

At each judged event, and in each shot without events, are the people and objects involved clearly visible? partial : small, partly blocked or partly out of frame; no : blocked, out of frame, too small, a blank or wall-only frame, the camera inside geometry, flicker or leftover helper objects.

Does the judge’s own blind recap contain each story beat ( yes : its gist; partial : part of it)?

With yes $=1$ , partial $=\frac{1}{2}$ and no $=0$ , each dimension averages its entries ( n/a left out), and VLM-based Screen Check is 100 times the mean over the applicable dimensions, which on photo tasks are V1 alone.


#### B.4 Is the evaluation trustworthy?


##### Determinism and direction.

Rule-based Scene Check is deterministic: each of the 2,786 deliveries that were scored more than once during development received an identical score every time. On 12 high-scoring video deliveries, injected defects lower the Rule-based Scene Check score monotonically and in the intended dimensions: delaying all action by 4 s costs 9.8 points and keeping one person costs 12.1. A 90 ∘ camera yaw costs 3.6 points when only the exported cameras are turned, and 21.0 when the ray-cast picture buffers are also re-projected to the turned camera. The physical-validity check misses floating people: lifting them by 1 m costs only 0.8.


##### Judges.

Re-judged with GPT-6.1 Sol and Qwen3.8 Omni Flash, the agents’ VLM-based Screen Check scores correlate with Gemini 4’s at Spearman 0.94–0.96, though only at 0.73–0.76 per run; Gemini 4 favors its own family by 1.3 points without changing any rank, and the overall ranking is identical whichever judge is used (Kendall $\tau$ = 1.00). ##### Error rates.

Figure 8 c reads errors from the per-check results of Rule-based Scene Check, over delivered runs. A check counts as an error when it scores below 0.5. ##### Shared errors barely change with capability.

In Fig. 8 c, errors in reading the picture fall steadily from the weakest to the best agent, while the shared errors stay within a few points. With 11 agents a weak $\rho$ does not prove that no relation exists, so the claim rests on the narrow ranges and on the best and weakest agents being close. Over-cutting depends on the measure. The share of runs with more shots than the reference gives $\rho$ = $-$ 0.18. The share of video runs with at least one extra cut gives $-$ 0.48 (52–86%, highest for GLM 5.3 Flash). Figure 14 a pairs each challenging request with a common counterpart scored the same way. ##### Looking does not fix.

Each agent’s runs are split into thirds by the images it views, ties broken by task id, and a run’s score is taken relative to the other agents’ mean on the same task, which removes task difficulty. Picture structure is treated the same way on the media tasks. Without this control the third that looks most scores 1.5 points lower at the median, since hard tasks draw more looking and score lower. Image counts include every image returned to the agent after its first Blender call, so on photo, continuation and video tasks they include reference frames viewed again. In the no-image ablation of Tab. 5 , all eight runs received zero images; it covers one mid-ranked agent and eight script tasks. These comparisons describe associations rather than the effect of looking, since agents may well look more when a task goes wrong.


##### Verdicts against errors.

We take the text an agent writes after each batch of images it views once it has made its first Blender call. A run counts as claiming a match when this text states that the render matches the reference, brief or photo, or that it looks right or correct, by a fixed pattern list. Per-shot error rates come from the Rule-based Scene Check items behind Fig. 8 c. A regression with agent and task fixed effects and a bootstrap over tasks compares runs with and without a claim. Camera-angle errors differ by $-$ 0.8 points (95% CI $-$ 4.0 to $+$ 2.1), camera moves by $+$ 0.1 and missing events by $-$ 0.6. A stricter pattern list and Claude Code runs alone leave camera angle, camera move and events within 1.5 points, while Claude Code runs alone show 4.7 fewer shot-count errors. The Codex CLI keeps only reasoning summaries, so GPT agents claim a match in 123 of 717 runs, and most claims come from the other eight agents. The analysis is correlational, and the pattern list is not hand-checked.


##### Failed Blender calls.

For each Blender call that ends in an error, we take the last line of its traceback. Of 3,796 failed calls, 36% use an attribute, keyword or enum value that the Blender 5.2 API does not have; errors from the GPU-less sandbox’s renderer are not counted as such. Action.fcurves , which Blender 5 replaced with layered actions, accounts for 23%. A run counts as calling it if any of its calls raises it. With agent and task fixed effects, runs with an API failure score $+$ 0.1 points (95% CI $-$ 0.8 to $+$ 1.0) against runs without, and runs calling Action.fcurves $-$ 0.3 ( $-$ 1.1 to $+$ 0.9), so later rounds repair these calls.


##### Build rhythm.

Agents spread their Blender calls differently over a run, measured in tool calls and pooled over each agent’s runs. Gemini 4 Argon and Opus 5.5 leave 61% and 54% of their Blender calls to the last third, whereas GPT-6 Astra and GLM 5.3 Flash leave only 35% and 37%, and both rhythms reach the top. In Claude Code, Opus 5.5 runs Blender 6 times per task and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 24, yet Opus 5.5 scores 4.5 points higher.


#### C.3 Event completion and legibility


##### Correct is not always legible, and events separate agents most.

The VLM judge is asked only about facts the rules accept. Of those, about a third of the actions cannot be read from the render (68% legible on average, from 86% for GPT-6 Astra to 50% for Muse Spark 1.3), and a blind recap retells only 55% of the story. Weaker agents thus err more and also make their correct parts harder to read. Chaining the two scores per event ( Fig. 16 ), only 36% of annotated events are both accepted by the rules and confirmed on screen: about half for the four strongest agents (44–54%) and a fifth for the four weakest, the widest gap of any error type. A further 31% pass the rules but are confirmed only partly (17%) or rejected (14%) by the judge, so the rules alone would overstate event completion. Conversely, the rules miss some posture events such as falls, which lowers the confirmed share.

#### C.5 Do proxy errors reach the real-world video?

Figure 2 gives the real-world video generation model Seedance 2.5 the proxy videos of a strong and a weak agent on two video tasks, with one prompt shared by both clips of a panel.
