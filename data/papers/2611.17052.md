## Auditing AI peer reviewers: a benchmark on real scientific papers

LLM-based peer review is now used at production scale, but its evaluation remains limited. We introduce a benchmark that injects objective, localisable errors of four types (general issues, mathematics, numerics, citations) at three severity levels into real papers and measures each reviewer’s detection rate by error type and severity, together with its false-positive rate on clean copies. We also present Skepthical , a multi-agent reviewer that verifies citation-backed claims by retrieving the cited papers, mathematics with computer algebra, and numerical claims with executed code. We apply the benchmark to two corpora of 15 recent arXiv preprints each, one in astrophysics and one in machine learning (ML), injecting a total of 357 errors, and evaluate twelve reviewer systems: six single-LLM reviewers, two Claude Code configurations, three publicly available reviewers, and Skepthical . The choice of model has the largest impact on detection rate: given the same prompt, three frontier LLMs differ by about $50$ percentage points in total detection on both corpora. Tools and scaffolding help when they contribute what the model cannot supply on its own. In particular, on astrophysics, Claude Code improves on a single Claude call in mathematics and numerics detection, and enforced citation retrieval gives Skepthical the highest citation error detection rate of all systems by a large margin. This citation lead, however, is not observed on ML, where the models appear to know the literature better and Skepthical ’s citation detection rate is statistically indistinguishable from that of the best single LLM. False-positive rates are below $10\,\%$ for most systems, but vary with prompt, model, and scaffolding, with mathematics flags the main source of false positives. The evaluation pipeline, the injected errors, and the adjudication logs are released as a benchmark.


### 1 Introduction

Peer review is a cornerstone of the scientific process. With AI tools becoming increasingly used in research and AI scientists starting to conduct research at scale [ 3 , 13 , 26 , 18 , 19 , 25 , 22 , 7 ] , the need for reliable and scalable peer review is greater than ever. In parallel, LLM-based peer review has reached production scale in 2026, with a growing ecosystem of AI reviewers and review venues [ 16 , 1 , 8 , 20 , 2 , 5 , 4 , 28 ] . Despite the publication of the first AI-reviewer benchmarks [ 10 ] , methodology for evaluating these AI reviewers has generally lagged behind. Evaluation is most often framed either as alignment with human-written reviews [ 11 , 5 , 16 ] or as synthetic error injection on Machine Learning (ML) papers only [ 20 , 2 , 24 , 6 ] , with important issues such as false-positive rates or error detection as a function of the error severity not being typically investigated.

This paper addresses these concerns with two primary contributions. First, we introduce an evaluation pipeline for reviewer systems that injects errors into a paper’s L a T e X source at three severity levels (subtle, clear, blatant) across four error types (general issues, mathematics, numerics, citations), and then measures i) the error detection rate for each severity level and error type and ii) the false-positive rates (on a copy with no injected errors). The verdicts are adjudicated by a cross-family LLM panel (Anthropic + OpenAI + Google) following recent LLM-as-judge prescriptions [ 15 , 21 , 27 ] , with additional mechanical verifiers for mathematics and numerics. Our ‘dose-response’ design extends perturbation-based behavioural evaluation [ 17 , 29 ] with graded severity levels inspired by [ 14 ] . We note that our benchmark focuses on whether reviewers can detect objective, localisable issues (e.g., numerical and mathematical errors, misrepresented citations, internal inconsistencies), leaving aside questions such as significance and novelty assessment or overall review quality, which are harder to quantify.

Second, we introduce Skepthical , an LLM-powered paper reviewer comprising four specialist modules: a citations module that retrieves and reads the cited papers, a mathematics module that verifies mathematical claims using computer algebra, a numerics module that verifies numerical results through executed code, and a general-issues module consisting of an ensemble of cross-family LLMs. Unlike prior reviewers that rely entirely on LLMs, Skepthical verifies LLM-flagged issues where possible: the mathematics and numerics modules use computer algebra and executed code, respectively, while the citations module checks the claims directly against the retrieved PDFs of cited papers.

We apply our evaluation pipeline to two corpora of 15 recent astrophysics arXiv preprints and 15 ML preprints, benchmarking Skepthical alongside eleven additional reviewer systems: six single-LLM reviewers, two Claude Code configurations, and three publicly available external AI reviewers (the Stanford Agentic Reviewer [ 16 ] , OpenReviewer-8B [ 8 ] , and the PaperAudit-Bench detector [ 20 ] ). To our knowledge, our application to the astrophysics corpus is the first controlled comparison of AI peer-reviewer systems on a real, non-ML scientific paper corpus. We release the evaluation pipeline, together with the paper lists, injection catalogues, and full adjudication logs, as a public benchmark at github.com/inigozubeldia/skepthical-benchmark .

### 2 Benchmark design


#### 2.1 Reviewer systems

We evaluate the following twelve reviewer systems.

- • Skepthical (full pipeline description in Appendix A ). Skepthical is a multi-agent reviewer with four parallel specialist modules: (i) general issues , which uses two cross-family LLMs (Claude Opus 4.7 and Gemini 2.5 Pro) to generate two independent reports that are then merged by a third-family model (GPT-5.1), alongside a free-form reading of the paper by a reasoning model (GPT-5.4); (ii) mathematics , in which the paper’s key mathematical statements are extracted and internally-verifiable claims are verified mechanically with either wolframscript or pint (the former for symbolic equality, the latter for dimensional analysis); (iii) numerics , in which the consistency of the numerical statements in the paper is verified with Python code; and (iv) citations , in which every key factual statement relying on external references is verified with an LLM with the cited papers provided in its context. To our knowledge, Skepthical is the first AI peer reviewer that combines mechanical verification of mathematics and numerics with retrieval-based verification of factual citations. It is publicly accessible at skepthical-ai.org . The configuration evaluated below is the public release frozen on 2026-04-21, one day before the 2026-04-22 arXiv submissions from which the astrophysics corpus is sampled (§ 2.2 ). No prompt or model choice was tuned in response to the papers or results reported in this paper.

- • Single-LLM reviewers. We consider two configurations of a single-LLM reviewer: a free-form configuration, run with a one-sentence prompt (‘ Write a referee report for this scientific paper as if you were a journal referee ’), and a structured configuration, run with the full operating instructions of all of Skepthical ’s agents compiled into one 978-line prompt (report-merging and tool-output adjudication agents removed and with analogous mechanical-verification-free instructions given for mathematics and numerics). Both configurations are evaluated for three different models (Claude Opus 4.7, GPT-5.5, and Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview, hereafter Gemini 3.1 Pro), giving six single-LLM reviewers in total. We further evaluate a concise version of the structured configuration in Appendix H .

- • Claude Code. We use the frontier general-purpose coding agent Claude Code [ 46 ] , running with Claude Opus 4.7 in a sandbox with the following allowed tools: Read , Bash , Grep , Glob , python3 (with NumPy, SymPy, SciPy, pint ), wolframscript , and arXiv access. As for the single-LLM reviewers, we consider two configurations (sharing model, sandbox, and tools): a free-form configuration, run with the same one-sentence referee prompt as in the free-form single-LLM reviewers, and a structured configuration, run with a prompt containing Skepthical ’s full operating instructions.

- • Three publicly available external reviewers. (i) The Stanford Agentic Reviewer at paperreview.ai [ 16 ] (closed-source, internals undocumented, queried on 2026-05-01; only run on the astrophysics corpus); (ii) OpenReviewer-8B [ 8 ] , an open-weights Llama-3.1-8B fine-tuned on $\approx 79\,$ K ICLR / NeurIPS reviews, self-hosted and invoked with the published ICLR-template prompt; and (iii) the PaperAudit-Bench detector [ 20 ] , an open-weights Qwen3-14B-based reviewer, run in Deep mode. We do not retrain or re-prompt any of these reviewers.

Together, the twelve systems allow for three comparisons and one external reference point. The first comparison is the effect of the prompt , as within the single-LLM group and within the Claude Code group, the free-form and structured configurations differ only in the prompt. The second is the effect of the agentic loop with tool-use : at each prompt level, the Claude Code configuration can be compared with the Claude Opus 4.7 single-LLM configuration with the same prompt, so the difference between them is not the instructions but the ability to perform several LLM calls, run code, read files, and fetch cited papers. The third is the effect of agentic scaffolding : structured Claude Code and Skepthical share the same operating instructions, but in Skepthical those instructions are executed by dedicated specialist agents that are enforced to run, whereas Claude Code has a much less strict scaffolding. Finally, the three external reviewers show how publicly available systems that an author could use today perform on the same benchmark, and the two open-weights reviewers also test whether small models fine-tuned on review data can compete with frontier systems. Two caveats apply: i) Skepthical uses models from all three providers, so its margin over any single-provider system mixes the effect of the pipeline with that of cross-provider ensembling, and ii) none of these comparisons is a capacity-matched ablation of Skepthical . Compute resources and asset licences are summarised in Appendix M .


#### 2.2 Corpora

We evaluate on two corpora of 15 arXiv preprints each, one in astrophysics and one in ML. Each corpus consists of submissions to a single arXiv category on a single recent day, capped at 20 pages to limit cost. The astrophysics corpus comprises 15 of the 24 astro-ph submissions of 2026-04-22 within the page cap (median 13 pages), whereas the ML corpus comprises 15 of the 74 eligible cs.LG submissions of 2026-07-15, drawn by stratified random sampling over eight subfields (median 9 pages, range 3–19). Both chosen days postdate the knowledge cut-off of every model we use. Both paper lists are given in Appendix B .

Astrophysics was chosen for its abundant formal mathematics and numerics, two key aspects that our benchmark evaluates, and because most existing AI-reviewer evaluations have run on ML papers [ 20 , 2 , 11 , 8 , 5 ] . ML was added as the second corpus i) to test whether our conclusions hold beyond a single field and ii) because it is the field on which the two fine-tuned open-weights external reviewers were trained, so their performance can be measured in-distribution as well as out of it. We note that no component or prompt in any of the reviewer systems is field-specific.


#### 2.3 Error injection

For each paper, four versions are reviewed: one clean copy (used to assess false positives) and three error-injected copies, each carrying one error of each of four types (general issues, mathematics, numerics, citations) at a chosen severity ( $S_{1}$ subtle, $S_{2}$ clear, or $S_{3}$ blatant). Across the three injected copies of a paper, every (error type, severity) combination appears exactly once (see Appendix C.1 ). Spreading the 12 injections across three copies (rather than concentrating them in one) keeps the per-copy error density small, which we take to be more representative of a typical real paper. Each corpus thus leads to 15 clean and 45 error-injected copies, with the astrophysics corpus containing a total of $15\times 12-3=177$ injected errors (one paper had too few equations to admit mathematics injections) and its ML counterpart, $180$ .

Representative examples of the three severity levels, by error type, are as follows (full taxonomy with examples in Appendix C ). Citations run from a 1 $\sigma$ value shift misquotation ( $S_{1}$ ) through a significant methodology misattribution ( $S_{2}$ ) to a reversed cited conclusion ( $S_{3}$ ); numerics from an abstract–body second-decimal drift ( $S_{1}$ ) through a digit swap or percent-of error ( $S_{2}$ ) to a structural inconsistency such as percentages summing above 100 % ( $S_{3}$ ); mathematics from a sign flip on a subleading term ( $S_{1}$ ) through an operator swap or normalisation drop ( $S_{2}$ ) to a dimensional absurdity ( $S_{3}$ ); and general issues from a typo ( $S_{1}$ ) through a methods–results inconsistency or phantom-evidence insertion ( $S_{2}$ ) to an abstract–body contradiction ( $S_{3}$ ). We note that the taxonomy may not fully reflect the distribution of naturally occurring errors in papers and that severity levels are designed to be calibrated within each error type but not across them, so a given severity label may not represent the same detection difficulty across error types.

Each injection is performed by an agent (Claude Sonnet 4.6 for the astrophysics corpus, Gemini 3.1 Pro for the ML corpus) that takes as input the full paper L a T e X source and returns a suggested edit. The suggested edit is accepted only if it passes a mechanical validator (Appendix C ); otherwise an injection of the same type is re-attempted.


#### 2.4 Adjudication pipeline

Once the reviewer systems have processed all 60 paper copies for each corpus (45 error-injected and 15 clean), we apply an adjudication pipeline to every resulting review to assess (i) which injected errors were detected on the error-injected copies ( detection matching ), and (ii) which of the issues raised on the clean copies are genuine false positives ( false-positive adjudication ).

Detection matching. For each (paper, injected copy, reviewer), the review plus the error-injection records are given to a three-judge cross-family panel (Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-5.4, and Gemini 3 Flash Preview, hereafter Gemini 3 Flash). Each judge returns a verdict for each injection ( $\{$ detected, confidence, matching_evidence, reason $\}$ ); the final verdict is the 3-way majority. Low-confidence or 2-1 ambiguous verdicts trigger a PDF-attached escalation pass, in which a single judge (Claude Sonnet 4.6) adjudicates the injection with the full paper PDF in context.

Across the $4{,}084$ per-error-type adjudications in the two corpora ( $2{,}104$ astrophysics, $1{,}980$ ML), 93.5% reach unanimous first-round agreement (3-0) and 6.5% are 2-1 splits. The PDF-attached escalation fires on 34.9% of injections (38.7% astrophysics, 30.8% ML), with the dominant trigger being panel uncertainty rather than disagreement. Of the escalated injections, 92.3% confirm the first-round majority, and 73.6% return at high confidence. Aggregating across both stages, 97.3% of all adjudications end with a verdict that was either accepted at first round or confirmed under PDF re-judgement, only 2.7% are reversed by the escalation pass, and 91.6% reach unanimous agreement across every judge that voted (first-round panel plus, where applicable, the escalation pass). Only 7.4% of adjudications retain a low-confidence verdict after escalation. The adjudication is therefore highly self-consistent (judge-level agreement statistics in Appendix D ).

False-positive adjudication on clean copies. Each review is processed by an agent (GPT-4.1) tasked with identifying clear positives (as opposed to low-confidence or open-to-interpretation points) and classifying them into four types: citations, mathematics, numerics, and general issues. These detections are then passed into a type-dependent pipeline. Citation detections are assessed by a two-judge cross-family panel with the cited PDFs attached (GPT-5 and Claude Sonnet 4.6; if needed, tie-break with Gemini 3.1 Pro). Numerics detections are checked mechanically: GPT-4.1 turns each one into a concrete numerical check to be carried out, a second independent GPT-4.1 call confirms that the numbers involved appear in the paper, and the check is then run in Python. Mathematics detections are checked with Skepthical ’s mathematics module (which uses wolframscript and pint ). Finally, general issues detections are adjudicated with a 3-judge cross-family panel with PDF attached (Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-5.4 with medium reasoning, and Gemini 3.1 Pro). Outcome verdicts are confirmed-issue , fp , or ambiguous .

In the following, we call a difference between two systems significant when a paired paper-level bootstrap puts less than $5\,\%$ probability on its sign reversing (Appendix G ). The two highest totals on each corpus belong to Skepthical and structured GPT-5.5: $70.6\pm 6.2$ and $61.3\pm 6.6\,\%$ , respectively, on astrophysics, a significant difference; and $76.7\pm 5.6$ and $81.1\pm 5.3\,\%$ on ML, where the difference is not significant. On general issues , the highest rate is Skepthical ’s on astrophysics ( $82.2\,\%$ ; next is structured GPT-5.5 at $72.7\,\%$ , a difference short of significance) and is shared by Skepthical and structured GPT-5.5 on ML ( $84.4\,\%$ each). On mathematics , the highest is structured GPT-5.5’s on both corpora, by a small, non-significant margin on astrophysics ( $75.6$ vs $73.8\,\%$ for Skepthical and free-form GPT-5.5) and a larger one on ML ( $91.1$ vs $77.8\,\%$ for Skepthical ) that falls just short of significance. On numerics the highest is structured Claude Code’s on astrophysics ( $91.1\,\%$ , obtained by writing and running ad-hoc Python checks; see below) and is shared by Skepthical and structured GPT-5.5 on ML ( $91.1\,\%$ each). On citations , Skepthical , the only system in which PDF retrieval is enforced, is at $46.7\,\%$ against at most $26.7\,\%$ for any other system on astrophysics, whereas on ML structured Claude Opus 4.7, structured GPT-5.5, and structured Claude Code are at $57.8$ – $60.0\,\%$ and Skepthical at $53.3\,\%$ ; we examine the reason for this behaviour below.

The two ML-tuned open-weights reviewers detect almost no injections on either corpus ( $\leq 2.3\,\%$ on astrophysics; $1.7$ and $2.8\,\%$ on ML, their home field). The Stanford Agentic Reviewer, run on astrophysics only, reaches $40.7\,\%$ , $21$ percentage points (pp) below the strongest single LLM and $30\,\mathrm{pp}$ below Skepthical .

The strongest systems detect a large fraction of the subtle injections ( $57$ – $73\,\%$ at $S_{1}$ for Skepthical , structured GPT-5.5, and structured Claude Code), whereas the weakest configurations miss most errors even when blatant (the open-weights reviewers below $5\,\%$ ). The ordering of systems is essentially the same at every severity level, so the comparisons that follow hold across the scale.

As shown by the per-provider rows of Table 2 and per-provider curves of Figure 1 , on the same free-form prompt, three frontier LLMs differ by up to $48.6\,\mathrm{pp}$ in total detection on astrophysics (GPT-5.5 $60.5\,\%$ , Claude Opus 4.7 $36.7\,\%$ , Gemini 3.1 Pro $11.9\,\%$ ) and by $52.8\,\mathrm{pp}$ on ML ( $63.9$ , $43.9$ , and $11.1\,\%$ ). Mathematics and numerics drive this discrepancy.

The structured prompt narrows the cross-provider spread considerably by boosting the performance of the weakest models: it has a very large impact for Gemini on both corpora ( $+25.4\,\mathrm{pp}$ on astrophysics, $+40.0\,\mathrm{pp}$ on ML) and a smaller but significant one on Claude ( $+16.6$ and $+28.3\,\mathrm{pp}$ ). For GPT-5.5, the effect of the structured prompt is near-negligible on astrophysics ( $+0.8\,\mathrm{pp}$ ), but substantial on ML ( $+17.2\,\mathrm{pp}$ ). In general, we can conclude that detection depends strongly on both the model family and the prompt content

The same prompt change inside the Claude Code group is the cleanest probe of what an operating-instructions prompt causes. With the model (Claude Opus 4.7), sandbox, allowed tools, and arXiv access held fixed, the only difference between the two configurations is the prompt, which moves detection by $+32.2\,\mathrm{pp}$ on astrophysics and $+38.9\,\mathrm{pp}$ on ML. The full output of the astrophysics runs, which we have analysed, shows how the agent uses its available resources. (i) The structured agent used tools extensively (median 9 agent turns, max 26), whereas the free-form agent simply read the PDF and replied. (ii) The structured agent wrote and ran 21 multi-line Python scripts (using NumPy, SymPy, SciPy, math , and mpmath ), whereas the free-form agent wrote none, although it knew it could. (iii) Neither used wolframscript , although the structured prompt encourages its use. In summary, with everything else fixed, the prompt alone drives qualitatively different tool use.

Holding the structured prompt fixed, going from a single Claude Opus 4.7 call to Claude Code to Skepthical takes detection from $53.3$ to $61.0$ to $70.6\,\%$ on astrophysics and from $72.2$ to $73.3$ to $76.7\,\%$ on ML. The first step adds tools at fixed model and prompt, and its gain on astrophysics comes from the error types that a script can check: numerics and mathematics, with $+12.0$ and $+16.4\,\mathrm{pp}$ (both significant); on ML, where there are fewer mathematical and numerical statements to check, there are statistically insignificant changes.

Going from Claude Code to Skepthical changes more than scaffolding, as Skepthical draws on three LLM providers. With this caveat in mind, Skepthical ’s gains over Claude Code on astrophysics are on citations ( $+20.0\,\mathrm{pp}$ ) and general issues ( $+26.7\,\mathrm{pp}$ ), both significant, while Claude Code is ahead on numerics (by $11.1\,\mathrm{pp}$ , significant); on ML, no difference is significant. The citation result is the clearest case for enforcing a verification step rather than offering one: Claude Code had the same arXiv access, but the execution traces show paper retrieval never took place, whereas in Skepthical retrieval is mandatory. On astrophysics this puts Skepthical $20\,\mathrm{pp}$ ahead of structured Claude Code on citations; on ML it does not ( $53.3$ vs $60.0\,\%$ , not significant). A possible explanation, developed in the next paragraph, is that the models are familiar enough with the ML literature to judge many cited claims without retrieval from the source, so that enforced retrieval adds little there.

Finally, compared with the best single-LLM reviewer, structured GPT-5.5 (the highest total among the six single-LLM configurations on both corpora), Skepthical is $+9.3\,\mathrm{pp}$ on astrophysics (significant) and $-4.4\,\mathrm{pp}$ on ML (not significant). The multi-agent pipeline therefore outperforms the best single-LLM reviewer only on the corpus where its enforced steps make a difference, citation retrieval on astrophysics in particular; on ML the two are statistically indistinguishable on overall detection rate. We note, however, that against the concise structured GPT-5.5 of Appendix H , which reaches $68.9\,\%$ on astrophysics and $76.1\,\%$ on ML, the margin is $+1.7$ and $+0.6\,\mathrm{pp}$ , neither significant.

On ML, the structured single LLMs have a higher citation detection rate than on astrophysics (all three providers; GPT-5.5 from $18$ to $58\,\%$ ), while Skepthical barely changes ( $47$ to $53\,\%$ ). We attribute this to two factors, as discussed in more detail in Appendix J . First, more of the ML citation errors can be detected by having a general knowledge of the field, e.g., a well-known method attributed to the wrong paper (14 of 45, against 4 of 45 on astrophysics). Second, the models appear to know the ML literature better than the astrophysics one, since they also detect more of the errors that we classified as needing retrieval from the cited paper. Skepthical , which reads the cited paper in every case, changes little. In conclusion, retrieval makes a difference when the model does not already know the literature.

The detection results understate the contribution of the mechanical verifiers. A frontier LLM matching Skepthical ’s $80\,\%$ numerics detection rate is not the same finding as Skepthical delivering $80\,\%$ recall through tool-mediated deterministic computation. Skepthical ’s checks invoke deterministic tools (Python calculations, wolframscript , pint , and arXiv-PDF retrieval) on every checked claim and are auditable in source, while the single-LLM equivalent is a single forward pass whose correctness on any given equation is opaque and non-reproducible. Claude Code with the structured prompt sits between the two: it has the same tool affordances as Skepthical , but only invokes them on a fraction of instances (and never wolframscript , despite the prompt’s encouragement), so its detections are mechanically grounded only on the instances where the agent chose to run a verification script.

Six further analyses are reported in the appendices: a test for judge–reviewer family overlap (Appendix E ); a human check of the detection adjudication for one paper (Appendix F ); a concise structured-prompt configuration (Appendix H ); the attribution of Skepthical ’s detections to its modules (Appendix I ); a test of whether errors in the same copy suppress each other’s detection (Appendix K ); and the agreement between reviewer systems on which errors they detect (Appendix L ).

We define a reviewer’s false-positive (FP) rate as the fraction of the flags it raises on the 15 clean copies of a corpus that the adjudication panel judges clearly false (§ 2.4 ). False-positive rates are generally low: apart from the two open-weights reviewers, every system is below $9\,\%$ on astrophysics, and six of nine are below $10\,\%$ on ML, the exceptions being the three structured single LLMs ( $11.5$ – $22.7\,\%$ ). Skepthical has a low FP rate on both corpora, $2.8\,\%$ on astrophysics (106 flags) and $4.0\,\%$ on ML (99 flags), against $3.4$ – $22.7\,\%$ for the structured single LLMs. The lowest rates overall belong to free-form single LLMs, which raise almost no mathematics or numerics flags: Claude Opus 4.7 on astrophysics ( $1.0\,\%$ ) and Gemini 3.1 Pro on ML ( $3.5\,\%$ ).

The effect of the structured prompt on the FP rate is mixed: on astrophysics it narrows the spread across providers, lowering GPT-5.5’s rate ( $8.7\to 3.4\,\%$ ) and raising Claude’s ( $1.0\to 5.2\,\%$ ); on ML it raises every provider’s rate; and for Claude Code it lowers the rate on both corpora. Three things, however, are consistent across reviewers and corpora. First, mathematics flags are the least reliable: pooled over the frontier systems, they are judged false at $30\,\%$ on astrophysics and $67\,\%$ on ML, against $3\,\%$ for general-issue flags, and they account for half of all false positives on both corpora. Second, the structured prompt roughly halves the number of general-issue flags, which are rarely wrong, so it can raise the FP rate even when the number of false positives falls (GPT-5.5 on ML: $12\to 9$ false positives on $131\to 69$ flags). Finally, the two open-weights detectors perform poorly on both corpora, featuring the highest rates.


### 4 Limitations

The benchmark measures one part of peer review: whether a reviewer detects objective, localisable defects (citation support, mathematical and numerical correctness, internal consistency) and whether it raises false alarms on clean papers. The benchmark does not address issues such as novelty, significance, adequacy of experiments, missing controls, interpretive judgement, or alignment with human-written reviews, as they are significantly harder to quantify and, in general, lack a clear ground truth. Therefore, our results should not be interpreted as holistic measurements of review quality.

Each corpus is small: 15 papers from one day in one field, capped at 20 pages. The rates reported here are therefore estimates for those two slices, and longer manuscripts are not represented. As already noted, severity labels are calibrated within each error type but not across them. In addition, each injected copy carries four errors, and a blatant one might draw attention away from a subtle one in the same copy. Because severities were assigned independently for each error type, the severity of the other errors in a copy varies at random. We find that whether an error is detected does not depend measurably on how severe its neighbours are (Appendix K ). However, this test cannot ascertain whether the mere presence of other errors has an effect; that would require copies carrying a single error, which was beyond the cost constraints of this work.

Adjudication is LLM-based for detection matching and for general-issue and citation false positives. Due to resource constraints, the blinded human check covers just one paper and detection only (Appendix F ). The comparisons involving Skepthical are not capacity-matched: it uses more calls and three providers, and we did not run a same-provider ensemble of equal cost, so its gains cannot be attributed to the pipeline design alone.

For the external reviewers, two specific fairness caveats apply. The Stanford reviewer is hard-capped at the first 15 pages of the PDF; the same page truncation is applied to OpenReviewer-8B’s input; and PaperAudit-Bench Deep mode chunks natively and is given the full paper.

Exact reproduction of any system here requires the frozen commercial model versions, which will be deprecated. The benchmark, however, remains reusable, since the evaluation pipeline, the paper lists, the injections, and the adjudication records are released. Finally, three risks accompany deployment of tools like Skepthical at scale: false confidence from uneven per-error-type detection, adversarial gaming of known weaknesses, and concentration of review infrastructure on a few LLM providers. The design choices here (cross-family ensembling, auditable verification of mathematics, numerics, and citations) mitigate these risks but do not remove them.


### 5 Conclusion

In this paper we have introduced an evaluation pipeline for AI peer reviewers that injects errors of four types and three severity levels into real papers and measures the detection rate as a function of error type, severity, prompt, and reviewer architecture, together with the false-positive rate on clean copies. We have applied it to two corpora of 15 real arXiv preprints each, one in astrophysics and one in ML, and benchmarked twelve reviewer systems: six single-LLM reviewers, two Claude Code configurations, three publicly available external reviewers, and Skepthical . Skepthical is a multi-agent reviewer, also introduced here, with a reference-attached citation verifier, a computer-algebra-backed mathematics auditor, a code-backed numerical auditor, and a cross-family general-issues ensemble. The evaluation pipeline, together with the injection catalogues, and the full adjudication logs, is released as a benchmark for the AI-reviewer landscape at github.com/inigozubeldia/skepthical-benchmark , and Skepthical is publicly available at skepthical-ai.org .

Our main conclusions are as follows.

- 1. The choices of model and prompt make a difference. Given the same one-sentence prompt, three frontier LLMs differ by about $50\,\mathrm{pp}$ in total detection on both corpora, mostly on mathematics and numerics. The longer structured prompt narrows this spread by lifting the weaker models. For Claude Code, with model and tools fixed, the same prompt change alone triggers extensive tool use and raises detection by over $30\,\mathrm{pp}$ on both corpora.

- 2. Tools and scaffolding help when they contribute what the model cannot supply on its own. In particular, on astrophysics, Claude Code improves significantly on a single Claude call in mathematics and numerics detection, and enforcing citation retrieval gives Skepthical a large citation lead there. On ML, however, where the models appear to know the literature better, enforced retrieval does not lead to a significant improvement.

- 3. Detection rises with severity for every capable system, whereas the two fine-tuned open-weights reviewers have a negligible detection rate.

- 4. False-positive rates are generally low, below $10\,\%$ for most systems apart from the open-weights reviewers, but depend on prompt, model, and scaffolding. Mathematics flags are the least reliable and account for half of all false positives; Skepthical keeps a low rate on both corpora.

Beyond the AI-reviewer landscape itself, peer review remains a cornerstone of the scientific process for both human-authored and increasingly AI-conducted research [ 3 , 13 , 26 , 18 , 19 , 25 , 22 , 7 ] . In this regard, we see Skepthical , and tools like it, both as standalone reviewing infrastructure for human-authored research and as components that AI scientists could integrate as an internal self-audit layer guiding autonomous research.

### Appendix A Skepthical pipeline detail

Skepthical reviews a paper with a pipeline of specialised agents rather than with a single LLM call. The paper is processed in parallel by four modules, one each for general issues, mathematics, numerics, and citations, and their outputs are merged at the end into a single structured review (Figure 3 ). Table 3 lists the model used by every agent in the configuration run in this work.

> Figure 3: The Skepthical pipeline. The paper PDF is processed in parallel by four specialist modules (general issues, mathematics, numerics, citations); each module is a short chain of LLM agents (robot icons) interleaved with mechanical or deterministic engines (gear icons). Module outputs converge in a final assembly step that produces the markdown review: main issues from the three audits are added to the structured general-issues report, the result is merged with the unstructured report by unstructured_report_merger , and the three audits are also appended in full.


#### A.1 General-issues module

The general-issues module produces the backbone of the review. It has two parts: a structured stage, in which two LLMs from different families are combined so that the issues raised do not depend on a single family, and a parallel free-form reading of the paper by a single reasoning model.

The first stage is the total_reviewer ensemble: two parallel calls, one on Claude Opus 4.7 and one on Gemini 2.5 Pro, each returning a summary, a list of strengths, and three lists of issues by severity ( major , minor , and very minor ) in a fixed JSON format. Both calls use the same operating instructions, which require a coverage checklist of five categories (general, logical consistency, evidence and citations, presentation and clarity, reproducibility), a consistency pass across sections on named choices and re-used quantities, an audit of rhetoric and scope intended to catch sweeping generalisations and contradictions in direction or kind, and a phantom-evidence audit in which every reference to a figure, table, or other reported result must be matched to the corresponding object in the paper. The two outputs are then merged by report_merger (GPT-5.1), which removes duplicate issues, combines the strengths, and harmonises the severity levels across the two calls. The merger belongs to a third family (OpenAI), so that neither of the two input families is favoured at the merge step [ 15 ] .

In parallel with the structured ensemble, the unstructured_reviewer agent (GPT-5.4, high reasoning) reads the paper without a checklist. Its purpose is not coverage, which the structured pass already provides, but to bring extended reasoning to a workflow whose other parts run without it. This serves two ends. First, the free-form pass can raise concerns about the paper as a whole that fall outside the checklist. Second, it helps to establish the relative importance of the issues found by the structured calls, which are not necessarily reported in order of importance. This pass runs on the third frontier family (OpenAI), so that, taken together, the structured and unstructured parts of the general-issues module span Anthropic, Google, and OpenAI.


#### A.2 Mathematics module

The mathematics module audits the paper’s mathematics with a five-stage pipeline. The first stage extracts the mathematical statements in the paper, the second cross-checks them, the third verifies them mechanically (if applicable), and the fourth and fifth interpret and aggregate the results.

In stage 1, the maths_extractor agent (Gemini 2.5 Pro, high reasoning) extracts every mathematical statement that the paper presents as central (e.g., an equation, a derivation chain, a limit or series identity, a dimensional or normalisation constraint, a definition, or an inequality) into a structured record. The record contains the L a T e X of the statement, the verbatim source snippet, its location, the kind of statement, a flag marking whether units matter, and an executable plan for checking it. When a derivation chain contains an asserted equation, that equation is also extracted on its own, so that the derivation steps go to mechanical verification while the asserted starting equation receives a separate knowledge audit. The extractor must record every numbered equation it does not extract in an excluded_equations list, with a reason. If that list is empty while some equations remain uncovered, the extraction is repeated with reinforced instructions.

Stage 2 is a cross-family check of the extracted statements. For each of them, the maths_transcription_checker agent (Claude Opus 4.7) verifies two things. First, that the code prepared for the mechanical check in stage 3 faithfully reproduces the statement as it appears in the paper. Second, for any equation that the paper asserts rather than derives, such as a definition or a standard result quoted from the literature, that the equation matches the canonical form of the quantity it describes. This second check targets errors in prefactors, exponents, scaling factors, and subleading terms, which a mechanical check of the paper’s own derivation cannot detect, since the derivation can be internally consistent while starting from a wrong equation. We note that stages 1 and 2 run on different model families, which makes it less likely that an error of the extracting model, for instance a misread sign or exponent, is shared by the model that checks its work.

In stage 3, the statements are verified mechanically. Symbolic equalities, limits, series identities, and solvability queries are checked with wolframscript . When a statement contains free symbols, it is also evaluated at ten random points of their parameter space. SymPy serves as a fallback if wolframscript crashes. In addition, pint performs the dimensional analysis of the statements flagged as unit-critical in stage 1. Each check returns one of six outcomes: confirmed , refuted , inconclusive , error , skipped , or unavailable .

In stage 4, the maths_interpreter agent (Claude Opus 4.7) combines the outcome of the stage-2 check and the stage-3 mechanical verdict for each statement into a final judgement of pass , fail , or uncertain . Finally, in stage 5, the maths_aggregator agent (Gemini 2.5 Pro, high reasoning) compiles the per-statement judgements into the mathematics audit and writes a mathematics audit report that is attached to the final review.


#### A.3 Numerics module

The numerics module verifies numerical statements with code. First, two extractor agents from different model families, numerics_extractor_a (Claude Opus 4.7) and numerics_extractor_b (GPT-5.5, high reasoning), read the PDF independently and each returns a list of numerical statements to be verified. Every statement is labelled with one of eleven kinds of check (agreement between abstract and body, a table-row total, a table-column total, percentages that should sum to one hundred, a percentage of a stated quantity, a ratio, a difference, an integer equality, a constant repeated across the paper, a rounded value, and a sum that should equal a stated total). To make sure that the statement refers to a number that actually appears in the paper, the extractor must also record where it found it. It does so by quoting a verbatim passage of at least eight consecutive words from the paper that contains the number, together with the number itself exactly as printed, without units or L a T e X markup.

Second, a deterministic Python step compares the two lists and keeps only the statements on which both extractors agree, that is, those for which the quoted passage, the kind of check, and the printed number all match. Statements found by only one extractor are set aside in a separate list of unverified items. Each surviving statement is then re-read by the numerics_rereader agent (GPT-4.1). This agent is given only the quoted passage and the kind of check, not the number reported by the extractors, and is asked to state which number is printed in that passage. The statement is accepted for verification only if the number the re-reader reports matches the one both extractors gave. This removes a whole class of errors in which an extractor misreads or invents a number.

Third, the accepted statements are verified by Python code, with no LLM involved. For each of the eleven kinds of check there is a fixed function that performs the corresponding arithmetic on the extracted numbers, for instance summing a table row and comparing the result with the printed total. When a check fails, a fixed rule assigns the severity according to the kind of check and the size of the discrepancy. Some problems cannot be found in this way, and a single LLM call is used for them. The numerics_reasoner agent (GPT-5.5) reads the full PDF once, with the results of the mechanical checks given to it so that it does not repeat them, and looks for two further kinds of problem: a quantity that is given different values in distant parts of the paper, and numerical errors that both extractors missed. Its findings are marked in the audit report as not mechanically verified. Each of them must be anchored to a verbatim quote from the paper, and a finding whose quote cannot be found in the paper is discarded. Finally, the numerics_reporter agent (GPT-4.1) compiles the mechanical and reasoner findings into a numerics audit report that is attached to the final review.


#### A.4 Citations module

The citations module checks the factual statements in the paper that rely on external references against the cited papers themselves, which it retrieves. It runs in two passes. In the first, the statement_extractor agent (GPT-5.1) reads the full text of the paper and returns the $N$ most important such statements. $N$ grows with the length of the paper, at one statement per ten thousand characters, up to a maximum of ten in order to bound the cost. Each statement is returned together with the reference identifier exactly as it appears in the paper (a BibTeX key, a numeric label, or an author-year string). Two further agents then connect the statements to the cited papers. First, the reference_extractor agent (GPT-4.1) copies the entries of the paper’s reference list verbatim. Then, the reference_resolver agent (GPT-5 mini) maps each in-text identifier to its entry in the reference list and from there to an arXiv identifier, resolving differences in numbering and in author-year forms (a smaller model suffices for this lookup step). The PDFs of the cited papers are then downloaded from the arXiv using the identifiers obtained in this way.

The statements are then verified. For each of them, the paper_verification_agent agent (Gemini 2.5 Pro, high reasoning) receives the statement together with the PDF of the cited paper and returns one of five verdicts: supported , weakly supported , partially supported , contradicted , or unrelated . The agent is instructed to use only the attached paper, which limits the influence of what the model may already know, or believe, about the cited work.

In the second pass, the statement_extractor agent is run again, this time with the first-pass statements and their verdicts given to it so that it does not repeat them, and asked for up to three further statements that look suspicious. These are verified in the same way by the paper_verification_agent agent, and their verdicts are labelled as coming from the second pass, so that the two passes can be weighted separately later in the pipeline.


#### A.5 Coordination, assembly, and deployment

The four modules run concurrently, on four worker threads inside the Skepthical worker process. Once all four have finished, an assembly step builds the review in a fixed order as follows: it merges the two total_reviewer outputs through the report_merger agent, adds the paper title, appends the mathematics and numerics audit reports, folds in the free-form pass through the unstructured_report_merger agent, and finally inserts the per-statement verdicts of the citations module. The assembled review is then compiled to markdown and PDF.

Skepthical is deployed on Google Cloud Run and is accessible at skepthical-ai.org . Submitted papers are placed in a queue and processed by worker instances that run in parallel, so throughput grows with the number of workers. A typical 12-page astrophysics paper takes approximately 24 minutes.

### Appendix C Error injection design and taxonomy

The injection pipeline has two pieces. First, a severity-balanced 3-copy schedule fixes how the twelve (error type, severity) combinations are distributed across the three injected copies of each paper, so that every combination is chosen exactly once. Second, a combination registry of 33 sub-types, distributed across those twelve (error type, severity) combinations, with two or three sub-types per combination, determines what each combination looks like in practice, with a verbatim mechanism block, an eligibility filter, and a fixed output format (a Pydantic schema) that the LLM-driven proposer must conform to.


#### C.1 Severity-balanced 3-copy schedule

Each paper features in four versions: a clean copy that carries the original LaTeX source, and three injected copies, each bearing one error per error type (four errors per copy). Across the three injected copies, every (error type, severity) combination is hit exactly once. A schematic schedule for one paper:

Reading down a column gives a permutation of $\{S_{1},S_{2},S_{3}\}$ , so that each error type sees each severity exactly once across the three copies. The permutations are drawn independently for each error type, so the severities of the four errors in a copy are independent of one another.

### Appendix D Panel-internal agreement

Two LLM-judge panels feature in the adjudication pipeline (§ 2.4 ): the detection-matching panel (Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-5.4, Gemini 3 Flash), which decides, for each injection, whether a given review detected it, and the general-issues false-positive panel (Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-5.4, Gemini 3.1 Pro), which classifies each general-issue flag raised on a clean copy as confirmed issue, false positive, or ambiguous. The rates in the main text rest on these panels, so we report here how well the judges within each panel agree with one another, on both corpora. For the matching panel we use the first-round votes only, so that the PDF-attached escalation pass is not mixed in.

Figure 4 shows the pairwise Pearson $r$ between judges on their binary votes. ### Appendix E Adjudication-panel family-pairing test

Several reviewer systems share an LLM provider family with one of the three judges of the detection-matching panel. Following [ 15 ] , this raises a self-preference concern: a judge might vote “detected” more readily on output from its own family than a judge from another family would on the same injection, which would inflate the detection rates of those reviewer systems. We test this for both corpora on the first-round panel votes themselves, comparing the judges within each injection, rather than by re-adjudicating with a substitute panel.

To define the test, let $\mathrm{A}$ , $\mathrm{O}$ , $\mathrm{G}$ denote the Anthropic (Sonnet 4.6), OpenAI (GPT-5.4), and Google (Gemini 3 Flash) judges. For each of the eight reviewer systems built on a single LLM family (the six single-LLM configurations and the two Claude Code configurations), let $r^{(s)}_{f}\in[0,1]$ denote the first-round detection rate of judge $f$ on system $s$ ’s injections. With $\mathrm{self}(s)$ the family of system $s$ and $\mathrm{disj}(s)=\{\mathrm{A},\mathrm{O},\mathrm{G}\}\setminus\{\mathrm{self}(s)\}$ ,

[Tableau] $$\Delta_{s}\;=\;r^{(s)}_{\mathrm{self}(s)}\;-\;\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{f\in\mathrm{disj}(s)}r^{(s)}_{f}.$$

A positive $\Delta_{s}$ means that the same-family judge detects more often than the other two. A judge that is more generous overall, voting “detected” more often on every system’s output, would produce a positive $\Delta_{s}$ for all systems of its own family without any self-preference. To separate the two, we also report an adjusted version $\widetilde{\Delta}_{s}$ , in which each judge’s rate on a system is measured relative to that judge’s rate pooled over all reviewer systems. These pooled rates are given in Table 6 , which shows that the three judges are close to one another, within about $3\,\mathrm{pp}$ on both corpora, with OpenAI slightly the most generous. Skepthical and the external reviewers are excluded from $\Delta_{s}$ , since Skepthical uses agents from all three judge families and the external reviewers either cannot be attributed to one family (Stanford) or share a family with no judge (open-weights). We note that all CIs in this appendix are $95\,\%$ paper-level bootstrap intervals.

> Table 6: Pooled first-round detection rate by judge, across all reviewer systems’ detection-matching injections ( $2{,}104$ on astrophysics, $1{,}980$ on ML). $95\,\%$ paper-level bootstrap CIs.

[Tableau] Judge Astrophysics ML Anthropic (Sonnet 4.6) $36.5\,\%\;[33.1,40.1]$ $44.2\,\%\;[41.0,47.4]$ OpenAI (GPT-5.4) $38.3\,\%\;[34.8,41.8]$ $46.4\,\%\;[43.3,49.5]$ Google (Gemini 3 Flash) $35.2\,\%\;[31.8,38.9]$ $43.1\,\%\;[39.4,46.8]$

Table 7 reports $\Delta_{s}$ and $\widetilde{\Delta}_{s}$ for each of the eight systems on each corpus. ### Appendix F Human adjudication

The detection rates in this paper are computed from the verdicts of an LLM panel. As a validation of these verdicts, one of the authors annotated every detection decision for one astrophysics paper, without knowledge of the panel’s verdicts or of which system had written each review.

The paper, 2604.20975 , was drawn at random from the 15-paper corpus. Its three injected copies carry four injections each, and each copy was reviewed by the 12 systems, giving 36 reviews and 144 adjudications, each on whether a given review detected a given injection. Twelve of the 144 decisions concern three reviews for which no panel verdict was recorded, leaving 132 comparisons.

On these 132, the annotator and the panel agree on 127 ( $96.2\,\%$ ; Cohen’s $\kappa=0.914$ ). The five disagreements are all in the same direction: the panel credited a detection that the annotator rejected. The panel’s detection rate on this paper is therefore slightly higher than the annotator’s, $34.8$ against $31.1\,\%$ .

Three limitations apply. First, the check covers a single astrophysics paper. Annotating every review of a paper against every injection is labour-intensive, and extending it to further papers or to the ML corpus was beyond the resources of this work, so the agreement reported here is evidence about the panel on one paper rather than a corpus-wide validation. Second, with a single annotator, inter-annotator agreement cannot be measured, so a disagreement with the panel cannot be attributed to the panel rather than to the annotator. Third, the check covers detection only: the false-positive panel, which Appendix D shows to be the less reliable of the two, was not checked against a human.

### Appendix I Module attribution

A natural question about a multi-module reviewer such as Skepthical is which of its modules its detections come from. The rates of Table 2 credit Skepthical as a whole, so we attributed its astrophysics detections to modules by examining the reviews themselves. Each Skepthical review contains a general review and separate citation, mathematics, and numerics audit sections written by the specialist modules. For every injection of those three types, we recorded whether the corresponding specialist section identifies the injected error and whether the review identifies it anywhere, which is what the panel judges and Table 2 reports. This locates where the supporting evidence appears, although it does not say what would happen if a module were removed. The attribution was carried out by Claude in a single pass.

The attribution results are as follows. The citation audit identifies 11 of the 21 citation errors the review finds. The main failure mode is that the citing claim was never selected for checking. The numerics audit identifies 27 of the 36 numerics errors the review finds ( $75\,\%$ ); its misses are statements never selected for a check, so claim selection is again its main failure. The mathematics audit identifies 7 of the 31 mathematics errors the review finds. Most mathematics detections thus come from the general-issues ensemble reading the whole paper. These results also argue against the explanation that Skepthical performs well merely because the benchmark’s error types mirror its module names: one specialist section supplies most of its corresponding detections, one about half, and one a minority.


### Appendix J Citation errors across the two corpora

On astrophysics, Skepthical detects $46.7\,\%$ of the injected citation errors and no other system more than $26.7\,\%$ . On ML, by contrast, two of the three structured single-LLM reviewers reach $57.8\,\%$ and Skepthical $53.3\,\%$ . In this appendix, we examine the origin of this discrepancy. We recall that the single LLMs receive one call with the paper attached and no tools, so they entirely rely on their pre-trained knowledge to detect the citation injections. Skepthical , on the other hand, retrieves and reads the cited papers.

This difference may arise because the ML corpus contains more citation errors that can be caught from familiarity with the field alone, because the models are more familiar with the ML literature, or through a combination of the two. To separate these contributions, we classified each of the 45 injected citation errors of each corpus into three groups. The first group contains errors that a reader familiar with the field could reject without opening the cited paper, for instance a well-known method attributed to the wrong paper. The second contains errors for which the cited paper is needed, for instance a dropped condition or a changed number. The third contains errors that could fall either way. The classification was made in a single pass by Claude. The two corpora differ markedly in this respect: errors of the first group are more than three times as common on ML as on astrophysics (14 against 4 of the 45).

This difference in composition, however, explains only part of the single LLMs’ gain. Taken together, the three structured single LLMs detect $16.7\,\%$ of the citation errors on astrophysics and $47.4\,\%$ on ML, and had the ML errors been distributed across the groups in the astrophysics proportions they would still have detected $39.6\,\%$ . About a quarter of the gain therefore comes from the composition and the rest from higher detection within every group. The largest increase is in the first group, where knowing the field is enough. Skepthical ’s rate, by contrast, is within a few points of its astrophysics value once the composition is accounted for. In short, the single LLMs gained on ML both because more of the errors can be caught from familiarity with the field and because they are more familiar with it, whereas Skepthical , which retrieves the cited paper in every case, changed little.


### Appendix K Error coupling within a copy

A reviewer reading an injected copy encounters four injected errors at once. A natural question to ask, which we examine in this appendix, is whether the detection of one error could depend on the severity of the other errors present in the same copy. A blatant error, in particular, may draw attention away from a subtle one. The most direct test would place each injected error in a copy of its own, which requires 12 injected copies per paper rather than three. For five papers alone this would amount to 60 additional reviewed copies, at a cost comparable to that of the entire astrophysics benchmark, so this experiment was not carried out.

Instead, we used the randomisation already present in the benchmark. Because severity was assigned independently for each error type, an error of a given severity appears beside three neighbours whose severities vary at random from copy to copy, so the effect of the neighbours can be read from the existing verdicts. For every verdict on every injected error of both corpora, we recorded whether the error was detected and the highest severity among the other three errors in its copy, taking together the verdicts of the systems of Table 2 and of the concise structured configurations of Appendix H .

We find no measurable dependence on the neighbours. Over both corpora and all severities combined, the detection rate is $47$ , $50$ , and $45\,\%$ when the most severe neighbour is subtle, clear, and blatant. The difference between a blatant and a clear neighbour, the only comparison with enough errors behind it, is $-4.9\,\mathrm{pp}$ with a $95\,\%$ paper-level bootstrap interval of $[-11.8,+1.2]$ , and the interval includes zero within each severity of the target error and within each corpus as well. A logistic model that controls for the target error’s own severity, its type, the reviewer system, and the paper likewise finds no significant effect in either corpus.

We note that this test cannot determine whether the mere presence of other errors has an effect, because every copy carries four. Answering this question would require analysing the single-error copies described above, which is beyond the resources of this work.


### Appendix L Inter-reviewer agreement

Per-system detection rates (Table 2 ) leave open the question of overlap: two systems with the same rate can detect the same errors or entirely different ones, and two systems with rates near zero can agree by chance. This appendix reports the pairwise agreement between reviewers on the astrophysics corpus, which shows which features of architecture and prompt lead different systems to detect the same errors. Figure 5 shows the pairwise Pearson $r$ between the binary per-injection detection vectors of six reviewer systems, overall and per error type: Skepthical , single-LLM (free-form), single-LLM (structured), Claude Code (free-form), Claude Code (structured), and the Stanford Agentic Reviewer. For the two single-LLM entries, an error counts as detected when any of the three providers detects it. The values lie between $0.2$ and $0.54$ : the reviewers are heterogeneous, with similar overall detection rates but visibly different sets of detected errors.

The highest pair is single-LLM (free-form) and Stanford ( $r=0.54$ ), consistent with the hosted Stanford reviewer behaving like a free-form referee. Single-LLM (structured) and Claude Code (structured) are at $r=0.48$ : a shared prompt produces moderately similar detections even between a single call and an agentic loop with tools. Skepthical and Claude Code (structured) are at $r=0.27$ overall and at $r\approx 0.04$ on citations and numerics, the error types on which each is the best of all systems: two top systems whose detections are essentially independent there.

This independence is above chance. Pearson $r$ on binary vectors measures agreement beyond what the two rates alone imply: two systems that detect $80$ and $91\,\%$ of the numerics errors already coincide on about $75\,\%$ of them for that reason, so $r\approx 0$ means that their detections coincide no more often than that, not that the systems disagree. As a check we also computed Cohen’s $\kappa$ . On the overall matrix it lies at or slightly below $r$ for every pair, by at most $0.10$ , as expected for binary votes with unequal rates, and the conclusions drawn above with $r$ hold with $\kappa$ as well. Skepthical and structured Claude Code still agree only weakly overall ( $\kappa=0.27$ ) and hardly at all on citations and numerics ( $\kappa\approx 0.04$ ). The single-LLM free-form and Stanford reviewers, the most similar pair under $r$ , remain among the most similar under $\kappa$ ( $0.48$ , against a maximum of $0.49$ ).

Beyond per-injection agreement (Figure 5 ), we also test convergence at the paper level. For each (paper, reviewer) we compute the reviewer’s detection rate on that paper (out of $9$ or $12$ injections) and form the $15\times 12$ rate matrix. The mean pairwise Pearson $r$ between reviewer columns is $+0.39$ ( $n=45$ pairs, restricted to the ten systems with non-negligible detection rates; the two open-weights baselines are excluded). Reviewers thus agree more on which papers are difficult in aggregate than on which specific injections each catches. The within-paper picture is the complement: a Pearson chi-square dispersion test on per-paper detection counts gives a mean ratio of $1.04$ across the same ten systems, indicating that within a paper detections behave approximately as independent Bernoulli draws across injections, so finding one injection does not strongly predict finding another one in the same paper.


### Appendix M Compute and cost


#### M.1 Compute resources

The benchmark uses three classes of compute. (i) Hosted-API LLM calls to OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google serve every Skepthical agent (Table 3 ), the six single-LLM reviewers, the two Claude Code configurations, and the detection-matching and false-positive adjudication panels; the local CPU footprint of these calls is negligible (orchestration drivers pinned to 10 cores via taskset ) since the work is dominated by external API latency. (ii) Skepthical itself runs as a Google Cloud Run worker (1 vCPU and 2 GiB of memory per task), with a per-paper wall time of approximately 24 minutes (Appendix A ). (iii) The two self-hosted open-weights baselines, OpenReviewer-8B (bfloat16, $\sim$ 16 GB) and the PaperAudit-Bench Qwen3-14B detector (bfloat16, $\sim$ 28 GB weights plus key-value cache), run sequentially on a single NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell Workstation Edition GPU (96 GB VRAM). The host workstation is an AMD Ryzen Threadripper PRO 9995WX (96 cores, 192 threads, 502 GiB RAM) with two such GPUs attached; only one of the two was used for the open-weights baselines.
