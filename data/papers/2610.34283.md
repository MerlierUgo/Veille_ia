## ChronoReplay: One Answer Interface Is Not Enough for Streaming Spatial Intelligence

Training a spatial VLM to answer which side an object lies on does not require it to preserve the metric displacement that determines that answer, while supervision for a distance estimate may leave categorical relations largely unconstrained. When post-training emphasizes one readout, improvements can therefore concentrate on that interface while transfer to others remains weak or even degrades; we call this observed specialization interface lock-in . ChronoReplay addresses it by replaying heterogeneous spatial supervision as chronologically ordered episodes, retaining source-native targets, and balancing supervision across families. Under the same observation access, a single ChronoReplay checkpoint improves OVO-S-Bench, OST-Bench, and UCS-Bench together, including a 13.41-point gain on OST-Bench, and achieves the highest three-benchmark mean among the supervision settings compared here. Same-data controls further separate the ingredients of this transfer: answer serialization redistributes gains across OST and UCS, family-balanced learning improves over ordinary joint SFT and natural turn weighting, and chronological replay exceeds a shifted-order control by about 2.41 OST points across two paired seeds. Adaptive frame selection then changes evidence access while keeping the trained model fixed, providing an additional inference-time gain at lower visual cost. Streaming spatial performance depends on what distinctions supervision preserves, how heterogeneous histories are organized during learning, and what causal evidence inference makes available at test time.

### 1 Introduction

A home robot follows you through your apartment and is later asked: Is the mirror on my left or right? How far is the sofa from here? When did we pass the plant? One walk, three questions, three answer forms: an option, a number in meters, a moment in time. Answering them from a video stream means associating objects across views, accumulating the observer’s motion, and recovering relations in changing reference frames [ 63 , 37 , 65 , 53 , 48 ] , using only what was observed before the question; streaming spatial benchmarks now pose all three over one causal history.

Recent progress comes from two directions. Architectural work retains the stream with visual memories, bounded caches, fast weights, maps, and scene graphs [ 46 , 43 , 29 , 15 ] ; data work post-trains VLMs on spatial instruction data [ 59 , 62 , 64 ] . Both are typically single-interface : a recipe supervises one answer format and reports the benchmark that shares it; even recipes that mix qualitative and metric questions [ 7 , 11 ] train on single images, so whether one model can serve categorical, metric, and temporal queries over the same causal history is untested. Building on this, we show that changing the supervision can achieve this without a new memory module or new annotation.

Post-training on individual supervision families exposes two challenges. On the one hand, ❶ training can improve its dominant readout while transferring unevenly to other native readouts . Starting from Qwen3-VL-8B, the categorical-only control reaches 58.03 on OVO-S but lowers OST from 50.52 to 47.48; trajectory-only training leaves OST nearly flat at 51.03. We use interface lock-in to name this empirical specialization across spatial readouts. The control motivates joint training, while differences in source and task coverage limit attribution to answer format alone (Appendix D.7 ). On the other hand, ❷ a metric or temporal target is valid only under the history that produced it . An observer that moves forward after facing north and one that does so after facing east share the same recent observations and the same local displacement, yet a later question about a previously seen object has different answers. Supervision that detaches a target from its valid prefix can therefore be ambiguous; it is crucial to keep each target attached to the chronological prefix under which it holds.

> Figure 1: Why reusable spatial state. Categorical preservation and trajectory supervision constrain complementary spatial distinctions. Causal Replay combines them in shared parameters. The illustrated readouts span categorical, metric, temporal, and reference-frame queries.

In this paper, we propose ChronoReplay, which consists of two components. First , to address ❶, family-balanced learning keeps every target in its native format (option semantics, units and precision, or timestamp) instead of collapsing it into one canonical answer type, and balances the two families in one objective to regulate their relative influence on the shared model. Second , to address ❷, Causal Replay construction replays both supervision families as chronologically ordered episodes in which every target is attached to the causal prefix under which it is valid, so an accumulated displacement or a retrieved timestamp is learned together with the origin and motion that give it meaning ( chronological alignment ). The main contributions are:

- • We document an empirical interface lock-in pattern (❶): single-family training transfers unevenly across spatial readouts.

- • To address ❶ and ❷, we propose ChronoReplay: family-balanced learning trains all interfaces in their native formats under one objective, and Causal Replay construction keeps every target attached to its chronological prefix. ChronoReplay gains +13.41 OST-Bench, +7.44 UCS-Bench, and +4.91 OVO-S-Bench points under identical observation access, with OVO-S numerically comparable to a published 235B reference.

- • Matched interventions show a repeatable chronological-order benefit and a task-dependent answer-format tradeoff; adaptive frame selection at inference adds a separate gain at lower visual cost.


### 2 Related Work

Streaming spatial intelligence. Streaming and long-video models process observations causally and keep history in compressed visual memories, hierarchical summaries, recurrent memory bridges, active response mechanisms, or bounded key–value caches [ 8 , 41 , 40 , 70 , 16 , 57 , 45 , 71 , 20 , 39 , 9 , 61 , 66 , 68 ] ; StreamingBench and OVO-Bench formalize timestamped evaluation under causal access [ 27 , 35 ] , and SimpleStream characterizes the trade-off between recent perception and historical retention [ 44 ] . Related memory designs for visual agents include L 2 -VMAS, which separates perception and thinking into latent memories [ 67 ] , and AtlasVA, which combines spatial heatmaps, visual exemplars, and symbolic skills to retain visually grounded experience [ 52 ] . Spatial benchmarks further require history to stay coherent under object and observer motion and changes of reference frame, from 3D question answering, embodied spatial tests, and long-form egocentric video [ 1 , 33 , 13 , 50 , 34 ] to multi-view, 4D, and mental-model probes [ 24 , 54 ] ; OVO-S-Bench, OST-Bench, and UCS-Bench extend them to causal streams with categorical, continuous, and timestamp-dependent queries. In this work, we hold observation access fixed and treat it as an orthogonal factor (Section 5.4 ), and we use these three benchmarks because their answer formats differ; the recipe adds no memory mechanism, and our evaluation covers indoor scenes, so other environments remain untested.

Learning reusable spatial representations. Spatial MLLMs acquire geometry through geometry-aware visual features, multi-view or 3D-reconstruction inputs, and large-scale spatial instruction or reinforcement data [ 4 , 28 , 73 , 38 , 14 , 36 , 60 ] , and persistent spatial state has been represented through 3D scene tokens and embodied generalist agents [ 17 , 19 ] , adaptive fast weights, voxelized allocentric maps, textual scene descriptions, evolving scene graphs, and action-conditioned belief histories [ 18 , 25 ] . Some mix qualitative and metric spatial questions on single images, and none that we know of asks whether categorical, metric, and temporal formats interfere when one checkpoint is trained on all of them over one causal stream. This is a multi-task question: which distinctions does a shared representation keep when objectives compete for the same parameters [ 5 , 42 , 47 , 72 ] , how should their losses be balanced, and what does fine-tuning on one distribution erase from another [ 22 , 32 , 69 ] ? Instruction-tuning mixtures raise the same question at the level of data [ 58 , 31 , 30 ] , and the answer format itself changes what a model appears to know. In sequential state tracking, PAR aligns recurrent predictions with intermediate states to improve length generalization [ 51 ] . In this work, we examine the chronology of training episodes and the format and weighting of their targets, and measure the outcome through native benchmarks.


### 3 Problem Formulation

At time $t$ a model receives the causal observation history $h_{t}=(o_{1},\ldots,o_{t})$ . A spatial query $q$ arrives with a native answer interface $\kappa\in\mathcal{K}$ (a categorical option set, a metric value with units, or a timestamp), and the VLM predicts

[Tableau] $$z_{t}=f_{\theta}(h_{t}),\qquad\widehat{y}_{t}^{(\kappa)}=g_{\theta}(z_{t},q,\kappa),$$ (1)

against the task-defined target $y_{t}^{(\kappa)}=A_{\kappa}(h_{t},q)\in\mathcal{Y}_{\kappa}$ . We call the representation $z_{t}$ reusable over a family of query–interface pairs when every native target in that family can be read out from the same $z_{t}$ . Reusability is a functional requirement, not a structural one: the state may stay distributed across the VLM, but histories that lead to different answers must remain distinguishable.

Two properties of spatial histories make this demanding. First, interfaces refine one another. A categorical target separates spatial regions, whereas a metric target for the same quantity resolves variation within a region, so a representation that keeps only the coarse partition cannot support the finer readout. Second, the state depends on the origin of the history. An observer that moves forward after facing north and one that moves forward after facing east share the same recent observations and the same local displacement, yet a later question about a previously seen object requires different answers. Histories with identical recent suffixes but different origins must therefore map to different states. Our goal is to learn one $f_{\theta}$ with both properties from heterogeneous native supervision; Section 4 builds training episodes that expose both.


### 4 ChronoReplay


#### 4.1 Overview

ChronoReplay post-trains a VLM so that one shared representation supports native spatial readouts while preserving the history each readout depends on. Figure 2 summarizes two stages. Causal Replay construction forms the replay pool: every target is kept in its native format and every episode is ordered chronologically. Family-balanced learning normalizes the single-turn preservation and metric–temporal trajectory risks, then controls their relative contribution to the shared parameters. Algorithm 1 in Appendix C lists both stages.

> Figure 2: Overview of ChronoReplay. Stage I (Causal Replay construction) builds ordered trajectory supervision $\mathcal{D}_{T}$ and single-turn preservation examples $\mathcal{D}_{P}$ . Stage II (family-balanced learning) combines their normalized native-format risks with weights $1-\lambda$ and $\lambda$ . Out of scope marks observations beyond a query’s causal frontier.


#### 4.2 Causal Replay Construction


##### 4.2.1 Native-Format Targets

Motivation. How should heterogeneous answer spaces be combined? Considering ❶, we keep each target in the value space of its source interface, avoiding an additional answer-space transformation when combining categorical options, metric quantities with units and precision, and temporal answers. This is a design choice for retaining source task semantics, rather than a claim that native encoding is uniformly superior. The format through which a model answers can change what it appears to know [ 55 ] and the decoder can emit metric values directly [ 49 ] .

Native readouts. Let $F_{\theta}(C)$ denote the VLM representation of a causal prefix $C$ (Section 3 ). For interface $\kappa$ , let $Y^{\kappa}$ denote its native target and $G_{\kappa}$ the readout applied to $Z=F_{\theta}(C)$ . Its population risk is

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{R}_{\kappa}(F_{\theta},G_{\kappa})=\mathbb{E}\left[-\log G_{\kappa}\left(Y^{\kappa}\mid Z,Q\right)\right].$$ (2)

For a fixed representation and a sufficiently expressive readout, the optimal risk equals the conditional entropy $H(Y^{\kappa}\mid Z,Q)$ , so a low risk requires $Z$ to retain the information needed to recover the native target. Proposition 1 in Appendix A makes this precise. Corollary 1 shows that lossless re-encoding preserves this information requirement. Our native/canonical control changes output serialization without losing target content and finds crossed OST/UCS transfer effects.

Retained value spaces. Categorical targets keep their candidate structure; metric targets keep magnitude, sign, units, and annotation precision; temporal targets keep timestamps, event indices, and transition-relative quantities; reference-frame information stays coupled to the query. All targets are produced by the original autoregressive decoder.


##### 4.2.2 Chronologically Aligned State Trajectories

What does a metric or temporal target mean without its history? With each target’s format fixed, we now fix its history. Considering ❷, the same local displacement yields different world states from different origins (Section 3 ), so a target detached from its prefix is ambiguous. We use temporal-origin alignment to denote a data-construction rule: supervision from the interfaces present in a source episode is placed on that episode’s video-time axis and replayed in its original order, with each target attached to its legal causal prefix. Unlike flattening multi-round annotations into independent question–answer pairs, chronological replay preserves the source order and legal conditioning history used by the streaming evaluation protocol.

Ordered episodes. Let $c_{ij}$ denote the legal causal prefix at turn $j$ of episode $i$ : all observations available at that turn together with the preceding interaction history. We represent an episode as

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{E}_{i}=\left(c_{ij},q_{ij},y_{ij},\kappa_{ij}\right)_{j=1}^{m_{i}},\qquad c_{ij}\preceq c_{i,j+1},$$ (3)

where $\preceq$ denotes causal prefix inclusion, $q_{ij}$ is a spatial query, $\kappa_{ij}$ identifies its native interface, and $y_{ij}=A_{\kappa_{ij}}(c_{ij},q_{ij})$ is the target valid at that frontier. Single-turn preservation examples correspond to $m_{i}=1$ , whereas multi-round metric–temporal examples form longer episodes. Because the prefixes are nested, one episode yields $m_{i}$ supervised states that share an origin and grow in history length, so the model is trained on accumulation itself, on how a displacement or an event order changes from turn to turn, rather than on isolated snapshots.

Implicit state trajectory. Applying the VLM to successive prefixes induces

[Tableau] $$z_{ij}=F_{\theta}(c_{ij}),\qquad\mathcal{Z}_{i}=\left(z_{i1},\ldots,z_{im_{i}}\right).$$ (4)

The trajectory stays implicit within the VLM, and its first prefix defines the episode origin. No map, memory module, or auxiliary state loss is added. The origin-shift control in Section 5.3 changes the ordering and preceding context while retaining each turn block and its local supervision. It tests the contribution of this construction rule as a whole; it does not identify a particular latent origin representation. Proposition 2 in Appendix A characterizes the risk when the changed context makes the target ambiguous.


#### 4.3 Family-Balanced Learning

Motivation. What if the two families differ in size? With targets and histories fixed, the remaining choice is how much each family may move the shared parameters. The preservation family $\mathcal{D}_{P}$ holds 33,180 single-turn examples (30,635 categorical and 2,545 numerical), whereas the trajectory family $\mathcal{D}_{T}$ holds 6,381 ordered episodes (45,006 supervised metric, counting, judgement, and temporal turns). Raw row counts could let one family dominate the shared parameters and reproduce ❶ inside joint training. Therefore, we normalize risk within each family and set their ratio explicitly rather than learning it [ 21 , 10 ] .

Family risks. Let $\ell_{r}^{P}$ be the native loss for preservation example $r$ , with source-defined weight $w_{r}$ (Appendix C ). Let $\ell_{ij}^{T}$ be the native loss for verified trajectory transition $(i,j)$ , and define $\mathcal{T}=\{(i,j):1\leq i\leq|\mathcal{D}_{T}|,\ 1\leq j\leq m_{i}\}$ . The two family risks are

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{P}=\frac{\sum_{r\in\mathcal{D}_{P}}w_{r}\ell_{r}^{P}}{\sum_{r\in\mathcal{D}_{P}}w_{r}},\qquad\mathcal{L}_{T}=\frac{\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{T}}\ell_{ij}^{T}}{|\mathcal{T}|}.$$ (5)

Balanced objective. ChronoReplay combines the risks as

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{replay}}=(1-\lambda)\,\mathcal{L}_{P}+\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{T},\qquad\lambda=\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha},$$ (6)

where $\alpha$ is trajectory loss mass relative to preservation. We use $\alpha=0.5{}$ in the main experiments: trajectory receives $1/3$ of total mass; preservation receives $2/3$ , split equally between categorical and numerical targets. Appendix D specifies token normalization and serialized weighting; Proposition 3 states their invariances. Table 9 compares ordinary token SFT and natural turn weighting on the same replay pool.

Answer-length normalization assigns each supervised turn an average token loss, preventing long responses from dominating solely through token count. Within the trajectory family, turns receive equal weight, so an episode contributes in proportion to its supervised turns. Family weighting controls preservation and trajectory loss mass separately. This distinguishes response length, supervision frequency, and family balance within the training objective.


### 5 Experiments

In this section, we evaluate ChronoReplay from multiple perspectives and organize the analysis around four research questions. RQ1: Does ChronoReplay transfer across categorical, continuous, and timestamp-causal benchmarks under fixed observation access (Section 5.2 )? RQ2: How do single-family and joint training transfer across spatial readouts (Section 5.3 )? RQ3: Which components carry the effect: native formats, chronological order, or family mass (Section 5.3 )? RQ4: How much of the OVO-S system score is observation access rather than training (Section 5.4 )?


#### 5.1 Experimental Setup

We evaluate on three benchmarks: OVO-S-Bench tests categorical reasoning on egocentric perception, object permanence, and allocentric mapping [ 23 ] ; OST-Bench scores agent state, visible information, temporal localization, counting, and agent–object relations over multi-round exploration [ 26 ] ; and UCS-Bench has 8,114 timestamp-causal questions on trajectories, pose, proximity, object identity, and quantity [ 56 ] . All checkpoints start from the same official Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct revision [ 2 ] . ChronoReplay trains the language model and visual merger (visual encoder frozen) on the 33,180-sequence preservation family and the 6,381-episode trajectory family with $\alpha=0.5{}$ , and canonical scene identities keep every test scene out of training. The single-family diagnostic compares Categorical-only and Trajectory-only variants with full ChronoReplay (Appendix D.7 ). Its categorical-only arm is distinct from the main preservation pool, which also contains numerical targets. Shifted cyclically rotates the turn blocks of every episode while keeping their content, controlling for chronological alignment; Canonical rewrites every target as a typed value field, controlling for native formats (the ChronoReplay default is Native ); and Uniform-81 matches the average frame count of adaptive access, an equal-cost control for observation access. Model-only comparisons hold causal observation access fixed (OVO-S Uniform-128, the complete scene-disjoint OST protocol, all UCS questions), while adaptive access (Appendix E ) is an inference-time policy applied separately to the same checkpoint. Published models are reference points under their own protocols, and mechanism claims rely on matched Qwen3-VL-8B comparisons. Appendix D lists optimization settings, matched controls, and a four-GPU replication.

Comparison of joint-training objectives. With the joint data, chronological prefixes, native targets, and training budget held fixed, ordinary token SFT obtains 51.8/60.7/49.8 on OVO-S/OST/UCS, and natural turn weighting obtains 52.9/62.3/51.5 (Table 9 ). ChronoReplay improves all three endpoints, with a mean of 56.86 versus 54.10 and 55.57. This comparison supports the balanced objective beyond joint data exposure under the tested setting. Its OST advantage is 3.23 points over ordinary token SFT and 1.63 over natural turn weighting; the corresponding OVO-S/UCS gains are 2.09/2.97 and 0.99/1.27. The advantage over natural turn weighting repeats on every benchmark in each of three paired seeds (Table 10 ). Mean paired gains are $0.92\pm 0.27$ on OVO-S, $1.43\pm 0.19$ on OST, and $1.17\pm 0.25$ on UCS (points, mean $\pm$ sample standard deviation).

Comparison of answer formats. With fixed response-only readers and matched four-GPU runs, Native is higher on UCS by 2.92 points and in the two-benchmark mean (58.75 versus 58.03), while Canonical is higher on OST by 1.48 points. This crossed result shows that answer format redistributes transfer rather than improving every endpoint uniformly. We retain native targets to preserve source task semantics without an additional answer-space transformation; the control does not make native format a universally superior encoding. The canonical wrapper preserves the original answer exactly. By Corollary 1 in Appendix A , this is a lossless serialization intervention, with unchanged target information.

Comparison of training orders. Against Shifted , which keeps every turn block, target, and family weight, chronological replay is ahead in both seed pairs, by 2.42 and 2.41 OST points (mean 2.41). The shift changes both the episode origin and the context preceding each target; the result supports the temporal-origin alignment construction as a whole (RQ3). The 13.41-point OST improvement over the official base belongs to the complete recipe, while this paired ordering intervention measures a 2.41-point contribution from source chronology.

Sensitivity analysis. Over a fixed replay pool, $\alpha=0.5$ gives the highest observed OVO-S–OST mean (61.20 versus 60.92 for $\alpha=1.0$ ), and retraining on four GPUs instead of eight reproduces the three-benchmark mean within 0.34 points (RQ3; Appendix D ).

### 6 Conclusion

ChronoReplay replays heterogeneous spatial supervision in source chronology, retains native targets, and improves OVO-S, OST, and UCS together under matched observation access. We call the uneven cross-readout transfer observed in single-family training interface lock-in , an empirical pattern rather than an isolated internal mechanism. The single-family comparison retains the tradeoff: categorical-only training favors OVO-S, trajectory-only training gives little OST Overall gain, and full ChronoReplay has the highest three-benchmark mean. Objective controls show further gains beyond ordinary joint SFT. The full recipe gains 13.41 OST points over the base; the paired cyclic-shift control isolates a smaller, repeatable 2.41-point contribution from preserving source chronology as a whole. Native and canonical answer formats redistribute gains across UCS and OST rather than establishing a universally best encoding. OVO-S improves across all four levels, including +5.01 points on L4, which remains the hardest subset. These findings support ChronoReplay as a simple recipe for balanced cross-interface transfer without assigning its full gain to one component or claiming dominance on every spatial subcategory.


#### AI use statement

Generative AI was used in two roles. AI assistants supported literature search, research brainstorming, manuscript revision, code editing, debugging, and data-pipeline implementation. In upstream data preparation, multimodal teachers were also used for bounded tasks such as proposing linguistic forms or assessing whether source evidence was visible and answerable. These teachers did not define spatial ground truth, and protected evaluation labels, predictions, and scores were unavailable to data construction. Every target used by ChronoReplay is bound to a source annotation, deterministic geometry, simulator state, or an audited public label; reported results are produced by frozen benchmark scorers. The authors designed the research, checked AI-assisted code, citations, claims, and prose against source papers and versioned experimental records, and take responsibility for the complete work.


#### Ethics statement

This work uses existing research datasets and public benchmarks; it recruits no human participants and collects no new video or indoor scans. Source media may contain identifiable people, private-like interiors, or contextual information. Any artifact release therefore follows the corresponding source licenses and does not redistribute benchmark media. Canonical scene isolation prevents reported evaluation scenes and labels from entering training. Streaming spatial models may nevertheless inherit demographic, geographic, and environmental biases from their source data, and their ability to retain people, objects, and locations over time could enable surveillance or unsafe embodied decisions. We have not audited these high-stakes uses and do not claim suitability for surveillance, autonomous navigation, or other safety-critical deployment.


#### Reproducibility statement

Appendices B – D specify the evaluation contracts, replay data, scene isolation, chronological construction, optimization, and controlled comparisons. Appendix E separately specifies the fixed-parameter observation-access intervention, and Appendix I records the executable configurations, generation parameters, scorer contracts, and source manifests. Every retained trainable comparison starts independently from the same pinned public Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct revision; no intermediate checkpoint continuation or model fusion is used. Reported OVO-S, OST, and UCS scores bind model, data, protocol, prediction, and scorer identities, and all reported values are defined once as macros in main.tex and traced to versioned result records. Code, configurations, frozen training-selection manifests, and scoring scripts are available at the code repository ; the project homepage provides the paper and an overview of the released resources. Model weights and converted training annotations are available through a single Hugging Face collection . The data release preserves the frozen sample order, native targets, training weights, and supervision masks; raw media and saved predictions are not bundled. Releasing the accepted records makes reproducing the reported training mixture independent of re-querying any external AI service.

### Appendix A Theoretical Analysis

This appendix formalizes the three arguments that motivate ChronoReplay (Sections 4.2.1 , 4.2.2 , and 4.3 ). Each result is stated at the population level with an unrestricted readout, so it describes what the objective asks of the representation; it does not certify that finite-data token optimization attains it. The controls in Section 5.3 examine the empirical relevance of these properties. Throughout, $C$ is a causal prefix drawn from the replay pool, $Q$ its query, $\kappa$ the native interface, $Y^{\kappa}=A_{\kappa}(C,Q)$ the native target, $Z=F_{\theta}(C)$ the representation, and $H(\cdot\mid\cdot)$ and $I(\cdot;\cdot\mid\cdot)$ denote conditional entropy and conditional mutual information.


#### A.1 Native Readouts as Sufficiency Constraints

For any representation $F_{\theta}$ and any interface $\kappa$ , the population risk of Eq. 2 satisfies

[Tableau] $$\inf_{G_{\kappa}}\mathcal{R}_{\kappa}(F_{\theta},G_{\kappa})=H(Y^{\kappa}\mid Z,Q)=H(Y^{\kappa}\mid C,Q)+I(Y^{\kappa};C\mid Z,Q),$$ (7)

where the infimum is over all conditional distributions of $Y^{\kappa}$ given $(Z,Q)$ . For each query $q$ in the support of $Q$ , $H(Y^{\kappa}\mid Z,Q=q)=0$ implies perfect prediction from $(Z,q)$ on that conditional support.

For fixed $(Z,Q)$ , $\mathbb{E}[-\log G_{\kappa}(Y^{\kappa}\mid Z,Q)]=H(Y^{\kappa}\mid Z,Q)+\mathbb{E}\,\mathrm{KL}\big(P(Y^{\kappa}\mid Z,Q)\,\|\,G_{\kappa}(\cdot\mid Z,Q)\big)$ , and the divergence vanishes exactly when $G_{\kappa}$ equals the true conditional, which is admissible for an unrestricted readout; this gives the first equality. Since $Z$ is a function of $C$ , $H(Y^{\kappa}\mid C,Q)=H(Y^{\kappa}\mid C,Z,Q)$ , and the chain rule gives $H(Y^{\kappa}\mid Z,Q)-H(Y^{\kappa}\mid C,Z,Q)=I(Y^{\kappa};C\mid Z,Q)\geq 0$ , which is the second equality. Zero conditional entropy means that the conditional target distribution is a point mass almost surely, yielding the query-conditioned prediction statement. ∎

We use this result to motivate supervision across heterogeneous query interfaces; cross-interface transfer is evaluated empirically in Section 5 .

Let $\tilde{Y}=g(Y^{\kappa})$ , where $g$ is invertible on the target support. Then for every representation, $H(\tilde{Y}\mid Z,Q)=H(Y^{\kappa}\mid Z,Q)$ and $I(\tilde{Y};C\mid Z,Q)=I(Y^{\kappa};C\mid Z,Q)$ . The two encodings impose the same target-sufficiency requirement at the population level.

Each target is a deterministic function of the other. Conditional entropy cannot increase under either deterministic mapping, giving equality. Applying the conditional data-processing inequality in both directions likewise gives equality of the mutual information terms. ∎

Our canonical control wraps the original answer string in a JSON value field, so the target remains recoverable exactly. It tests transfer under a lossless change in output serialization. The crossed OST/UCS result in Table 4 therefore concerns finite-model training and readout behavior; it cannot be explained by a loss of target information in the wrapper. Equality at the population level does not imply identical token losses or optimization trajectories.


#### A.2 Cyclic Shifts Change the Conditioning Context

Let $O$ denote the identity of an episode’s first turn block, $C_{j}$ the legal chronological context for target $j$ , and $C^{\prime}_{j}$ its preceding context after the full episode is cyclically shifted (Appendix D.5 ). The shift preserves each block’s local content, but $C^{\prime}_{j}$ can omit earlier blocks and include blocks that originally occurred later. Knowing $O$ therefore need not recover $C_{j}$ from $C^{\prime}_{j}$ .

Let $Y=A(C_{j},Q)$ be a deterministic native target. With an unrestricted readout, the population log-loss floor under shifted conditioning is

[Tableau] $$\inf_{G}\mathbb{E}[-\log G(Y\mid C^{\prime}_{j},Q)]=H(Y\mid C^{\prime}_{j},Q)=I(Y;O\mid C^{\prime}_{j},Q)+H(Y\mid C^{\prime}_{j},O,Q).$$ (8)

This floor exceeds $H(Y\mid C_{j},Q)=0$ if, on a set of shifted inputs $(C^{\prime}_{j},Q)$ of positive probability, compatible chronological histories induce at least two distinct targets with positive conditional probability.

Expected log loss is conditional entropy plus the expected KL divergence from the true conditional distribution to $G$ . The divergence vanishes at $G=P(Y\mid C^{\prime}_{j},Q)$ , giving the first equality. The second is the chain rule for conditional mutual information. Under the stated ambiguity condition, the target distribution has positive entropy on a set of positive probability, so $H(Y\mid C^{\prime}_{j},Q)>0$ . Chronological conditioning has zero entropy because $Y$ is a function of $(C_{j},Q)$ . ∎

The residual term $H(Y\mid C^{\prime}_{j},O,Q)$ captures ambiguity that remains even when the origin is known, including ambiguity from missing history. Cyclic shifts need not satisfy the ambiguity condition: later observations introduced into a prefix may preserve or reveal the answer. The proposition therefore gives a conditional account of context corruption; it does not imply a strict penalty for every shift or determine downstream accuracy. The measured advantage of chronological replay (2.41 OST points averaged over two seeds in Table 12 ) supports preserving source order and legal causal prefixes together, without isolating a latent origin representation.


#### A.3 What Family Balance Does and Does Not Control

Let $\mathcal{L}_{P}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{T}$ be the family risks of Eq. 5 and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{replay}}=(1-\lambda)\mathcal{L}_{P}+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{T}$ with $\lambda=\alpha/(1+\alpha)$ . Then: (i) replacing $\mathcal{D}_{P}$ or $\mathcal{D}_{T}$ by a family with the same within-family empirical distribution but a different number of sequences or supervised turns leaves $\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{replay}}$ unchanged, while $\alpha$ sets the explicit ratio of the two family weights; (ii) $\mathcal{L}_{T}$ is invariant to whether the supervised transitions of an episode are grouped into one sequence or presented as separate examples, provided each transition keeps its causal prefix; (iii) any common positive rescaling of all example weights, such as the one that sets the mean weight to one (Appendix D ), leaves the minimizers of $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{replay}}$ and the direction of its gradient unchanged.

(i) Each family risk is a weighted mean, so it depends only on the empirical distribution of $(\ell,w)$ within the family and not on the number of terms; the same holds for its gradient. (ii) By Eq. 5 , $\mathcal{L}_{T}$ is the plain average of $\ell_{ij}^{T}$ over all supervised transitions $(i,j)\in\mathcal{T}$ , and each $\ell_{ij}^{T}$ is computed from the prefix $c_{ij}$ alone (Eq. 11 ); grouping changes only how the same terms are batched. (iii) A common factor $s>0$ on all weights cancels between the numerator and denominator of each family risk when applied before normalization, and multiplies the objective by $s$ when applied after; in either case the arg-min and the gradient direction are unchanged. ∎

Proposition 3 is why the family-mass sweep of Table 11 is a sweep over a single scalar: with normalization in place, changing $\alpha$ changes the relative influence of the two families and nothing else. It also makes explicit what balance does not control: coverage of states within the trajectory family still grows with episode length, and no choice of $\alpha$ recovers information that a supervision family never supplied, which is the content of Proposition 1 .


### Appendix B Evaluation and Attribution Protocol


#### B.1 Attribution Framework

Performance depends jointly on the trained model and its observation access. Let $J(\theta,\pi)$ denote benchmark performance with model parameters $\theta$ and observation policy $\pi$ . We separate the two effects as

[Tableau] $$\Delta_{\mathrm{train}}=J(\theta^{\star},\pi_{0})-J(\theta_{0},\pi_{0}),\qquad\Delta_{\mathrm{access}}=J(\theta^{\star},\pi_{1})-J(\theta^{\star},\pi_{0}),$$ (9)

where $\theta_{0}$ is the official base model, $\theta^{\star}$ is the model trained with ChronoReplay, $\pi_{0}$ is the standard observation policy, and $\pi_{1}$ is adaptive access (Appendix E ).

The first contrast changes the trained model under identical observation access. The second changes observation access while holding the model parameters fixed. The complete system evaluates $J(\theta^{\star},\pi_{1})$ , while the two controlled contrasts separate the training and access effects.

We examine design choices through matched training interventions. The origin-shift intervention preserves the same multiset of turn blocks and supervision weights while changing their episode-level chronology and the accumulated context preceding each target. Initialization and optimization settings are held fixed. The target-interface experiment changes the answer representation while preserving the causal histories and training configuration. These comparisons probe sensitivity to the corresponding design choices of ChronoReplay.

ChronoReplay combines two supervision families. The single-turn preservation pool contains 30,635 categorical and 2,545 numerical examples. Preservation sources and weights. Let $s=13{,}187/12{,}635$ . Each of the three views of a categorical pair has weight $s/3$ , and each of the remaining 3,635 categorical examples has weight $s$ . Every numerical example has weight $13{,}187/2{,}545$ . A trajectory episode with $m_{i}$ supervised turns has serialized weight $13{,}187\,m_{i}/45{,}006$ and averages its supervised turn losses. Thus the categorical, numerical, and trajectory weight sums are each 13,187, and the mean serialized-example weight is one. These are loss coefficients; they do not assert equal measured gradient norms.

The preservation and trajectory families retain their original questions and answer spaces. Categorical targets preserve candidate semantics, metric targets preserve magnitude and units, and temporal targets preserve event indices or timestamps. ChronoReplay therefore combines the two families through a shared model while maintaining the distinctions supplied by each source.


#### C.2 Episode Construction

Each trajectory is replayed in its original temporal order. At turn $j$ , the model receives the observations available by that turn and all preceding interaction:

[Tableau] $$c_{ij}=\left(h_{ij};q_{i1},y_{i1},\ldots,q_{i,j-1},y_{i,j-1}\right).$$ (10)

The current target $y_{ij}$ follows this prefix as the next assistant response. Later observations and responses remain outside $c_{ij}$ .

This construction preserves the trajectory origin and the state accumulated before each target. Object identity, observer pose, reference frame, and source units remain consistent throughout an episode. The resulting data contain 47,096 turns, comprising 45,006 supervised states and 2,090 observation turns that provide context.

Keeping an episode intact also preserves dependencies across turns. A metric displacement is interpreted relative to the preceding observer state, while a temporal answer is interpreted relative to the events already observed. Training therefore associates each target with the causal context under which its spatial meaning is defined. Algorithm 1 summarizes the construction together with the family-balanced objective of Section 4.3 .

#### C.3 Scene Separation

We separate training and evaluation by physical scene. A scene may appear under different frame rates, crops, filenames, or video encodings, so media files derived from the same scan are assigned a common scene identity. This identity is established before replay examples are constructed.

#### D.2 Loss Normalization

We normalize supervision at the answer, transition, and family levels. For an answer sequence $y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{N})$ following causal context $c$ and query $q$ , the native loss is

[Tableau] $$\ell_{\theta}(c,q,y,\kappa)=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log p_{\theta}(y_{n}\mid c,q,\kappa,y_{<n}).$$ (11)

Averaging over answer tokens gives each target a contribution normalized by its length.

For episode $i$ with $m_{i}$ supervised turns, we average the turn losses and assign the episode a weight proportional to $m_{i}$ , which is the same as giving every supervised transition one common weight within the trajectory family while retaining its complete causal context. Longer episodes therefore contribute in proportion to their number of supervised turns.

Preservation examples retain the weights supplied by their source. The total trajectory weight is then scaled to $\alpha$ times the total preservation weight, and a common rescaling sets the mean example weight to one. This implements the family risks defined in the main text.

For a fixed set of causal contexts and targets, this weighted empirical objective is invariant to grouping turns into episodes or separate examples. The grouping used during optimization determines how those contexts are presented.

#### D.5 Chronological Alignment

We examine the contribution of chronology by cyclically shifting the turn blocks within each episode. Let $b_{i}$ denote the total number of turn blocks, including turns that provide observation context. For $b_{i}>1$ , a deterministic offset $d_{i}\in\{1,\ldots,b_{i}-1\}$ defines

[Tableau] $$\sigma_{i}(j)=1+\bigl((j+d_{i}-1)\bmod b_{i}\bigr).$$ (12)

### Appendix E Adaptive Access: Temporal Observation Selection

Spatial queries differ in the history needed to answer them. Current relations can often be inferred from recent observations, whereas motion and event order depend on changes over time. Layout and historical references call for broader coverage. We use this distinction to adapt observation selection to the temporal scope of each question. The policy is fixed before evaluation and applied to the same trained model on OVO-S; OST and UCS retain their original observation protocols.


#### E.1 Matching Observations to Query Scope

Table 15 summarizes three complementary forms of temporal coverage. Local sampling concentrates on the current state. Multiscale sampling emphasizes recent changes while retaining earlier observations. Global sampling covers the complete history available at the query time.

> Table 15: Temporal coverage for different spatial queries. Frame budgets are upper limits; short histories and duplicate removal can yield fewer frames.

[Tableau] Local Multiscale Global Query scope Current state Motion and event order Layout and historical references Coverage Recent observations Last 30 s; 30–300 s; earlier history Full observed prefix Sampling Dense tail at 4 fps 60% / 30% / 10% of the frame budget Uniform Frame budget 8 128 128

Selection follows the visible question text. Named historical endpoints and persistent relative pose take priority and receive global coverage. Expressions of motion, change, or chronological order receive multiscale coverage. Explicit questions about the current state receive local coverage. Static layout and ambiguous temporal scope default to global coverage. A named historical reference therefore takes precedence over an incidental mention of the current state.

The selector uses question text alone. Answer choices, reference answers, benchmark categories, model predictions, and scores are excluded. Each question is answered once using the selected observations.


#### E.2 Sampling the Causal History

Let $t_{q}$ be the query time and $f$ the video frame rate. We retain the evaluated boundary convention $e=\max(1,\lfloor ft_{q}\rfloor)$ , with frame indices starting at zero. The benchmark timestamps place this boundary within the video.

Local and global coverage. Global sampling takes the integer floors of 128 equally spaced positions on the inclusive interval $[0,e]$ , then sorts and removes repeated indices. Local sampling uses spacing $d=\max(1,\operatorname{round}(f/4))$ and the index sequence

[Tableau] $$I_{\mathrm{local}}=\{\max(0,e-7d),\max(0,e-7d)+d,\ldots,e\},$$ (13)

retaining the final eight indices if rounding produces an extra sample.

Multiscale coverage. With budget $B=128$ , the most recent 30 seconds receive $\operatorname{round}(0.6B)$ samples. The preceding interval extending to 300 seconds before the query receives $\operatorname{round}(0.3B)$ samples, and the remaining budget covers the earlier history.

Define $s_{30}=\max(0,e-30f)$ and $s_{300}=\max(0,e-300f)$ . Let $\operatorname{Lin}(a,b,n)$ return the integer floors of $n$ equally spaced positions in $[a,b)$ . The requested indices are

[Tableau] $\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{multi}}={}$ $\displaystyle\operatorname{Lin}(s_{30},e,\operatorname{round}(0.6B))$ (14) $\displaystyle\cup\operatorname{Lin}(s_{300},s_{30},\operatorname{round}(0.3B))$ $\displaystyle\cup\operatorname{Lin}(0,s_{300},n_{\mathrm{early}}),$

where $n_{\mathrm{early}}$ is the remaining budget. Empty intervals are skipped and repeated indices are removed. Global and local sampling include the query boundary; multiscale intervals exclude their right endpoint. These conventions preserve the sampling procedure used for the reported results.


#### E.3 Contribution of Observation Selection

Adapting temporal coverage benefits both the official model and ChronoReplay. The official model improves from 48.98 to 54.08, while ChronoReplay improves from 53.89 to 57.98 under our fixed OVO-S evaluation protocol. Each comparison holds the model parameters fixed and varies the observations presented at inference.

The comparison at a similar frame budget further examines temporal placement. Uniform sampling of 81 positions from the ordered set of 128 uniform indices obtains 52.40, whereas adaptive selection obtains 57.98 using 80.50 frames on average. The advantage of 5.58 points supports matching temporal coverage to query scope at approximately the same visual cost. Table 5 separates the training and access effects using the same 12-family mean.

### Appendix H Relation-Supervision Diagnostics

This appendix reports a negative diagnostic: whether adding a more discriminative auxiliary relation interface, a binary compatibility judgement between a causal state and a typed relation claim, yields a more reusable native representation. These variants do not contribute to the headline ChronoReplay checkpoint or its training data. Relation rows are compiled from verified source relations (signed heading, metric displacement, temporal retrieval, counting, topology, and local state) paired with controlled incompatible alternatives that stay in the same value domain; positive and incompatible claims are independent continuations of one observation prefix, and only the final compatibility token receives loss. Source question, candidate order, option letter, benchmark identity, and measured score are absent from the state prefix. Within-state normalization assigns fixed mass to each causal state regardless of the number of relations $K$ ; the local $K=1$ control adds 1,920 rows over 960 states, and cross-type $K=4$ adds 7,680 rows over 960 states.

> Table 19: Overview of relation-supervision diagnostics. Native columns are complete model-only benchmark scores; Readout is the matched withheld compatibility diagnostic (%).

Cross-type supervision improves the withheld relation readout over the matched same-type control, but its native three-endpoint mean is lower. The state-path firewall, which restricts relation gradients to the visual-state route, raises UCS while reducing OVO-S and OST. Formulation controls on complete OST and the 480-row withheld readout behave the same way: removing explicit type names gives 64.45 OST and 66.67 readout, presenting candidates jointly gives 64.57 and 63.96, and removing state normalization gives 64.54 and 67.92, so each moves the local readout without improving native transfer.
