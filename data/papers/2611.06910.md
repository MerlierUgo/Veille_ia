AI agents are playing an increasingly important role in physics research. However, existing physics benchmarks only evaluate fixed questions, isolated sub-tasks, or paper reproduction with given solutions, and rarely check research quality alongside correctness. We introduce FrontierPhysics, an end-to-end benchmark with 56 tasks made by 42 physicists based on their own research, covering nine domains across theoretical, computational, and experimental physics. ### 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are evolving from passive scientific assistants into agents that take on parts of the research process. When equipped with external tools, these agents can search and summarize literature, formulate hypotheses, write and debug scientific code, and produce technical reports. Recent systems have demonstrated tool-augmented reasoning in chemistry, iterative code-based experimentation, multi-agent hypothesis generation, scientific-software optimization, and even interaction with laboratory instruments [ 30 , 6 , 29 , 37 , 17 , 4 , 33 , 41 , 20 , 22 , 18 , 31 , 15 ] . These systems point toward agents that contribute to more complete scientific workflows and projects, not only isolated subtasks [ 9 ] .

Evaluating progress toward this goal requires benchmarks that capture the open-ended, multi-stage nature of scientific research. Scientific benchmarks are accordingly shifting from textbook-level questions [ 16 , 23 , 48 , 35 , 44 ] toward expert-level closed-ended questions [ 8 ] and research-level problems [ 40 ] . Among existing research-level benchmarks, some assess scientific reasoning [ 10 , 50 , 34 , 25 , 46 ] , while others evaluate the agents’ ability to complete individual tasks within realistic scientific workflows [ 43 , 19 , 31 , 28 , 7 , 21 ] . However, these benchmarks target isolated capabilities, and because many of their tasks are closed-ended with deterministic answers, models quickly saturate them, so the benchmarks lose signal. To move toward end-to-end research, some benchmarks formulate tasks as full-paper reproductions [ 36 , 12 , 49 , 14 ] . Yet because agents are given the original publications, the research questions and solution structures are largely specified in advance, limiting their ability to evaluate open-ended scientific inquiry. More recent benchmarks address this limitation by converting published studies into research problems without revealing their solutions [ 32 , 45 , 47 , 24 ] . Nevertheless, designing and evaluating such tasks involves many subtleties that require the expertise of the original authors: for example, how to write instructions that preserve as much open-endedness as possible while avoiding false negatives, and how to determine if an agent’s failure reflects true scientific misunderstanding or a lack of expert intuition and experience. To our knowledge, no existing benchmark is fully agentic, built by physicists from their own research, graded on both paper quality and result correctness, and designed to address these subtleties through manual task review and failure-mode analysis (Table 1 ).

In this paper, we introduce FrontierPhysics (Fig. FrontierPhysics: Evaluating Agents for End-to-End Physics Research ), a benchmark evaluating whether AI agents can conduct end-to-end research across theoretical, computational, and experimental physics. FrontierPhysics tasks are designed by active physics researchers (including Ph.D. students, postdocs, and research scientists) from research projects they have personally conducted. On average, each task author has 420 citations. To preserve open-endedness while minimizing false negatives and positives, each task goes through manual general review, domain-expert peer review, and failure-mode analysis. For each task (Fig. 2 ), an agent receives a high-level prompt that resembles the instructions provided by a principal investigator (PI) at the beginning of a research project. The agent must then carry out the research and produce both a scientific manuscript and numerical outputs that can be verified deterministically. Much of this process is open-ended: the agent must investigate the topic, formulate a research plan, select appropriate methods, validate the results, and establish the significance of its findings. To evaluate these capabilities, FrontierPhysics combines a deterministic verifier (test scripts that check the numerical outputs) with rubric-based grading by reviewer agents.

#### 2.1 Task Design

Tasks are designed to reconstruct end-to-end research projects. Figure 3 b shows an example task and Appendix A gives its full instruction and proposal. Some important questions to figure out or directions to explore might be given, but no explicit methodology or solution pathways will be specified. When necessary, input data and other supporting files are provided in the task environment (Fig. 3 b) to enable the agent to configure and execute the relevant computational or experimental workflow.

Each task requires two types of final deliverables: deterministic artifacts, such as computed quantities, simulation outputs, or processed data, and a paper manuscript submitted as paper.pdf . The paper is expected to meet the standards of a peer-reviewed paper by clearly presenting the research context, methodology, results, and conclusions, while faithfully incorporating the evidence and computational artifacts produced during task execution.

The rubric-based assessment includes global rubrics and task-specific rubrics. The global rubrics evaluate behavior alignment including reward hacking [ 1 , 38 ] , hallucination and general research practices including literature synthesis, result validation, and the use of evidence to support scientific claims, whereas task-specific rubrics are developed by domain experts to assess the agent’s understanding of the underlying physics, scientific presentation, and execution of the research task. Because rubric design requires task-specific expertise, we ask each contributor to design the rubrics for their own task and research project. Depending on the task, the rubric criteria may assess whether the agent identifies and correctly uses key references, discovers the key scientific ideas through its investigation, considers realistic experimental constraints, or provided good motivations for its work in the paper.


#### 2.2 Task Curation and Quality Review


##### General quality review.

When a contributor creates a task submission, we first assess its authenticity and provenance (Fig. 3 a): the contributor must provide supporting information (e.g., reference papers or patent links) to demonstrate that they have personally conducted the project. Contributors are asked to provide the project background, a timeline of the original research process, and the amount of time they spent completing it. We require the underlying project to have involved at least two weeks of research effort. We then check that task.md and rubric.json are human-readable and contain genuine human input, rejecting tasks that appear purely AI-generated.


##### Domain peer review.

We aim to have every task reviewed by an expert in a closely related field. To scale this, we invite contributors to become domain reviewers once their own tasks pass the first-stage general quality check. Our domain reviewers have therefore all conducted research in their fields, and consist mostly of Ph.D. students (47%) and Ph.D. holders (37%). Each new reviewer is onboarded through a one-on-one meeting, and we audit the quality of their first review to align standards across reviewers. When a domain reviewer judges a task ready to merge, we perform a final check against our standards.

Each domain reviewer is provided with a domain-review checklist which covers three aspects: First, the reviewer verifies that task.md does not over-specify the problem: the prompt should not contain domain knowledge that a competent researcher is expected to know or figure out, so that the task remains as open as possible. Second, the reviewer confirms that rubric.json encodes real physics insight that only someone who has conducted the research would possess, rather than the low-signal criteria typical of AI-generated rubrics. Third, the reviewer examines failure modes after getting agents trajectories: contributors must perform at least three local runs with SOTA agents and an initial failure-mode analysis, and the reviewer inspects these traces for finding potential false positives and false negatives. Common issues include unjustified numerical tolerances that fail agents implementing a correct physical model or ambiguous instructions that lead to agents’ misunderstanding.

When all checks pass, an admin team with six physics PhD researchers from diverse subfield backgrounds approves and merges the submission into the final task set. In total, 56 tasks were accepted from 42 physics researchers from 26 institutions worldwide in 9 different physics domains out of 182 initial task submissions. A summary can be seen in Fig. 4 . For 18 tasks, the underlying research has been published in papers co-authored by the task contributors. These include four tasks based on papers published in Nature, with other journals including Nature Communications, Physical Review Letters, and Physical Review X. On average contributors report to have spent about 270 days per task for the original projects, and in total more than 8,000 hours of research.


#### 2.3 Evaluation

In each task rollout (Fig. 3 c), our evaluation scheme is designed to assess both the objective correctness of the agent’s results and the overall quality of its research. To this end, we combine programmatic deterministic verifiers with an Agent-as-judge. The deterministic verifier checks structured outputs against reference values. After the agents finish running, agent trajectory files, verifier results, and output files including paper.pdf are recorded and saved. These files are then copied into another sandbox for a reviewer agent to review and grade the trajectory files and paper.pdf based on a rubric.json . We use an agent rather than a single LLM call as the judge because many rubric criteria, such as those on reward hacking, hallucinated results, and literature search, must be checked against the agent’s trajectory, code, and output files rather than paper.pdf alone. All rollouts are graded by the same reviewer agent, GPT-5.6 Sol (xhigh) in Codex CLI. Section 3.3 checks its grades against an expert audit (Table 5 , Fig. 6 ) and on the authors’ own papers (Table 11 ). Regrading rollouts of the first evaluation round with Claude Opus 5.5 in Claude Code CLI as the reviewer agent gives the same pass/fail decision for almost all GPT-6 Astra rollouts, but the Opus 5.5 reviewer is more lenient on rollouts from its own model family (Table 6 ). Workspace files and trajectory files of the reviewer agents are collected the same way to HuggingFace datasets for later analysis.

Rubric-based grading has been widely adopted in benchmarks involving open-ended exploration or qualitative dimensions of agent performance [ 39 , 3 , 13 , 36 , 11 ] . Whereas deterministic verification establishes whether an output is correct, rubric-based evaluation assesses more aspects, for example whether the agent conducted and communicated the research well. Inspired by the rubric design of FrontierCode [ 11 ] , we divide our rubric criteria into blockers and non-blockers . Blockers are binary gates that represent requirements essential to the scientific validity of a task. Thus, an agent must satisfy every blocker as well as the deterministic verifier to pass. Non-blockers capture qualitative aspects of the work. Each non-blocker is scored on a three-level scale: 0 indicates that the requirement is absent or incorrect, 1 indicates partial satisfaction, and 2 indicates complete satisfaction. The precise anchors for these levels are defined separately for each criterion. The task author also gives every criterion an integer weight from 1 to 10 for its relative importance. Blocker weights are retained as rubric metadata but do not contribute to the numerical quality score. The final score of a task:

[Tableau] $$S_{\mathrm{final}}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}w_{i}s_{i}}{2\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}w_{i}}\times 100,&\text{if }D=1\text{ and }b_{j}=1\;\;\forall j\in\mathcal{B},\\[10.0pt] 0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}$$ (1)

Here, $\mathcal{N}$ and $\mathcal{B}$ denote non-blockers and blockers respectively, $s_{i}\in\{0,1,2\}$ , $w_{i}$ is the criterion weight, $D$ is the deterministic verifier result, and $b_{j}$ is the blocker result.

$S_{\mathrm{final}}=0$ indicates that the results are not publishable, while the value of $S_{\mathrm{final}}$ when $S_{\mathrm{final}}\neq 0$ measures the quality of the publication. High scores on non-blockers cannot compensate for an incorrect deterministic result or a failed blocker. Conversely, an agent may pass all deterministic checks while producing a poorly motivated, inadequately supported, or badly communicated manuscript. Such a submission passes the verifiers and blockers but receives a low $S_{\mathrm{final}}$ .


### 3 Experiments

We evaluate 22 frontier models in four agent harnesses. Each model attempts all 56 tasks three times at its highest available inference effort, and four GPT models are also run at xhigh.


#### 3.1 Experimental Setup

As in SkillsBench [ 27 ] and ClawsBench [ 26 ] we use BenchFlow CLI [ 5 ] , a research infra command line tool for creating RL environments, post-training, and evals, to run experiments as well as track agent trajectories, verifier, and rubrics results. We use Codex CLI for GPT models, Claude Code CLI for Claude models, Grok build for Grok models, and Antigravity CLI for Gemini models. The other seven models, DeepSeek V4 Pro, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, GLM-5.3, Kimi K3, Kimi K3.1 Pro, MiMo V2.6 Pro and Mistral Large 4, also run in Claude Code CLI. Each agent runtime is started in parallel cloud sandboxes. For tasks with resource requirements under 4 vCPUs, 16GB RAM, and 10GB storage, we use Daytona as a sandbox provider. For tasks with higher resource requirements, we use Docker in Azure VMs with Lambda API access so the model can call GPU resources as needed.

All results are for FrontierPhysics v0.1, evaluated on all 56 tasks. Where a task changed after the first evaluation round in a way that could alter a result, the affected rollouts were re-run or regraded; the other changes were judged unable to alter a result.

Each rollout has a uniform 10-hour timeout cap. Endings caused by the model, such as reaching the budget or exhausting the sandbox’s memory, are final and stay in the denominator; endings caused by the infrastructure, such as a lost sandbox or provider errors that outlast retries, are re-run. #### 3.3 Assessing Verifier and Rubric Reliability

Verifier and rubric defects may remain after review. One major source of defects is adversarial verifier or rubric engineering designed to suppress task pass rates, such that even correct solutions produced by human experts may fail. To mitigate this, we have a three stage process:

First, we ask every task contributor to do a failure-mode report of the three GPT-6 Astra rollouts, as this is the best performing model with single agent harness when the analysis started, check the agent trajectories and output files, and explain where the agent failed and why. Second, our admin team reviewers re-read these contributors’ failure-mode reports and double check them against the agent trajectories and output files. After reviewing around 60 rollouts, reviewers consolidated the recurring failures into the taxonomy in Table 4 . False negatives, where agents deliver the correct results but failed by verifier or reviewer agent, are found during this process and are labeled in Appendix E . As highlighted in Table 5 , rollouts with exclusively false failures lead to an underestimated pass rate. In total, nine of the 168 rollouts incorrectly failed. This coincides with the recent research [ 2 ] , in which domain experts regrade some leading benchmarks and find that many failures are due to grader errors, incorrect reference solutions, and ambiguous or underspecified questions. This suggests that finding false negatives takes both domain expertise and time, but is crucial for maintaining high benchmark quality.

The non-blocking rubric scores are harder to assess because they involve more subjective judgments. Third, as a sanity check of the rubric quality we asked authors to include their own papers or manuscripts as reference materials in the oracle when possible. Thirty-three tasks included these materials. For those with available reference, we selected the manuscript-level rubric criteria used for grading these materials, including blocker and non-blocker scoring criteria against the authors’ reference work. We then use the same configuration as main experiment to run reviewer agents in sandboxes to grade these files with rubrics. The authors’ own materials score 96.2% on average under corresponding rubrics (Table 11 ), showing good alignment between rubrics grading with the human expert materials.

Figure 6 shows that agents mostly do not fail because of physics mistakes (e.g., a wrong calculation, misunderstanding of the physics concepts or lacking of physics knowledge), contrary to what one might expect from a physics benchmark. Instead, they often struggle to reason rigorously (48%), apply good physical intuition when making decisions (34%), and synthesize the literature (27%). Typical rigorous thinking failures are: circular self-verification, where the agent’s own validation method depends on its wrong assumption, so it can never find out its mistakes; and edge cases, where the agent fails to consider all the edge cases of its method or code, so that it fails at some hidden tests. The third most frequent failure is in the literature. In many cases, agents fail to find the key literature for the research (as identified by the task author), struggle to synthesize information from its literature search and provide a motivation for its project, or struggle to support its argument in the manuscript with literature. Missing physics intuition is another major failure mode. Typical examples of this failure mode include: agents fail to determine the major error source of an experiment, which is not because they do not understand it, but because they do not have the expert intuition to identify what is important; when debugging, agents cannot realize the root of the problem while keeping tuning the wrong knob. Besides the top three failure modes, instruction following accounts for 26% of the failures. It could be agents missing some requirements in the instructions or misunderstanding the instructions. In many cases, the task authors believe the meaning should be clear to human domain experts, while the agent cannot get the explicit/implicit message.

The current failure mode analysis focuses on the GPT-6 Astra model (see Section 3.3 ) since it is challenging for tasks authors to manually read trajectory files with subagents involved. Other models, or other model-harness pairs, may fail in different ways, including ways outside this taxonomy. Also because of this, we can only accurately tag the false negatives of GPT-6 Astra’s rollouts and therefore correct the final pass rate of GPT-6 Astra.


### 4 Conclusion

We have introduced FrontierPhysics , a benchmark dataset with 56 tasks evaluating the ability of AI agents to conduct physics research across nine domains. FrontierPhysics shows end-to-end physics research remains challenging to current models, given that Opus 5.5 in Claude Code CLI, the configuration with the highest pass rate, passes only 46 of its 168 rollouts. Based on the human experts auditing of 168 SOTA agents rollouts trajectory and output files, we build a failure mode taxonomy and label all these 168 rollouts manually. From the taxonomy distribution, we conclude that lack of rigorous thinking (for example, circular self-verification) followed by missing expert intuition, instead of insufficient physics knowledge or execution mistakes, are the main weaknesses of frontier LLM agents.

Capable physics research agents should go beyond specified tasks to explore scientific problems independently, from problem definition to novel findings, with only high-level guidance. Training and evaluating such agents will require more open-ended research tasks and flexible evaluation frameworks that can assess exploratory reasoning, partial progress, and unexpected findings.
