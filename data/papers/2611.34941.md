## Improving and Expanding Video Editing Capabilities Without Videos

Image editing models can follow a wide range of instructions, but video editing models still lag behind, limited in part by the difficulty of collecting paired source and edited videos. Is it possible to transfer advanced image editing capabilities to video editors without new paired videos? We introduce Image-to-Video Transfer ( I2VT ), which improves pretrained video editors and expands them to new editing tasks using an image editor. It enables efficient post-training with no architectural changes and no new videos, using only individual images. Our key idea is to let the video editor role-play as an image editor : it edits an image as a one-frame video , with the image editor guiding the video editor’s predictions at each denoising step. Although the video editor sees only single images during training, at test time it applies the improved and expanded capabilities to whole video clips while preserving temporal consistency. To push the boundaries of video editing, we introduce OpenVE++ , an extension of OpenVE-Bench that adds four new editing tasks useful for video production, including action changes , expression changes , body movements , and scene relighting . Our experiments across three different video editors show that I2VT improves existing editing capabilities and expands them to new tasks, and our models achieve state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance on both OpenVE-Bench and OpenVE++.

### 1 Introduction

> Figure 2 : Motivation and intuition. Left: image editors handle complex edits such as actions and relighting on single frames, whereas video editors lack the paired data to learn them. Our goal is a video editor that makes these edits while keeping the video temporally consistent. Middle: can a video editor learn to edit images by treating each image as a one-frame video? Right: video editing ability improves while temporal quality remains comparable.

Recent image editing models have advanced rapidly and can follow detailed instructions and offer fine-grained control over a wide range of edits [ 58 , 68 , 5 , 89 , 110 , 17 , 4 , 73 , 8 ] . This progress has been supported by increasingly diverse image editing datasets [ 6 , 106 , 111 , 103 , 87 ] . However, current video editors [ 13 , 93 , 116 , 28 , 2 , 24 ] still perform only moderately well on basic edit types such as object removal. They struggle even more with edits that change a person’s actions, facial expressions, body movements, or a scene’s lighting, although video production often needs these edits [ 7 , 63 , 107 , 115 , 18 ] ( Figure 2 , left).

This gap remains even though modern image and video editors are built in similar ways: they use the same flow-matching objective and similar diffusion transformer (DiT) architectures [ 64 ] , and many recent models also share a variational autoencoder (VAE) [ 37 ] latent space [ 77 , 89 , 79 , 100 ] . One reason video editors still lag behind is training data. A good training pair for video editing must include an edited video that follows the instruction closely over the whole clip while leaving unrelated regions unchanged. Yet current data pipelines struggle to produce such videos, especially when edits become complex and diverse [ 2 , 7 , 62 ] . This raises a question: can a video editor improve its existing editing capabilities and expand them to new tasks without new paired videos?

To answer this question, we introduce Image-to-Video Transfer ( I2VT ), which efficiently improves and expands a pretrained video editor using only images instead of new paired videos. Our intuition is that an image is simply a one-frame video, so editing images well could be a foundation for editing videos well. We therefore let the video editor role-play as an image editor : it edits a source image as a one-frame video under the supervision of a frozen image editor. A direct way to give this supervision is offline distillation, which fine-tunes the video editor on the image editor’s pre-computed edit results. I2VT instead lets the image editor tell the video editor how to update its current noisy prediction at each step of its own denoising trajectory ( Figure 3 ). Although the video editor sees only single images during training, at test time it applies the improved and expanded capabilities to whole video clips while preserving temporal consistency ( Figure 1 ).

To push the boundaries of video editing, we introduce OpenVE++ , an extension of OpenVE-Bench [ 24 ] that adds four editing tasks useful for video production: Action Change , Expression Change , Body Movement , and Scene Relighting ( Figure 4 ). These tasks test whether video editors can change a person’s performance and a scene’s lighting, which video production often requires [ 7 , 63 , 115 ] . Across three video editors, our experiments show that I2VT improves basic edits and enables new tasks that their pretrained base editors barely perform, and the resulting models achieve state-of-the-art (SOTA) results among the open-source video editors we compare. Our contributions are as follows:

- $\bullet$ We propose I2VT, which improves and expands pretrained video editors by letting them role-play as image editors, without new paired videos or architecture changes.

- $\bullet$ We introduce OpenVE++ , an extension of OpenVE-Bench that adds four new editing tasks useful for video production: action changes, expression changes, body movements, and scene relighting.

- $\bullet$ We validate I2VT across three video editing models: editing capabilities improved and expanded with single images generalize to whole video clips while preserving temporal consistency, and the resulting models achieve SOTA results on OpenVE-Bench and OpenVE++.


### 2 Method

> Figure 3 : I2VT and the offline distillation baseline. Left: offline distillation fine-tunes on teacher-edited images. Middle: I2VT supervises the student along its own rollout. The cross marks stop-gradient. Right: at inference, the student edits whole video clips.

Image-to-Video Transfer (I2VT ) lets a pretrained video editor role-play as an image editor : it edits an individual image as a one-frame video , with a frozen image editor guiding its predictions at each denoising step. During fine-tuning, the video student takes one source image and an editing instruction at a time. I2VT matches the teacher’s velocity predictions on the student’s own denoising trajectory. As a baseline, we also consider offline distillation, which fine-tunes the student on source and edited image pairs prepared by the image teacher.


#### 2.1 Preliminaries

Latent Diffusion and Flow Matching. Latent diffusion models [ 70 ] use a pretrained variational autoencoder (VAE) [ 37 ] to encode an image into a clean latent $\mathbf{x}_{0}$ and decode generated latents back to images. In flow matching [ 50 , 54 ] , a noisy latent is constructed as $\mathbf{x}_{t}=(1-t)\mathbf{x}_{0}+t\epsilon$ , where $\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ and $t\in[0,1]$ , so $\mathbf{x}_{1}=\epsilon$ is the noise endpoint. The model predicts the velocity $\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}/\mathrm{d}t=\epsilon-\mathbf{x}_{0}$ , and during sampling its velocity field $\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,c)$ moves the noisy latent $\mathbf{x}_{t}$ from noise at $t=1$ toward the clean latent at $t=0$ , conditioned on an editing instruction $c$ and the source image.

Image and Video Editors. The frozen image teacher $\phi$ and the pretrained video student $\theta$ predict velocities $\phi(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,c_{\mathrm{img}})$ and $\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,c_{\mathrm{video}})$ , respectively. Each example in our training set $\mathcal{D}$ contains a source image $I_{s}$ , which the student treats as a one-frame video , and two instructions, $c_{\mathrm{img}}$ and $c_{\mathrm{video}}$ , that describe the same edit.


#### 2.2 Offline Distillation Baseline

Since an image is simply a one-frame video, a good video editor should also edit images well. We therefore first try offline distillation, in which the video editor is fine-tuned on image pairs edited by the image editor. For each training example, the image editor applies the instruction $c_{\mathrm{img}}$ to the source image $I_{s}$ and produces an edited image $I_{e}$ . We fine-tune the pretrained video editor on each source and edited image pair $(I_{s},I_{e})$ , treating both images as one-frame videos and using $c_{\mathrm{video}}$ as the editing instruction. To form the flow-matching target, we encode $I_{e}$ into a clean latent $\mathbf{x}_{0}^{e}$ and add noise at a random time $t$ to obtain $\mathbf{x}_{t}^{e}=(1-t)\mathbf{x}_{0}^{e}+t\epsilon$ , where the superscript $e$ denotes the edited target. The student learns to predict the velocity $\epsilon-\mathbf{x}_{0}^{e}$ :

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{off}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D},t,\epsilon}\big\lVert\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{e},t,c_{\mathrm{video}})-(\epsilon-\mathbf{x}_{0}^{e})\big\rVert_{2}^{2}.$$ (1)

Offline distillation requires a separate stage in which the teacher generates the edited targets. This offline approach fine-tunes the student on noised teacher outputs rather than states from its own rollouts, which may disrupt the student’s original rollout distribution [ 1 , 22 ] .

#### 2.3 I2VT

I2VT is designed to fine-tune the video student along its own denoising trajectory with supervision from an image editor: the student edits each source image as a one-frame video from noise, and the teacher guides its prediction at each step. We take advantage of the shared VAE latent space used by existing image and video models, including JoyAI-Image-Edit, Qwen-Image, Wan2.1, and Wan2.2-14B [ 77 , 89 , 80 , 83 ] , which lets the teacher predict a target velocity directly from the student’s noisy latent.

As shown in Figure 3 (middle), for each training example we initialize the student at $\mathbf{x}_{t_{K}}=\epsilon$ with $\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ and roll out $K$ denoising steps, $\mathbf{x}_{t_{K}}\to\cdots\to\mathbf{x}_{t_{0}}$ . The timesteps decrease from $t_{K}=1$ (noise) to $t_{0}=0$ (the edited image), and each step updates the state as follows:

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{x}_{t_{k-1}}=\mathbf{x}_{t_{k}}+(t_{k-1}-t_{k})\,\underbrace{\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t_{k}},t_{k},c_{\mathrm{video}})}_{\text{rollout velocity}}.$$ (2)

At each step $k$ , we feed the state $\mathbf{x}_{t_{k}}$ to the teacher, which returns a target velocity for the edited frame, and we fine-tune the student by matching its own velocity to this target:

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{I2VT}}=\lVert\underbrace{\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t_{k}},t_{k},c_{\mathrm{video}})}_{\text{student velocity}}-\mathrm{sg}(\underbrace{\phi(\mathbf{x}_{t_{k}},t_{k},c_{\mathrm{img}})}_{\text{teacher velocity}})\rVert_{2}^{2},$$ (3)

where $\mathrm{sg}$ is the stop-gradient operator, and we average the loss over the $K$ rollout steps, training examples, and noise samples. Rollout states and teacher targets are treated as constants, so gradients flow only through the student velocity.

Guidance. Image and video editors follow the instruction through classifier-free guidance (CFG) [ 26 ] . For a diffusion model $\theta$ and a guidance scale $\omega$ , the guided velocity is

[Tableau] $$\theta^{\omega}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,c)=(1-\omega)\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\varnothing)+\omega\,\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,c),$$ (4)

where $\varnothing$ denotes an empty or negative prompt. In I2VT, the rollout, student, and teacher velocities each use their own scale, $\omega_{R}$ , $\omega_{S}$ , and $\omega_{T}$ . We set all three to $1$ by default and raise the teacher guidance to $\omega_{T}=2$ where the student is weak, namely on action changes and body movements and for VideoCoF ( Section 4.2.1 ). The settings for each model and task are listed in the appendix.

Inference. At inference time, the teacher is discarded, and the student edits a source video using the sampler and the same settings as the pretrained editor. Although fine-tuned only on single frames, it generalizes the improved and expanded editing capabilities to the whole video ( Figure 6 ).


#### 2.4 Task and Training Data

We build I2VT-17K by sampling individual frames from OpenVE-3M [ 24 ] , a dataset already commonly used to train video editors [ 49 , 108 ] . We organize these images and their instructions into two pools: the OpenVE pool for basic edits and the OpenVE++ pool for four new task groups. Action Change covers head, gaze, and arm edits where the person stays in place, distinct from Body Movement , which involves whole-person movements like crouching, jumping, sitting down, standing up, and turning ( Figure 4 ). Expression Change covers facial expressions such as sadness, happiness, and anger, and Scene Relighting changes the light type and direction.

For the new tasks, a Vision-Language Model (VLM) describes the source frame so that we can choose a suitable edit and write its instruction. We accept an instruction only if the frame contains what the edit needs and does not already show the requested change, so raising an arm, for example, requires that arm to be visible and not already raised. The two instructions specify the same edit, with wording adapted to each model: $c_{\mathrm{img}}$ refers to a single image, while $c_{\mathrm{video}}$ refers to a video and may include temporal requirements such as “throughout the video.” This conversion can be automated with simple text replacements.


### 3 Effectiveness of I2VT

We apply our approach to three pretrained video editors and study three research questions:

- • Can I2VT strengthen basic editing capabilities ( Section 3.3 )?

- • Can I2VT teach video editors new editing tasks using only images ( Section 3.4 )?

- • How does online distillation compare with offline distillation ( Section 3.5 )?


#### 3.1 Experimental Setup

We evaluate I2VT on Bernini-R-1.3B [ 79 ] , Bernini-R-14B, and VideoCoF [ 100 ] , using the powerful image editor JoyAI-Image-Edit [ 77 ] as their shared teacher. The teacher and students share the 16-channel Wan 2.1 VAE latent representation [ 80 ] , and their detailed architecture descriptions are in the appendix.

We fine-tune each base editor for 200 steps with low-rank adaptation (LoRA) [ 27 ] , where each I2VT update uses $K=10$ rollout steps per image and a global batch size of $24$ , or $48$ when training jointly on both pools. Because training on single frames instead of videos reduces the number of visual tokens and lowers the cost of spatiotemporal attention, fine-tuning takes only 20 A100 GPU-hours for Bernini-R-1.3B and 20–25 B300 GPU-hours for Bernini-R-14B and VideoCoF.


#### 3.2 Evaluation Benchmarks and Metrics

Existing video editing benchmarks mainly evaluate basic edit types. Inspired by ImgEdit-Bench [ 102 ] and GEdit-Bench [ 53 ] , we introduce OpenVE++, which extends OpenVE-Bench with four new categories defined in Section 2.4 . OpenVE++ comprises 207 video–instruction pairs across four categories (42–73 per category), keeping category sizes roughly balanced and matching the per-category scale of OpenVE-Bench [ 24 ] . Each category covers diverse fine-grained edits, and all source videos are strictly disjoint between the training and evaluation splits. Figure 4 illustrates group sizes and examples for these categories.

Following the OpenVE-Bench protocol [ 24 ] , we evaluate basic editing on OpenVE-Bench and new abilities on OpenVE++. Our automatic evaluation combines two complementary dimensions: (i) Semantic and visual alignment , using Gemini 2.5 Pro [ 20 ] and Gemini 3.8 Flash to rate instruction compliance, source consistency, and visual quality, and (ii) Temporal consistency and motion dynamics , using widely adopted VBench [ 30 ] metrics (Sec. 4.3 ). Together, they jointly capture editing fidelity and motion execution. We use Gemini 2.5 Pro to score edits before and after fine-tuning and to compare distillation methods, guidance scales, and training losses. For the comparison with existing editors ( Table 3 ), we report scores from Gemini 2.5 Pro on OpenVE-Bench and from both Gemini 2.5 Pro and Gemini 3.8 Flash on OpenVE++. Evaluation details, Gemini prompts, and a user study validating alignment with human judgments are provided in the appendix.

### 5 Related Work

Early video editors adapt a text-to-image or text-to-video prior at inference time without paired editing data, through inversion [ 56 , 65 , 52 , 9 , 14 ] , attention control and sharing across frames [ 25 , 36 , 109 , 35 , 46 , 31 , 99 ] , or feature propagation [ 21 , 97 , 15 , 98 , 85 ] , and some tune the model or a video representation on each source video [ 92 , 10 , 59 ] . Learning-based editors instead fine-tune a pretrained video backbone [ 80 , 38 , 101 , 23 ] on synthetic source, instruction, and target triplets [ 13 , 93 , 116 , 28 , 2 , 24 , 108 , 100 , 48 , 113 ] or attach task adapters to it [ 33 ] , and recent unified models combine generation and editing across modalities [ 88 , 34 , 78 , 11 , 61 , 49 , 90 , 57 , 51 , 29 ] .

Image editors also help construct paired training videos by editing a source frame and using a video model to generate the edited clip [ 2 , 79 , 86 ] . However, training data still concentrate on basic edit types such as object addition, removal, replacement, and style transfer [ 2 , 24 ] , while reliably changing a subject’s actions, expressions, or body movements remains difficult.

Paired image editing data are far more abundant than paired video edits [ 6 , 106 , 111 , 103 , 87 ] , and many prior works bring them to video. One group edits the first frame with an image editor and fine-tunes an image-to-video (I2V) or video diffusion model to propagate the edit, either on each source video [ 60 ] or by training added modules on videos [ 62 , 74 , 42 ] . Another group trains on image editing pairs by repeating frames or warping them into synthetic motion [ 86 , 105 ] , uses image edit pairs as in-context examples [ 66 ] , or mixes image and video training in unified editors [ 94 , 34 , 88 , 69 , 11 , 55 ] . A third group modifies the architecture of pretrained image or video generative models, or adds adapters to them, to enable video editing [ 76 , 96 , 104 , 3 ] . In contrast, I2VT fine-tunes an existing video editor directly on single source images along its own denoising trajectory, as in on-policy distillation [ 71 , 1 , 19 , 44 , 114 , 43 ] , without constructing video targets, engineering artificial motion, or altering the core architecture.


### 6 Conclusion

We introduced I2VT, which transfers image editing capabilities to pretrained video editors using only individual source images, without paired videos or architectural changes. Across three video editors, it improves basic edits and enables new editing tasks, while outperforming offline distillation in matched comparisons. These gains carry over to whole video clips, showing that image editing supervision can improve video editing without new video training pairs.

### Appendix A Discussion


#### A.1 Relation to Other Work

Three lines of work are closest to I2VT: video editors trained from image pairs instead of videos, editors trained without edited targets, and on-policy distillation from image models into video models.

ViFeEdit [ 104 ] and ImVideoEdit [ 96 ] also learn video editing without video data. Both build a new video editor from the text-to-video model Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B by adding spatial attention modules, keeping the pretrained 3D attention frozen, and fine-tuning on synthetic image-edit pairs treated as single-frame videos, with edited targets rendered in advance by an image editor. I2VT differs from them in three ways. First, instead of building a new editor from a text-to-video model, we keep the architecture unchanged and strengthen existing instruction-based video editors with a LoRA. Second, we need no edited targets because an existing frozen image editor acts as the teacher and supervises the student’s own denoising rollout at every step. Third, while both works evaluate only appearance edits (style, replacement, color, addition, and removal, plus relighting and background replacement in ImVideoEdit) on their own test sets, we extend editing to action, expression, and body-movement edits . Because these edits change how a subject moves across frames, they demand strong temporal consistency, and we show that the temporal quality of the edited videos stays on par with that of the base editors ( Tables 8 and 9 ). We do not compare with ViFeEdit or ImVideoEdit directly, since ImVideoEdit released no code and ViFeEdit released code without trained weights.

EVE [ 76 ] also trains a video editor without supervised video data, building it from a text-to-image model and distilling from two adapters on that model, an image-editing adapter for per-frame edits and a video-generation adapter for temporal consistency. In contrast, we strengthen an existing video editor without changing its architecture, using a single frozen image editor and no second teacher , and temporal quality still stays on par with the base editors. BYG [ 81 ] uses no external teacher at all and trains flow-matching editors without paired examples from the frozen base model’s own instruction-following cues and cycle consistency, whereas our external image editor can also teach edits that the base video editor does not yet make well , such as edits that change how a subject moves. UIP2P [ 75 ] and NP-Edit [ 41 ] also train editors without edited targets, though they work only on images.

MILD [ 32 ] improves text-to-video generation with image experts for composition, aesthetics, and text rendering. Its student rolls out whole videos, and the image experts supervise slices of the video latents along that rollout. Because its image and video models use different VAEs, MILD learns connectors to map those slices into the image latent space, anchors the transferred corrections to a frozen copy of the video model, and adds an optical-flow motion reward. In contrast, I2VT rolls out single images : our video editor role-plays as an image editor and edits each source image as a one-frame video, the same input the image editor receives. Since the teacher and the students share a VAE latent space, we need no connectors, and training renders no multi-frame videos.


#### A.2 Constructing Paired Video Edits

An editing pair needs a source video and a second video that differs from it only in the requested edit. Such pairs almost never occur naturally [ 13 , 62 ] , so existing datasets synthesize them at a high cost [ 2 ] and mostly for appearance edits such as addition, removal, replacement, and style [ 24 , 7 ] . Motion edits are harder still, because the target must change how a subject moves while the rest of the scene, the camera, and the timing follow the source.

A common shortcut edits one frame with an image editor and lets an image-to-video model generate the rest [ 40 , 60 ] . These methods steer the image-to-video model with inverted source features [ 40 ] or per-video motion LoRAs [ 60 ] , and where this guidance is weak, the model regenerates the motion instead of keeping the source motion. Content that is not visible in the first frame is hallucinated [ 7 ] , the result drifts from the source dynamics under camera or object motion [ 62 ] , and the quality of each pair is capped by the propagation model [ 2 ] . Goku [ 47 ] builds part of its Subject Movement data in this way: an image editor moves the subject in the first frame, and a video model generates the clip from it. For action changes, the same video model generates both the source and the target from two text descriptions, so the source is itself synthetic, which transfers poorly to real videos [ 93 ] . Its filter discards about 88% of the generated samples, yet the released targets still do not follow the source motion ( Figure S1 ). Asked to lower a waving hand, the target lowers it and then freezes the person, and asked to turn the torso by 20 degrees, it replaces the whole dance. I2VT builds no video targets: the student learns from single images and relies on the pretrained video editor for motion.

### Appendix B Implementation Details

> Algorithm S1 One training step of I2VT

Bernini-R [ 79 ] adopts the Wan architecture [ 80 ] , including the Wan VAE and the UMT5 text encoder. Bernini-R-1.3B follows Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B, and Bernini-R-14B follows the two-expert layout of Wan2.2-14B, which denoises high noise levels with one expert and low noise levels with the other. To edit a video, Bernini-R places the source and target latents in one token sequence and gives the two segments different rotary position embeddings. It reads the instruction through cross-attention and denoises only the target tokens. At inference, Bernini-R uses adaptive projected guidance (APG) [ 72 ] with separate scales for the source video ( $1.25$ ), the image ( $4.5$ ), and the text ( $4.0$ ).

VideoCoF [ 100 ] adapts Wan2.1-T2V-14B [ 80 ] with a LoRA for instruction-based editing without masks. It arranges the source frames, a reasoning frame, and the edited frames in one sequence along the temporal axis and reads the instruction through cross-attention. The reasoning frame marks the region to edit, so the model first predicts where to edit and then generates the edited frames. Its rotary position embeddings align the edited frames with the source frames in time, which keeps the source motion and lets VideoCoF edit videos longer than its training clips. Following its official inference, VideoCoF samples in four steps with the FusionX acceleration LoRA for Wan2.1-T2V-14B [ 82 ] .

The student generates the reasoning and edited frames together, but the loss supervises only the edited frame. In the loss equations, states and velocities therefore refer to the edited frame, whereas the rollout advances all generated frames.

During each I2VT training step ( Algorithm S1 ), we use the Euler update with $K=10$ denoising steps on a normalized schedule $1=t_{K}>\cdots>t_{0}=0$ and a rollout time shift of $5$ (Appendix F compares other rollout lengths and time shifts). At inference, each editor instead uses its official sampler and settings ( Table S1 ).

The teacher is JoyAI-Image-Edit [ 77 ] , used frozen and without modification. It receives the image-edit instruction and the student’s edited-frame latents and returns a velocity on those latents. For CFG, the teacher uses an empty instruction and the video student uses its pretrained negative prompt, while both retain the source conditioning. When $\omega_{T}>1$ , the teacher rescales its guided velocity to the norm of its conditional velocity, as the JoyAI-Image-Edit sampler does.

We build two training pools, the OpenVE pool and the OpenVE++ pool ( Table S2 ). Their union, I2VT-17K, trains I2VT-1.3B, I2VT-14B, and I2VT-CoF in Table 3 . Every source frame is the first frame of a clip from OpenVE-3M [ 24 ] , at the clip’s resolution (mostly $1280\times 720$ ). The videos are distributed as a split archive, so we take frames by streaming the archive and decoding each clip’s first frame in memory. Before building a pool, we place every clip already used by an earlier pool, by OpenVE-Bench, or by OpenVE++ on an exclusion list. We use each remaining clip at most once within a pool, so no source appears under two instructions.

The OpenVE++ pool covers the four new task groups. We build its action, expression, and body-movement data in four stages. (1) We extract frames from clips not on the exclusion list. (2) A VLM (Qwen3.5-27B [ 67 ] ) describes each frame once, including the people in shot and their pose, gaze, expression, and visible arms. (3) A frame is eligible for an edit if it shows one person with a visible face and upper body, does not already show the edit, and contains what the edit needs, such as the arm to be raised. (4) We assign frames to the edits with the fewest eligible frames first to balance the pool. We state directions in frame terms (“the arm on the left side of the frame”), because “turn to the left” leaves open whose left is meant and the teacher resolved it inconsistently. The data cover 15 action and expression edits and five body-movement edits. We leave out turning to face the camera, for which only three frames were eligible.

The relighting data of the OpenVE++ pool ( $2{,}590$ images) ask for a scene light, such as window sunlight, or a colored light, from one of up to ten directions, including front and back light. Most of the 35 light types in the Scene Relighting group of OpenVE++ also appear in training. For most frames, the VLM first classifies the scene (indoor or outdoor, whether a window, water, or street is in shot, and the current light). We then assign only lights that are plausible for the scene and would change the image, so window sunlight goes only to indoor scenes with a window. The student’s instruction ends with a clause asking to preserve the scene content, motion, and camera, whereas the teacher’s instruction omits motion.

The OpenVE pool takes 6,026 source frames from the OpenVE-3M training split in seven categories ( Table S2 ). For the six categories that the training split covers, it keeps each clip’s original instruction as the student’s video-edit instruction. The training split has no Creative Edit category, so we build its 1,326 rows with the same pipeline as the OpenVE++ pool: a VLM describes each frame and proposes a creative edit that suits it, written as separate instructions for the teacher and the student.

> Figure S4 : Teacher guidance for offline distillation targets. JoyAI-Image-Edit edits training frames at guidance $1$ , $2$ , and $5$ . Instructions are abridged.

The teacher renders one edit per source image with $50$ sampling steps and guidance $2$ for every offline baseline. We chose guidance $2$ after rendering training frames at guidance $1$ , $2$ , and $5$ with the same $50$ steps ( Figure S4 ). At guidance $1$ , the teacher can leave an edit undone, such as a requested frown or head turn. At guidance $5$ , it over-edits by exaggerating expressions, removing objects the instruction does not mention, or moving the camera in background changes. Guidance $2$ is a compromise between these two failure modes and gives stable edits, completing all three examples in Figure S4 without these side effects. The teacher resizes its input to a fixed aspect bucket and center-crops what does not fit, so we crop the source to that bucket first and train the student against the cropped source, which keeps source and target aligned.

#### F.2 Matching Losses

We compare mse and cos losses on velocity predictions ( $\mathbf{v}$ ) and predicted clean latents ( $\mathbf{x}$ ), using Bernini-R-1.3B on the OpenVE pool with $\omega_{R}=\omega_{S}=\omega_{T}=1$ , and report the five-category comparison in Table 7 .

Writing $\mathbf{v}^{S}_{k}$ and $\mathbf{v}^{T}_{k}$ for the student and teacher velocities in Equation 3 , at the same rollout state $\mathbf{x}_{t_{k}}$ the student and teacher predict clean latents as

[Tableau] $$\begin{gathered}\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{S}_{0,k}=\mathbf{x}_{t_{k}}-t_{k}\mathbf{v}^{S}_{k},\\ \hat{\mathbf{x}}^{T}_{0,k}=\mathrm{sg}(\mathbf{x}_{t_{k}}-t_{k}\mathbf{v}^{T}_{k}).\end{gathered}$$ (S1)

We apply either distance below to the velocity pair $(\mathbf{v}^{S}_{k},\mathrm{sg}(\mathbf{v}^{T}_{k}))$ or the clean-latent pair $(\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{S}_{0,k},\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{T}_{0,k})$ :

[Tableau] $$\begin{gathered}\ell_{\mathrm{mse}}(a,b)=\frac{1}{NC}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lVert a_{j}-b_{j}\rVert_{2}^{2},\\ \ell_{\mathrm{cos}}(a,b)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(1-\frac{\langle a_{j},b_{j}\rangle}{\lVert a_{j}\rVert_{2}\lVert b_{j}\rVert_{2}}\right).\end{gathered}$$ (S2)

Here $N$ is the number of latent positions and $C$ the channel dimension, with losses averaged over rollout states as in Equation 3 .

Because the two predictions share the state $\mathbf{x}_{t_{k}}$ , the clean-latent difference equals $-t_{k}$ times the velocity difference. A change of loss space thus effectively reweights the loss [ 45 ] : the clean-latent mse is a reweighted form of the velocity mse, with weight $t_{k}^{2}$ at step $k$ . The comparison between $v$ and $x$ therefore changes both the matched quantity and the weighting across rollout steps.

### Appendix I Evaluation Details

We follow the OpenVE-Bench protocol [ 24 ] , whose authors report close agreement with human judgment. For each edit, the Gemini judge receives the source video followed by the edited video as two video-file inputs, with a category-specific scoring prompt supplied as the system instruction. We upload the complete videos without local frame extraction and leave the temperature at the API default for the Gemini 2.5 Pro runs.

The prompts, including those for OpenVE++ in Appendix J , rate instruction compliance, consistency with the source, and visual quality from 1 to 5 [ 39 , 112 ] . They penalize flickering and other temporal artifacts according to the category-specific rubric, and instruct the evaluator not to assign consistency or quality scores above instruction compliance. We average the three ratings for each clip, then average clip scores within each category and category means for Overall.

For comparisons with published editors, we report five OpenVE-Bench categories: Global Style , Local Change , Background Change , Local Remove , and Local Add , following the five-category comparison of prior work such as Kiwi-Edit [ 49 ] and JoyAI-Video-Edit [ 95 ] .

For the unmarked rows of existing editors in Table 3 , we take VACE, OpenVE-Edit, ICVE, Ditto, and Lucy-Edit scores from the OpenVE-3M table “Quantitative Comparison on OpenVE-Bench with Gemini 2.5 Pro” (Table 3 of arXiv v2) [ 24 ] , and Kiwi-Edit scores from Table 2 of Kiwi-Edit [ 49 ] . VInO scores come from its Table 7 [ 11 ] and LoomVideo scores from its OpenVE-Bench comparison [ 90 ] . We count VInO as 17B, its 13B HunyuanVideo generation backbone plus its Qwen3-VL-4B encoder [ 11 ] . We map columns by category name and recompute Overall as the mean of the five reported categories. We evaluate the starred rows (Bernini-R-1.3B, VideoCoF, Bernini-R-14B, and JoyAI-Video-Edit) with this OpenVE-Bench protocol, running each with the default inference settings of its official repository.
