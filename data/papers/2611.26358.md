#### 1 Introduction

We are introducing Beam, Reflection’s first open-weight model. Beam is a sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model with 501.3B total parameters and 23.4B active parameters across 52 layers, 256 experts, and a 152K vocabulary size, specifically built and optimized for coding, reasoning, and agentic workloads.

Beam’s capabilities stem from major investments across pretraining, midtraining, post-training, and infrastructure. Beam advances the Western open-weight frontier and achieves competitive performance with larger models like GLM-5.2 while approaching Qwen 3.8-Max on LongBench-v2, while delivering frontier-level reasoning, while requiring 3-4x less inference compute than many models of its class.

The foundation of Beam’s efficiency and numerical stability lies in rigorous data curation and controlled scaling. Pretrained on 23.8T diverse, curated, high-quality tokens—including high-value STEM, math, and code tokens preserved via novel domain-specific curation pipelines and internet-scale OCR—Beam’s scaling held predictably across four orders of magnitude in pretraining compute, training models from 300M to 23.4B active parameters. Beam’s base architecture employs auxiliary-loss-free load balancing with cosine decay, sequence-level balancing, and depth-based residual scaling to achieve near-uniform expert utilization and bounded residual norms. It was pretrained end-to-end in under four weeks on 6,144 NVIDIA GB300 GPUs with a 92.3% goodput toward the end and 21% MFU during steady-state training, after accounting for restarts.

To bridge foundational knowledge and agentic execution, a novel two-stage midtraining pipeline expanded Beam’s effective context length to 1M tokens on correctness-anchored data without degrading general capabilities. Post-training was centered around a high-compute reinforcement learning (RL) campaign, where our high-compute RL run generated over 100M rollouts on 10.5K NVIDIA GB300 GPUs over four weeks of training. We believe this is one of the largest scale RL runs conducted by any open lab to date, enabled by a fully asynchronous platform with 110K concurrent rollouts, fast 12-second weight updates, and 99.99% dynamic batch packing, Beam organically developed tool-use, web browsing, and problem-decomposition capabilities. Across our evaluation suite, capabilities continued to improve as we increased RL compute, with no sign of a plateau. For serving, Beam leverages hardware-model co-design, FP8/NVFP4 quantization, and DFlash 2 (a 1.93B-parameter speculative decoding drafter) to reduce memory footprint and maximize throughput.

Safety and alignment were prioritized across the entire model development cycle. Pretraining filtered harmful content and PII, while post-training combined deliberative alignment SFT with context-aware RL across multi-turn, jailbreak, and agentic tasks. Dedicated safety and RL teacher capabilities were merged using multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD). Under Reflection’s Open Safety Framework (OSF), we assessed Beam as Limited across all four systemic-risk categories: Biological Weapons, Chemical Weapons, Loss of Control, and Cyber Offense. A Limited rating means there is no material shift in the offense-defense balance attributable to the model and no material expansion of the threat landscape relative to comparable open-weight models. These findings are complemented by external third-party assurance evaluations. In the Iris Safety Evaluations (ISE) benchmarks we developed in-house to span single-turn, multi-turn, jailbreak, and agentic evaluations, Beam achieves an 88.9% refusal/pivot rate on harmful requests while complying with 81.5% of harmless requests. Scaling the alignment teacher from 100B to 501B parameters cut false-positive over-refusals by 48.7%, proving greater model capacity can substantially improve responses to permissible requests under the same safety-training setup.

##### 2.1 Recipe

Beam is a decoder-only sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Transformer [ 1 , 2 ] , designed for efficient long-horizon agentic reasoning. Beam leverages SandwichNorm [ 3 , 4 , 5 ] , Group Query Attention (GQA) [ 6 ] with QK normalization [ 7 ] , interleaved local and global attention [ 8 , 4 ] , Rotary Embeddings [ 9 ] , SwiGLU [ 10 ] experts, and DeepSeek MoE-style [ 11 ] auxiliary-loss-free load balancing. We used RMSNorm [ 12 ] for all normalization layers, and used untied input/output embeddings. We applied soft capping to cap the final output logits at $30$ [ 13 ] . #### Attention

We adopted an interleaved local-global attention pattern [ 4 ] with a local-to-global ratio of 5:1; local attention layers use a sliding window size of 512 tokens. To further reduce the KV-cache footprint, we employed GQA, coupling 48 query heads with 8 key-value heads at a head dimension of 128 [ 6 ] . We use RoPE [ 9 ] with a base frequency of 10,000 in local attention layers, and NoPE [ 14 ] in global attention layers.

To improve attention stability, we leveraged QK normalization [ 7 ] . While removing the learnable gain parameters from QK normalization’s RMSNorms strictly bounds the attention logits, we observe that doing so degrades long-context extrapolation, and so we retain them. We observed the model was susceptible to significant activation outliers after the attention output projection even when using QK normalization, so we adopted elementwise gated attention [ 15 ] . We compute a gate vector $\mathbf{g}_{h}$ for each attention head $h$ as:

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{g}_{h}=\sigma(\mathbf{G}_{h}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}_{h}),$$ (2.1)

where $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ is the RMSNormed input to the self-attention sublayer, $\mathbf{G}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{h}\times d},\mathbf{b}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{h}}$ are trainable parameters, and $d$ and $d_{h}$ are model and head dimension, respectively. We effectively suppress the attention stream by initializing the bias vector to -2.5 and initializing the gating matrices to be very small. Our preliminary experiments with headwise gated attention [ 16 ] and attention sinks [ 17 ] were less effective in either reducing gradient spikes or in promoting long-context modeling.

To improve length extrapolation, we followed the scalable softmax approach of Nakanishi [18] , which scales the attention logits of each query based on its absolute position. To improve stability, we used the following root-compression formulation to scale logits:

[Tableau] $$\left(1+\frac{p}{2048}\right)^{0.1},$$ (2.2)

where $p$ is the position index, rather than the formulation used by Nakanishi [18] .


#### Mixture of Experts

Our MoE architecture largely follows that of DeepSeek-AI et al. [11] , with the exception that we did not use shared experts. We leveraged a top-k router with sigmoid gating. Expert biases were added to the router logits for top-k selection. The router probability is computed from the unbiased logits. We normalized routing scores before combining the expert outputs.

To ensure balanced expert utilization, we adopt the auxiliary-loss-free load balancing method of Wang et al. [19] with additional sequence auxiliary loss. To improve expert balance even when exposing the model to datasets not drawn from the pretraining data distribution, we used a relatively high sequence auxiliary loss coefficient of $5\times 10^{-4}$ . To avoid router logit saturation, we added a router z-loss term with a coefficient of $10^{-5}$ . We used an expert bias update rate of $10^{-3}$ .

Despite the above mechanisms, MoE routing can become unstable at large scale, particularly later in training. We opined that it is detrimental to use a constant learning rate to update the expert bias once the routing distribution has largely converged, when learning may be more sensitive to routing outliers. With that, we replaced the constant expert bias update with an adaptive update schedule:

[Tableau] $$\eta_{\mathrm{bias}}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\eta_{\mathrm{bias}}^{\max}\left[1+\cos\left(\pi\max\left(0,\frac{t-T_{w}}{T-T_{w}}\right)\right)\right]$$ (2.3)

where $\eta_{\mathrm{bias}}^{\max}$ is the maximum learning rate ( $10^{-3}$ in our case), $T$ and $T_{w}$ are the total training and warm-up steps, respectively. The above scheme uses a constant expert bias update during the warm-up period to ensure early routing convergence. Then it follows that the cosine learning rate decays to 0. This simple change significantly improves routing stability, and generalizes to models at Beam’s scale.

#### Residual Stability

At a large scale, the residual stream can suffer from unbounded magnitude growth and activation outliers. Below we describe several interventions to prevent blowup. We note that we computed the entire residual stream in FP32, by upcasting sublayer outputs before adding them.

To keep activations within a reasonable range, we apply SandwichNorm to all sublayers [ 3 , 20 , 4 , 5 ] ; SandwichNorm normalizes both the input to and output from each sub-layer. In this way, the residual activations are decoupled from the internal dynamics of each sub-layer and largely depend on SandwichNorm’s output normalization. We initialize SandwichNorm’s output gains to $\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}$ , where $L$ is the total number of layers [ 21 ] , and input gains to $1.0$ , which we found to be the most empirically effective.

In pre-normalization architectures, including SandwichNorm, the residual stream $\mathbf{x}_{n}$ is effectively a direct summation of all preceding sublayer outputs $\mathbf{h}_{t}$ (e.g., attention, MLP, MoE): $\mathbf{x}_{n}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbf{h}_{t}$ , and so the activation magnitude inevitably increases with network depth.

To prevent the residual stream from becoming too large, we proposed a depth-scaled residual connection (DSRes) , which rescales the stream by 1 over the square root of the number of accumulated sublayers:

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{x}_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbf{h}_{t}$$ (2.4)

Assuming the $\mathbf{h}_{t}$ are independent (i.e., $\mathbf{h}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{h}_{j}=0$ ) and that $\text{RMS}(\mathbf{h}_{t})=c$ , this formulation ensures that $\text{RMS}(\mathbf{x}_{n})\approx c$ , preserving the activation norm across depth.

DSRes has two desirable properties:

- • Scale-Invariant Drop-In Replacement: Because RMSNorm is approximately scaling invariant (i.e., $\text{RMSNorm}(\mathbf{x}\cdot c)\approx\text{RMSNorm}(\mathbf{x})$ ), DSRes preserves the input distribution or the subsequent sublayer outputs in pre-normalization models.

- • Efficient Recurrent Formulation: DSRes can be computed in a recurrent way:

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{x}_{n}=\frac{\sqrt{n-1}}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbf{x}_{n-1}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbf{h}_{n}$$ (2.5)

The above simplifies into a weighted sum of the previous residual stream and the current branch output, and avoids materializing the direct summation completely. Note that the index $n$ indexes sub-layers (e.g., attention, MoE) rather than full transformer layers.

Empirically, we observe that the activation norm in early layers can be quite small due to the depth-scaled gain initialization in SandwichNorm, but that this does not interfere with learning.


#### Initialization

Specific initialization schemes for the gains in the output RMSNorms of SandwichNorm and for gated attention parameters were described above. Linear layers were initialized with standard fan-in initialization, RMSNorm gains were initialized with 1s, and input embeddings were initialized from a normal distribution with standard deviation equal to $1/\sqrt{d}$ , where $d$ is the hidden dimension.


#### Hyperparameters and Optimization

For optimization we used the AdamC algorithm [ 22 ] and a linear warm-up plus cosine decay learning rate schedule, for more details see Appendix B . We use decoupled weight decay [ 23 ] , with the weight decay term following the squared warm-up and cosine decay schedule reaching zero at the end of training. We apply weight decay to all parameters except biases and RMSNorm gains. The AdamC weight decay schedule correction is used for all regularized parameters, not only for parameters followed by normalization layers.

#### Scaling Ladder and Ablations

Pretraining ablations are conducted by testing a modeling or data intervention on each tier of a “ladder” of increasingly large model configurations. We set up a scaling ladder [ 24 ] where every tier is trained at a constant number of tokens per (active) parameter (TPP) for any ablation. We maintain a constant aspect ratio (width-to-depth) throughout the ladder. The ladder tiers contain model configurations ranging from approximately 300M to 6B before the target tier of 23B active parameters. In practice, we have found little signal in configurations with fewer than 0.5B active parameters.

After fitting a FLOPs-to-validation-loss scaling law to a baseline model’s performance at each tier, we can determine whether an intervention has increased compute efficiency by calculating the number of FLOPs necessary to attain the intervention’s loss under the baseline scaling law [ 24 ] . We accept the intervention if it reliably increases compute efficiency across tiers (i.e., it results in validation losses that would require significantly more FLOPs under the baseline scaling law), and does not hurt model stability or downstream evaluations.

Compute efficiency (CE) is the ratio between the compute the baseline would require to achieve the intervention’s validation loss and the compute used by the intervention. For each validation dataset, we fit a power law to the baseline ladder, $L_{\mathrm{base}}(C)=aC^{b}+c$ , where $C$ is training compute in FLOPs, $a>0$ is the scaling coefficient, $b<0$ is the scaling exponent, and $c$ is the fitted asymptotic loss. For an intervention achieving loss $L>c$ with compute $C$ , its compute efficiency is:

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{CE}(C,L)=\frac{L_{\mathrm{base}}^{-1}(L)}{C}=\frac{1}{C}\left(\frac{L-c}{a}\right)^{1/b}.$$ (2.6)

Where $CE>1$ indicates an improvement over the fitted baseline. In practice, we found that a statistically significant change in either direction must register a CE change above 5% to cancel training-seed noise and sampling noise.

Crucially, every pretraining recipe intervention must be ablated on the scaling ladder to measure its compute efficiency trend. We applied this same standard to ML recipe changes, data mixture changes, and numerics changes. Periodically, we bundled many proposals and trained the next-largest tier of the ladder, establishing a new baseline if the scaling projection passed validation. This allowed us to discover interactions between data, recipe, and implementation early; re-establish baselines for fast iteration; and collect intermediate feedback on the impact of the latest pretraining recipe on the midtraining and post-training workflows.

For scaling-ladder ablations to predict improvements at the target scale, smaller tiers must reflect the data constraints of the final pretraining run. The target run repeats some datasets many times to pad its token horizon and re-expose the model to selected valuable training data. In the ladder, smaller model tiers are trained on shorter horizons, and scaling down the mixture naïvely would make them train on unique tokens instead. This setup would not accurately model the effect of data repetition in the target model, and would not project its effect across the scaling ladder tiers.

Following Muennighoff et al. [25] , we applied the procedure we coined data-limited subsampling (DLS) to include these effects in the scaling ladder. For each tier, we reduced the available data in proportion to the number of training tokens. This kept the number of passes through each source approximately equal to that of the final training run. This makes the tradeoff between data quality and quantity visible throughout the ladder and helps distinguish improvements that transfer to the final training regime from those that depend on access to fresh data.


#### Scaling Laws

As our ladder varies the active parameters and the token horizon, we fit a scaling law to predict the peak learning rate and weight decay for each ladder tier. We used a multiplicative form following Bjorck et al. [26] , which suggests that the active parameters, batch size, and token horizon dependencies can be isolated. Our peak learning rate and peak weight decay scaling law takes the functional form:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle\eta^{*}_{\max}(N,D,B)$ $\displaystyle=C_{\eta}\,N^{-\alpha_{\eta}}\,D^{-\beta_{\eta}}\,B^{\gamma_{\eta}},$ (2.7) $\displaystyle\lambda^{*}_{\max}(N,D,B)$ $\displaystyle=C_{\lambda}\,N^{-\alpha_{\lambda}}\,D^{-\beta_{\lambda}}\,B^{\gamma_{\lambda}}$

Here, $N$ is the number of active non-embedding parameters, $D$ the token horizon, and $B$ the batch size. We keep $B$ explicit because it varies across our ladder. For $\gamma_{\eta}$ we use the exponent $0.5$ following e.g. Malladi et al. [27] , and for $\beta_{\eta}$ we use $0.32$ following Bjorck et al. [26] . To keep the effective learning rate constant across parameters, when varying the batch size and the token horizon, we set $\lambda\propto\eta^{2}$ to fix the exponents $\gamma_{\lambda}$ and $\beta_{\lambda}$ to $1.0$ and $0.64$ , respectively. With these exponents fixed, the constant term and the exponent for the model size, namely, $C_{\eta}$ , $C_{\lambda}$ , $\alpha_{\eta}$ , $\alpha_{\gamma}$ , are the only remaining degrees of freedom. We fit these four terms by using linear regression data collected from extensive hyperparameter sweeps conducted at the lower tiers of our ladder. The tuning procedure is detailed in Appendix A , with scaling results in Figure 72 .

We validated our scaling recipe on the ladder using diverse in-house code and web validation sets, with all training data decontaminated against them. As shown in Figure 3 , the recipe scales predictably across four orders of magnitude in compute: Beam Base matched its predicted performance and matched or outperformed the comparable open-source base models evaluated.

Our web data is sourced from a mixture of third-party providers and a high-value subset of Common Crawl, comprising 245B raw HTML pages. We parsed each page with a proprietary parser that preserves mathematical and code content that open-source parsers not optimized for pretraining use cases often corrupt or drop. We applied exact deduplication using an MD5 hash of the parsed text, resulting in 97B surviving documents. We then enriched each document with heuristic quality signals and learned classifier scores that support flexible selection throughout the downstream pipeline. The initial set of quality signals is listed in Appendix C .

MassiveWeb-style heuristic filters are commonly applied near the start of web-data curation pipelines [ 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 ] . At their canonical thresholds, however, we found that they remove substantial amounts of high-quality technical content. We instead partitioned the pool by domain and apply domain-specific filtering pipelines.

We partitioned the pool into five domains: math, code, STEM, English, and non-English. We trained three lightweight classifiers [ 33 , 34 ] to score each document’s relevance to the technical domains. To maintain high precision across 97B diverse, unfiltered web documents, we iteratively refined them using examples where their predictions disagreed with an internally configured judge models.

We routed documents through a fixed-priority cascade (math, code, then STEM), assigning each to the first domain whose score exceeded its threshold. We split the remaining documents into English and non-English partitions using fastText’s lid-176 language identifier. Each partition was then fuzzy-deduplicated against itself using the deduplication pipeline described in Section 2.2.4 . Table 4 reports token volume before and after deduplication. We omit the non-English partition from the discussion because it was processed separately using similar curation procedures.

To test whether domain partitioning preserves technical data that a standard unpartitioned pipeline would discard, we applied the canonical MassiveWeb filters to the curated technical datasets in our final mixture. Across web-STEM, web-code, and web-math, these filters would remove 80–88% of tokens in each domain, totaling roughly 1.8T tokens, or 13% of the mixture’s 13.73T unique tokens.

Every filtering decision trades corpus quality against token volume ( Figure 5 ). For a filter $F$ applied to a pool $D$ , let $r_{\mathrm{tok}}(F)$ denote the fraction of token mass removed and let $\bar{q}_{\mathrm{tok}}(S)$ denote the token-weighted mean judge score of subset $S$ . We define filter efficiency as

[Tableau] $$e(F)=\frac{\bar{q}_{\mathrm{tok}}(D_{\mathrm{keep}})-\bar{q}_{\mathrm{tok}}(D)}{r_{\mathrm{tok}}(F)}\,.$$ (2.8)

Random removal has expected efficiency zero, while removing documents in increasing judge-score order traces the oracle envelope.

To obtain these scores, we used an internally configured judge models with a handwritten, domain-specific prompt to score a uniform random sample of thousands of documents per domain from 0–4, similar to FineWeb-Edu [ 31 ] .

To discover filters for each domain, we began with basic document-length and mean-word-length filters, then extended the pipeline through the following iterative search-and-review loop:

- 1. Enumerate one- and two-condition rules over a quantile grid of continuous signals and the discrete labels of categorical signals, then measure each rule’s marginal token-weighted efficiency on the current survivors.

- 2. Inspect the documents that each leading candidate would remove and retain, then select a rule, sometimes adjusting its threshold or choosing a candidate below the top-ranked rule.

- 3. Apply the selected rule and remove the matched documents from the active sample.

- 4. Repeat until the marginal efficiency of the remaining candidates no longer justifies further removal.

The resulting domain-specific heuristic pipelines reduced total unique token volume to 19.5T, excluding the non-English partition. We then trained English web, STEM, and code quality classifiers and used them in a second filtering stage. English and STEM applied a threshold to their quality scores; code applied compound rules whose heuristic conditions fired only when a quality score was also low; math received no classifier-based filtering.

Our downstream quality-aware upsampling procedure assigns different weights to fine-grained quality tiers, thus the classifier must rank documents reliably across its full score range rather than merely separate high- from low-quality data. We first evaluated two widely used open-source classifiers, DCLM [ 30 ] and FineWeb-Edu [ 31 ] , on the hand-curated binary evaluation in Table 5 . They obtained AUCs of 0.65 and 0.52, respectively. Agreement with human judgments does not necessarily predict downstream compute efficiency, so we subsequently evaluated each classifier on both judgment agreement and data ablations; their weak agreement nonetheless motivated training our own.

We used downstream data ablations as the primary evaluation, supported by three cheaper classifier-level evaluations:

- 1. Data Ablation (Primary): We used each classifier to partition a fixed English web corpus into token-weighted quality tiers, apply a common exponential quality upsampling schedule matched to the final design’s mean repeat rate and effective-token budget, and train a scaling ladder. Compute-efficiency differences isolated the ranking induced by the classifier.

- 2. Natural Holdout: We report Keep-AUC, the ROC-AUC for predicting the judge’s binary keep label, on a sample of documents from the natural web distribution excluded from classifier training.

- 3. Binary Evaluation: An small, adversarial set of web documents with unambiguous binary quality labels, hand-selected to probe specific failure modes (e.g. well-written documents on taboo subject matter).

- 4. Ordinal Evaluation: A pairwise-comparison tournament, run by an internally configured “oracle” judge model, produced a reference ranking over roughly 100 held-out documents; classifiers are scored by rank agreement (Spearman) against it.

We developed a binary quality judge using a strong LLM and a handwritten prompt. After tuning primarily against external development examples to limit overfitting to the binary evaluation, the final judge reached a Cohen’s kappa of 0.82 on that evaluation. We then labeled several hundred thousand documents selected from a 522M-document sample using weighted reservoir sampling over document format, topic, and a “weak” quality score from a prior internal quality classifier. The format and topic labels are supplied by internally developed WebOrganizer-style taxonomy classifiers [ 35 ] . The best-performing sampling recipe preserved a natural-distribution backbone while upweighting rare formats and documents assigned mid-range or high scores by the prior classifier. Finally, we used the labels to train an in-house small classifier.

Using our final quality classifier, we partitioned the corpus into quality tiers and study both choices with scaling-ladder ablations.

We first retained the top $N$ quality tiers and sample uniformly from them, increasing their repeat rate as $N$ decreases to fill the fixed effective-token budget. The best measured setting shifts from retaining the top three quality tiers at the smallest tested scale to the top six at the largest scales, suggesting that the optimal retained fraction widens with scale. Because the production horizon substantially exceeds the largest scale tested in this sweep, we erred toward broader coverage and retained the top nine quality tiers, or 45% of web tokens.

Fixing the top nine quality tiers, we swept several quality upsampling schedules and selected the strongest: a truncated-exponential taper capped at approximately 2 $\times$ repetition with a mean repeat rate of approximately 1.5 $\times$ . Our final schedule yields roughly 10% higher compute efficiency than uniform sampling.

Our non-English partition followed the same high-level curation approach, using GlotLID [ 36 ] for language identification and language-aware variants of the filtering and quality-scoring stages in Section 2.2.1 .

The code corpus comprises three categories: source code files, source code notebooks, and web-code documents. Web-code processing is described in Section 2.2.1 . Below, we detail the processing of source code and notebooks.

We first enriched all files with metadata and quality signals. We used go-enry [ 37 ] and Magika [ 38 ] for language detection, chardetng [ 39 ] for encoding detection, and additional detectors for licenses, binary content, generated code, and vendored dependencies. We removed files with restrictive licenses and oversized files, then perform exact deduplication using Git blob content hashes. When identical files occur in multiple repositories, we preferentially retained the copy from the repository with more stars and forks, followed by deterministic tie-breakers.

We separated notebooks from ordinary source code files because notebooks are often experimental working documents rather than polished software artifacts. They commonly contain exploratory work, repeated or partially executed code, prose, and embedded media, making them noisier and less structurally consistent than source code files. Moreover, notebooks are stored natively in a verbose JSON container format, to which ordinary source-code filters cannot be applied reliably. We parsed each notebook and convert its cells, in their original order, into a deterministic script-like representation. We removed cell outputs, discarded empty cells, and replaced media references with compact textual placeholders. Malformed notebooks that cannot be repaired and parsed are excluded.

Next, we applied separate, multi-step filtering pipelines to conventional source-code files and notebooks to remove low-quality, unlearnable data.

- • Source Code: We identified and removed files containing automatically generated code markers or originating from vendored dependencies. We also removed binary-like files, highly compressible files dominated by repetitive content, and structured noise such as hexadecimal and hash dumps, Intel HEX and Motorola S-record files, numeric and metrics tables, serialized pickle data, OCR markup, subtitles, repeated digit-heavy tables, and documents with a high fraction of non-printable characters. We then examined the distributions of curation signals separately for each programming language and use these distributions to establish language-specific thresholds for minimum and maximum file size, minimum alphabetic-character ratio, and maximum line length.

- • Notebooks: We examined the distributions of notebook-specific signals and establish separate thresholds for minimum and maximum document size, minimum number of lines, maximum line length, minimum alphabetic-character ratio, compressibility, and the presence of encoded data.

Finally, we inspected the corpus distribution and deterministically downsample overrepresented languages and artifact-heavy file categories, including markup and configuration formats, generated stubs, IDE-generated files, and framework-specific assets.

PDF documents are a large fraction of our training corpus. We converted the PDF documents to structured text with an OCR model operated as a horizontally scaled Ray pipeline over GPU clusters. Downstream of OCR, a conversion and quality layer turns per-page structured output into complete documents, with dedicated detectors for degenerate model output, blank or uninformative pages, and incomplete documents.


#### High-Level Architecture

Figure 7 illustrates the pipeline: a controller plus a fleet of mini-clusters . The controller is a single Ray/KubeRay process per run that owns the workset, decides how work is partitioned, launches and monitors mini-clusters, and reconciles what finished against what was assigned. Each mini-cluster is a self-contained Ray cluster that processes one slice of the corpus to completion and is torn down.

> Figure 7: Scalable OCR and document curation. A run controller assigns work to warm Ray mini-clusters; co-located CPU rendering and GPU OCR actors process pages before document assembly and curation.

A mini-cluster maps to a given GPU node (H100, H200, RTX Pro 6000, etc). Both rendering and OCR run on the same nodes: the render workers (CPU actors) are co-located with the GPU actors, so a node’s CPUs are rendering the next documents while its GPUs are transcribing pages that are already rendered. Since transcribing takes twice as long as rendering, and with roughly a hundred render workers feeding eight GPUs per node, the GPUs set the node’s ceiling. We feed the GPUs through bounded queues with backpressure detection, throttling rendering when they fill and draining stalled work when they starve, so that the GPUs stay saturated and every available GPU-second goes to transcription.

A render worker takes a whole document, then validates, and rasterizes it into pages. Rendered pages from many documents are interleaved in the GPU queue and packed into batches. The structured OCR output ( Figure 8 ) is then reassembled per document: a document’s pages are written back to storage only once every one of them has been transcribed, so downstream consumers never see a partially processed document. Failures follow the same shape: a problematic page fails alone and is recorded as such, and a document is dead-lettered only when its page failures exceed a per-document budget.


#### Serving Optimizations

OCR runs on vLLM as an in-process engine inside each GPU actor. Because OCR is the bottleneck, two changes to how the engine is driven account for most of the serving-side throughput gain, and both were accepted on controlled measurements.

The serving parameters that govern occupancy (admission width, page-pipeline depth, and the prefill token budget) interact with each other and with the accelerator’s memory and compute shape, so a single global default leaves throughput on the table on every node type. We tuned them empirically per accelerator class on production hardware: we performed a parameter sweep for each accelerator (H100, RTX Pro 6000, H200, etc) to identify the correct parameter values that will maximize the throughput for said accelerator.


#### Reliability and Auto Remediation

- • Pages Whose Prompt Exceeds the Model’s Context: Very tall or very dense pages produce more vision tokens than the context allows. Pages are now pre-validated from their image headers against the model’s own processor geometry, with a threshold deliberately looser than the engine’s so a page the engine would accept is never refused, and any rejection that still reaches the engine evicts the largest page and retries until the batch settles.

- • Long Skinny Pages: Images with aspect ratio above 200:1 (for example an 8 $\times$ 1650 strip) make the vision processor’s resize routine raise unconditionally, deterministically poisoning any batch they join. Rejected terminally at pre-validation.

- • Decompression Bombs: Images whose decoded size exceeds the imaging library’s pixel limit fail on every load attempt. Rejected terminally at pre-validation.

- • Large PDFs: We found PDFs that are many thousands of pages to usually be very low quality, so we pre-filter them to reduce compute waste and improve processing throughput.

Failure is inevitable due to the scale at which we operate. The pipeline was therefore designed to absorb failures as they come, catching and containing failures at the smallest possible granularity and page level. There are three main classes of failure:

- • Page Level: Every page is its own inference request. After three failed retries, a page is classified as failed.

- • Document Level Due to Page Failure: If a document has more than two pages that have run out of retries, the entire document is marked as failed and is removed from downstream processing.

- • Defective Documents: Documents that are deemed unsuitable (corrupt, encrypted, or over the page limit) go to a dead-letter table with a specified reason listed.

As a part of failure handling, we keep a thorough record of each action. Each document writes status events to a status tracking table with the attempt number, final status, and error code. Each page row records whether OCR succeeded, the error code if it did not, and how long it took. Granular record keeping adds resiliency by allowing long running OCR jobs to continue from where they left off in the event of failure.

Each page produced by the OCR model includes a confidence score. We aggregate these scores at the document level and filter documents whose average confidence is below 0.8. This removes documents where the transcription is broadly unreliable.

Some pages are transcribed correctly but contain little self-contained information such as indexes, bare reference lists and predominantly decorative pages. The classifier uses the normalized page text together with a coarse start, middle, or end position signal. Essentially, this classifier ensures we increase the information density of our corpus instead of wasting compute on boilerplate and low-information pages.


#### Document Assembly

Our VLM-based OCR model returns structured HTML for each page. We converted it to Markdown, removing page headers and footers while preserving elements such as equations, tables, code, lists, and footnotes.

We first checked that all expected pages are present and that none are partial or failed. Pages flagged as repetitive, blank, or low-information are excluded, and the rest are stitched together in order. We discarded the entire document if more than 10% of its pages contain repetition. Blank and low-information pages do not count toward this threshold.

Finally, we removed documents with low OCR confidence, too little text, or too few characters per page. This catches image-heavy and otherwise sparse documents that contain little useful training content.


#### Key Results of Quality Improvements

Under controlled experiments, we found that improving document quality and post-processing techniques produced gains comparable to increasing data volume. The benefits were most pronounced on mathematics and STEM tasks and were consistent across multiple evaluations. Within the range tested, we did not observe clear diminishing returns from adding more high-quality documents. These results suggest that better filtering and data selection are as important as corpus scale.

Across all pretraining data pipelines we applied a single, unified deduplication process to remove exact and near-duplicates within individual sources and across the entire data mixture. We designed this pipeline with recall and speed/scale as first-class constraints, and we accept some false positives at scale. This let us run the pipeline regularly as new data is sourced or new ablations start.

The pipeline has three stages: (a) an initial exact-deduplication step based on content hashing, (b) a cheap MinHash-based fuzzy prefilter that generates candidate clusters and duplicate pairs, and (c) a precision filter using Levenshtein-style edit distance that narrows matches based on token similarity, rank, and order.

For fuzzy matching, we represent each document using overlapping token n-grams and compute compact MinHash signatures. Locality-sensitive hashing groups documents with similar signatures, avoiding an exhaustive comparison of every document pair. We then compared candidate pairs using normalized token-level edit distance, which accounts for insertions, deletions, and substitutions. When a pair meets the similarity threshold, we remove the lower-ranked document according to configured source priorities, document quality, and deterministic tie-breaking rules. To bound computation on highly repetitive clusters and very long documents, some cases used rank-based removal without full edit-distance verification.

We separately decontaminated training data against designated evaluation and validation collections. This check measures the fraction of an evaluation sample’s distinct token n-grams that appear in a training document. Using evaluation-side containment allowed us to detect evaluation text embedded in a much longer document without diluting the match with unrelated surrounding content. After ablations on both precision and recall of decontamination, we chose to use overlapping token 13-grams and remove a training document if it contains at least 30% of the distinct 13-grams of any indexed evaluation or validation sample.

We also implemented a number of infrastructure optimizations so that each stage can be horizontally scaled as far as needed with minimal map-reduce -style coordination, and we rely on object storage as a cheap backend. Together, these let us use CPU machines across most providers and instance shapes and be fault-tolerant if capacity is preempted or individual tasks fail.


#### Offline Materialization

We materialize all pretraining data offline: a dedicated data job compiles the weighted document mixture into an immutable artifact of fixed-length 16K training sequences, in exact training order, with every token span carrying provenance to its source document. The training loop performs no sampling, mixing, shuffling, or packing of its own. This buys three properties.

- • Immutability: the data is frozen before the first step and cannot drift with input-pipeline changes, worker scheduling, or restarts.

- • Reproducibility: every run and restart consumes data in identical order, keeping run-to-run variance controlled.

- • Verifiability: the full artifact exists before training, so an independent offline job validates provenance, realized mixture ratios, token accounting, and shuffle quality before any accelerator time is committed; the final artifact for this run passed all such checks.

The same contract makes continued pretraining tractable. When valuable new data lands after a run is underway, we tailpatch—preserve the consumed prefix exactly, let untouched sources continue their epochs, and materialize only the future suffix under an updated mixture. The materialized data is bitwise equivalent to what a run planned on the new mixture from that point would have consumed.


#### Packing

We pack using the concat-then-split (CtS) algorithm: documents are concatenated, separated by a single EOS at each document’s true end, and split into fixed 16K sequences. Within a packed sequence, we also assign segment IDs that mark document boundaries, reset positions at each document boundary, and loss-mask the first token of a new document. Sources that already arrive as coherent sequence-length chunks pass through unchanged; everything else is packed at the document level. This enables document-level attention masking , so that each token attends only to tokens within its own document.

The variant of concat-then-split used in our pipeline we coin split-shuffle packing (SSP) . It addresses the main packing hazard of CtS—long documents that are packed continuously. One document can dominate several adjacent batches, visible at scale as periodic loss dips and gradient-norm spikes, and fixed cut points re-expose exactly the same windows on every repeat epoch.

SSP addresses both issues. Long documents are cut into sequence-length pieces with deterministic, per-epoch randomized boundaries, and the pieces are dispersed by a strong global shuffle before packing. That way, SSP changes local batch composition without changing the selected document mixture. Matched SSP-versus-baseline training ablations at smaller ladder tiers showed smoother gradient-norm traces, lower loss variance, and a small compute efficiency improvement.

We applied the DLS subsampling procedure, described in Section 2.1.3 , before SSP. Given a source, seed, epoch, and target ratio, DLS deterministically permutes logical document identifiers and selects the required portion. The same configuration therefore produces the same membership independent of loader parallelism or storage partitioning. Selecting before splitting also means that a document is sampled as one semantic unit rather than in proportion to the number of chunks it produces.

For the scaling ladder, epoch-0 selections are nested across tiers: the documents used by a smaller tier are contained in the corresponding larger-tier selection. Moving up the ladder adds data instead of replacing one random subset with another, making tier comparisons easier to interpret. Mixture sizing uses exact selected-token counts, rather than only document counts, to determine source repeats and the final partial epoch.


#### 3 Midtraining

Our midtraining stage focuses on sharpening the knowledge, reasoning, and core capabilities that allow Beam to perform agentic tasks. We divide midtraining into two stages. Stage 1 focuses on developing the core logical and reasoning capabilities of the model by training on 850B tokens at 32K context length. Stage 2 extends the context length to 512K tokens using an additional 100B training tokens.


##### 3.1 Optimization

We used a sawtooth schedule for training hyperparameters in midtraining. We linearly warm up the learning rate from zero to the peak value, after which it follows the tail phase of the cosine scheduler curve used in pretraining. We reset the optimizer state at the start of each stage and warm up the learning rate to allow the optimizer moments to adapt to the new data distribution, keeping weight decay constant during warmup. After warmup, we scaled the weight decay by the square of the cosine-tail learning-rate schedule, following the AdamC methodology [ 22 ] . We conducted sweeps on the peak LR and found 5% of maximum pretraining learning rate to balance forgetting and learning in the midtraining stages.


##### 3.2 Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Training Health

Our data distribution in midtraining differs from pretraining because it assigns greater weight to specialized domains like STEM and coding. As a result, the token distribution per batch shifts between these two training stages, which affects the expert load balancing in MoE layers. We observed that enforcing expert balancing via the same expert bias and auxiliary loss recipes used in pretraining can hinder specialization and lead to lower model performance overall. To allow for expert specialization, we disabled router z-loss, sequence aux loss, and expert bias update. Empirically, updating router weights was sufficient to stabilize the MoE during midtraining, as measured by expert loads and router probabilities for the selected experts. To ensure expert diversification, we also track router weight rank and per-token entropy of the router probabilities.


##### 3.3 Sequence Packing

We found that it is beneficial to preserve full data samples during training and prevent splitting data between consecutive sequences within a batch. We therefore used a filter-best-fit packing algorithm for offline data preparation. This algorithm keeps documents whole, filters out oversized documents, then places remaining ones in a bin with the least remaining space that still fits it. When a document fits no bin, all occupied bins are emitted and padded to a fixed length. Figure 12 shows the four steps in the algorithm: filtering, best-fit selection, flushing, and reset/finish. We still use document-level attention masking as in pretraining to limit attention only within each document’s tokens.

We adopted Unicron ( Section 6 ) with FlashAttention-4 [ 40 ] with zigzag-style sequence reordering for efficient (long-context) training. When used with context parallelism, our attention kernel enforces certain constraints on the length of the packed sequence as well as the length of each document. We address these constraints by adding padding tokens to each document whenever necessary during the offline packing.

Padding enters the packed batches from two sources: the flushing step of the packing algorithm and the kernel-alignment constraints above. Depending on the data’s structure and length distribution, the resulting padding can be non-trivial, and these tokens are no-ops for training. We patched Megatron [ 41 ] to mask padding tokens from expert routing in the MoE layers, which saves computation and avoids expert overload and imbalance.

> Figure 12: This figure demonstrates an overview of the filter-best-fit algorithm. Oversized documents are first filtered out, then a group of bins is allocated for packing. Each sample is assigned to the bin with the tightest fit. If no fit is found, the group is flushed and a new set of empty bins are allocated. The process continues until all data is exhausted.


##### 3.4 Data Ablation Methodology

The midtraining mixture includes many small datasets that span different domains, sources, and quality tiers. To derisk each individual data source and guide our final data mixture composition, we conducted iterative ablations at a smaller scale using smaller models and shorter token horizons. These proxy training runs allow us to examine how mixture selection, data quality, and training format affect both training dynamics and downstream performance, informing decisions for large-scale runs. All proxy runs use a consistent mixture recipe. For each ablation, we draw 50% of the data from the stable mixture used in the previous large-scale run and 50% from the new source under ablation. This allows us to ablate individual sources and also identify undesirable interactions between data and capabilities early on.

We enforce determinism in our training, which allows us to carefully study and diagnose the source of training instability manifested as spikes in loss and gradient norm in our ablations. Aside from hardware and modeling issues, we used spike analysis as a tool to investigate bad data and flag batches for targeted inspection. Repeating this methodology of data cleaning across our ablations allowed us to create customized data filters to catch corrupted content, malformed formatting, excessive repetition, and preprocessing errors across a wide variety of our data sources and domains.

#### Seed Curation

We curated seeds from technical web content like academic papers as well as licensed advanced textbooks. We classified documents by subject area and filtered for educational value and technical substance, prioritizing material containing substantive explanations, derivations, and problem-solving. Within each domain, we sought coverage of finer-grained subtopics using hierarchical subject labels. We organized these labels into a knowledge graph, connecting broad disciplines to increasingly specialized subtopics and linking each topic to its associated source documents and seeds. This structure made coverage gaps and concentrations visible, supporting targeted curation of underrepresented areas and balanced sampling across related subtopics.


#### Data Transformation

A model does not learn reasoning best from raw technical material like papers, or dense passages. We focused our efforts on reshaping the raw data into formats that make the reasoning and logic itself explicit. For research papers, we drew on derivations and calculations and recast them as self-contained tasks, supplying the needed context while withholding the target result, which surfaces specialized intermediate steps that research writing tends to skip. For multi-step problems, we used the knowledge graph and concept co-occurrence patterns to select related ideas. We then synthesized tasks with dependencies between steps and checked that the combined concepts and reasoning were coherent. This approach broadened conceptual coverage while keeping the synthesized tasks grounded in established problem structures. For explanatory material, we rewrote technical passages as tutorials and multi-turn discussions that mirror how reasoning actually unfolds, from decomposing a problem to exploring approaches and revising when something doesn’t hold up, giving content from varied sources a clearer, more consistent presentation without losing technical substance. Together, these approaches turned source material into reasoning-rich training data that is grounded in real scientific work, broad in its concept coverage, and pedagogically clear.

We tailored quality checks to the type of examples and formatting applied to the source data. For any generated data, we used available source answers and derivations, and symbolic, numerical, or execution checks where applicable. Multi-hop tasks were also checked for correct intermediate results and meaningful dependencies between steps. For tutorials and discussions, LLM judges assessed technical accuracy, clarity, and fidelity to the source. After rewriting, we checked the text for remaining unwanted behaviors. Examples with errors, weak supporting evidence, or unwanted behaviors were repaired, rewritten, or regenerated while preserving style and necessary checks. Refinement can also increase task complexity by adding constraints or adding further dependent seeds and steps.

For portions of our data that have a computational final answer, we used pass@k calibration to measure difficulty and select data samples near the model’s capability frontier: challenging enough to require substantive work from the model, while remaining within reach. Calibration was relative to the evaluated checkpoint and informed adjustments to task selection and formatting of the raw data as capabilities improved. Low solve rates also prompted checks for ambiguity or low quality of the training document due to lossy reformatting. We allowed Beam to also leverage written code as a computational medium where reasoning alone tended to fail, thus allowing the model to reproduce the complex computations encountered in the source documents.

Our data pipelines for competitive programming provide examples that emphasize algorithm selection, step-by-step reasoning, and reaching the correct implementation. These pipelines combine human-authored artifacts with model-generated supervision, emphasizing coverage, correctness, and useful training signals.


#### Seed Curation

Our competitive-programming data is curated from programming datasets, official contest archives, and repository-derived algorithmic examples. We normalize these heterogeneous sources, reconcile overlapping records, and review statements for completeness and clarity. We audit all data for semantic contamination against coding benchmarks that we use for signal. We use lexical checks as well as embedding-based comparisons to flag potential paraphrases, and use LLM-assisted review to assess whether a training solution would solve an evaluation task with only mechanical changes.

We organize the decontaminated seeds under a topic hierarchy spanning dynamic programming, graph and string algorithms, advanced data structures, number theory, and geometry, with finer-grained techniques such as Digit DP, rerooting on trees, and rollback union-find. This hierarchy guides how we control diversity and expand the seed collection.


#### Data Transformation

Using the topic hierarchy over seeds, we expanded our training corpus by conditioning on a topic and a small set of relevant seeds to generate similarly structured problems, while reusing the reference solutions or an explanation of the underlying method. We vary difficulty, structure, programming language, and interface.

Every problem, whether original or adapted, passes an LLM-assisted quality review covering clarity, internal consistency, difficulty, novelty, and learning value. Where tests or verification checks exist, solutions are run against them. Where only reference solutions exist, we built test suites from cases on which multiple solutions agree, and audit that trusted solutions pass and known-incorrect ones are rejected. Problems lacking both are solved via a collection of different models and reviewed for correctness. Where reference solutions are available, we also rewrite them into tutorial-style documents covering the key observations, algorithms, correctness arguments, complexity analysis, and pitfalls, surfacing concepts that are only implicit in source code.

This category of data teaches models the fundamental knowledge for understanding and improving codebases, extending their capability beyond code generation in isolation. The data broadly covers two complementary capabilities: repository understanding , which involves identifying relevant code and reasoning about its structure and behavior, and feature implementation , which involves translating natural-language requirements into concrete code changes. Together, these capabilities support the broader software engineering workflow, from understanding an unfamiliar repository to implementing and validating a change.


#### Seed Curation

We constructed all software-engineering data from real GitHub repositories, drawing on issues, pull requests, and commit histories. Across all data pipelines, we first apply a common set of repository filters: we exclude low-quality repositories, including those with few stars, no activity in recent years, or primarily non-code content. We remove repositories that are duplicated, have non-permissive licenses, or are benchmark-contaminated. From the remaining repositories, we further filter down the issues, commits, or pull requests (PRs) based on various heuristics of quality, completeness, complexity, and capability coverage.


#### Data Transformations

One of the fundamental capabilities needed for software engineering is reasoning and understanding across files, interfaces, and dependencies. To build this capability in the model, we constructed targeted repository-understanding data grounded in real GitHub issues that call for explanation or analysis rather than implementation, covering topics such as design rationale, dependency relationships, feature extensibility, and data or control flow. We further expand this set by synthetically generating new questions that are attached to real code, broadening coverage beyond the distribution of organic issues. The entire pool is filtered to retain questions that are answerable, grounded in the in-context code, and non-trivial. We further produce design documents that describe the structure of code repos at multiple levels of abstraction. Together, these training samples help models reason about repositories as a whole rather than individual code excerpts, enabling them to navigate unfamiliar codebases, connect information across files, and explain code structure and behavior.

We built various forms of supervision from real repository history, focusing on building foundational SWE knowledge and skills at scale, and applying those skills through repository exploration and feedback-driven refinement.

Starting from the retained seed commits, we reconstructed a natural-language task from the commit description and code changes, and pair it with repository context assembled from the changed files and their dependencies.

We use LLM-assisted refinement to improve the reference changes and to produce accompanying rationales and explanations for the seed code patches. Finally, we reformat the training samples into several output styles, including unified diffs and Bash commands, so the model learns different code editing interfaces.

We also create template data that teaches the model how to work with a repository over multiple steps, including skills such as inspecting files, searching for definitions and usages, and applying edits as its understanding develops.

In addition to commit data, we also created additional targeted data by removing selected functionalities in existing code and writing specifications for reconstructing it. Varying the scope of the removed code, from single functions to groups of files, yields tasks from localized edits to complex multi-file features.

We developed specific data pipelines to make the model familiar with problem solving using the terminal. The focus at the midtraining stage was to introduce broad horizon coverage of terminal related skills including shell scripting, systems programming, networking, data processing and more. Our pipelines are grounded in human written scenarios from our seed sources and are expanded to produce similarly diverse and realistic training examples.


#### Seed Curation

Our training data is grounded in real-world raw artifacts. This gives us two benefits:

- • Distribution: Human-created issues, questions, and discussions capture the full spectrum of problems solved via terminal, including the long tail of flags, quoting mistakes, and environment quirks.

- • Verifiability: A post with an accepted answer gives us a reference to grade against or a concrete problem to turn into a sandbox-checked task.

We curated seeds from various available web sources and technical documentation like manual pages, --help , and library docs. For community sources, we retained only examples with accepted answers and removed low-quality or incomplete records, including those that depended on missing images. Separately, we collected thousands of documentation pages covering command-line tools, shell builtins, shell-language constructs, and Python libraries. We then create a comprehensive taxonomy of all skillsets the model is required to have in order to solve terminal tasks.


#### Data Transformation

The first family of training documents is question-and-answer data built from community threads with accepted solutions. Each thread is rewritten into a standalone question with its implicit assumptions made explicit, personal and site-specific context removed, and the original technical setting preserved. The rewrite is deliberately varied so it is never a verbatim copy. Each question is then answered independently, without access to the accepted answer, and both the rewrite and the answer are scored for fidelity, correctness, completeness, and methodology. Answers that reach a valid conclusion by a different route than the original post are retained rather than discarded, while weak rewrites and incorrect answers are removed.

The second family covers hands-on terminal work. Starting from the skillset taxonomies, we augment them with detailed metadata, such as what the skill teaches, canonical commands and their commonly used flags, and related Python packages. We also add verifier hints for writing tests that check outcomes rather than methods. We then pair each seed artifact with their related skill augmentations and expand them into many complete, executable tasks that include a clear instruction, a realistic workspace, a setup procedure, an initial-state check, a hidden final test, and a reference solution. We control diversity of the training samples by covering various capability combinations and difficulty axes, such as hidden faults, distractors, or subtle traps. We apply two levels of quality control using 1) inexpensive static checks to catch obvious problems and mismatches with the original raw seeds, and 2) a validation step in a real environment to confirm that each task starts from a valid state, fails when nothing is done, and passes once the reference solution is applied.

##### 3.6 Stage 2 Data

The Stage 2 mixture combined recall data from pretraining and Stage 1 with the following new data sources:

- • Organic long-context data, such as books, research articles, and code repositories. This data naturally encodes long-range dependency, providing crucial signals for long-context learning. Repositories are carefully selected following natural programming language distribution. We pack the coding files in a repository into a single sequence based on their cross-file dependencies.

- • Synthetic long-context data. We use a mix of in-house and open-source datasets to enhance the model’s long-range retrieval and reasoning capability. The in-house portion is question-answer (QA) pairs generated from high-quality books across various domains with grounded context. We also adopt several open-license datasets such as LongCite [ 42 ] , LongData, and LongAlign [ 43 ] .

In experiments, we noticed that the mixing weight significantly affects the trade-off between long-context performance and other general model capabilities in the context extension stage: often, we saw consistent gains on long context benchmarks like RULER but with regressions of varying degrees on other benchmarks. Following a grid search, we selected a mixture of 50% organic long-context data, 15% synthetic long-context QA and instruction-following data, and 35% recall data from pretraining and Stage 1. #### 4 Post-Training

Beam’s post-training pipeline combines two separately trained experts, an agentic expert and an alignment expert, through multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD). Each expert undergoes a short supervised fine-tuning (SFT) phase to establish response formats and tool-use syntax, followed by reinforcement learning (RL). This section describes our training approach, data generation and curation, and supporting infrastructure.


##### 4.1 Agentic Expert

We train the agentic expert to solve reasoning and long-horizon tasks across mathematics, STEM, competitive programming, software engineering, terminal use, web search, general tool use, and professional knowledge work. Training consists of a short supervised fine-tuning (SFT) phase followed by large-scale reinforcement learning (RL). SFT establishes the response and tool-call formats and seeds reasoning behaviors; RL develops these capabilities through interaction with tasks and environments. We first describe the training procedure and its empirical behavior, then detail the training data.

We initialize the agentic expert with a short SFT phase focused on chat formatting, tool-call syntax, and reasoning behavior. We generate trajectories using an earlier version of our model on a subset of the final task pool spanning all training domains. These trajectories are filtered for correctness and quality before being used for supervised training.

We train the agentic expert using a GRPO-style policy-gradient method [ 44 ] in a fully asynchronous setting. Asynchronous execution accommodates the wide variation in rollout durations, but introduces policy staleness as training proceeds while trajectories remain in flight. Stable optimization also requires consistency between training and inference, particularly in sampling and mixture-of-experts routing. Below, we describe our approach to these challenges, our reward design, and the resulting training dynamics and performance.

Agentic tasks produce a heavy-tailed distribution of rollout durations. In our RL run, the median rollout completes in approximately 12 minutes, while roughly 2% take longer than an hour. For approximately one in five training steps, at least one contributing rollout took more than six hours. Waiting for all rollouts in each batch would therefore introduce substantial synchronization overhead.

We decouple generation from optimization and update the inference workers after every optimizer step. In-flight rollouts continue under the updated weights without interruption, following the general approach of in-flight weight updates [ 45 ] and partial rollouts [ 46 ] . A single trajectory may consequently span multiple policy versions. We record the policy version associated with each generated token.

To accommodate differences in task duration, we use task-dependent rollout deadlines. Rollouts that exceed their deadlines are discarded, and rollout groups are retained only when enough rollouts have completed.

Long trajectories create many opportunities to sample low-probability tokens. Such tokens can disrupt reasoning or invalidate tool calls, potentially derailing an otherwise successful trajectory. To reduce this risk, we use nucleus sampling with $p=0.99$ [ 47 ] and temperature set to 1.0.

Nucleus sampling changes the behavior policy by truncating and renormalizing the token distribution. Computing full-vocabulary log-probabilities during training would leave this transformation unaccounted for and introduce gradients through tokens outside the sampled nucleus. Following the approach used in DeepSeek-V3.2 [ 48 ] , we retain the nucleus $\mathcal{N}_{t}$ used at each generation step and renormalize the training policy over that same set.

This aligns the support used for generation and training, eliminating the mismatch due to applying different truncation masks. It does not eliminate differences caused by policy updates or numerical effects. Logits outside the recorded nucleus receive no direct gradient from the corresponding token-level objective.

Mixture-of-experts routing introduces another source of training–inference mismatch: small numerical differences can change a token’s top- $k$ expert selection and therefore its subsequent computation. We use routing replay [ 49 ] to preserve the expert assignments made during generation. The inference engine records the selected experts for each token, and the trainer reuses those assignments while recomputing the gate values.

Router weights and load-balancing biases remain frozen during RL. Although this fixes the router parameters, expert assignments can still change as the representations entering the router evolve, making replay relevant even with a frozen router. To further reduce numerical discrepancies, we run the LM head, router, and residual stream in FP32 in both training and inference.

For software-engineering and terminal tasks, passing an automated verifier does not necessarily establish that a solution is valid. A model may modify tests, hard-code expected outputs, or exploit weaknesses in the evaluation harness. We therefore apply a judge 1 1 In developing both the agentic and alignment experts, we made use of internally configured models which are variously referred to as “graders”, “agents”, and “judges” throughout this chapter. with a reward-hacking rubric to rollouts that pass their task verifier. Only the final patch was passed to the judge for evaluation. We additionally monitor the chain-of-thought of the model to detect hacking intention but do not apply direct optimization pressure to it.

If the judge flags a rollout as reward hacking, we set its correctness score to zero while retaining its length cost. Such a rollout receives the same reward as a failed attempt of equal length. The rubrics were updated during the run to track new reward hacks as the model discovered them.

We sourced a wide pool of several million verifiable RL tasks by drawing from open source repositories, purchasing them from vendors, or generating them synthetically with in-house pipelines. This broader pool was of variable quality and difficulty, and we aggressively filtered it down to nearly one million of the most difficult, high-quality tasks to support the final RL run.

Across all domains, our approach to RL environment curation was one of first ensuring a high bar for both the quality and difficulty of tasks. Then, given those constraints, we scaled to as many diverse tasks as possible to support large-scale RL. Our process was also iterative, after scaling we often filtered again based on our observations during RL.

By “quality”, we mean tasks that have zero noise in their rewards . This means the tasks are not misleading (e.g., they should not be missing instructions or context, they should be well-formed, and the premise of the task should be sound), hackable (i.e., reward should be given only when the task is truly solved), or otherwise broken. Additionally, verifiers/reward functions must be robust and consistent. They should always give the same reward for the same answer. Some measures of quality are also model specific; for example, tasks where the model can give the correct answer without chain-of-thought, or in which a successful hack was observed.

By “difficulty”, we mean difficulty for training a particular model. This means the task must neither be too hard (i.e., all or mostly zero rewards in a group) nor too easy (i.e., all or mostly full rewards in a group). Moreover, we work to ensure the task is not classified as “too hard” or “too easy” for the wrong reasons, such as: tasks where the answer is leaked in the question (making it seem artificially easy), evaluating the model with insufficient context (making it seem artificially hard), chat template formatting errors (masking the true difficulty), or impossible questions (which can lead to hacking behavior during evaluation, see [ 50 ] ). In particular, there are a long tail of domain-specific failure modes that can make tasks seem artificially hard, such as not being able to access a particular website, overly strict memory/time requirements, and various sandboxing issues. Some of these are described in more detail below in the domain-specific sections. The sandbox service’s network controls are described in Section 6.6.5 .

We sourced tasks from open-source repositories, purchased them from vendors, or generated them from our own in-house synthetic pipelines. Existing open-source environments were valuable earlier in our development cycle; however, we found that when used in large-scale RL with a performant model like Beam, they required extensive quality and difficulty filtering. Vendor-sourced tasks are high-quality and high-difficulty, but expensive. We found that developing our own in-house pipelines allowed us to satisfy the quality, difficulty, and scale requirements for large-scale RL on Beam.

To ensure the generality of our model, we curated tasks across a wide diversity of task formats, programming languages, tool combinations, and topics. We applied the deduplication and decontamination procedure described in Section 4.1.4 .

We considered our task pools to be ever-changing: following every round of experimentation, we analyzed rollouts and tried to understand if agent successes or failures could be attributed to issues in the tasks. For example, reward hacks newly observed in a training run led to tasks being removed. Although the pool of tasks shrank over time, we converged on a set that had the highest quality possible.

For post-training task curation, we checked incoming training samples against a shared collection of previously registered samples and excluded identified exact and near-duplicates from training. These checks addressed both repetition within the training data and contamination from evaluation samples. We used a Jaccard similarity threshold of 0.95 for comparisons between training samples. When either sample belonged to an evaluation, we lowered the threshold to 0.80 to detect a broader range of potential contamination. The following section describes the matching procedure.

We represented each sample as a set of overlapping character 7-grams: every consecutive sequence of seven characters in its text. For two sets $A$ and $B$ , Jaccard similarity measures the fraction of distinct n-grams shared by both samples relative to those present in either. Jaccard distance is one minus this similarity:

[Tableau] $$J(A,B)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\cup B|},\qquad d_{J}(A,B)=1-J(A,B).$$

Identical n-gram sets have distance zero; sets with no shared n-grams have distance one. Small edits can preserve much of the overlap, allowing detection of near-duplicates as well as exact copies.

To avoid comparing every pair of samples, we used MinHash to summarize each n-gram set in a compact signature and locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) to retrieve likely matches. This allowed searches over a large collection without comparing each incoming sample directly with every stored example. We then calculated exact Jaccard similarity for each retrieved candidate and accepted a match only when it met the relevant threshold.

MinHash LSH provides probabilistic retrieval: more similar pairs are more likely to be retrieved, but some pairs that meet the threshold may be missed. Exact verification ensures that accepted matches satisfy the similarity criterion; it does not guarantee that every near-duplicate is found.

The method does not assess semantic similarity. Paraphrases, translations, and short questions embedded in much longer documents can have low Jaccard similarity. We therefore treat deduplication as a practical but imperfect control on repetition and contamination.

Training software engineering agents with reinforcement learning requires diverse repository-level tasks and reliable feedback on whether a solution implements the requested behavior. We constructed executable environments from changes mined from software repositories and from synthetic feature-implementation tasks. Our approach combined environment reconstruction, behavioral verification, and task-quality assessment, building on recent work on scalable SWE environment construction [ 51 , 52 ] .


#### Task Curation

We used agents to turn mined repository changes into executable training tasks, drawing on methods for agentic environment construction described in SWE-Universe [ 53 ] and DockSmith [ 54 ] . Starting from the repository at its pre-change revision, an agent inspected the source and test patches, dependency manifests, and continuous integration configuration to discover the required dependencies, build steps, and affected tests. It specified the environment setup and wrote a verifier, using execution feedback to diagnose failures and iteratively refine both. The agent packaged the resulting setup into a reproducible container image which included a problem statement, an executable repository snapshot, a reference implementation, and a verifier.

We independently validated the constructed environment by running the verifier before and after the reference change, requiring it to fail before the change (“fail-to-pass”) and pass after it while rejecting observed regressions (“pass-to-pass”). We also revalidated tasks through the patch-submission and grading path used in training, including submission extraction and application in a separate verifier environment. This checked that the task remained executable under the training environment’s resource and isolation constraints.

Execution validity alone does not establish that a task provides a useful learning signal—for example, task prompts can be under-specified, making it difficult for an agent to solve the task in a way that the verifier expects. We separately assessed specification clarity and test adequacy, properties also studied in SPICE [ 55 ] and SWE-rebench V2 [ 51 ] . The requested behavior had to be sufficiently specified by the problem statement and visible repository context (which we refer to as the “solver-visible specification”), and the verifier had to meaningfully test that behavior.

We developed judges to assess the problem statement, reference implementation, tests, and verification command against separate criteria. These models checked that:

- • Statements describe an actionable change without leaking the solution or depending on unavailable attachments or discussions.

- • Tests agree with the stated requirements, assert meaningful behavior, and permit equivalent correct implementations.

- • Reference fixes are relevant to the requested change, and do not have hard-coded answers or modifications to test files.

- • Verifier commands do not manufacture success, suppress failures, or substitute superficial checks for behavioral tests.

Repairable descriptions were rewritten and reassessed; tasks with unresolved integrity defects were excluded.

A second, stricter review examined whether (1) every tested requirement could be determined from the solver-visible specification, (2) whether the tests enforced the requested behavior, and (3) whether trivial or incomplete implementations could pass. The reference implementation was withheld from this assessment to avoid biasing the review with information unavailable to the solver. Each of these criteria received three independent model calls, with any valid negative judgment rejecting the task.

We complemented artifact-based checks with agentic audits that inspected repositories, traced verification logic, and executed candidate implementations. Two judges assessed the agreement between the solver-visible specification and the verifier in complementary directions.

- • The ambiguity judge checked whether the requirements enforced by the tests were justified by the specification. It looked for cases where the available information permitted multiple reasonable implementations, but the verifier accepted only some of them. The agent examined repository code, documentation, and established interfaces for context that resolved the apparent ambiguity. Where practical, the agent constructed and executed an alternative implementation to demonstrate that the verifier rejected behavior consistent with the visible specification.

- • The verifier-strength judge checked whether the tests enforced the requested behavior. Before inspecting hidden tests or the reference implementation, the agent recorded the independently testable requirements established by the prompt and starting repository. It then mapped each requirement to the verification logic, checking whether violating that requirement would cause a failure. The agent then attempted to generate incomplete or incorrect implementations that exploited gaps in verification. Such bypasses had to be discoverable from solver-visible information, rather than depend on privileged knowledge of hidden tests.

Any tasks which failed to pass the static and agentic validation steps were discarded. We curated the resulting pool across programming languages and repositories, limited repeated variants of the same underlying change, and removed tasks that a reference model solved reliably. We also excluded task identities and patch matches associated with held-out evaluations. These stages produced a broad corpus of executable software changes spanning multiple language ecosystems.

We curated an initial pool of candidate tasks, including those from our own pipeline, from proprietary vendor tasks, and from existing open-source SWE corpora: SWE-Gym [ 56 ] , R2E-Gym [ 57 ] , SWE-rebench V2 [ 51 ] , Multi-SWE-RL [ 58 ] , MEnvData [ 59 ] , SWE-smith [ 60 ] , and Scale-SWE [ 61 ] . We subsampled from this set by balancing programming languages and filtering for quality and difficulty. Figure 18 visualizes the distribution of a subset of tasks from this final pool.

#### Synthetic Feature-Implementation Tasks

In addition to the pipeline described above, we found synthetic task generation to provide a complementary way to expand the range of software changes available for training. We generated feature-implementation tasks by having an agent examine a working repository’s documentation and source code. The agent identified a substantial feature, and removed its implementation and associated tests. The removed behavior was specified as a new implementation task and evaluated with hidden integration tests designed to permit alternative implementations. We required the remaining repository tests to pass after removal, and the feature verifier to fail without the feature and pass when the reference implementation was restored. This construction extended the task distribution to larger changes spanning multiple files and subsystems.

Synthetic tasks received additional checks specific to feature removal. We inspected prompts for leaked implementation details, rebuilt derived artifacts from the modified repository, and scanned images for surviving copies of the removed code or tests. Adversarial agents attempted to recover leaked artifacts and use them to obtain a passing result. Verifier guards also rejected unauthorized changes to test-selection configuration and import hooks. Tasks were required to pass three independent environment-validation trials under the training resource limits, excluding tasks with inconsistent outcomes.


#### Reward Hacking

We also addressed reward hacking during training. To minimize the risk of hacking, the solver’s submitted patch was evaluated in a separate environment, preventing it from directly carrying changes to its execution environment into the verifier. We also cleaned repository images to remove post-change commit history and artifacts that could expose the reference solution. For submissions that passed the tests, an additional model-based judge examined the submitted code and verifier for evidence of reward hacking; submissions classified as successful hacks received zero reward.

We additionally analyzed groups of rollouts for submission failures, inconsistent grades on identical or closely similar patches, and suspicious passing solutions. Task-health judges investigated whether groups with no successful solutions reflected genuine difficulty, underspecified requirements, broken environments, or grading failures. Reward-integrity judges assessed whether successful solutions exploited test gaps or whether hidden tests required unsupported assumptions. These analyses supported ongoing diagnosis of task quality, complementing construction-time filtering and online reward checks.

Equipping an agent with a bash tool—a single interface for executing arbitrary shell commands—has become the dominant way to give a text-only language model autonomous control over a computer. Through it, an agent can perform highly skilled work spanning software engineering, system administration, scientific computing, machine learning, and cybersecurity [ 62 ] .


#### Task Curation

We began by sourcing tasks both from vendors and our own synthetic pipelines, all following the Harbor format [ 63 ] . We also investigated open-source terminal-use tasks, but we found these did not meet our quality bar. Through our curation pipelines, tasks were filtered based on diversity, quality, and difficulty (described in the sections below).

With terminal agents operating across a wide range of downstream tasks, we needed to ensure appropriate diversity in our training environments. We considered task diversity along three dimensions. These dimensions were used to develop taxonomies that allowed us to better characterize each task:

- • Subject matter: 20 categories and 182 domains capturing realistic workflows and activities that can be performed in a terminal.

- • Task properties: 14 properties and 244 subproperties that describe the intent of a task, initial state of the environment, applicable constraints, and deliverables types.

- • Instruction style: 7 styles and 34 substyles representative of the ways in which users typically interact with terminal agents.

All tasks were labeled against the taxonomies, providing up to 460 tags per task. Shown in Figure 19 is an example of the “task intent” across the environments used in training. Each task may have multiple intents to better represent complex multi-step workflows. Together with embedding-based similarity checks, these tags allowed us to filter out near-duplicates or templated tasks, and ensured that each task seen in training covered a distinct aspect seen in real terminal workflows.

> Figure 19: Intent distribution across terminal use tasks. Circles denote synthetic tasks; squares denote vendor tasks.

All of our tasks went through multiple filtering stages to ensure that only the highest-quality tasks were retained for training. These stages focused on flagging and removing tasks that could be detrimental for RL, such as those with flawed environments that made the goal unachievable, noisy or misaligned verifiers, and tasks that exposed privileged information allowing agents to bypass the intended work.

Upfront rule-based methods were used to check for basic compatibility with Reflection’s internal sandbox infrastructure and task completeness:

- 1. Tasks had to be fully Harbor compatible.

- 2. Dockerfiles had to be well-structured to maximize layer reuse (see Section 6.6.3 ).

- 3. Any images associated with a task had to be buildable.

- 4. An “Oracle” script containing the reference solution had to consistently return a reward of 1.0 across 5 attempts.

- 5. A “no-op” script had to consistently return a reward of 0.0 across 5 attempts.

- 6. Checks for privileged information had to show no unintended information leakage in the agent sandbox.

All tasks went through a comprehensive set of over 15 quality checks using an agentic judge to measure properties that rule-based methods couldn’t detect. For instance, we checked for consistency between all of the requirements or constraints explicitly mentioned in the instructions, or those directly inferrable from the environment, and the behaviors tested for in the verifiers. The definitions for each of these checks were updated over time based on the following feedback:

- • Expert human annotators were used to measure the efficiency of the automated checks and surface misalignment between the intent of the check and the model-produced judgments.

- • Newly discovered quality issues or systemic flaws observed in batches of vendor or synthetic tasks resulted in definitions being expanded. For instance, we found that a subset of synthetic tasks required the agent to interact with “black-box” binaries that the agent was freely able to access and reverse-engineer.

By continuously monitoring task quality, and the reliability of our automated quality checks, we were able to make targeted improvements to synthetic pipelines and work with vendors to improve upstream task quality.

Static checks through oracle scripts are necessary but not sufficient to assess the health of a task: the oracle scripts do not always exercise the solution paths that an agent may find, and they can obfuscate fundamental issues with the agent environment. We therefore validated tasks through end-to-end rollouts with live agents. We discarded tasks that a strong model failed to solve within $k$ attempts. Although repeated failure does not prove that a task is ill-specified or impossible, it provided a practical filtering criterion. For successful rollouts, we used a judge to check for reward hacking and environment issues, such as missing tools, that may have interfered with the intended solution.

We also performed substantial filtering of reward-hackable tasks. To do so, we built an adversarial agent that actively attempted to obtain a reward of 1.0 with an insufficient or incorrect solution. The agent was provided with a full taxonomy of reward hacks and instructed to test out different approaches to fully assess the robustness of the verifiers. We filtered out all tasks that were hacked by this agent.

Verifiers are central to RL: they evaluate an agent’s work and produce the reward used for training. Their quality directly shapes the training signal. For terminal tasks, verifiers were typically custom shell or Python scripts that check an agent’s solution (e.g., inspecting files, validating computed values, or measuring runtime). Good verifiers have three essential properties: alignment with the task description, comprehensive coverage, and robustness to reward hacking.

The task’s state requirements needed to be enforced without imposing unstated constraints or assuming a particular implementation. As an example, if the task left the deliverable’s file format unspecified the verifier had to be capable of checking a range of reasonable formats, rather than arbitrarily allow for a single format.

Each individual constraint or requirement required by the task had to be tested for by the verifier. If a task asked the agent to compute results across a range of inputs, the verifier had to validate all those inputs thoroughly. Incomplete checks could reward partial solutions, reinforcing behavior that fell short of the task’s requirements.

A verifier should reward successful task completion and resist attempts to manipulate the evaluation. For example, test inputs and expected outputs should be protected from agent modification; otherwise, an agent may change the test data to make an incorrect solution pass.

It is challenging to measure these properties with static checks alone as it requires comparing the verifier against the task’s requirements, identifying gaps in coverage, and reasoning about how an agent could exploit the evaluation. Through both human and automated review of the tasks and real rollouts, we were able to identify robustness issues, and either fix or filter tasks with problematic verifiers.

To control the diversity of the task descriptions, we relied on different “diversity knobs” for generation, including task specificity, and instruction style/tone. These knobs were used to steer the model generation in certain directions and proved useful in preventing diversity collapse. For instance, we could steer the task description toward a technical, informal or conversational tone. Another example was the task specificity knob, which determined how prescriptive and detailed the task description should be. We set specificity as a scale where high specificity meant the task description would explicitly mention every aspect of the deliverables including the kinds of edge cases the solution should support. Lower specificity meant the task description would omit certain requirements that could be easily inferred from context, agent environment or common sense. The third stage took a task description and turned it into a fully fledged Harbor -compatible task. It combined a coding agent operating in a sandbox environment with a quality gate that relied on the set of judge quality checks and static checks described above. The agent was given 10 rounds to iterate on the task and pass the quality gate; we gave up on the task past that. Tasks generated by this pipeline were later subject to further filters after monitoring behavior during RL. We originally followed the “legacy” Harbor format for tasks where the verifier shared the same runtime as the agent. As this was generally not robust to reward hacking, we subsequently switched to separating runtimes for agent and verifier.

Synthetic task creation is iterative rather than sequential. We started with an initial batch and ran it through the full pipeline: tests, quality checks, rollouts, and finally training. This surfaced issues such as undersized sandboxes, verifier timeouts, ambiguous task descriptions, insufficient difficulty, and hackable verifiers. We turned these issues into improvements to the pipeline, strengthening the generation prompts and quality checks. Through that improvement cycle, the quality of our synthetic tasks increased significantly over time.


#### Question Curation

We began by sourcing over one million math and science tasks from open-source corporate [ 64 , 65 , 66 , 67 , 68 ] , vendors, and our own synthetic pipelines. Each task consisted of a question and “gold” or ground-truth answer. Of these, many were filtered for being too easy or for having quality issues. In the final pool we had 65% math questions, 17% physics, 11% chemistry, 4% biology, and the rest from other scientific disciplines.


#### Verification

To grade the model’s answers to math and science questions, we instructed the model to output its final answer within \boxed{} and used that syntax to extract the answer from the model’s response. We restricted math questions to those with a numeric or symbolic answer which could be compared to the model’s answer using SymPy -based verification [ 69 ] . Science questions were graded using a judge verifier which was prompted to compare the gold answer with the model’s answer, given the question.

For verification of math questions, we started from an implementation based on the math-verify library [ 70 ] . We noticed that the original implementation lacked several crucial normalizations: ( a ) limited support for stylistic commands, ( b ) no support for European style interval markers, ( c ) ASCII style answers ( sqrt(13) instead of \ sqrt{13} ), etc. We added support for these additional normalizations, reducing the false negative rate in verification. We also noticed that the original implementation was prone to false positives, due to incorrect SymPy object resolution. We resolved this by routing the answer to the appropriate SymPy object resolution class— LaTeX for symbolic answers, and Expr for numeric answers. In addition to these, we filtered questions if the verification function could not resolve a SymPy object for the gold answer. This avoided situations where the ultimate verification degenerated to string match, a highly false negative prone mechanism.

We found that some questions under-specified the form of the answer, making it difficult to grade. For example, if the gold answer is 3.5 m/s, we found that judge verifiers were often inconsistent in accepting more precise answers (e.g. 3.51 m/s) or those in different units (351 cm/s). Similarly, some questions required the use of well-known constants to calculate the answer, but the model can end up with different answers—and receive different rewards—depending on the precision chosen for those constants (e.g. 9.8 m/s 2 vs 9.81 m/s 2 for gravitational acceleration). Because of these inconsistencies, the model may be sometimes rewarded and sometimes penalized for what is otherwise correct reasoning. To avoid this issue of noisy rewards, we rewrote questions to specify the precision, units, and any relevant constants needed. This ensured that if the model followed correct reasoning it always ended up at the gold answer.


#### Quality Filters

We applied extensive quality filters both to the question and to the answer.

Following Cheng et al. [71] , we filtered out questions where a strong model performed worse than a weak model, as these questions are more likely to have incorrect gold answers. It also served the purpose of informing whether or not deeper reasoning abilities are needed to solve a problem.

Questions where the answers are guessable pose a serious problem to the model. These questions can cause the model to learn taking a guess at the answer in its chain of thought, rather than arriving at it through sound derivation. Such behavior hinders the scaling of chain of thought tokens, as the model degenerates to a guess-based policy. Thus, we filtered out questions whose answer could be guessed without doing the work, such as yes/no or multiple choice; whose wording hands the reader a short list of candidate answers; where what is asked (such as a rank of a matrix, or finite outcome probability mass questions) leaves only a few possible answers; whose reference answer is unusually frequent across the full task pool (such as 0, 1, $\pi$ , etc.); which are answerable by reciting a memorized definition or formula; and which accidentally reveal or trivialize the answer.

On the other end of the difficulty spectrum, we found many questions to be broken. Similarly to guessable questions, broken questions can introduce noise into the rewards if the model can sometimes get the answer right by assuming things unstated in the question. Thus, we filtered out questions which refer to a figure, table, earlier sub-question, or passage that is not included; which are self-contradictory, missing an assumption, or open to more than one reading; or which otherwise leave out information needed to pin down a single answer.

The gold answers may themselves be incorrect. We leveraged several strategies to detect wrong answers, including: asking a model to review the gold answer and judge it as correct or incorrect; asking a model to re-derive the gold answer with tools; and asking several independent solvers to re-derive the gold answer and only accepting the question if they agreed.

We also filtered out questions whose text requested a format contradicting the standard training format ( \boxed{} ); which contained spurious characters or formatting defects; and those in non-English languages.

As described above, we restricted ourselves to short answers which could be graded by SymPy or a simple judge. To meet this requirement, we filtered questions asking for several separate answers at once (which cannot be checked as one right-or-wrong result) and those asking for a proof or written explanation (which require more sophisticated judge or rubric-based verification). We limited the length of model-generated answers to 200 characters (or, for the small fraction of tasks having long gold answers, 3 $\times$ the number of characters of the gold answer) to avoid hedging or other forms of reward hacking. We also found that some symbolic answers could not be parsed correctly by SymPy , and filtered these out. Finally, there were questions which could not be reliably graded by the judge (e.g., the judge would sometimes give different rewards to the same answer), and we filtered these out as well.

Questions that are very easy for the model can cause wasted inference compute during RL training, failing to provide non-zero gradients. To this end, we also filtered out questions which the initial checkpoint could solve frequently, with a low thinking budget.


#### Synthetic Pipelines

Online science forums such as the Physics, Chemistry and Biology StackExchange sites, as well as open-source science Q&A corpora, contain a large volume of expert-level questions, but we found that few of them are directly usable for RL: forum posts have no reference answer, questions often ask for an explanation or a derivation rather than a value, and many depend on context from the surrounding thread. We built a pipeline that turns this seed material into verifiable short-answer tasks.

The pipeline starts by extracting question-answer pairs. A model rewrites each forum post into one or more self-contained questions, turning requests for a proof, derivation or explanation into questions with a short, checkable answer (e.g. “derive the moment of inertia of a uniform disk” becomes “what is the moment of inertia of a uniform disk of mass M and radius R?”). It then synthesizes a concise answer from the replies and extracts a short reference answer (a number with units, a formula, a name, or a short phrase) grounded in the replies.

The remaining steps aim to make each question reliably gradable. Even when the reference answer is correct, grading is inconsistent whenever the question leaves room for equally correct answers: different units or precision, an unstated sign convention, or a synonym for a phrase answer. A model therefore reviews each question against its reference answer, and rewrites the question so that the reference becomes the unique correct answer, without changing what is asked or hinting at the value, or flags it as unrepairable. The rewritten question is then solved several times by a model, each attempt is graded by the same judge used in training, and a final diagnosis step reviews the question, the reference, and the graded attempts together. It distinguishes wrong references (several attempts agree against the reference), ambiguous questions (rejected attempts that are defensible under another reading) and judge noise (equivalent answers graded differently) from genuine difficulty.

Open-source math corpora are dominated by competition-style problems, and we also wanted a way to produce hard questions on the many topics that are underrepresented there. To this end, we developed a top-down pipeline that generates questions from a synthesized university curriculum ( Figure 22 ). Starting from a list of 38 course areas (e.g. measure theory, commutative algebra, random matrix theory), a model first designed a graduate-level course for each area, broken into 8 to 12 modules. It then wrote the teaching material for each module: a handful of precisely defined concepts, the main theorems and techniques, and synthesis links pointing out combinations of concepts that interact in non-obvious ways (e.g. how concepts such as Borel $\sigma$ -algebra, complete measure space, and Lebesgue $\sigma$ -algebra relate). The result was an inventory of several thousand concepts, each tied to a module and a course.

Questions were then generated by mixing and matching concepts from this inventory. A planner allocated the question budget across five question types of increasing scope (a single concept in depth, several concepts from one module, concepts from different modules of the same course, course-wide synthesis, and combinations across courses). For each planned combination, the model was asked to write one question built around exactly those concepts, subject to a set of constraints: the question had to be single-part and self-contained, had to have a numeric or closed-form answer that can be checked automatically, and had to be difficult because of the depth of reasoning required (rather than because of heavy notation or large numbers). The model also needed to solve its own question and revise it if a one- or two-line shortcut existed, and it had to account for every stated premise in its solution, so that the question carries no decorative conditions.

We generated additional expert-level mathematics tasks through an adversarial generation–solving–refinement loop. A generator combined a broad set of mathematical concepts into self-contained problems, while independent solvers exposed ambiguities, errors, and unintended shortcuts. Verification gates assessed reference-answer correctness and reasoning difficulty using derived solutions, solver attempts, and judging feedback. This feedback guided revisions to repair defects and strengthen conceptual demands.

We adapted this loop to research-level physics tasks grounded in scientific papers. Extracted calculations, assumptions, and reasoning outlines guided problem construction. Verification additionally checked consistency with the source material and whether each problem supplied sufficient information for an independent solution. Refinement addressed missing assumptions and discrepancies while preserving the underlying physics.

We used competitive-coding tasks to train algorithmic reasoning through executable feedback. Each task paired a problem statement with a test suite and comparison script, and asked the model to produce a runnable solution.

We ingested and assessed problems both from open-source corpora [ 72 , 73 ] and licensed from online coding platforms.

All the prompts of the tasks are language agnostic: they ask for code that receives inputs via stdin and emits outputs via stdout, and the test cases are simply files of inputs and expected outputs. This means that these tasks can theoretically be used to train the model in any language that supports stdin and stdout. However, the test cases and timeouts are often calibrated for a specific language; specifically, test cases calibrated for higher performance languages (such as C++) may become impossible for programs written in lower performance languages (such as Python). In these cases, we generated reference solutions in Python and excluded the task from Python candidacy if the reference solution runtime was out-of-distribution.

The final languages we chose to use for these tasks were Python and C++. The choice of which language a task was to use was randomly selected at runtime. All tasks were eligible to be completed in C++, and 70% of these were also eligible to be completed in Python. In a similar manner, we mixed in the ability for the model to use a bash tool, so the model learned both to write logically and syntactically correct problems without relying on code execution, as well as test its code when possible.

Verification extracted the final submitted program and ran it in an isolated grading sandbox with time limits. The task reward was binary: 1 only if every test passes, otherwise 0. Compilation errors, runtime failures, wrong outputs, and timeouts received no partial credit.

For quality screening, we used deterministic checks, judges,classifiers, reference execution, and deduplication. We checked that questions had a minimum number of test cases, that output comparison scripts aligned with prompt requirements (e.g. regarding case sensitivity, whitespace, numerical precision), that reference solutions passed in our environment, and that there were no broken prompts (e.g. prompts that rely on images or make requests about output contrary to the final reward, such as claiming partial scoring). Deduplication was conducted both using the MinHash algorithm on the problem statement as well as using agents to find canonical problem IDs where available. The main curation challenges were ensuring correct scoring semantics and separating genuine difficulty from broken prompts or harnesses.

The difficulty of these problems spanned nearly the entire spectrum, from trivial to national olympiad level, with the median task being roughly equivalent to what would be classified as “hard” in LiveCodeBench. At the beginning of training, only roughly 10% of these problems were reliably solved by the model; this increased to roughly 70% by the end of training.

To teach the model how to find information on the internet, we sourced a number of high-quality and difficult multi-hop web search tasks. Each task consisted of a question, “gold” answer, and web search tools which accessed the internet via a third-party search provider.


#### Tasks

We began by sourcing web search tasks from open-source corpora, vendors, and a synthetic pipeline based on InfoSeek [ 74 ] . The open-source tasks came from OpenSeeker [ 75 ] , whose questions are similar to InfoSeek’s but obfuscated with multiple constraints per answer, and from DeepDive [ 76 ] , a mix of questions based on Wikipedia (and other knowledge-graph sources) and academic publications. Our tasks covered a broad range of topics, including history, art, finance, sports, science & technology, media, law, and more.

All search environments exposed two tools to the model: a search tool and a browse tool. The search tool accepts a query string and returns URLs along with page titles and snippets that preview relevant page content, with content averaging around 200 characters per snippet. The browse tool accepts a URL and returns the page rendered as Markdown, capped at 8K tokens. It also supports arguments from the command-line tool ripgrep [ 77 ] . When supplied, these arguments are applied to the full Markdown representation, allowing the model to search within the page and retrieve targeted excerpts.

We used a regular expression to extract the excerpt of the agent’s final message starting with “The answer is:” and ending at a newline or citation. We assigned rewards based on this excerpt using a three-stage procedure:

- 1. Exact Match: We normalized the proposed and reference answers and compared them. An exact match received a reward of one. Answers that could not be extracted received a reward of zero.

- 2. Length Check: If the answers did not match, we assigned a reward of zero when the proposed answer exceeded max(150, len(gold_answer) * 4.5) . This discouraged increasingly verbose answers that hedged or offered multiple alternatives.

- 3. Model-Based Judgment: Answers that passed the length check were evaluated by a judge for equivalence to the reference answer. The judging prompt included few-shot examples illustrating how to handle ambiguity and reject hedged answers.


#### Quality and Difficulty Filtering

We excluded questions that met any of the following criteria:

We dropped questions containing answer-revealing text, as determined by lexical string comparison and judge-based checks. We also examined URLs searched by the model, and implemented blocklists to target URLs which exposed the question/answer pair. We used a two-layered approach: (1) explicitly flagging URL patterns corresponding to known answer dumps, including datasets, papers, and blogs; (2) a dynamic blocklist that fuzzy matched both question and answer in a page.

We dropped questions which could be answered by a model without access to search tools, indicating questions whose answers likely appear frequently on the internet and may be memorized.

We dropped questions whose solution paths relied on websites which our search tool could not access. For example, not all search engines use the same index, and some questions required pages that were not present in the index of our search provider. Some pages were also inaccessible despite being indexed; e.g. gated behind a CAPTCHA.

We also dropped questions for which a model, given both the question and gold answer, judged the answer invalid. Similarly, we also filtered questions where a model preferred a common alternative answer over the gold answer, where it judged the question contradictory (because neither answer satisfied its constraints), or where it judged the question underspecified (because both answers satisfied its constraints). We identified common alternative answers by sampling multiple rollouts per question and extracting answers that occurred multiple times.

Sometimes, the judges graded equivalent answers differently, accepted substantively different answers as correct, or exhibited subjectivity in its judgments. We dropped any question that triggered this inconsistent behavior in the judge.

We excluded questions whose primary language was not English, as determined by a language-model classifier.

We retained tasks for which a strong model’s solve rate exceeded a weak model’s by at least 12.5 percentage points, or the weak model’s solve rate was zero. This was to ensure that all questions contained reward signal; questions for which a weak model outperforms a strong model often indicate a problem such as an incorrect but plausible answer.


#### Synthetic Pipeline

In addition to open-source and vendor tasks, we experimented with synthetic task generation following InfoSeek [ 74 ] , in which a planner agent and a browser agent build a question backwards from a single Wikipedia answer entity, adding hops and constraints until the answer is uniquely identified. The main differences between our pipeline and the original InfoSeek pipeline were:

- 1. Our pipeline truncated each article to 20K tokens, instead of the original 1K.

- 2. We required answers to have a Wikipedia page exceeding 200 words.

- 3. We implemented retries for validation failures.

- 4. We further filtered questions through a multi-stage grading funnel. This grading funnel included all the quality and difficulty filters described in the previous section, plus two additional filters. First, we also asked another model to validate whether the question was valid given the claims from the parent/children trees built during the synthesis pipeline. Second, given the source pages built during the synthesis pipeline, we asked another model to judge whether the answer was grounded in the supporting evidence.

Although we found tasks generated by the InfoSeek pipeline to be useful earlier in our development cycle, we found that by the end of Beam’s training they were largely too easy.

Beyond terminal and software-engineering tasks, we trained Beam on a broader class of professional work. Frontier agents need to do more than write code: they need to understand source material, use the right tools, and produce the artifacts that real organizations rely on, including reports, spreadsheets, presentations, and other business documents. Our real-world corporate tasks were built around this idea: we put the agent in a realistic professional setting and asked it to deliver the kind of finished work one would expect from someone in that job ( Figure 24 ).

When producing artifacts like reports or presentations, there is usually no simple unit test to check whether the output is right: it has to make sense, be consistent, be backed up by evidence, and be useful in practice. The central technical challenge was therefore how to evaluate and reward this kind of work. Since we could not run a test suite and get a pass or fail, we used an agentic judge to inspect the final deliverables and decide whether they were high-quality and correct. This changed how we approached task design, reward construction, and defense against reward hacking compared to classic software engineering.

#### Sourcing

We began with a broad pool of tasks covering a wide range of occupations and industry sectors, with an emphasis on realistic artifact-producing professional work. Tasks came from both our synthetic generation pipeline and external vendors, and were filtered through our quality, difficulty, and integrity checks.

The largest sectors in the final pool were finance & insurance, health care & social assistance, and professional, scientific & technical services. Required deliverables included .docx , .xlsx , .md , .csv , .pdf , and .pptx , alongside other formats. Many tasks required multiple output formats, and tasks were evaluated against weighted rubric criteria, including penalty criteria for undesirable outputs.

Every task was authored as a Harbor task directory and ships as a digest-pinned container image built from its environment, together with its source assets.


#### Synthetic Task Generation

Our synthetic knowledge-work tasks were produced by the same three-stage recipe as our terminal tasks ( Figure 20 ), re-grounded in professional work. Seeds were real professional documents drawn from a curated web corpus, each paired with an occupation sampled from the pre-defined skill set and taxonomy (sector, then occupation group, then O*NET code). An authoring agent working in a Docker-enabled sandbox then built the complete task folder in one episode: a manager-voice instruction, two to five reference documents with the seed preserved byte-for-byte, golden deliverables, the weighted rubric, and the environment image. As with terminal tasks, every submission had to clear a quality gate of static checks plus a tool-using a judge: eight deterministic validators and nine judged criteria spanning professional meaningfulness, difficulty, rubric coverage, and ground-truth protection. Per-criterion feedback was returned to the authoring agent for up to ten revision rounds. Because authoring and in-loop judging shared a model family, accepted tasks additionally passed an independent majority-vote quality filter from a different model family (three votes over five defect classes, retaining roughly 42% of published tasks), before the solvability screening.

Each task was packaged in Harbor format as a reproducible workspace containing the task instructions, source artifacts, and the tools and applications needed for execution, so the agent operated in a controlled environment that could be reproduced consistently across validation and training. Agent execution took place in an untrusted sandbox, while grading was performed separately in a trusted evaluation sandbox by a tool-using judge that inspected generated files, verified calculations and sources, and applied the task-specific rubric.

We designed both the task and how we evaluated it at the same time. Rubrics were authored alongside each task by decomposing the intended professional deliverable into atomic, independently verifiable criteria spanning content, structure, functionality, and presentation. We calibrated their coverage and granularity so that distinct facts, calculations, comparisons, recommendations, and failure modes could be scored separately, while weighting each criterion according to its professional consequence rather than simply its level of detail. Rubrics were then further refined through downstream quality checks and adversarial analysis (especially white-box attacking): grader failures, reward-hacking traces, and model rollouts were used to identify criteria that were underspecified, overly coarse, redundant, or vulnerable to shortcuts, and to improve both the rubric and grader before training.

A tool-using judge evaluated each submitted artifact against the resulting rubric by inspecting files, checking calculations and source material, and scoring each criterion independently. We then aggregated these criterion-level judgments and applied a threshold selected through extensive experiments for training stability and learning effectiveness; submissions that met the threshold received a binary reward of 1, while those that did not received 0.


#### Quality Control

Tasks passed through a staged and heuristic-based quality-control and derisking funnel before entering training.

We first applied rubric-level quality checks for coverage, atomicity, observability, non-duplication, and appropriate weighting.

Next, we adversarially probed the surviving tasks using both white-box and black-box attackers. White-box attackers were given the task together with its rubric and grading specification, allowing them to directly probe weaknesses in rubric design and identify ways to earn credit without achieving the intended goal. Black-box attackers saw only the solver-facing task and environment, and were used primarily to test the intrinsic quality of the task itself, including ambiguity, missing information, unintended shortcuts, and other specification gaps. Separate rubric and goal judges then evaluated the same frozen deliverables: the rubric judge checked whether the stated criteria were satisfied, while the goal judge independently checked whether the artifact actually achieved the underlying professional objective. Disagreement between the two exposed potentially underspecified or exploitable tasks.

Candidate tasks were subsequently validated through model rollouts to establish empirical solvability, followed by RL derisking runs under the same reward setup used for training. Failures discovered at later stages fed back into the earlier quality-control process: problematic tasks were revised or removed, while rubric criteria and grader checks were updated to close the observed gaps before the task was re-evaluated.

Letting a judge assign rewards introduces its own failure modes. An agent may produce outputs that superficially satisfy the rubric without completing the intended work, exploit weaknesses in how evidence is judged, or attempt to influence the evaluator through the artifact or environment. Reward integrity therefore depends on the model, task environment, and evaluator—not just the scoring prompt. We addressed this by auditing both the final deliverable and the trajectory used to produce it. During training, an online integrity judge checked whether the policy earned, or attempted to earn, rubric credit through shortcuts such as unsupported provenance, surface-level criterion gaming, or evaluator manipulation; detected cases received zero reward. Offline audits then inspected artifacts and trajectories for new or ambiguous failure modes and fed those findings back into the task, rubric, and online judge. Tasks with credible shortcuts or evaluator exploits were removed from subsequent training mixtures until they were repaired and revalidated.

To teach the model to perform general tool use, we sourced single- and multi-turn tasks requiring the model to call a diverse set of tools. Our tool use tasks involved a prompt, a set of environment-provided tools, a set of artifacts, and either a rubric, a rule-based verifier, and/or an expected environment state-hash. Tasks were provided to the agent in Harbor format [ 63 ] . Across all tasks the agent had the opportunity to interact with a total of 1,107 unique tools, with individual tasks exposing 61.9 tools per task on average. To further increase the model’s exposure to a wide range of tools, we also supplemented the SFT set described in Section 4.1.1 with SFT traces drawn from open-source corpora.

A subset of SFT traces supporting general tool use capabilities were included in the agentic teacher’s SFT phase ( Section 4.1.1 ), while the full set were used for the alignment teacher. The RL tasks were used in training of the alignment teacher ( Section 4.2 ), rather than the agentic teacher.


#### Sourcing

We began with tasks both from an open-source corpus [ 78 ] and from proprietary vendor deliveries covering enterprise workflows (manufacturing, finance, insurance, legal and revenue operations), consumer and productivity applications, and multi-turn conversational assistance.

Our SFT dataset consisted of traces which were either sourced from open source SFT sets [ 79 , 80 ] or generated synthetically from rollouts on our RL tasks, as in Section 4.1.1 . Some of the open-source traces lacked reasoning, which we backfilled. We also applied our trace-level graders, described below. For traces generated from our RL tasks, we only kept rollouts that earned a full reward, passed our trace graders, and fit in a 64K context, with at most three traces per task.


#### Quality and Difficulty Filtering

We used graders and programmatic checks to ensure that our RL tasks and SFT traces were high quality. Task-level graders decided whether an RL task was high quality; trace-level graders decided whether a specific rollout could be imitated in SFT. We developed graders to check for the following pathologies.

As in other domains, a task whose answer can be produced without doing the work is a reward-hacking behavior we wish to avoid completely. We required that the expected answer was not guessable from the prompt alone, and that tasks did not pass if the environment was unchanged. We also checked that the simulated user could never reveal the expected answer by checking both the task’s held-back details and rollouts generated from the task.

We filtered out tasks where the verifier demanded things the instruction never stated, and where the reward was unreasonable given the initial user requirements. Where golden solutions were available, we also checked that by replaying the golden solutions we could obtain a reward of 1 and score 0 when no actions were taken.

A judge read the prompts, rubrics and example trace tool calls to flag tasks whose requirements were not covered by its reward function or whose reward function could not be inferred from the task—if it introduced an unrequested requirement, if its tools were insufficient, if its reward function criteria were vague or unscorable, or if its request was unclear or contradictory.

A trace had to be structurally valid: reasoning on every assistant turn, only known tools called, arguments that validated against the tool schema, and every call answered exactly once. A tool-use judge then checked instruction adherence, that reported facts were grounded in tool outputs, and that reasoning was consistent with the action taken. A conduct grader additionally rejected traces that pasted raw tool output or made useless calls or repeated tool calls. We also checked for broken reward functions at a trace level, ensuring that a rollout was earned by a genuine completion of the task and not a shortcut. Any missing reasoning was backfilled (as described in Section 4.1.11 ) and then graded to ensure causal consistency. We also checked to ensure that any reasoning or assistant messages were in the same language as the original user message.


#### Training Considerations

Tasks were graded via programmatic rules and/or rubrics. In all cases, a score of one was assigned only when the task passed all scoring criteria; there was no partial credit.

A task’s tools were presented in one of two ways. With passthrough, every tool was advertised under its own name with its full JSON schema in the developer prompt, which gave the policy typed schemas immediately and ate into the model’s context length. With the meta-tool, only three tools were advertised: a listing tool that returned a compact registry (one line per tool, required parameters marked), a describe tool that returned full descriptions and typed parameters on demand, and a call tool that routed the invocation and rejected unknown names. This resembled some common harnesses. During RL we presented tools through one of the two surfaces, chosen with equal probability.


#### Task Distribution Analysis

We classified tool surfaces into the 27 categories from Xu et al. [79] with a classifier over each task’s tool set. The open-source SFT corpora are broad but skew toward consumer categories (weather, web search, travel, e-commerce), whereas the RL tasks concentrated in business and enterprise categories (database operations, finance, customer relationship management, productivity, legal).

While all of our tasks were multi-step (i.e., requiring multiple tool calls), some of our tasks were additionally multi-turn (i.e., involving multiple user messages). Single-turn tasks (41%) contained a single user message where the user stated the request once and the agent worked autonomously until it stopped calling tools or submitted. The remainder (59%) were multi-turn tasks in which the assistant asked follow-up questions to a simulated user, which was implemented either as a scripted persona or as a judge persona that disclosed details when asked.
