## Wave Forcing: Towards Speed-of-Light Streaming Video Generation

Real-time long-video generation requires continuous output with consistent appearance and natural motion. Existing chunk-wise autoregressive methods face challenges in both generation efficiency and long-video quality: serialized denoising and history-cache refresh require two forward passes even with one-step denoising, and longer rollouts can degrade quality and cause camera drift. We propose Wave Forcing (WF) , which co-designs generation, training, and execution for continuous video output with multi-step denoising. WF uses chunk-wise causal attention to access earlier, cleaner chunks before their denoising is complete, and distributes staggered denoising and cache-refresh stages across full-model replicas to achieve 1F1C : one output chunk per concurrent forward round after pipeline fill. To adapt the model to this continuous generation process, we develop a three-stage training procedure that successively introduces mixed-noise denoising, motion supervision, and causal adaptation using self-generated history. To execute this process efficiently, Wave Runtime (WaveRT) uses Wave Parallelism (WP) for early K/V delivery and communication–computation overlap, together with optimized kernels and pipelined VAE decoding. Experiments on Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B show that 5-step WF maintains overall quality comparable to Rolling Forcing while achieving higher Dynamic Degree on both short and long videos. Our system efficiency experiments show that 4-step WF achieves 147.6 DiT FPS and 134.8 end-to-end FPS on eight H200 GPUs, yielding $\mathbf{7.07\times}$ the single-GPU end-to-end throughput. In DiT-only tests with matched SageAttention+W8A8 backends, denoising steps, and GPU counts, WP delivers up to $\mathbf{2.53\times}$ the throughput of the faster of the Ulysses and Ring context-parallel baselines across six model scales (1.3B–14B).


### 1 Introduction

> Figure 1: Causal wavefront and continuous output ( $S=4$ ). (a) Denoising and store advance on anti-diagonals, with dashed cells marking the next round. (b) One later-layer K/V edge arrives before use. Other edges and initial waits are omitted. (c) Both schedules retain four denoising forwards plus store per chunk. Over five rounds, one SF replica emits one chunk, whereas five WF replicas emit five after fill. Wall-clock gains depend on stage costs and overlap.

Real-time streaming video generation must sustain playable output with consistent appearance and natural motion. Chunk-wise autoregressive methods such as Self Forcing (SF) and Causal Forcing (CF) denoise each chunk conditioned on preceding content ( Huang et al., 2025a ; Zhu et al., 2026b ) . Under the clean-history caching convention adopted in this work, an additional forward pass, termed store , refreshes the key/value (K/V) history from the clean result. Sequential execution places denoising and store between consecutive outputs, yielding NF1C (N Forward Rounds, 1 Chunk Output) . Thus, even one-step denoising requires two forward passes when store is included.

We target 1F1C : one completed chunk per concurrent forward round after pipeline fill, while retaining every denoising update. Inspired by wavefront scheduling ( Jin et al., 2016 ) , earlier chunks execute later denoising stages while newer chunks execute earlier ones. These staggered tasks form a moving wavefront (Fig. 1 a). Assigning a full-model replica to each denoising stage and the store stage creates a bounded-width pipeline (Fig. 1 c).

To advance chunks before their predecessors finish, we use chunk-wise causal context : full attention within each chunk, cached history, and live context from earlier, cleaner chunks, with no dependence on later chunks. This directional conditioning accommodates differences in stage-wise progress and enables overlapped execution (Fig. 1 b). Our staged training builds on mixed-noise joint denoising ( Liu et al., 2026 ) , uses teacher videos to supervise motion and framing, and adapts the model to causal inference with self-generated history. This generation structure and its training recipe together constitute Wave Forcing (WF) .

To turn this scheduling potential into shorter output intervals, Wave Runtime (WaveRT) implements Wave Parallelism (WP) . Following GPU software-pipelining principles ( NVIDIA, 2026b ; NVIDIA, 2026a ) , WP exploits differences in stage-wise progress for early layer-wise K/V delivery and communication–computation overlap, reducing exposed dependency waits. Data placement, kernel optimization, and load-balanced VAE pipeline parallelism complete the execution path from concurrent denoising to sustained video output .

On Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B ( Wan et al., 2025 ) , 5-step WF achieves overall quality comparable to 5-step Rolling Forcing (RF), with VBench Total scores of 81.64 versus 81.15 and VBench-Long Quality scores of 81.26 versus 81.18 ( Huang et al., 2024 ; Huang et al., 2026b ) . Dynamic Degree increases from 51.39 to 94.44 on short videos and from 36.63 to 91.45 on 100-second videos. These gains are accompanied by better Subject Preservation (97.10 versus RF’s 92.75) while maintaining low Camera Drift (0.37 versus RF’s 0.17) .

In terms of system performance, 4-step WF with the BF16/FA3 backend achieves 147.6 DiT FPS and 134.8 end-to-end FPS on eight H200 GPUs, yielding a $\mathbf{7.07\times}$ end-to-end speedup over the 19.06-FPS single-GPU baseline (Table 4 ). At this denoising speed, residual output-path overhead remains visible even with three-stage VAE pipelining, as discussed in Section 7 . Across six model scales from 1.3B to 14B, WP outperforms the faster context-parallel (CP) baseline at five scales under BF16 and all six under Sage+W8A8, achieving $\mathbf{1.025\text{--}2.530\times}$ its throughput under Sage+W8A8. System ablations and profiling further analyze how communication overlap, K/V placement, compute backends, and VAE stage balance affect runtime performance.

Our contributions are threefold:

- • Causal wavefront generation. We introduce Wave Forcing, allowing newer chunks to condition on earlier, cleaner chunks before their denoising completes. By pipelining denoising and clean-history cache refresh across full-model replicas, WF enables one completed chunk per concurrent forward round (1F1C) after pipeline fill while retaining all denoising updates.

- • Training for continuous generation. We develop a staged training recipe combining mixed-noise initialization, teacher-guided motion supervision, and causal adaptation with self-generated history. This adapts the model to wavefront inference, enabling strong motion and subject preservation over long rollouts at overall quality comparable to Rolling Forcing.

- • Efficient streaming execution. We build Wave Runtime to realize wavefront concurrency through early layer-wise K/V delivery, communication–computation overlap, and direct placement into attention buffers. With optimized local computation and load-balanced VAE decoding, the runtime translates stage concurrency into sustained end-to-end throughput, with efficiency validated across 1.3B–14B models.


### 2 Preliminaries


##### Chunk denoising and store.

A VAE decodes latent-frame chunks into video ( Wan et al., 2025 ) . Following SF ( Huang et al., 2025a ) , chunk $i$ undergoes $S$ history-conditioned denoising updates, from noise $Z_{i}^{(0)}$ to clean output $B_{i}=Z_{i}^{(S)}$ . At noise timesteps $\nu_{0}>\cdots>\nu_{S}=0$ ,

[Tableau] $$Z_{i}^{(s+1)}=\Psi_{\nu_{s+1}}\!\left(G_{\theta}(Z_{i}^{(s)},\nu_{s}\mid\mathcal{C}_{i,s}),\epsilon_{i,s}\right),\qquad 0\leq s<S,$$ (1)

$G_{\theta}$ predicts clean latents, which $\Psi_{\nu}$ re-noises using draw $\epsilon_{i,s}$ , with $\Psi_{0}(x,\epsilon)=x$ . $\mathcal{C}_{i,s}$ denotes layer-wise K/V context, with text conditioning omitted for brevity. Denoising K/V represents the noisy input, so our clean-history convention adds store : a full-model forward on $B_{i}$ that retains K/V and discards the prediction. Sequential generation thus uses $N=S+1$ forwards per chunk (NF1C).


##### Wavefront states and execution rounds.

At filled runtime tick $k$ , the denoising inputs are

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{F}_{k}=\{Z_{k}^{(0)},Z_{k-1}^{(1)},\ldots,Z_{k-S+1}^{(S-1)}\}.$$ (2)

Adding store at $s=S$ gives $\mathcal{W}_{k}=\mathcal{F}_{k}\cup\{B_{k-S}\}$ , of width at most $S+1$ excluding anchor/history caches. Each round advances active stages and admits fresh noise. With stage-owned replicas, this schedule permits 1F1C after fill without changing per-chunk work.


### 3 Wave Forcing

We introduce Wave Forcing (WF) to advance successive chunks before their predecessors finish denoising. WF couples a causal wavefront—newer, noisier chunks read earlier, cleaner states, but not vice versa—with training for this mixed-noise context and self-generated history.


#### 3.1 A bounded-width causal wavefront

Cell $(i,s)$ , $s<S$ , in Fig. 1 a updates $Z_{i}^{(s)}$ to $Z_{i}^{(s+1)}$ in round

[Tableau] $$k=i+s,\qquad i=k-s.$$ (3)

Store at $(k-S,S)$ refreshes $B_{k-S}$ ’s K/V. For $N_{\mathrm{chunk}}$ requested chunks, active denoising stages are $\mathcal{A}_{k}=\{s:0\leq s<S,\ 0\leq k-s<N_{\mathrm{chunk}}\}$ . Store is active when $0\leq k-S<N_{\mathrm{chunk}}$ .

WF retains full attention within each chunk and uses chunk-wise causal attention across video time. Query slot $s$ sees K/V slot $j$ iff

[Tableau] $$M_{\mathrm{WF}}(s,j)=\mathbf{1}[j\geq s],\qquad s,j\in\mathcal{A}_{k}.$$ (4)

In chunk order, $i^{\prime}=k-j\leq i=k-s$ gives a lower-triangular mask over self and all cleaner predecessors .

At Transformer layer $\ell\in\{1,\ldots,L\}$ , the ordered context is

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{C}_{k,s}^{\mathrm{WF},\ell}=A_{k}^{\ell}\oplus H_{k}^{\ell}\oplus U_{k}^{\ell}\oplus\Bigl[\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\mathcal{A}_{k}\\ j\geq s\end{subarray}}(K_{k,j}^{\ell},V_{k,j}^{\ell})\Bigr],$$ (5)

Here $\oplus$ concatenates initial-chunk anchor $A_{k}^{\ell}$ , bounded clean history $H_{k}^{\ell}$ , live store $U_{k}^{\ell}$ , and denoising K/V in increasing $j$ (self first). Store appears once, is empty when inactive, and enters history after the round. Appendix B details fill/drain and caching.

This removes Rolling Forcing’s (RF) reverse refinement ( Liu et al., 2026 ) . Without post-training, short-video Quality and Dynamic Degree fall in our evaluation (Sec. 5.1 ), motivating adaptation.


#### 3.2 Training for the deployment graph

The causal mask removes access to later, noisier active chunks, changing RF’s learned conditioning. We adapt Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B ( Wan et al., 2025 ) through mixed-noise initialization, teacher guidance, and on-policy training (Fig. 2 ). The 4-step student starts from SF’s ODE initialization, whereas the 5-step student reuses released RF weights and skips Stage 1.

> Figure 2: Training flow. Stage 1 applies DMD to one sampled RF or SF rollout. Stage 2 re-noises teacher chunks and applies DMD and LPIPS to the same prediction $\widehat{Y}_{\theta}$ . Stage 3 samples one self-generated RF, WF, or SF rollout for on-policy DMD.


##### Stage 1: mixed-noise initialization.

We adopt RF’s mixed-noise joint-denoising setup ( Liu et al., 2026 ) , using bidirectional attention across chunks at different noise levels. We sample either an RF rollout or an SF rollout with equal probability and train the student with distribution matching distillation (DMD) ( Yin et al., 2024 ) . SF rollouts help stabilize motion during initialization.


##### Stage 2: off-policy DMD.

Following LPM 1.0 ( Zeng et al., 2026 ) , we re-noise consecutive teacher chunks $Y_{\mathrm{win}}$ at staggered timesteps, with preceding teacher chunks as clean K/V history. Retaining Stage 1’s bidirectional attention, this supplies stronger motion and subject guidance (Sec. 5.2 ). DMD and LPIPS ( Zhang et al., 2018 ) supervise the same text-conditioned prediction $\widehat{Y}_{\theta}$ :

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{2}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{DMD}}(\widehat{Y}_{\theta};\mathbf{c})+\lambda_{\mathrm{p}}\operatorname{LPIPS}\!\left(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{perc}}(\widehat{Y}_{\theta}),\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{perc}}(Y_{\mathrm{win}})\right)\right],\qquad\lambda_{\mathrm{p}}=0.25,$$ (6)

Here $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{DMD}}$ is the DMD objective, and $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{perc}}$ decodes downsampled latents to sampled RGB frames.


##### Stage 3: causal context and self-generated history.

Each iteration uniformly samples a self-generated RF, WF, or SF rollout for on-policy DMD. WF uses Eq. ( 5 ), including store-refreshed history from its own outputs. RF and SF provide complementary training distributions, but only WF is deployed. For branch objective $\mathcal{L}_{b}$ ,

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{3}=\mathbb{E}_{b\sim\mathrm{Unif}\{\mathrm{RF},\mathrm{WF},\mathrm{SF}\}}[\mathcal{L}_{b}]=\tfrac{1}{3}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RF}}+\tfrac{1}{3}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{WF}}+\tfrac{1}{3}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SF}}.$$ (7)

Appendix A gives stage transitions and implementation details. Together, these stages adapt the model to causal wavefront inference: chunks denoise using cleaner, still-evolving predecessors and self-generated history.


### 4 Wave Runtime

WF exposes stage concurrency, but K/V dependencies can still stall attention. Wave Runtime (WaveRT) realizes Wave Parallelism (WP) by publishing K/V early from stage-owned replicas and overlapping delivery with computation when cleaner producers run ahead. We use SGLang Diffusion’s model implementation as the core execution backend ( Zheng et al., 2024 ) of WaveRT.

> Figure 3: Hiding K/V delivery with identical schematic operation costs. (a) Layer barriers expose communication. (b) Early publication hides later waits, not dependencies or the initial wait. (c) Direct writes to receiver slots precede readiness signals and consumption.


#### 4.1 Stage placement and dependencies

Each denoising stage and store owns a full-model GPU replica. Denoising rank $s$ updates $Z_{k-s}^{(s)}$ and passes it to rank $s+1$ for the next round (Fig. 1 ). Within a round, let $P_{s,\ell}$ produce Q/K/V and RoPE, and $C_{s,\ell}$ perform attention and the remaining block:

[Tableau] $\displaystyle P_{s,\ell}\rightarrow C_{s,\ell},\qquad C_{s,\ell}\rightarrow P_{s,\ell+1}\quad(\ell<L),$ (8) $\displaystyle P_{j,\ell}\rightarrow C_{s,\ell},\qquad j>s,\quad s,j\in\mathcal{A}_{k}.$ (9)

At each layer, local projection can proceed without waiting for remote K/V, but attention must wait until store and all legal cleaner ranks, including nonadjacent ranks, have supplied their K/V. RF additionally depends on noisier ranks, introducing reverse edges that WF removes through its causal mask. The synchronized reference enforces these dependencies with a per-layer all-rank K/V gather.


#### 4.2 Producer lead and asynchronous delivery

In a filled round, rank $s$ reads $N_{C}$ query tokens and $N_{s}$ keys, where

[Tableau] $$N_{s}=N_{\mathrm{prefix}}+(S-s)N_{C}.$$ (10)

Here $N_{C}$ is tokens per chunk and $N_{\mathrm{prefix}}$ covers anchor, history, and store. Cleaner ranks read fewer keys, permitting later-layer producer lead. WaveRT publishes K/V after QKV projection and RoPE, then checks readiness immediately before attention (Fig. 3 ).

On a common time base, edge $e$ has payload-ready time $t_{e}^{\mathrm{ready}}$ , consumer local-ready time $t_{e}^{\mathrm{need}}$ before remote waits, and delivery cost $\tau_{e}$ including queueing, transfer, and signaling:

[Tableau] $$\Delta_{e}=t_{e}^{\mathrm{need}}-t_{e}^{\mathrm{ready}},\qquad w_{e}=\max(0,\tau_{e}-\Delta_{e}).$$ (11)

A consumer’s exposed wait is the maximum $w_{e}$ over required inputs. Thus, delivery is fully hidden only when every $\tau_{e}\leq\Delta_{e}$ (Appendix B.7 ).

Following software-pipeline principles ( NVIDIA, 2026b ) , paged delivery writes K/V directly into ordered attention slots and signals readiness before use, avoiding remote concatenation. When weights, noise draws, positions, and local operators are held fixed, attention consumes the same K/V values as in synchronized execution (Appendix B.6 ). Appendix B details buffer lifetimes and readiness signaling.


#### 4.3 Local computation and streaming decode

> Figure 4: Streaming VAE schedules. Panels (a,b) compare blocking and asynchronous decoding with matched costs. Panels (c,d) compare unbalanced and load-balanced PP under identical workloads and arrivals, with darker teal indicating longer stage service times. Panel (e) shows profile-guided contiguous decoder partitions. Each comparison pair uses its own schematic time scale, with transfers omitted and partition boundaries shown schematically. The first output incurs pipeline fill latency (Appendix B.5 ) .

Local computation and K/V delivery require joint optimization. SageAttention ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) and W8A8 target faster local operators but alter producer–consumer progress. K/V quantization reduces payloads, and paged writes reduce receiver-side context assembly. The goal is K/V readiness before each layer’s attention, reducing exposed communication and synchronization waits .

Asynchronous decoding overlaps VAE and DiT, but the slower pipeline limits E2E throughput. We use profiled streaming times to balance contiguous VAE partitions across dedicated GPUs while preserving request-local causal caches (Fig. 4 ). Decoding and inter-stage transfers must sustain DiT’s output rate. The first decoded output still incurs pipeline fill latency (Appendix B.5 ).

WP uses full-model replicas, with execution width determined by the denoising-step count. Logical K/V fan-out remains quadratic in the number of stages, while attention workloads vary across stages. Appendices B.8 and B.4 quantify communication traffic and pipeline fill/drain costs, respectively.


### 5 Experiments


##### Table notation.

In all experimental tables, teal marks the best value for each metric within its comparison group, and coral marks unusable outcomes.


##### Models and training.

We evaluate 4-step and 5-step Wave Forcing built on Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B ( Wan et al., 2025 ) against Rolling Forcing ( Liu et al., 2026 ) and Causal Forcing ( Zhu et al., 2026b ) . All methods retain their native denoising schedules and three latent frames per chunk. Training and checkpoint details appear in Sec. 3.2 and Appendices A and C .


##### Evaluation.

For short videos, we report VBench ( Huang et al., 2024 ) Total, Quality, Semantic, and Dynamic Degree, generating one 129-frame video per original prompt. For long videos, we report VBench-Long Quality and Dynamic Degree on 200 MovieGen ( Polyak et al., 2024 ) prompts, generating 100-second videos. All videos use $832\times 480$ resolution, 16 FPS, and seed 42, and benchmark scores follow official normalization and weighting. Tab. 1 summarizes the results, with the full protocol in Appendix C .


##### Long-video stability diagnostics.

High motion scores can coexist with subject exit or unidirectional camera drift. We assess these failures on a fixed 100-prompt, subject-focused MovieGen subset shared across methods and training ablations. Subject Preservation measures visibility of the SAM3-tracked ( Carion et al., 2026 ) principal entity, with boundary weighting for partial disappearance. Camera Drift penalizes a subject’s approach to a frame boundary only with persistent outward background motion in the same direction. Appendix C details prompt selection and both metrics.


##### Compute configurations.

Execution tests use BF16 with FlashAttention-3 (FA3) ( Shah et al., 2024 ) as reference. Sage uses SageAttention ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) with BF16 linear layers, while Sage+W8A8 adds 8-bit linear weights and activations. Both Sage tiers use FA3 cross-attention. These labels describe local computation, not K/V transport precision.

#### 5.3 End-to-end inference efficiency

We measure 4-step WaveRT at the resolution of $832\times 480$ on a single $8\times$ H200 141-GB node, allocating four GPUs to denoising, one to store, and three to VAE decoding. These runs use randomly initialized (dummy) DiT weights, since the corresponding pretrained weights are unavailable and we lack sufficient compute resources to adequately train models at the 14B scale. We report FPS as 1593 decoded frames divided by the median elapsed time over three runs and compute tick p50 from pooled steady-state ticks. DiT timing covers diffusion, communication, store, and pipeline fill/drain, while E2E timing extends through VAE completion. Because VAE decoding overlaps DiT execution, the per-run gap between these timings measures the exposed decoding tail. Loading and file encoding are excluded (Appendix C.3 ) .

### 6 Related Work


##### Efficient streaming video generation.

Self Forcing ( Huang et al., 2025a ) reduces exposure bias by training autoregressive video diffusion on self-generated histories. SGF ( Zhuang et al., 2026 ) restores gradient supervision for historical K/V through two-pass replay, while SGF+ ( Su et al., 2026b ) separates context-writing and denoising parameters to reduce gradient conflict. Other approaches include few-step causal distillation ( Yin et al., 2025 ; Zhu et al., 2026b ) and mixed-noise denoising ( Liu et al., 2026 ) . AnyFlow ( Gu et al., 2026 ) learns two-time flow maps with on-policy distillation for variable sampling budgets; Mask Forcing ( Zhao et al., 2026b ) perturbs DMD self-rollouts with dual-noise masking to improve teacher-mode coverage. For long-horizon streaming, Helios ( Yuan et al., 2026 ) compresses historical and noisy context and simulates drifting during training. Vidu S1 ( Zhang et al., 2026c ) uses stage-aware noisy and clean history caches for real-time, voice-controlled character generation. Vidu S2 ( Zhang et al., 2026b ) introduces Self-Replay Forcing and supports dynamic references and streaming editing. These methods motivate studying training objectives together with the context available during streaming inference.


##### Multi-chunk generation.

Blockwise diffusion also appears in language modeling and action generation. D2F ( Wang et al., 2025 ) conditions language generation on partially denoised predecessors, while MBD-LMs ( Jin et al., 2026 ) align training with concurrent decoding of a bounded set of blocks at heterogeneous noise levels. In vision-language-action models, BlockVLA ( Wang et al., 2026c ) adapts pretrained autoregressive models through block diffusion finetuning, and TBD-VLA ( Lee et al., 2026 ) organizes actions into temporal blocks and supports asynchronous chunk execution through temporal inpainting. Multimodal Diffusion Forcing ( Huang et al., 2025c ) assigns noise levels across time and modalities to model dependencies within multimodal robot trajectories. These approaches illustrate different choices of block dependencies; WF specifically trains video chunks to consume cleaner intermediate context through a directed causal wavefront.


##### Parallel execution.

Ulysses ( Jacobs et al., 2023 ) and Ring ( Liu et al., 2024 ) partition individual forward passes, using all-to-all exchange and K/V circulation, respectively. Live Avatar and HiAR ( Huang et al., 2025b ; Zou et al., 2026 ) explore pipelined execution with fixed-timestep or matched-noise designs. WP instead schedules chunk–stage tasks on full-model replicas (Sec. 5.5 ), adapting wavefront scheduling ( Hou et al., 2018 ; Jin et al., 2016 ) alongside optimized attention and runtimes ( Dao et al., 2022 ; Dao, 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2025 ; Zheng et al., 2024 ) .


##### Video evaluation.

VBench ( Huang et al., 2024 ) provides standardized quality, semantic, and motion measures. Our long-video comparisons (Sec. 5.1 ) show that high Quality and Dynamic Degree can coexist with subject loss and camera drift. We therefore supplement benchmark scores with targeted stability diagnostics; their scope and future alternatives are discussed in Secs. 7 and 8 .

These lines frame WF’s joint design of causal conditioning, training, and execution, together with the evidence used to assess it. Section 7 examines the remaining methodological and system limits; Sec. 8 connects them to better evaluation, interactive generation, and world modeling.


### 7 Limitations

Our evaluation establishes streaming generation under fixed conditioning and the tested model, sampling, and hardware configurations. The following limits of the training and execution design discussed in Sec. 6 motivate the agenda in Sec. 8 .


##### Quality evaluation and diagnostic coverage.

VBench-based scores do not fully characterize long-stream quality: in our comparisons, high Quality and Dynamic Degree coexist with subject disappearance and camera drift (Sec. 5.1 ). Subject Preservation and Camera Drift expose these failures, but rely on a 100-prompt subject-focused subset, tracking and identity association, and geometric heuristics (Appendix C.1 ). Their sensitivity to thresholds and missing observations, coverage of intentional scene changes, and agreement with human judgments remain to be established. They provide targeted diagnostics, with broader perceptual and semantic quality still requiring assessment.


##### Output-path capacity and attribution.

VAE pipelining overlaps and partitions decoding but does not remove its finite service capacity. The 1.3B BF16 configurations reach 147.58/134.81 DiT/E2E FPS at four steps and 104.82/96.46 at five steps (Tab. 1 ), with three and two VAE GPUs under the eight-GPU budget, respectively. These gaps are consistent with residual decoder/output-path limits. Aggregate timings, however, do not isolate VAE compute, transfers, queueing, or backpressure, and system DiT timing may reflect decoder interference. For 14B BF16, the gap narrows to 28.74 versus 28.64 DiT/E2E FPS (Tab. 4 ). Matched VAE-on/off runs and stage-level measurements are needed to identify bottlenecks and guide end-to-end resource allocation.


##### Training complexity.

WF currently uses a complex multi-stage training procedure. An important direction is to simplify this procedure while preserving generation quality and long-video stability.


##### Scalability at a fixed sampling budget.

For $S$ DiT denoising steps, WP uses $S$ denoising stages and one cache-refresh stage, each with a full-model replica. Stage parallelism is therefore bounded at a fixed step count, limiting the scale of a single pipeline. Our experiments also show that WP’s throughput advantage over CP narrows for larger models. These constraints motivate studying finer-grained or hybrid parallelism and resource sharing across streams.


##### Interaction and serving scope.

High steady-state throughput alone does not establish low input-to-visible-output latency. Online condition changes require deciding when inputs take effect in active chunks and how cached state is refreshed; the fixed-context equivalence argument in Appendix B.6 does not cover these updates. Prompt switching, camera/action control, closed-loop tasks, and multi-stream deadline guarantees require dedicated evaluation beyond the present fixed-conditioning experiments.


### 8 Future Work

The limits in Sec. 7 define our research agenda: strengthen end-to-end streaming execution, enable online interaction, and then extend toward interactive world models. Serving research and richer quality evaluation support these capability extensions throughout. Appendix E develops the task-specific questions.


##### End-to-end execution and scalability.

We plan to profile decoder stages, transfers, queues, and backpressure to guide DiT/VAE resource allocation. To address WP’s fixed-step scaling limit, we will study finer-grained execution and combinations with context parallelism, checking communication costs and causal dependencies under each design. Simplifying WF’s training recipe while preserving motion and long-horizon stability remains a parallel objective.


##### Serving concurrent streams.

StreamDiffusion’s image-stream batching ( Kodaira et al., 2025 ) and StreamDiffusionV2’s video-stream scheduling ( Feng et al., 2026 ) motivate latency-aware batching. SlackServe ( Xia et al., 2026 ) uses playout slack for resource and quality decisions, while PhysisServe ( Physis AI Infra Team, 2026 ) explores fine-grained stage pipelining. Building on these directions, we aim to study admission control, batching, and resource allocation for concurrent WF streams while respecting their context dependencies. Evaluation should include startup delay, output jitter, playback stalls, fairness, and quality alongside throughput.


##### Interactive streaming generation.

The next capability is updating prompts during generation. LongLive’s KV recaching ( Yang et al., 2026 ) provides a starting point for coordinating new conditions with visual history. Extending WF requires defining which active chunks receive each update, refreshing affected state, and adapting training to condition changes. We will assess prompt-to-visible-output latency, prompt adherence, and visual continuity, rather than inferring responsiveness from output FPS.


##### Interactive world models.

Camera and action inputs further introduce feedback-dependent state evolution. We aim to explore both learned world prediction ( Zhao et al., 2026a ; Su et al., 2026a ) and systems in which code or an engine maintains world state while a video model renders observations ( Chen et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2026a ) . The latter motivates adapting WF for structure-conditioned streaming rendering. Both routes require training under the corresponding control interface and evaluating control alignment, state consistency, and closed-loop behavior. They build on the execution and interaction work above and remain prospective extensions of WF.


##### Richer quality evaluation.

To address the metric limits above, we plan to incorporate streaming-interaction benchmarks such as StreamEval ( StreamEval Team, 2026 ) , which evaluates task execution and state consistency under timed inputs alongside visual quality. Long-video understanding methods such as StreamMeCo ( Wang et al., 2026a ) could help organize temporal evidence for an auxiliary quality judge. We aim to combine automatic diagnostics, understanding-based judgments, and human assessment, with explicit criteria for subject continuity, intended motion, and instruction execution. Such judges require timestamped evidence, calibration against human annotations, and checks for missed failures before their scores can support model comparisons.


### 9 Conclusion

We present Wave Forcing, which jointly designs causal context, training strategies, and multi-GPU pipelining to denoise multiple video chunks concurrently. WF uses cleaner intermediate states of preceding chunks as context for subsequent chunks, with three-stage training adapting the model to mixed-noise contexts and self-generated histories. A pipeline of full-model replicas then produces one video chunk per concurrent forward round after fill, while WaveRT overlaps K/V delivery with computation to reduce dependency waits. Experiments show that WF improves motion while maintaining overall generation quality and achieves high-throughput streaming video generation. These results demonstrate that jointly designing generation context, training, and execution can translate cross-chunk parallelism into practical gains in video generation efficiency.

### Appendix A Training Implementation Details


##### Noise schedules and score networks.

The 4-step schedule is $[1000,750,500,250]$ and the 5-step schedule is $[1000,800,600,400,200]$ , before timestep warping with shift factor 5. Both use three latent frames per chunk and preserve their respective schedules through all stages and inference. The frozen DMD teacher is bidirectional Wan2.1-T2V-14B. The trainable fake-score model starts from bidirectional Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B and does not share weights with the causal student. It learns to denoise noisy, detached student predictions. Stage transitions transfer raw student weights and restart the fake-score model, optimizer, and EMA states. Evaluation uses the resulting EMA student.


##### Off-policy targets and perceptual loss.

Stage 2 uses 2048 training and 128 validation teacher examples generated by Wan2.1-T2V-14B with 50 denoising steps, guidance scale 3, and timestep shift 5. Each example contains 21 latent frames. The off-policy implementation samples $S$ consecutive chunks ( $3S$ latent frames), constructs student K/V from preceding clean teacher chunks, and re-noises the sampled chunks to form a set of chunks at staggered noise levels. DMD and LPIPS are applied to the same clean prediction, with LPIPS weight 0.25 for both model versions. The fake-score update uses student predictions from these teacher-derived states.

For LPIPS, latent spatial resolution is bilinearly reduced from $60\times 104$ to $30\times 52$ , then decoded by a frozen Wan VAE to $240\times 416$ RGB frames. Every third decoded frame is evaluated. The 4-step training input contains 12 latent frames and decodes to 45 frames, giving 15 perceptual comparisons. The 5-step training input contains 15 latent frames and decodes to 57 frames, giving 19 comparisons. Decoded values are clamped to $[-1,1]$ , and VGG-based LPIPS is averaged over the sampled frames.


##### Optimization and updates.

The student and fake-score learning rates are $1.5\times 10^{-6}$ and $4\times 10^{-7}$ , with Adam moments $(0,0.999)$ . EMA uses decay 0.99 and starts after 200 training iterations. The training used eight workers with one sample each and no gradient accumulation, giving an effective batch size of eight. For the three-stage 4-step model, the 3000/1000/2000 training-loop iterations correspond to 600/1000/400 student updates and 3000/1000/2000 fake-score updates. The 5-step model starts from released RF weights and proceeds directly to Stage 2 (Sec. 3.2 ), so the Stage 1 iteration and update counts apply only to the 4-step model. Stage 1 and Stage 3 update the student once per five fake-score updates, whereas Stage 2 updates both models each iteration .


##### Rollout visibility.

The Stage 3 sampler selects RF, WF, or SF for the same student with equal probability. The WF path reuses the RF rollout implementation with chunk-wise causal attention to advance the wavefront: clean history and the same or earlier active chunks are visible, while later active chunks are masked. This matches Eq. ( 4 ). The RF and SF paths remain complementary training distributions.


### Appendix B Runtime Implementation and Analysis


#### B.1 Process ownership and reference execution

WaveRT uses $S$ denoising processes and one store process, each owning a GPU and a full DiT replica. A dedicated group of $P_{\mathrm{VAE}}$ GPUs owns contiguous decoder partitions, giving $S+1+P_{\mathrm{VAE}}$ GPUs. GPU allocations list denoising + Store + VAE: $4+1+3$ for 4-step and $5+1+2$ for 5-step WaveRT, both totaling eight GPUs. The store process performs a full clean-state cache refresh, rather than an additional denoising update .

The local forward uses SGLang Diffusion for loading, conditioning, scheduling, dense operators, and cross-attention. WaveRT interposes after self-attention QKV projection and RoPE to publish K/V and construct the legal context. It owns the temporal pages and replaces the base runtime’s unused large self-attention cache with a placeholder. The execution tests use BF16/FA3 as the reference backend. The approximate compute modes replace self-attention with SageAttention and optionally use W8A8 linear layers, while retaining FA3 for cross-attention.

The synchronized reference gathers every rank’s layer K/V, including store:

[Tableau] $$\bigl((K_{k,r}^{\ell},V_{k,r}^{\ell})\bigr)_{r=0}^{S}=\operatorname{AllGather}_{r=0}^{S}(K_{k,r}^{\ell},V_{k,r}^{\ell}).$$ (12)

Idle ranks provide dummy payloads during fill/drain, but their slots never enter attention. Each active rank keeps all query heads. The RF path reads every active denoising slot, whereas WF selects the context in Eq. ( 5 ). Synchronized and asynchronous WF share the same context rules. Native RF comparison additionally changes the visibility graph and requires matching history and positions.


#### B.2 K/V publication and buffer lifetime

At initialization, consumers allocate remote K/V slots and device-side generation flags indexed by producer, round buffer, and layer, then exchange IPC memory handles. After rank $j$ produces layer $\ell$ K/V, its communication stream waits on the producer event, copies the payload to each active consumer $s<j$ , and publishes the generation flag. A consumer’s compute stream waits for the matching flag immediately before attention. All legal cleaner ranks, including nonadjacent chunks and active store, must be ready .

This produce–publish–wait-before-use order follows buffered software-pipeline principles [ NVIDIA, 2026b , NVIDIA, 2026a ] , applied here to newly computed inter-GPU K/V rather than prefetched static tiles. Causal visibility permits producer lead but does not guarantee it. WaveRT enforces the order explicitly because ordinary CTA scheduling supplies no such guarantee. The CUDA IPC implementation uses cuMemcpyDtoDAsync , whose peer copies may execute through an SM kernel.

The paged path writes remote segments directly into their final attention locations:

[Tableau] $$\mathrm{KPage},\mathrm{VPage}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times L\times N_{\max}\times n_{\mathrm{head}}\times d_{\mathrm{head}}}.$$ (13)

The leading dimension double-buffers rounds, and $N_{\max}$ bounds context tokens. Consumers fill anchor, retained history, and self slots, while producers fill legal remote slots. Attention reads the resulting ordered prefix without per-layer remote torch.cat . Here, “paged” means fixed-slot placement and does not eliminate local/history copies. Outgoing copies complete before the round boundary permits reuse, and generation flags distinguish successive uses of a slot.


#### B.3 Round schedule and cache semantics

Algorithm 1 implements the coordinates and active set defined in Sec. 3 . It runs $N_{\mathrm{chunk}}+S$ rounds, including fill/drain. The latent All-Gather at each round boundary remains in the asynchronous implementation and is distinct from the removed per-layer K/V collective.

> Algorithm 1 WaveRT round schedule (one request, $N_{\mathrm{chunk}}$ chunks)

Context order is exactly Eq. ( 5 ): anchor, bounded clean history, live store, self, then cleaner denoising slots in increasing stage order. The live store page becomes retained history after the round, avoiding a second steady-state broadcast. The first clean chunk serves as a persistent anchor, with its unrotated key retained so RoPE can be reapplied when the bounded window shifts. Other pages reuse their stored RoPE-applied keys.

Store processes $B_{k-S}$ at the configured context_noise timestep, discards its prediction, and keeps each layer’s K/V. This timestep is specified independently of $S$ . Store attends to its anchor, retained history, and self. Its history budget reserves one query chunk, whereas denoising ranks reserve $S$ chunks, so their prefix lengths can differ despite sharing the same clean-page list. Refresh timestep, prefix budgets, positions, and promotion order are part of the fixed semantics.

Conditioning, attention pages, and VAE convolution caches are request-local. The resident launcher handles one queued request at a time.


#### B.4 Output cadence, work, and fill/drain

Let $T_{s}$ be stage $s$ ’s local service time and $T_{\mathrm{store}}$ the clean-refresh time, measured for a specified graph, context, and backend. Sequential generation has period

[Tableau] $$T_{\mathrm{serial}}^{(S)}=\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}T_{s}+T_{\mathrm{store}},\qquad T_{\mathrm{serial}}^{(1)}=T_{0}+T_{\mathrm{store}}.$$ (14)

With $S+1$ stage-owned replicas, the filled wave period satisfies

[Tableau] $$T_{\mathrm{wave}}^{(S)}\geq\max\{T_{0},\ldots,T_{S-1},T_{\mathrm{store}}\},$$ (15)

with equality only if communication and synchronization are fully covered. This permits an $S$ -update pipeline to emit faster than serial one-step inference, but uses more replicas and does not reduce work or first-output latency. Across $N_{\mathrm{chunk}}$ chunks it still executes $N_{\mathrm{chunk}}(S+1)$ full forwards. Structural slot occupancy is

[Tableau] $$\eta_{\mathrm{slot}}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{chunk}}}{N_{\mathrm{chunk}}+S}.$$ (16)

All replicas are active only for $S\leq k<N_{\mathrm{chunk}}$ , so requests with $N_{\mathrm{chunk}}\leq S$ never fill the pipeline. The 399-latent-frame workload has 133 chunks, giving $133/137=97.1\%$ slot occupancy at $S=4$ . First-output latency includes all denoising and store stages, VAE pipeline fill, and delivery .


#### B.5 Streaming VAE partitioning

Completed latents enter the decoder queue. Each latent-frame microbatch traverses contiguous decoder stages in order, preserving per-request causal-convolution caches. Profiling $M_{\mathrm{VAE}}$ ordered units at representative streaming shapes gives service times $\tau_{m}^{\mathrm{VAE}}$ . Dynamic programming chooses $P_{\mathrm{VAE}}$ partitions by

[Tableau] $$\min_{0=b_{0}<\cdots<b_{P_{\mathrm{VAE}}}=M_{\mathrm{VAE}}}\max_{1\leq p\leq P_{\mathrm{VAE}}}\sum_{m=b_{p-1}+1}^{b_{p}}\tau_{m}^{\mathrm{VAE}}.$$ (17)

This balances estimated local service, including costs missed by unit counts or FLOPs. Inter-stage delivery must also fit the arrival interval. In per-latent-chunk units, the steady decoded-output interval is approximately

[Tableau] $$T_{\mathrm{emit}}^{\mathrm{E2E}}\approx\max\!\left(T_{\mathrm{round}}^{\mathrm{WP}},\max_{p}T_{\mathrm{VAE},p}^{\mathrm{chunk}}\right)+T_{\mathrm{delivery}}^{\mathrm{exposed}},$$ (18)

where decoder service amortizes all frame microbatches in one chunk, and the last term counts residual delivery not already covered by overlap or the service terms. Slower decoding grows queues or triggers bounded-buffer backpressure. The model excludes fill/drain and file encoding. Tab. 8 reports profiling, while Fig. 4 illustrates the schedules.


#### B.6 Same-graph semantic preservation

Wave Forcing changes the model graph relative to RF. Once the WF graph is fixed, synchronized and asynchronous execution can be compared at the operator level.

Fix the model parameters, denoising schedule, initial noise, per-update re-noising draws, text conditioning, positional embeddings, and history/store rules. Assume each local operator evaluates the same mathematical function and every attention operation receives the ordered K/V context in Eq. ( 5 ), after the corresponding K/V production and delivery have completed. Then dependency-respecting asynchronous execution and synchronized execution produce the same mathematical latent sequence.

At the first tick, both executions start from identical inputs and empty history. At each layer, local Q/K/V projections depend only on that rank’s current hidden states and fixed conditioning. The synchronized execution gathers all projections before consuming them. The asynchronous execution may publish and consume them at different times, but the readiness condition guarantees the same legal K/V values and sequence order at attention. Consequently each attention result and the remaining local layer outputs agree. Induction over layers gives identical clean-latent predictions, from which the fixed scheduler and noise draws produce identical stage outputs. Store receives the same clean latent, runs the same cache-refresh computation, and promotes the same pages after the tick. Induction over ticks establishes identical histories and emitted latents through fill, steady state, and drain. ∎


#### B.7 Timing contract and multiple producers

On a common time base, let $t_{v}^{\mathrm{local}}$ be consumer $v=C_{k,s,\ell}$ ’s local-input/compute-stream ready time before remote waits. For each required edge $e$ , set $t_{e}^{\mathrm{need}}=t_{v}^{\mathrm{local}}$ and $t_{e}^{\mathrm{arrive}}=t_{e}^{\mathrm{ready}}+\tau_{e}$ . Eq. ( 11 ) then gives

[Tableau] $$t_{v}^{\mathrm{start}}=\max\!\left(t_{v}^{\mathrm{local}},\max_{e\in\operatorname{In}_{\mathrm{KV}}(v)}t_{e}^{\mathrm{arrive}}\right),\qquad w_{v}=t_{v}^{\mathrm{start}}-t_{v}^{\mathrm{local}}=\max_{e\in\operatorname{In}_{\mathrm{KV}}(v)}w_{e}.$$ (19)

The incoming set includes all legal cleaner ranks and active store. When it is empty, $t_{v}^{\mathrm{start}}=t_{v}^{\mathrm{local}}$ and $w_{v}=0$ . Earlier waits already affect these timestamps. Round critical paths additionally include transfer serialization, contention, and the boundary collective. Summing concurrent durations would double-count overlap. Measuring this contract requires correlated timestamps across calibrated device clocks, not just aggregate rank times.


#### B.8 Bounded attention storage and communication

The two K/V arrays in Eq. ( 13 ) have fixed capacity for anchor, bounded history, live store, and at most $S$ denoising chunks, so attention-page storage is independent of generated video length. This bound excludes model weights and other request state. Explicitly retained video context is limited to the anchor and recent history.

For direct publication at steady state, denoising rank $j$ sends its K/V chunk to $j$ noisier ranks, for $S(S-1)/2$ payload destinations among denoising stages per layer. Store adds $S$ destinations within the full wavefront. This quadratic count in $S$ covers logical payload copies, while physical link traffic depends on transport and topology. The practical gain is reduced exposed communication under the timing condition in Eq. ( 11 ).

#### C.1 Long-video stability diagnostics


##### Prompt subset.

Both diagnostics require a principal subject named in the prompt. A fixed text rule first removes, for every method, group scenes, scenery, first-person views without a stable visible subject, ambiguous principal entities, and prompts that ask the subject to disappear or transform. Of the 200 MovieGen prompts, 131 pass this rule. We then sample 100 of these prompts once at random and hold that subset fixed across methods, ablation studies, and both diagnostics. This subject-focused subset is distinct from the full 200-prompt VBench-Long evaluation.


##### Subject Preservation.

We measure the prompt’s principal-subject visibility.

SAM3 tracks the text-specified entity at 8 FPS, giving 797 sampled frames per video. The initial binding is the largest detection in the first eight frames, and masked DINO [ Caron et al., 2021 ] features decide which later detections share that identity. Let $A_{t}$ be the current mask area and $A_{0}$ the median mask area of the bound identity over the eight frames that begin at this binding. An associated box within $1\%$ of the image border has visibility weight $\min(1,A_{t}/(0.18A_{0}))$ , while any other associated detection has weight 1. A new instance elsewhere keeps its own track while the recognized subject still has weight at least $0.5$ and mask area at least $0.18A_{0}$ . Frames without an associated detection have weight 0. The video score is 100 times the mean weight over all sampled frames, including frames before the binding and frames without support. Videos that never receive an initial binding, and videos whose binding was reviewed as incorrect, are left unscored.


##### Camera Drift.

Camera Drift measures persistent background motion with the subject moving toward an image boundary. We use the union of SAM3 detections for the text-specified entity at 8 FPS and point trajectories seeded outside those masks at 4 FPS. All distances are normalized by the image diagonal. For each image edge $e$ , let $g_{e}$ and $q_{e}$ be the 5th and 90th percentiles of mask-pixel distances to that edge. Foreground outward velocity is the decrease in $q_{e}$ divided by the 0.25-second pair duration. Background velocity is the component-wise median of per-cell median track velocities over supported spatial cells, projected onto the same outward axis. We apply $z(v)=\operatorname{sign}(v)\max(|v|-0.002,0)$ to both velocities.

Within the preceding five seconds, we use pairs with observed foreground and background motion. For either corrected velocity sequence, define outward displacement $A=\max(0,0.25\sum_{t}v_{t})$ , path length $P=0.25\sum_{t}|v_{t}|$ , and response $H=(A/P)A^{2}/(A^{2}+0.1^{2})$ , with $H=0$ when $P=0$ . These give foreground and background factors $F_{e}$ and $B_{e}$ . Edge proximity is $E_{e}=\max(0,1-g_{e}/0.1)$ . The instantaneous risk is $\max_{e}E_{e}F_{e}B_{e}$ over edges with positive current foreground and background velocities, or zero if no edge qualifies. Thus, background motion with a stationary subject does not incur a drift penalty.

To retain evidence after an exit, we require one second of consecutive outward foreground and background motion, at least two native pixels of foreground displacement, a final $g_{e}$ within two native pixels of the boundary, and $q_{e}\leq 0.1$ . After one second of consecutive explicitly empty masks, we hold the exit’s $E_{e}F_{e}$ fixed and update $B_{e}$ from current background observations, without backfilling the confirmation interval. Missing background tracks can be replenished by points consistent with the existing motion layer, but new points contribute only after tracking through a subsequent pair. This is a geometric association, not an independent semantic background label. A returning detection, uncertain mask, or scene cut ends the extension. Stopped or reversed background motion immediately gives zero risk and ends the extension after one consecutive observed second. Background gaps remain unscored, and later continuation is distinguished from uninterrupted support.

The video score is 100 times the mean risk over valid 0.25-second intervals, so lower is better. Startup intervals before five seconds, unavailable current measurements, and windows crossing a detected or unresolved cut remain unscored. Missing motion within a window is neither interpolated nor extrapolated. We average video scores equally over the prompt subset, requiring at least 20 scored seconds per video and using each method’s own valid intervals.

#### C.3 Runtime implementation and measurement protocol

The WaveRT runtime sweep uses one NVLink-connected node with eight H200 141-GB GPUs, $832\times 480$ resolution video, and three latent frames per chunk. We implement WaveRT using Python 3.12, PyTorch 2.11.0+cu128, and SGLang Diffusion.

For the WaveRT measurements in Tabs. 4 and 5 , each repetition generates 399 latent frames and 1593 decoded frames after a 399-latent-frame warmup. System FPS is 1593 divided by the median elapsed time across three fixed repetitions. Tick p50 pools steady-state ticks across those repetitions, and steady FPS is $12000/\text{tick p50 (ms)}$ , using 12 decoded-equivalent frames per steady chunk. DiT timing covers pipeline fill and drain and may be affected by concurrent VAE decoding, while E2E timing additionally includes the remaining time until VAE completion. #### C.6 Exposed communication, synchronization, and payload size

WaveRT targets time exposed on the consumer’s critical path, not only bytes on the link. Early publication overlaps delivery with computation. One-sided writes replace matching collective coordination with point-of-use readiness checks, and paged placement avoids rebuilding remote context. Together, these changes reduce exposed communication, synchronization, and destination-assembly costs without eliminating data dependencies. At fixed BF16 computation, the 4-step 14B Sync/Overlap/One-sided/Paged tick p50 values are 464.270, 449.689, 425.907, and 413.795 ms (Tab. 5 ).

Payload size determines how much delivery must fit under computation. One chunk sent to one consumer at one layer carries $m_{e}(b)=2N_{C}n_{\mathrm{head}}d_{\mathrm{head}}b$ bytes, with $b$ bytes per element. Larger K/V width increases each payload, while more layers and destinations increase round traffic (Appendix B.8 ). Fixed hardware has unchanged peak link capacity, but effective bandwidth varies with topology and contention. Taking unquantized K/V readiness as the reference, a simple usable-delivery model is

[Tableau] $$\widetilde{\tau}_{e}(b)\approx t_{e}^{\mathrm{quant}}+\alpha_{e}+\frac{m_{e}(b)}{B_{e}^{\mathrm{eff}}}+t_{e}^{\mathrm{dequant}},$$ (20)

where $\alpha_{e}$ covers queueing and signaling, and $B_{e}^{\mathrm{eff}}$ is achieved payload bandwidth. BF16 requires no conversion, whereas FP8 halves payload bytes, excluding metadata, at the cost of conversion. With unchanged local timing, a previously exposed dependency can become hidden if

[Tableau] $$\widetilde{\tau}_{e}(1)\leq\Delta_{e}<\widetilde{\tau}_{e}(2).$$ (21)

Every required edge, including store, must meet its deadline: local attention readiness, not projection start. Quantization can change execution timing, so actual slack must be measured per configuration.

The component profiles in Tab. 8 illustrate reduced transfer volume with FP8 K/V and reduced receiver-side assembly with paged placement. FP8 conversion can offset transfer savings under SageAttention. In the paged backend sweep (Fig. 7 e,f), K/V remains unquantized, while W8A8 changes local DiT linear computation. These configurations vary the local compute backend while retaining the same K/V transport precision.

##### Sequential and joint-chunk generation.

Self Forcing [ Huang et al., 2025a ] completes all updates of one chunk before starting the next, reusing clean-history K/V. Its bounded history cache can evict the initial chunk, whereas RF retains it as an anchor. RF [ Liu et al., 2026 ] advances consecutive mixed-noise chunks jointly, emitting one clean chunk per joint call after fill. Distributed RF retains every active chunk’s corresponding-layer K/V dependencies in both temporal directions. Communication overlap is still possible, but every consumer must respect those inputs. Both RF and WF are DAGs when unrolled over layers, so WF is distinguished by cleaner-only visibility, not merely by a topological order.


##### Stage-local and matched-noise pipelines.

Live Avatar [ Huang et al., 2025b ] assigns a fixed denoising timestep to each process. Earlier stages retain their original K/V snapshots as a chunk progresses, and only latents move across stages. HiAR [ Zou et al., 2026 ] instead builds a causal hierarchy: each level uses freshly produced K/V from earlier chunks at its output timestep $\nu_{s+1}$ . The cache write for chunk $i$ can be fused with denoising chunk $i+1$ . Its pipelined levels retain independent matched-noise caches and exchange latents, not layer-wise K/V between processes. This refreshed output-state context distinguishes HiAR from fixed-timestep snapshots.


##### Wavefront execution.

WF preserves current-layer live K/V from all legal cleaner chunks and store, and WP publishes this K/V across full-model replicas. Thus an anti-diagonal schedule alone does not imply WF conditioning. Its causal mask, cache rules, and training establish the model, while publication and readiness checks implement it. Appendix B.4 gives the cadence and resource accounting without equating steady output rate with first-output latency.

### Appendix E Extended Future Work

This appendix develops the interaction, serving, and world-model extensions in Sec. 8 , following the training and execution limits in Sec. 7 . These extensions have not been evaluated with WF.


##### Online interaction and condition changes.

LongLive [ Yang et al., 2026 ] uses KV recaching to align visual context with an updated prompt; EditStream and LPM [ Zhou et al., 2026 , Zeng et al., 2026 ] explore interactive editing and character performance. A WF extension must specify which prompt applies to each active chunk, which cached states need refreshing, and whether outstanding work is completed or discarded. The fixed-context equivalence argument in Appendix B.6 does not establish correctness across condition changes. Sustained FPS and interaction delay are distinct: evaluation should measure when an input first changes the displayed video, as well as adherence and continuity.


##### Serving under streaming deadlines.

StreamDiffusion’s image-stream batching [ Kodaira et al., 2025 ] and StreamDiffusionV2’s video-stream scheduling [ Feng et al., 2026 ] motivate deadline-aware batching around WF. SlackServe [ Xia et al., 2026 ] relates scheduling and quality choices to buffered playback time, while PhysisServe’s technical blog [ Physis AI Infra Team, 2026 ] describes pipelining over transformer blocks and denoising steps, together with GPU time sharing. Future WF serving should study admission control, stream priorities, resource allocation, and finer-grained placement under its live-context dependencies. Changing sampling budgets also changes WP’s available stage parallelism (Sec. 7 ); adopting another system’s batching or state-reuse rules would therefore require checking WF’s conditioning graph. Startup delay, output jitter, stall rate, and fairness should accompany throughput and quality measurements.


##### Learning controlled world evolution.

One route extends video prediction with structured controls. minWM and ForgeWM [ Zhao et al., 2026a , Li et al., 2026 ] study controllable few-step video generation; SolarWM [ Huang et al., 2026a ] emphasizes data standardization and causal adaptation; SANA-WM [ Zhu et al., 2026a ] explores hybrid linear attention for long-horizon generation; and OneWM [ Su et al., 2026a ] jointly learns world prediction and action generation. Integrating WF would require specifying when new camera or action inputs become available to in-flight chunks, validating state dependencies for each architecture, and assessing control fidelity, prediction consistency, and policy performance in closed-loop tasks.


##### Executable world state and generative rendering.

A complementary route delegates persistent state, rules, and dynamics to code or an engine. Code World Model and Matrix-Game 4.0 [ Chen et al., 2026 , Zhang et al., 2026a ] connect such state to video generation through visual proxies. StreamRender-H3 [ Lu et al., 2026 ] and SolarRenderer [ Fang et al., 2026 ] further motivate structure-conditioned streaming rendering. Here WF could supply an execution foundation for a renderer trained to follow incoming structure, rather than taking responsibility for the engine’s dynamics. This would require aligned structure/video training and validation of temporal conditioning: StreamRender-H3 uses bidirectional attention within a streaming window, whereas SolarRenderer includes a block-causal variant. Neither establishes compatibility with WF’s wavefront. Evaluation should check whether rendered observations preserve the underlying state and expose action effects quickly enough for feedback.


##### Understanding-assisted quality evaluation.

Following the metric limits in Sec. 7 , a future evaluator could combine local defect checks with long-horizon, instruction-aware judgments. StreamEval [ StreamEval Team, 2026 ] evaluates timed requests and retained state alongside visual quality. StreamMeCo [ Wang et al., 2026a ] addresses efficient streaming video understanding, while MemForest [ Wang et al., 2026b ] provides event-centric memory compression and retrieval. These mechanisms could help a video-understanding judge retrieve temporal evidence about entities, actions, and failures. Adapting them to quality judging requires human-calibrated validation: we should report supporting timestamps and unscorable intervals, assess agreement with human annotations, and test whether compressed memory loses brief defects or earlier facts. The goal is to complement our targeted diagnostics with broader, auditable quality assessment.
