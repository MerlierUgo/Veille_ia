## PDRVLA: A Vision–Language–Action Model with Physical Dynamics Representations

Vision–Language–Action (VLA) models generate robot actions from visual observations and language instructions, but their understanding of physical dynamics remains limited. Video world models can capture scene dynamics, yet generating future videos introduces high latency into the control loop. We introduce PDRVLA, whose lightweight Physical Dynamics Module extracts physical tokens from vision–language features to condition action generation without online video generation. The module learns through direct prediction of future RGB, depth, and optical-flow features, with auxiliary supervision through a frozen pretrained video world model. Training relies entirely on publicly available data, expanded through human-to-robot synthesis, subtask annotation, and embodied chain-of-thought (ECoT) annotation. Across four simulation benchmarks, PDRVLA achieves success rates of 99.3% on LIBERO, 89.9% on LIBERO-Plus, 93.6% on RoboTwin 2.0, and 81.5% on SimplerEnv. Ablations show improved future prediction with world-model supervision and evaluate how subtask conditioning, future object-pose supervision, and ECoT training affect action success.

### 1 Introduction

Vision–Language–Action (VLA) models connect visual observations and language instructions to robot control, drawing on pretrained vision–language models (VLMs) and demonstrations from heterogeneous robots ( Black et al., 2024 ; Physical Intelligence, 2025 ) . Their understanding of the physical world, however, remains limited. For example, a recent analysis reports weak encoding of object physical properties across several VLA policies and unreliable adaptation of their motions to changes in object mass ( Xu, 2026 ) . For manipulation, recognizing the current scene is only part of the problem: grasping, moving, and placing objects also change their configurations and spatial relationships. Anticipating how a scene evolves during interaction can provide useful context for action selection, motivating representations that connect current observations with future appearance, geometry, and motion.

Pretrained video world models offer a complementary way to learn physical dynamics by predicting how scenes change over time ( Team Wan et al., 2025 ) . Incorporating these models into robot policies can connect future prediction with action generation ( Kim et al., 2026 ) . However, generating future videos within the control loop adds latency to action generation. Recent approaches instead use video prediction during training to shape compact representations that can condition a policy without online video generation ( InternRobotics, 2026 ; Tang et al., 2026 ) .

Following this direction, we introduce PDRVLA, a VLA model with a lightweight Physical Dynamics Module (Figure 1 ). The module extracts a fixed set of physical tokens from intermediate VLM features. The action expert uses these tokens alongside the final-layer VLM features to generate actions. We train the module with two complementary objectives. Direct supervision uses future RGB, depth, and optical-flow latent features as targets, encouraging the tokens to encode future appearance, geometry, and motion. A frozen pretrained video world model conditioned on the physical tokens provides auxiliary supervision for temporal prediction. Joint policy training then combines action and vision–language (VL) objectives with continued direct future prediction. The resulting policy uses physical tokens for action generation without running the video model during control.

Our training corpus is built entirely from publicly available datasets. It combines real-robot, handheld UMI, and simulated trajectories with general and robotics-oriented VL data, and is expanded through egocentric human-to-robot synthesis. Subtask and embodied chain-of-thought (ECoT) annotations provide additional supervision for task stages, scene understanding, progress assessment, and next motion. Together, these sources support training across heterogeneous embodiments and complementary forms of action and language supervision.

We evaluate PDRVLA on LIBERO ( Liu et al., 2023 ) , LIBERO-Plus ( Fei et al., 2025 ) , RoboTwin 2.0 ( Chen et al., 2025a ) , and SimplerEnv ( Li et al., 2024 ) , achieving success rates of 99.3%, 89.9%, 93.6%, and 81.5%, respectively. Ablation studies show that world-model-assisted supervision improves future prediction. Additional experiments examine the effects of subtask conditioning, future object-pose supervision, and ECoT annotations on policy performance.

The report makes three contributions:

- • We introduce PDRVLA, whose lightweight Physical Dynamics Module extracts compact physical tokens from intermediate VLM features to complement the final-layer features used for action generation. We train these representations through direct prediction of future RGB, depth, and optical-flow features, with auxiliary supervision from a frozen video world model. Direct future prediction continues during joint policy training, while deployment uses the learned physical tokens without running the video generator.

- • We assemble a cross-embodiment training corpus entirely from publicly available datasets, integrating real-robot, handheld UMI, and simulated demonstrations with general and robotics-oriented VL data. Our data pipeline standardizes heterogeneous trajectories, expands embodiment coverage through egocentric human-to-robot synthesis, and adds subtask and ECoT annotations, supporting joint learning of robot actions and language responses about task stages, scene context, progress, and next motion.

- • We evaluate PDRVLA on four simulation benchmarks and conduct targeted analyses of physical-representation learning and policy performance. The results show improved future prediction with world-model assistance and characterize the effects of subtask conditioning, future object-pose supervision, and ECoT annotations on action success across tasks and inference settings.

#### 2.2 VLA Data


##### 2.2.1 Real-Robot Data

Real-robot trajectories expose the model to real-world dynamics, sensor variation, and contact-rich interactions. We aggregate public datasets spanning different robots, environments, and task families.

Our single-arm tabletop and in-the-wild manipulation data come from RoboSet ( Bharadhwaj et al., 2024 ) , DROID ( Khazatsky et al., 2024 ) , BridgeData V2 ( Walke et al., 2023 ) , BC-Z ( Jang et al., 2022 ) , and RH20T ( Fang et al., 2024 ) . Together, they cover diverse scenes, objects, operators, and manipulation skills, including pick-and-place, articulated-object interaction, and tool use.

We extend the embodiment coverage with bimanual and multi-platform data from RoboMIND V1/V2 ( Wu et al., 2025a ; Hou et al., 2025 ) , RoboCOIN ( Wu et al., 2025b ) , and ABC-130K ( Allshire et al., 2026 ) ; humanoid, arm, and dexterous-hand data from Humanoid Everyday ( Zhao et al., 2026a ) and the Unitree UniFoLM releases ( Unitree Robotics, 2026a ; Unitree Robotics, 2026b ; Unitree Robotics, 2026c ; Unitree Robotics, 2026d ; Unitree Robotics, 2026e ; Unitree Robotics, 2026f ) ; and large-scale multi-robot data from AgiBot World Colosseo ( Bu et al., 2025 ; AgiBot World Colosseum contributors, 2024 ; AgiBot World Team, 2026 ) .

REASSEMBLE ( Sliwowski et al., 2025 ) contributes contact-rich assembly and disassembly trajectories, while FuSe ( Jones et al., 2025 ) and MolmoAct ( Lee et al., 2025 ) provide additional manipulation trajectories.

Selected embodiment-specific subsets from the LeRobot community collections ( HuggingFaceVLA Community, 2025a ; HuggingFaceVLA Community, 2025b ; HuggingFaceVLA Community, 2025c ) add further cross-institution and cross-embodiment coverage.


##### 2.2.2 UMI Data

UMI data are collected with a handheld gripper and wrist-mounted camera rather than by teleoperating a particular robot ( Chi et al., 2024 ) . The resulting demonstrations record real-world interaction as camera observations and end-effector pose trajectories without tying the motion to a robot’s joint configuration.

We use six UMI datasets with different scales, capture systems, and task coverage: UMI ( Chi et al., 2024 ) , FastUMI ( Zhaxizhuoma et al., 2025 ) , FastUMI-100K ( Liu et al., 2025 ) , Hy-Embodied-0.5-VLA-Data ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) , HiFi-UMI-2K ( Simple AI et al., 2026 ) , and RealOmni-Open Data ( GenRobot.AI (2025), Jianzhi Robotics ) . After source-specific filtering, they contribute approximately 1.22 million episodes and 10,259 hours.

Because UMI demonstrations do not provide robot joint-space commands, we use their temporally aligned end-effector poses directly to construct the unified Cartesian action representation in Section 2.4 .


##### 2.2.3 Simulation Data

Simulation complements real-world data with controllable, reproducible interaction and access to ground-truth states and success signals. It also makes it practical to vary tasks and embodiments without additional hardware collection.

The simulation corpus covers three complementary settings: standardized skills and long-horizon tasks from ManiSkill2 ( Gu et al., 2023 ) , Meta-World ( Yu et al., 2019 ) , and RoboCasa ( Nasiriany et al., 2024 ) ; large-scale cross-embodiment trajectories from InternData-A1 ( Tian et al., 2026 ) , GR00T-X-Embodiment-Sim ( NVIDIA, 2025a ) , and InternVLA-M1 ( Chen et al., 2025b ) ; and bimanual or dexterous manipulation from RoboTwin 2.0 ( Chen et al., 2025a ) and DexMimicGen ( Jiang et al., 2025 ) .


#### 2.3 VL Data


##### 2.3.1 General VL Data

The general VL mixture includes vision-centric instruction data from Cambrian-1 ( Tong et al., 2024 ) ; captioning and open-ended visual question answering from COCO-Caption ( LMMs-Lab, 2026 ) and VQAv2 ( HuggingFaceM4, 2026 ) ; and image- and video-reasoning examples from OneThinker ( Feng et al., 2025 ) .


##### 2.3.2 Spatial and Embodied VL Data

Spatial and embodied VL data bring the supervision closer to manipulation. We divide them into spatial reasoning over general imagery and embodied or procedural reasoning drawn from robot data, simulation, and web video.

The spatial-reasoning group covers spatial question answering, geometric relations, coordinate-aware grounding, referring-expression localization, and multi-view or 3D reasoning. It includes SpaceThinker ( Remyx AI, 2026a ) , OpenSpaces ( Remyx AI, 2026b ) , SpaceOm ( Remyx AI, 2026c ) , RefSpatial ( An, 2026 ) , data released with Scaling Spatial Intelligence with Multimodal Foundation Models ( Cai et al., 2026a ) , and cross-view correspondence examples linking egocentric, third-person, and robot-camera observations ( Wang et al., 2025 ) .

The embodied and procedural group includes point supervision for object, free-space, and affordance localization from RoboPoint ( Yuan et al., 2024 ) ; visual question answering and long-horizon reasoning over manipulation videos from Robo2VLM ( Chen et al., 2025c ) and RoboVQA ( Sermanet et al., 2024 ) ; interleaved perception and reasoning from EO-1 ( Qu et al., 2025 ) ; physical-commonsense supervision from Cosmos-Reason1 ( NVIDIA, 2025b ) ; and web-derived procedural supervision for step decomposition and progress reasoning from ProcCorpus-60M ( Feng et al., 2026 ) .


#### 2.4 Data Processing

Trajectories from different datasets vary in action format, coordinate conventions, hardware names, and video configuration. We standardize action representations, filter invalid trajectories and frames, unify embodiment metadata, and align and encode visual observations. Processing rules account for differences in hardware and collection procedures.

To standardize end-effector (EEF) poses across datasets, we convert positions to meters and orientations to rotation matrices, and normalize the coordinate axes to a right-handed convention with $+x$ forward, $+y$ left, and $+z$ up. Each manipulator’s poses remain relative to the arm base or the world frame, as specified by the source dataset. We express its current and target poses in the same reference frame and record that frame in the metadata.

We represent EEF actions as changes from the current pose to a target pose. The target is the dataset-provided absolute EEF target pose when available, or the next observed pose after resampling otherwise. For a given manipulator, let $r$ denote its reference frame, $e$ the EEF frame, and $t$ a timestep after resampling. The current and target positions are $\mathbf{p}_{r,t},\mathbf{p}_{r,t}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$ , and their orientations are $R_{re,t},R_{re,t}^{*}\in SO(3)$ . The superscript $*$ denotes the target. Here, $R_{re,t}$ maps vectors from the EEF frame $e$ into reference frame $r$ . The action consists of a translation delta and a rotation delta, both expressed in $r$ :

[Tableau] $$\Delta a^{\mathrm{eef}}_{t}=\left(\Delta\mathbf{p}_{r,t},\Delta R_{r,t}\right),\qquad\Delta\mathbf{p}_{r,t}=\mathbf{p}_{r,t}^{*}-\mathbf{p}_{r,t},\quad\Delta R_{r,t}=R_{re,t}^{*}R_{re,t}^{\mathsf{T}},$$ (1)

For grippers with opening measurements, we normalize the current and target openings to $[0,1]$ using the gripper’s physical travel range, with 0 denoting fully closed and 1 fully open. The gripper action is the normalized absolute target opening.

We reject entire episodes that are too short, lack usable action targets or state sequences, contain values whose units cannot be recovered, or have image and state streams that cannot be aligned. We also reject episodes whose task descriptions conflict with the source metadata. Filtering thresholds are set separately for each dataset or subset to account for differences in hardware and collection procedures.

For otherwise usable episodes, frames containing non-finite values, abnormal repeated observations, abrupt spikes, or out-of-range values are excluded from supervision but retained at their original temporal positions to preserve image–state–action alignment. If the corruption cannot be isolated to individual frames, we reject the entire episode.

Robot hardware names vary across datasets, and the same arm can be paired with different grippers or dexterous hands. We record robot, arm, and end-effector types separately in robot_type , arm_type , and hand_type . We standardize aliases such as panda and franka_panda to a common arm name while retaining the identity of the attached end effector. Missing or inconsistent metadata are checked against source documentation and recorded images or videos.

Camera observations and robot states may be recorded at different rates. We align them at the lowest rate among the streams, capped at 30 Hz. At most four camera views are retained per episode, prioritizing wrist and third-person views, with view selection configured for each dataset. The selected videos are resized to 640 pixels on the longest side and encoded with a maximum group-of-pictures (GOP) size of 2 frames to accelerate random frame access. Post-processing checks verify that video frames remain aligned with robot states and actions.


#### 2.5 Data Synthesis and Annotation

We expand the training corpus through human-to-robot synthesis and language annotation. The former adapts egocentric demonstrations into robot observations and actions; the latter adds subtask labels and ECoT descriptions of the scene, task progress, and next motion to existing trajectories.


##### 2.5.1 Egocentric Human-to-Robot Synthesis

Human egocentric data cover diverse real-world scenes and manipulation tasks, but human hands and arms differ from robot manipulators in both appearance and kinematics. We therefore adapt both the recorded motion and the visual observations before using these demonstrations for VLA training.

We apply the human-to-robot synthesis pipeline of Qwen-RobotManip ( Yuan et al., 2026 ) to EgoDex ( Hoque et al., 2026 ) , generating demonstrations for 12 robot-arm models: Panda, UR5e, UR10e, FR3, Kinova Gen3, IIWA, ARX-L5, xArm7, ViperX, WidowX, Piper, and YAM.

We map EgoDex’s per-frame 3D hand keypoints to an end-effector (EEF) pose trajectory and gripper commands. The EEF trajectory is mapped into each target robot’s coordinate system, and inverse kinematics (IK) produces the corresponding arm-joint trajectory. We discard demonstrations with too few high-confidence hand keypoints and retargeted trajectories whose EEF poses, computed through forward kinematics, deviate excessively from the desired poses.

We segment the human hands and arms with SAM3 ( Carion et al., 2026 ) and use ProPainter ( Zhou et al., 2023 ) to reconstruct the background in the masked regions across the video. We render the target robot arm and gripper using the retargeted motion, then composite them into the reconstructed video. DA3 depth estimates ( Lin et al., 2025 ) determine which scene surfaces occlude the rendered robot during compositing. The resulting videos, arm-joint trajectories, and gripper commands form the synthetic VLA examples.

After filtering and standardization, the synthesized corpus contains 896,832 bimanual robot trajectories, totaling approximately 2,646 hours. These trajectories derive from 74,813 distinct EgoDex demonstrations, each of which may contribute variants for multiple target robots. Because the robot variants share a common source pool, the resulting datasets have similar sizes across robot models, with approximately 74,000–75,000 trajectories and 219–221 hours per target arm model.


##### 2.5.2 Subtask Annotation

Long-horizon manipulation tasks contain stages with distinct intermediate goals. Following prior work on hierarchical task structure ( Zhang et al., 2024 ; Chen et al., 2023 ) , we segment trajectories into ordered subtasks and assign a subtask label to each frame. These annotations yield both subtask-annotated VLA trajectories and VL examples about task stages and transitions.

Source-provided subtask annotations are reused when available; the remaining trajectories are automatically annotated with Qwen3.8-27B ( Qwen Team, 2026a ) on sparsely sampled observations. We sample one frame per second and increase the sampling rate for short trajectories to provide more observations. Observations at each sampled timestamp come from a selected camera view, with multiple views spatially concatenated when needed, such as two wrist-camera views for bimanual interactions.

The sampled observations are divided into sliding windows of at most 128 frames with a 16-frame overlap. For each window, the model predicts subtask descriptions and segment boundaries. For each sampled frame in an overlap, we use the prediction from the window whose midpoint is closest to that frame. We merge these predictions into an episode-level segmentation and propagate the segment labels to every frame, producing subtask-annotated VLA trajectories. Frames after the last predicted boundary receive a done label indicating the end of the annotated sequence.

Following $\pi_{0.5}$ ( Physical Intelligence, 2025 ) , we further convert these annotations into VL examples that ask the model to identify the current subtask from a visual observation and the episode-level instruction. Additional queries ask for the next subtask, the remaining subtask sequence, or whether the current segment has reached its annotated end. ##### 2.5.3 ECoT Annotation

Subtask labels identify the current intermediate goal but provide limited detail about the scene, task progress, and the motion that follows. We therefore augment the segmented trajectories in Section 2.5.2 with ECoT annotations ( Zawalski et al., 2024 ; Feng et al., 2026 ; Sun et al., 2026 ) that connect each subtask to its visual context and subsequent motion. Following Qwen-RobotManip ( Yuan et al., 2026 ) , we annotate selected keyframes with scene descriptions, progress assessments, and next-motion descriptions derived from recorded poses.

Keyframes are drawn only from frames marked valid during data processing (Section 2.4 ). Within each subtask, we retain its first and last valid frame and select additional frames when their visual similarity to the previous keyframe falls below a threshold. We compute image hashes with the Python ImageHash library 1 1 https://github.com/JohannesBuchner/imagehash to measure this similarity.

For each keyframe, Qwen3.8-27B describes task-relevant objects, spatial relations, and gripper state, and assesses whether the active subtask is complete. To account for earlier events, we uniformly sample frames between the episode’s first frame and the current keyframe. Qwen3.8-27B uses these observations to generate a textual memory summary of gripper motion, object motion, and state changes. The model then uses the summary and the current frame to assess progress and explain its assessment. The summary is used during annotation and omitted from the training input.

Where recorded EEF poses are available, we derive the next-motion description by comparing the EEF poses and gripper openings at the current keyframe and 0.5 seconds later. We ignore translation, rotation, and opening changes below their respective thresholds and map the remaining changes to directional phrases describing EEF motion and gripper opening or closing. These phrases are combined into a sentence such as “ The gripper moves forward, tilts down, and rotates clockwise. ”

We apply this pipeline to selected real-robot and UMI datasets, producing 2,564,851 annotated keyframes (Table 4 ). The annotations yield both ECoT-annotated VLA trajectories and VL examples whose targets contain the scene description, progress assessment, and, where available, next-motion description. We additionally use the released annotations from ProcCorpus-60M ( Feng et al., 2026 ) .

### 3 Model Architecture

As shown in Figure 3 , PDRVLA consists of a Qwen3.5-4B VLM backbone ( Qwen Team, 2026b ) , the Physical Dynamics Module, and an action expert. Given visual observations and a task instruction, the backbone provides intermediate features to the physical module, which produces a fixed-length sequence of physical tokens. The action expert combines these tokens with the final-layer VLM features and the current proprioceptive state to generate a chunk of robot commands.

> Figure 3: PDRVLA architecture. (a) Physical tokens complement final-layer VLM features as inputs to the action expert. Starting from Gaussian noise, Euler updates integrate predicted velocities into an action chunk. (b) Learnable queries attend to shared VLM features at every physical-module block before projection to physical tokens. Camera views and token sequences are schematic.


#### 3.1 Input Construction

The VLM input is a single user message containing the current camera views followed by the task text, formatted with Qwen3.5’s native chat template. Views follow the order specified in each dataset’s camera configuration. We prefix the task instruction with the robot embodiment, arm and end-effector types, and control frequency when available, using the format embodiment <robot>, arm <arm>, hand <hand>. control <rate> Hz. <instruction> . For action generation with or without subtask conditioning, we use the VLM features directly without appending an assistant-generation prefix. The current proprioceptive state is supplied separately to the action expert.

Subtask descriptions can be appended to the task instruction as Current step: <subtask> . During training, this text comes from the trajectory annotations. At inference, PDRVLA can first generate a subtask description from the current observations and task instruction, then append it to the instruction for the action-conditioning pass. Without subtask conditioning, the policy generates actions directly from the current observations and original task instruction, together with the robot state.

For ECoT-conditioned action generation, the VLM first generates a response describing the scene, task progress, and next motion, which provides additional context to the action expert.


#### 3.2 Physical Dynamics Module

Later VLM layers may suppress visual information useful for predicting physical change. Layer-wise studies have found stronger perceptual representations at intermediate layers of large language models and a prominent role for these layers in accessing visual information ( Jain et al., 2025 ; Kaduri et al., 2025 ) . Motivated by these findings, at timestep $t$ , we use the VLM’s layer- $l$ features $H_{t}^{(l)}$ for the current multi-view observations and task instruction as input to the Physical Dynamics Module. In our implementation, $l=8$ for a backbone with 32 decoder blocks. The action expert also uses the final-layer features from the same backbone pass.

As shown in Figure 3 (b), the module uses 50 learnable queries to extract a fixed set of physical tokens $C_{t}$ from $H_{t}^{(l)}$ . Across 4 Transformer blocks, the queries retrieve information from the VLM features through cross-attention, exchange information through self-attention, and pass through a feed-forward layer. Each block has a hidden width of 1024, 4 attention heads, and a feed-forward expansion ratio of 1. A final projection maps the query outputs to the VLM hidden width, producing 50 physical tokens of width 2560. Future-prediction supervision trains the module to encode dynamics-related information in these tokens (Section 4.1 ), which are subsequently supplied to the action expert alongside the final-layer VLM features. The module adds approximately $57.3$ M parameters ( $1.4\%$ of the 4B backbone).

#### 3.3 Action Generation

At timestep $t$ , the action policy receives the final-layer VLM features $H_{t}^{\text{last}}$ , the physical tokens $C_{t}$ , and a proprioceptive vector $q_{t}$ encoding the current robot state. For Cartesian control, the EEF pose in $q_{t}$ comprises three position coordinates and a continuous 6D orientation representation obtained by flattening the first two columns of its rotation matrix. The policy predicts a chunk $\mathbf{A}_{t}=[\mathbf{a}_{t,0},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{t,K-1}]$ of $K=8$ robot commands, where $k$ in $\mathbf{a}_{t,k}$ indexes a step within the chunk predicted at $t$ . The complete chunk is executed before the policy replans from a new observation.

Training a single policy across heterogeneous robots requires a common representation that accommodates different controller interfaces. We use a fixed-dimensional action vector with separate left and right manipulator slots. Each slot reserves dimensions for four component types:

- • arm-joint positions: 7 dimensions;

- • end-effector (EEF) pose: 9 reserved dimensions, of which 6 encode the action;

- • dexterous-hand joint positions: 12 dimensions;

- • gripper opening: 1 dimension.

The two slots together form a 58-dimensional vector. The EEF slot shares its reserved width with the 9D proprioceptive pose representation; its action occupies the first 6 dimensions, with the remaining 3 padded and masked. EEF actions are expressed relative to the pose at the beginning of the chunk, while arm-joint positions, dexterous-hand joint positions, and gripper openings are absolute targets. Dimensions unsupported by an embodiment’s controller are padded and excluded from the training loss; execution uses only the supported components.

For Cartesian control, all EEF targets within a chunk are expressed relative to the EEF pose at the chunk’s start. Let $\mathbf{p}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$ and $R_{t}\in SO(3)$ denote this starting position and orientation, and let $\mathbf{p}^{*}_{t+k}$ and $R^{*}_{t+k}$ denote the target position and orientation for step $k=0,\ldots,K-1$ . The superscript $*$ denotes a target. Omitting the left/right manipulator index, each 6D EEF action comprises a translation delta and a rotation delta:

[Tableau] $$\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{eef}}_{t,k}=\left[\mathbf{p}^{*}_{t+k}-\mathbf{p}_{t},\;\operatorname{Log}\!\left(R^{*}_{t+k}R_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{6},$$ (2)

where $\operatorname{Log}:SO(3)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}$ maps the relative rotation to an axis–angle vector. Target poses are obtained as described in Section 2.4 .

We train a Diffusion Transformer (DiT)-based action expert with conditional flow matching. It receives a noisy action chunk, whose action vectors are mapped to action tokens by an input projection shared across embodiments. We concatenate the final-layer VLM features and physical tokens along the token dimension to form the conditioning sequence:

[Tableau] $$X_{t}=\left[H_{t}^{\text{last}};\,C_{t}\right],$$ (3)

The expert alternates self-attention over its input tokens with cross-attention to $X_{t}$ . The current proprioceptive state $q_{t}$ is encoded as a prefix token, while flow time conditions the action-token encoding and the Transformer’s adaptive layer normalization. A shared output projection maps the resulting action-token features to a velocity field over the chunk. The action expert contains approximately $150$ M parameters. At inference, we initialize the chunk with Gaussian noise and integrate the predicted velocity field from flow time 0 to 1 using four Euler steps. Section 4 defines the interpolation path and masked velocity-matching loss.

#### 4.1 Physical Dynamics Pretraining

We pretrain the Physical Dynamics Module (Section 3.2 ) to encode future appearance, geometry, and motion in its physical tokens. A direct prediction objective supervises these tokens using future RGB, depth, and optical-flow latent features. Because the module and its prediction head are trained from scratch, we also use a frozen pretrained video model to provide auxiliary supervision for representation learning.

For physical pretraining, each example pairs the current observations and task instruction with a five-frame video sampled from a recorded clip spanning the current timestep and the following 30 action steps. The RGB frames provide appearance targets, while DA3 ( Lin et al., 2025 ) and DPFlow ( Morimitsu et al., 2025 ) generate depth and optical-flow pseudo-labels for geometry and motion supervision. Following RynnWorld-4D ( Zhao et al., 2026b ) , depth values are clipped to a fixed range and mapped to grayscale intensities, while optical-flow fields are color-encoded to form the corresponding depth and flow videos. We encode the three video targets into compact latent features rather than predicting them in pixel space. LingBot-Video’s frozen Wan video variational autoencoder (VAE) ( Team Wan et al., 2025 ) provides these representations, and the same encoded targets are reused for direct prediction.

The direct prediction head applies layer normalization and a linear projection to the physical tokens $C_{t}$ , then uses learned spatial queries for scaled dot-product attention pooling. Three modality-specific linear readouts predict future RGB, depth, and optical-flow features. Let $z_{\mathrm{future}}^{m}$ denote the future portion of the VAE-encoded video for modality $m\in\{r,d,f\}$ , corresponding to RGB, depth, and optical flow. We apply adaptive average pooling to obtain $y^{m}=\operatorname{Pool}(z_{\mathrm{future}}^{m})$ . This reduces the spatial resolution of the prediction target while retaining a coarse spatial layout, so the lightweight head need not reconstruct the full-resolution latent features.

To reduce differences in target scale across modalities and feature dimensions, we standardize the pooled targets as

[Tableau] $$\widetilde{y}^{m}=\frac{y^{m}-\mu^{m}}{s^{m}},$$ (4)

where $\mu^{m}$ and $s^{m}$ are estimated from training samples separately for each modality, channel, and spatial position. A lower bound on the standard deviation prevents excessive amplification of low-variance features. Denoting the head’s prediction for modality $m$ by $g_{\eta}^{m}(C_{t})$ , the direct prediction loss is

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{direct}}=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{m\in\{r,d,f\}}\operatorname{MSE}\left(g_{\eta}^{m}(C_{t}),\,\widetilde{y}^{m}\right).$$ (5)

Beyond direct feature regression, we use a pretrained video model to guide physical representation learning, following related video-conditioning approaches ( InternRobotics, 2026 ; Tang et al., 2026 ) . The frozen LingBot-Video-Dense-1.3B model ( Ma et al., 2026 ) receives an RGB latent clip, the physical tokens $C_{t}$ as conditioning inputs, and a noise timestep. Future-prediction losses train the Physical Dynamics Module to provide context for this video predictor. In addition to its frozen RGB output head, two trainable heads read LingBot’s final hidden features to predict depth and optical-flow velocities, extending the auxiliary supervision to future geometry and motion.

Within the RGB latent clip, the current features remain clean and the future features are mixed with Gaussian noise. Let $z_{\mathrm{current}}^{r}$ and $z_{\mathrm{future}}^{r}$ denote these two parts of the encoded clip. We sample a noise level $\sigma\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)$ and Gaussian noise $\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ with the same shape as the future latent. The RGB input is constructed as

[Tableau] $$z^{r}_{\sigma,\mathrm{current}}=z_{\mathrm{current}}^{r},\qquad z^{r}_{\sigma,\mathrm{future}}=(1-\sigma)z_{\mathrm{future}}^{r}+\sigma\epsilon.$$ (6)

The three output heads predict future latent velocities $\widehat{v}_{\mathrm{future}}^{m}$ for $m\in\{r,d,f\}$ . For the linear data-to-noise path in Eq. 6 , the target velocity is the difference between the noise and the clean latent. We use the same noise tensor $\epsilon$ for all three modalities, yielding targets $\epsilon-z_{\mathrm{future}}^{m}$ . The auxiliary loss sums the three equally weighted velocity errors:

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{WM}}=\sum_{m\in\{r,d,f\}}\operatorname{MSE}\!\left(\widehat{v}_{\mathrm{future}}^{m},\epsilon-z_{\mathrm{future}}^{m}\right).$$ (7)

Gradients from $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{WM}}$ train the depth and optical-flow heads and pass through the frozen LingBot backbone to train the Physical Dynamics Module. The RGB head and LingBot backbone are not updated.

The complete physical-pretraining objective combines direct future prediction with the auxiliary flow-matching loss:

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pre}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{WM}}+\lambda(k)\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{direct}},$$ (8)

where $k$ denotes the optimization step and $\lambda(k)=0.5\min(k/2000,1)$ increases the direct prediction weight linearly from 0 to 0.5 over the first 2000 steps, after which it remains constant.

Physical pretraining updates the Physical Dynamics Module, the depth and optical-flow output heads, and the direct prediction head. The VLM and the pretrained LingBot-Video components remain frozen.


#### 4.2 Action Policy Training

After physical pretraining, we attach the action expert and jointly train the complete policy on VLA examples and the general, spatial, embodied, ECoT, and subtask VL data. The direct prediction head is retained to supervise future RGB, depth, and optical-flow features during action learning. The video world model and its RGB, depth, and optical-flow output heads are no longer used.

The VLM, pretrained Physical Dynamics Module, action expert, and direct prediction head are all trainable. Physical tokens are concatenated with the final-layer VLM features to form $X_{t}=[H_{t}^{\text{last}};C_{t}]$ for the action expert (Section 3.3 ). The intermediate VLM features entering the physical module are detached: action and direct prediction losses update the physical module but do not propagate into the VLM through this input. The action loss updates the VLM through the final-layer conditioning path, while the direct losses also update their prediction head. VL supervision updates only the VLM.

Conditional flow matching trains the action expert to transform Gaussian noise into an action chunk. Given a target chunk $\mathbf{A}_{t}$ containing $K=8$ actions, we sample noise $\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ of the same shape and a flow time $\tau\in[0,1]$ . The interpolated chunk and target velocity are

[Tableau] $$x_{\tau}=(1-\tau)\xi+\tau\mathbf{A}_{t},\qquad v^{*}=\mathbf{A}_{t}-\xi.$$ (9)

The action expert $v_{\psi}(x_{\tau},\tau,X_{t},q_{t})$ predicts the velocity from the interpolated chunk, flow time, conditioning features $X_{t}=[H_{t}^{\text{last}};C_{t}]$ , and current proprioceptive state.

Frames marked invalid during data processing (Section 2.4 ) are excluded as starting observations for training samples. Within a retained chunk, action steps whose target frames are invalid are excluded from the loss, as are padded steps beyond the episode boundary. For each sample $i$ , a two-dimensional binary supervision mask $M_{i}$ has rows corresponding to chunk steps and columns to action-vector dimensions. An entry is 1 only if the step is valid and the dimension is supported by the robot’s controller; otherwise, it is 0. The masked velocity-matching loss over a minibatch $\mathcal{B}$ is

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{act}}=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\|M_{i}\odot(\widehat{v}_{i}-v_{i}^{*})\|_{F}^{2}}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\|M_{i}\|_{1}+\delta},$$ (10)

where $\widehat{v}_{i}$ and $v_{i}^{*}$ are the action expert’s predicted velocity and the target velocity for sample $i$ , respectively, and $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication. The loss is averaged over all supervised action elements in the minibatch, with $\delta=10^{-6}$ preventing division by zero.

Alongside action prediction, VL examples supervise the VLM through next-token prediction of target responses. The VL loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VL}}$ is the cross-entropy on response tokens only, averaged first within each example and then across examples. It updates only the VLM, without passing through the action expert or Physical Dynamics Module.

For VLA examples with future targets, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{direct}}$ averages the retained direct prediction head’s RGB, depth, and optical-flow prediction errors, as defined in Eq. 5 . Target construction follows Section 4.1 , including pooling and the standardization in Eq. 4 . Each optimization step combines VLA and VL supervision with these direct prediction losses:

[Tableau] $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{policy}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{act}}+0.1\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VL}}+0.1\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{direct}}.$$ (11)

To reduce closely spaced training examples while retaining changes in end-effector control, we select chunk starting frames from a regular temporal grid at approximately 8 Hz and from frames with changes in gripper or dexterous-hand target commands. Selection is performed when building the training index. Each selected frame anchors an eight-step action chunk read from the original, temporally aligned stream without further subsampling.


### 5 Experiments

We evaluate PDRVLA on four simulation benchmarks covering single-arm and bimanual manipulation, robustness to observation and instruction perturbations, and WidowX manipulation under visual-matching settings. Performance is reported as task success rates and compared with published VLA baselines. Section 5.3 examines the effect of world-model-assisted pretraining on future prediction. Additional studies examine subtask conditioning and its update frequency, auxiliary future object-pose supervision, and ECoT supervision during training versus generation at inference.


#### 5.1 Experimental Setup

LIBERO Liu et al. (2023) evaluates single-arm manipulation across four suites: Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long, each containing 10 tasks. We fine-tune the pretrained policy jointly on LIBERO demonstrations from all four suites for 40,000 updates with a global batch size of 128. Adaptation uses dense valid starting frames rather than the frame-selection procedure used during policy pretraining. We evaluate 500 episodes per suite (2,000 in total) and report success rates for each suite and their average.

LIBERO-Plus Fei et al. (2025) evaluates the LIBERO tasks under camera, robot, language, lighting, background, noise, and layout perturbations. We evaluate the LIBERO checkpoint and report success rates for each perturbation category and across all 10,030 evaluation cases.

RoboTwin 2.0 Chen et al. (2025a) evaluates bimanual manipulation on 50 Aloha-AgileX tasks. We fine-tune the pretrained policy for 150,000 updates with a global batch size of 1024, using the RoboTwin 2.0 Aloha-AgileX clean50 and randomized500 demonstration sets. Valid starting frames are sampled densely. We evaluate the resulting checkpoint for 100 episodes per task in each setting and report clean and randomized success rates together with their average.

SimplerEnv Li et al. (2024) evaluates WidowX manipulation on four Bridge visual-matching tasks. We fine-tune the pretrained policy on the full Bridge training set for 50,000 updates with a global batch size of 128, using dense valid starting frames. We report per-task success rates and their average.

### 6 Conclusion

We presented PDRVLA, a VLA model that uses future prediction to learn compact physical representations for action generation. Direct supervision and a pretrained video world model shape these representations during training; at deployment, the policy uses the learned physical tokens without running the video model or prediction heads. Results across four simulation benchmarks demonstrate strong manipulation performance, while the prediction study shows that world-model assistance improves accuracy over direct supervision alone.

Future work will explore predictive targets beyond RGB, depth, and optical flow, including object-centric geometry, contact states, and material properties. We will investigate how these cues complement one another and contribute to action learning while retaining a lightweight policy.

### Appendix A Implementation and Training Details


#### A.1 Action-Expert Configuration

The action expert has 16 Transformer blocks, alternating cross-attention and self-attention, with a token width of 768 and 12 attention heads of width 64. A final token projection maps features to 1024 dimensions before they are decoded into action velocities. The expert’s input sequence consists of a proprioceptive prefix token, 32 learnable tokens, and 8 action tokens with learned positional embeddings. Attention and feed-forward dropout is 0.2, and the proprioceptive input is dropped with probability 0.2 during training.

During action flow-matching training, we randomly sample the flow time $\tau$ to vary the mixture of Gaussian noise and target actions in Eq. 9 . Smaller values give more weight to noise, while larger values give more weight to the target actions. Specifically, we draw $b\sim\operatorname{Beta}(1.5,1)$ and set $\tau=1-\min(b,s)/s$ , with $s=0.999$ . Flow time is discretized into 1000 buckets and embedded both in the action encoder and in the adaptive normalization of the Transformer blocks and final output projection. Each example is repeated 8 times with independently sampled flow times and noise for the flow-matching objective. Inference uses four Euler steps of size 0.25 over $[0,1]$ .


#### A.2 Frame Selection

We select starting frames for VLA training at regular intervals of approximately 0.125 s (8 Hz). We also add frames where a gripper or dexterous-hand target command changes by more than a threshold between consecutive frames. Each manipulator’s available gripper and hand channels are checked separately. We combine the two sets of candidates and remove frames that are too close together, keeping frames with command changes in preference to regularly sampled frames. The minimum spacing is 0.01 s, converted to a frame interval using the episode’s frame rate. The remaining candidates must pass the sample-validity checks described in Section 4.2 .


#### A.3 World-Model-Assisted Pretraining Comparison

The comparison in Section 5.3 tests whether adding world-model supervision improves future prediction. ### Appendix B Physical-Module Design Choices

We select four physical-module settings in sequence: the VLM input layer, cross-attention placement, direct-supervision weighting, and query count. At each stage, we retain the settings selected in the preceding stages and compare candidates for the next choice. For each candidate, we train the physical module with the original Qwen3.5-4B backbone, then freeze both the VLM and physical module and fit a new linear readout to predict future features. The readout measures how readily future information can be recovered from the learned representations. We use validation scores to select configurations, with the single-seed results summarized in Table 15 .

The design study uses a 500k-example training pool, 6,000 optimization steps, seed 42, and learning rate $3\times 10^{-4}$ . Physical-module hidden width, block count, head count, output width, and feed-forward expansion ratio are fixed at 1024, 4, 4, 2560, and 1. The world-model-assisted loss has weight 1 throughout these design comparisons.

We first compare VLM input layers using 256 queries, cross-attention at every physical-module block, and a constant direct-loss weight of 0.1. We then fix the selected VLM Block 8 and compare cross-attention placements, retaining 256 queries and weight 0.1; parameter counts vary with the number of cross-attention blocks. Next, we retain Block 8, every-block cross-attention, and 256 queries while comparing direct-loss weights and warm-up schedules. Finally, we compare query counts using Block 8, every-block cross-attention, and the selected direct-loss schedule, which warms up to 0.5 over 2,000 steps.

During readout fitting and evaluation, the VLM and physical module remain frozen. Masked mean pooling produces a 2560-dimensional representation, from which an affine ridge readout predicts pooled future RGB, depth, and color-encoded flow latent targets. The probe data span five source families, with 2,048 clips for readout fitting and a separate set of 1,024 validation clips for scoring each configuration. Within the fitting pool, an episode-separated split of 1,665 fitting clips and 383 validation clips is used to select the regularization strength. We then refit the readout on all 2,048 fitting clips before scoring it on the separate validation set. Readout fitting uses FP64 standardized spectral ridge, with standardization statistics estimated from fitting data only.
